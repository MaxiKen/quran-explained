## Verse 121

Verse 121 says: *"Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it. As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers."*

In simple terms, the verse introduces the positive counterpart to the whole preceding section. After the long indictment of the People of the Book who cast the Scripture behind their backs, the Qur'an now says: those to whom the Book has been given and who follow it as it should be followed — it is they who truly believe in it. The verse recognizes the existence of the faithful among the People of the Book, and it defines the standard by which genuine belief is measured.

"Follow it as it should be followed" — the Arabic is *yatlūnahu ḥaqqa tilāwatihi*, literally "they recite it with true recitation." The classical scholars explain that this "recitation" is not merely the sound of the words; it is the complete reception of the Book. Ibn Abbas explained it as making lawful what the Book makes lawful, prohibiting what it makes prohibited, reciting it as it was revealed, not changing the words from their places, and not interpreting it with other than its real meaning. In other words, the true recitation is the living of the Book: the reciter is the one who obeys, follows, and does not distort. Ibn Kathir notes that the verse's standard applies generally to the community of the Prophet as well: the one who reads the Qur'an with true recitation is the one who receives it with faith and practice.

The verse also answers a question implicit in the whole section: is the People of the Book as a whole condemned? No. The Qur'an has been indicting the majority who rejected the messenger, distorted the Scripture, and broke the covenant. But it now names the exception — and, importantly, it identifies the exception by the standard of the Book itself. The faithful among them are not the ones with the identity; they are the ones whose recitation of the Book is true. And the reverse is stated with equal force: "As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers." It is not the rejection of the new messenger that is the test in this verse; it is the rejection of the Book they already hold. The People of the Book who kept their Scripture properly could not reject the messenger who confirmed it — the two rejections are one.

In modern life, the verse is a criterion for every reader of revelation. The question is not whether one owns the Book — every believer owns it — but whether one follows it as it should be followed: making lawful what it makes lawful, prohibiting what it prohibits, reading it as it was revealed, and refusing to twist its meaning. The verse's verdict is that the one who rejects the Book after this is the loser; the one who follows it truly believes, whatever his community.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 122

Verse 122 says: *"O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others."*

In simple terms, the verse is a direct address to the Children of Israel: remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others. The command of remembrance is the recurring refrain of the surah (2:40, 2:47), and it is the method of the message: the people whose history is a history of favours and failures are called to remember the favours, so that the failures can be measured.

The verse has two objects of remembrance: the favours (*ni'mah*) and the honour (*faddala*). The favours are the concrete acts: the deliverance from Pharaoh, the parting of the sea, the manna and the quails, the Book, the prophets, the covenant. The honour is the status: "how I honoured you above the others." As the surah stated in 2:47, this honour is to be understood in its historical context — the Children of Israel were honoured among the nations of their era, chosen for the prophetic line and the covenant of the age before the final messenger. It is not a claim of perpetual or unconditional superiority; it is the record of the divine choice in history.

Why does the Qur'an call this to remembrance after the long indictment? Because the privilege is the measure of the obligation. The people who were given more are asked for more. The address is therefore not a compliment but a summons: the favours are evidence, and the evidence stands against those who have responded to the favours with the rejection of the messengers. The same command appears in the surah's opening address to the Children of Israel (2:40) and returns here as the surah moves from the history of the covenant to the memory of Abraham — the ancestor whose religion both the Jews and the Christians were claiming.

In modern life, the verse is addressed to every community that has been given more — the community of every prophet, every nation that has received the Book, every person who has been given faith, health, security, and knowledge. The command is the same: remember. The person who forgets the favours becomes ungrateful in the most subtle way: not by denying that they happened, but by no longer being changed by them. Remembrance — in prayer, in gratitude, in the daily turning to the Giver — is the Qur'an's medicine for the amnesia that makes privilege a possession rather than an obligation.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 123

Verse 123 says: *"And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given."*

In simple terms, the verse now completes the address to the Children of Israel with the warning: guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. The verse then makes the terror of that Day precise in three negations: no ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given.

The verse is the second appearance of this description in the surah (2:48 used nearly the same words), and it is placed here deliberately, after the citation of the favours and before the story of Abraham. The logic is the warning of accountability: the people who were given the favours and the honour, and who are now being addressed about their response, are being told that the Day of the reckoning cannot be paid off. The negations close every exit: the ransom (the payment), the intercession (the mediator), the help (the ally) — the three forms of rescue that human beings trust in.

The three negations require careful understanding, because the Qur'an elsewhere affirms intercession — the Qur'an itself states that intercession with Him can only take place with His permission (2:255) and that those whom He permits will intercede. The verse before us is not denying that intercession will happen; it is denying the assumption that it is available to everyone without qualification. The intercession of the verse's context is the automatic rescue that the claimants of privilege expected: the belief that their ancestors, their prophets, their lineage would stand for them on the Day regardless of their own deeds. That intercession — the purchased, inherited, assumed intercession — does not exist. Intercession, where the Qur'an affirms it, is a grace granted by Allah to whom He wills, and it is granted to those who have believed and done good — not to those who demanded it as a right.

"Guard yourselves" — *ittaqū* — the command is the whole message: the protection of the Day is not bought or inherited; it is built in this life by the awareness of Allah. The people of the verse had been assuming the protection of their history; the verse says the protection is personal. The Day arrives with no helpers and no payment, and the only thing that stands is what the person has done.

In modern life, the verse remains the most direct answer to the religion of safety. Every community and every person has a version of the assumed rescue: the membership, the ancestry, the connections, the reputation, the good standing that will stand in for the deed. The verse removes all of them at once. The Day is the most personal event in existence — no soul will be of any help to another — and the only currency that survives it is the one the person has sent ahead.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 124

Verse 124 says: *"˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, "I will certainly make you into a role model for the people." Abraham asked, "What about my offspring?" Allah replied, "My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.""*

In simple terms, the verse turns from the Children of Israel to Abraham — the ancestor whom both the Jews and the Christians claimed as the founder of their religion. The verse records the divine test: Abraham was tested with certain commandments, and he fulfilled them. The reward: "I will certainly make you into a role model for the people." Abraham asked about his offspring, and the reply cut through every claim of inheritance: "My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers."

The "commandments" — *kalimāt* — are a famous subject of discussion among the commentators. They are the divine commands and trials through which Abraham was tested: the exegetes differ in enumerating them — the rites he was commanded to establish, the sacrifice of his son, the settling of his family in the barren valley, the commands of the hajj — but the point the verse makes is the outcome, not the list: "which he fulfilled." The verse's emphasis is on the completion: the trials were not met halfway, not negotiated, not explained away. Abraham fulfilled them. The word *atammahunna* — he completed them — is the standard: not merely obedience but completion.

The reward is the appointment: "I will certainly make you into a role model for the people" — the word *imām*: a leader, a model, one who is followed. The appointment is the point of the whole passage: Abraham did not receive his standing by inheritance; he earned it through the fulfilment of the trials. The Qur'an's history is the exact opposite of the assumption the People of the Book had built: their claim was that the prophetic and religious standing belonged to their line by birth; the Qur'an's answer is that the standing belongs to those who are tested and fulfil, and that the line only shares in it through the fulfilment.

"And from my offspring?" — Abraham's question is the natural one of a father and a prophet: the man who has been made a leader wants the leadership to continue in his descendants. The answer is the most important sentence for the entire dispute: "My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers." The covenant is not a blood possession; it is a covenant with righteousness. The children of Abraham are those who follow Abraham's way — the path of submission and fulfilment — not those who merely descend from him. As the commentators note, the principle severs every claim of entitlement based on lineage: the divine honour and the divine covenant belong to those who are upright, not to those who simply inherit the name.

In modern life, the verse is the definitive correction of inherited religion on every level: the religious identity that is assumed from the family, the leadership that is claimed from the name, the security that is drawn from the ancestry. The verse says the covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers — and the one who wants the covenant must ask the same question Abraham was tested with: what are the commandments, and have I fulfilled them?

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 125

Verse 125 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, "˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer." And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺."*

In simple terms, the verse now moves from the test of Abraham to the House: the Sacred House was made a centre and a sanctuary for the people. The verse then gives the two commands that belong to the House: take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer; and the covenant entrusted to Abraham and Ishmael: purify the House for those who circle it, meditate in it, and bow and prostrate in prayer.

The "centre" — *mathābah* — carries the sense of a place of return, a place to which people come back repeatedly. Ibn Kathir explains that Allah made the House a place of resort for people and a place of safety: the hearts are eager to visit it and never tire of it, and those who enter it are safe even from the conflicts that surround it. The House is not a building that belongs to one community; it is the place of return for the people, and the verse's "sanctuary" — *amnan* — is the security that belongs to it: the place where the warring and the feuding come under the peace of Allah.

The "standing-place of Abraham" is the stone on which he stood while raising the walls of the House, and the Qur'an commands it to be taken as a site of prayer. The narration recorded in Sahih Muslim relates that Umar suggested to the Prophet that the Maqam be taken as a place of prayer, and the verse was then revealed — and the Prophet himself, in his farewell hajj, recited the verse and prayed two units after the tawaf with the Maqam between him and the House. The verse's command is thus a piece of the worship and a sign of the continuity: the prayer of the believers is the prayer of Abraham, and the place of his standing is the place of their worship.

"Purify My House" — the command entrusted to Abraham and Ishmael is the purification of the House from all that does not belong to it: the idols, the filth, the corruption, and, in every age, the turning of the sacred into the commercial. The verse describes the worshippers in four states — those who circle it (the tawaf), who meditate in it (the staying, the contemplation), and who bow and prostrate (the prayer) — the whole range of the worship of the House. The House is purified for them, and the purification is the covenant that the builders and the worshippers inherit together.

In modern life, the verse is the Qur'an's teaching on sacred space: the place of worship is not an end in itself; it is a centre to which the hearts return and a sanctuary in which the conflicts cease. And the verse's command of purification applies to every "House of Allah" — the masjid of the neighbourhood, the heart of the believer, the religion itself: each is to be kept clear of what does not belong to it, for those who circle, who meditate, and who bow and prostrate.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 126

Verse 126 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day." He answered, "As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!""*

In simple terms, the verse records the prayer of Abraham for the city: make this city secure and provide fruits to its people — those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day. The prayer is pointed: Abraham prays for the city, but he qualifies the people: the believers among them. And the divine answer extends the request, with a warning: as for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire.

The prayer of Abraham is the prayer of the builder of the city. He had left his family in a barren valley, and he asks Allah for what only Allah can give: security and provision. The city he prays for is the city of the Kaaba — the same city that the surah has described as the centre and the sanctuary. Abraham's prayer is concrete: peace — the absence of the fear that makes life impossible — and provision — the fruits of the earth in a land that produces nothing on its own. The commentators note that the prayer was answered: Mecca became the secure city, and the provision of the earth was brought to it — the caravans, the trade, and the fruits of every land.

The qualification in Abraham's prayer — "those among them who believe" — is the same principle the previous verse established: the covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers. Abraham the intercessor cannot include the disbelievers in his prayer, because the connection to Allah is not a matter of location but of faith. The city may be secure for the believers; the blessings of the city belong to the people of faith.

The divine answer contains the most important note of the verse: "As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire." Allah answers Abraham's prayer by extending the provision: even the disbeliever in the city will receive the fruits — the world is not denied to the rejecter. The Qur'an's message is that the worldly blessing is not the proof of the divine favour; the disbeliever receives the security and the fruits and uses them for a while, and the "a little while" is the measure of the worldly life. The final destination is then named with the words of the verse: "What an evil destination!"

In modern life, the verse corrects the most common error of prosperity theology: the belief that the blessing of this world proves the approval of Allah. The verse says the blessing is given to the disbeliever too — security and fruits, for a little while. The believer's security is the city he cannot lose: the Hereafter. And the verse is also the prayer of every founder and every parent: the prayer for the place, the security, the provision — qualified by the prayer that the people of the place be people of faith.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 127

Verse 127 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ "Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*"

In simple terms, the verse now gives one of the most moving scenes in the Qur'an: Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundation of the House together, and praying as they work: "Our Lord! Accept this from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing." The scene is not primarily architecture; it is worship. The building of the House of Allah is itself an act of worship, and the prayer that accompanies it is the worship's completion.

The classical commentators give the details of the scene. Ibn Kathir relates, from the account transmitted in the tafsir, that as the walls rose, Ishmael brought his father the stone — the Maqam — on which Abraham stood while laying the stones, and the two prayed together, father and son, at every stage: "Our Lord! Accept this from us." The verse's picture is of the partnership: the father raising the foundation and the son bringing the stones, and both making the same prayer. The House of Allah, in the Qur'an's record, was built by a father and a son in a state of submission, and it was built with the prayer for acceptance.

The prayer begins with "Our Lord! Accept this from us" — *taqabbal minnā*. The prophets, the ones closest to Allah, do not presume that the work is accepted; they pray for the acceptance. The verse is the Qur'an's teaching on the relation between the deed and the divine reception: nothing is accepted automatically; the acceptance is sought, and the seeker knows that the All-Hearing hears the prayer and the All-Knowing knows the intention. The finest building, done for Allah and left without the prayer, is incomplete; the humblest deed, done with the prayer, is complete.

The partnership is also a message: the worship that Allah built into the world is not the worship of the solitary worshipper. Abraham and Ishmael — the father of the Arabs and the father of the Jews, both prophets — raised the House together, and their prayer is the same prayer: "Our Lord! Accept this from us." The House is the inheritance of the two lines, and the lesson of the scene is that the religion of the House is the religion of both.

In modern life, the verse teaches the ethic of the work that is done for Allah: the work is not its own reward, and the doer prays for the acceptance. The verse is for every builder of a mosque, a school, a family, a life — the work is raised, and then the prayer: accept this from us. And the verse's final sentence teaches the believer to work in the company of others: the greatest works are the ones in which the hands of the many are raised, and the prayer of the many is the same prayer.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 128

Verse 128 says: *"Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse continues the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael: make us both fully submit to You; and from our descendants a nation that will submit to You; show us our rituals; and turn to us in grace. The prayer is the definition of Islam in the form of a petition: submission — of themselves and of the descendants — and the rites that the submission takes.

"Make us both fully submit to You" — the word for submission is the same root as *islām*: the complete surrender of the self to Allah. Abraham and Ishmael, already the builders of the House and the prophets of Allah, pray that the submission be made complete. The verse's language reveals the Qur'an's understanding of the religion: it is not a condition that is reached once; it is a state that is prayed for, a submission that deepens, a surrender that is requested. The prophets do not claim to have finished submitting; they ask that the submission be made.

"And from our descendants a nation that will submit to you" — the prayer extends beyond the self to the community. The builders of the House pray that the House's worshippers be a nation of submitters — not merely a nation associated with the House, but a nation whose relation to Allah is surrender. The verse is the Qur'an's answer to every claim of descent: the descendants of Abraham are those who submit — the nation of the prayer, not the line of the blood.

"Show us our rituals" — *manāsik* — the rites of the worship of the House: the hajj, the tawaf, the standing, the sacrifice. The prayer asks Allah to teach the rites — the rituals are not invented by the people; they are shown by the Lord. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of worship: the form of the worship comes from Allah, and the worshipper receives it. "And turn to us in grace" — the prayer of repentance: the builders ask that Allah turn to them with mercy, for the work is not done without the need of the turning.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the two dimensions of the religion: the inner submission and the outer rites. The person who has the rites without the submission has the form without the surrender; the person who claims the submission without the rites has the spirit without the form. Abraham and Ishmael prayed for both, and both are named in the prayer that is recited in the supplications of the pilgrims to this day: the submission of the self, the nation of the submitters, the rites shown, and the mercy that turns to the repentant.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 129

Verse 129 says: *"Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. Indeed, You ˹alone˺ are the Almighty, All-Wise."*"

In simple terms, the verse completes the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael with the greatest of their requests: raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. The prayer is the prayer for the final Messenger — and the classical commentators identify its fulfillment in the Prophet Muhammad with a confidence the whole Islamic tradition shares.

The prayer's vision of the messenger is precise. The messenger does four things: he recites the revelations — he delivers the message as it was revealed, aloud, so that the people hear it; he teaches the Book — the meaning, the understanding, the law; he teaches wisdom — the application, the discernment, the way the Book is lived; and he purifies them — the inward cleansing from the idols of the heart, the corruption of the soul, and the falsehood of the habits. The four verbs together are the mission of Islam: the recitation, the teaching, the wisdom, and the purification. The order matters: the message comes first, then the understanding, then the wisdom, and then the life that the whole process produces.

The fulfillment of the prayer is the theme of the surah's later verses: the Qur'an describes the Prophet in the same terms — sent to recite, teach, and purify (2:151) — and the tradition records a narration in which the Prophet said that he was the answer to the prayer of his father Abraham. The scholars note the significance: the messenger prayed for by the builders of the Kaaba came from among them — an Arab, from the descendants of Ishmael — and the verse's "from among them" is the answer to the objection of the People of the Book, who had claimed that the prophecy belonged only to their line. The prayer of the first builder of the House was for a messenger from the people of the House.

The verse ends with the divine names: "the Almighty, All-Wise." The prayer is answered by the One who is able to raise the messenger and wise in the choice of him. The "Almighty" is the capacity; the "All-Wise" is the selection: the mission is not only the power to send but the wisdom to choose the one who can bear it.

In modern life, the verse is the believer's prayer for the teachers, the guides, and the parents: raise from among them one who recites, teaches, and purifies. And it is the criterion by which every claimant to the mission of the messenger is measured: the one who recites the revelation, teaches the Book, teaches the wisdom, and purifies the people — this is the mission; anything less is a corruption of the prayer.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 130

Verse 130 says: *"And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous."*

In simple terms, the verse now turns from the prayer of Abraham to the verdict on his religion: who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool? The question is rhetorical, and its answer is the condemnation: the rejection of Abraham's way is the act of the fool — the one who has fooled himself. The verse then sets Abraham's standing: We chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous.

"The faith of Abraham" — *millat Ibrāhīm* — is the religion of the man who submitted, the religion that the Qur'an defines as the upright faith, free of polytheism. The verse's question is addressed to the claimants of Abraham: the Jews and the Christians who had each claimed him while rejecting the other, and who had now come to the Prophet offering their own paths. The Qur'an says: the one who rejects the faith of Abraham has rejected the religion itself — and the only person who does that is the fool, the one whose foolishness has turned against his own interest.

The verse's identification of the fool is pointed: *safiha nafsahu* — "he has fooled his own self." The fool is not the one who lacks intelligence; he is the one who has traded against himself. The rejection of Abraham's faith is not a failure of reasoning but a failure of self-interest: the person who rejects the religion of submission — the religion that his own ancestor lived and taught — has cheated himself, and the one he cheated is the only one who will pay.

"We certainly chose him in this life" — the divine choice of Abraham: the prophethood, the leadership, the friendship — the standing that the verse has already described. "And in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous" — the choice is not only the world; it is the hereafter. The verse is the Qur'an's answer to the dispute over Abraham: the two communities were arguing over his religion as though they owned him; the verse says he was chosen — not by them, but by Allah — and the proof of his standing is not the claim of his descendants but the choice of his Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the standard for every claim to a religious inheritance. The question of who belongs to Abraham, to Moses, to Jesus, to Muhammad — the question of which community owns the ancestor — is settled by the verse in one stroke: the faith of the ancestor is the submission to Allah, and the one who rejects it is the fool. The person who wants the ancestor must take the religion of the ancestor: not the name, but the surrender, the uprightness, and the refusal of the polytheism of his own age.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 131

Verse 131 says: *"When his Lord ordered him, "Submit ˹to My Will˺," he responded, "I submit to the Lord of all worlds."*"

In simple terms, the verse gives the moment of the religion in its purest form: when his Lord ordered him, "Submit," Abraham responded, "I submit to the Lord of all worlds." The whole of the religion, the whole of Islam, the whole of the dispute between the communities, is in these two sentences: the command and the response.

"Submit" — *aslim* — is the imperative of the religion. The verse does not say "believe," or "understand," or "reason"; it says submit. The command is the first and the final form of the relation between the creature and the Creator: the surrender of the will. Abraham was not commanded to debate, to investigate, or to negotiate the terms; he was commanded to submit, and the verse is the record of the response that the command received.

The response is the definition of the believer: "I submit to the Lord of all worlds." The verse uses two nouns: *aslamtu* — I submit — and *rabb al-'ālamīn* — the Lord of all worlds. Abraham submits to the Lord of the worlds — not to the god of a tribe, not to the god of a nation, not to the god of his ancestors alone, but to the Lord of everything that exists. The religion of Abraham is not provincial; it is universal. The one who submits to the Lord of all worlds has submitted to the Lord of every person, every nation, and every age.

The verse also sets the standard for the whole Islamic call: the message of the Prophet Muhammad was, in its essence, the same command that the Qur'an records here — "submit to the Lord of all worlds" — the same command that was given to Abraham. This is the reason the verse sits at the centre of the surah's dispute with the People of the Book: the Jews and the Christians were arguing over the religion of Abraham, and the verse says the religion of Abraham is the religion of the submission — a religion that neither the Jews nor the Christians of that time could claim as their exclusive possession, because it was the religion that preceded them both and that Abraham received directly from his Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the point at which every person's religion is tested. The test is not whether one can defend the creeds, or argue the doctrines, or claim the inheritance; the test is the response to the command of submission. The person who has the arguments without the submission has the shell of the religion; the person who has the submission — the surrender of the will, the acceptance of the command, the trust in the Lord of all worlds — has the religion of Abraham, whatever his community and whatever his age.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 132

Verse 132 says: *"This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children, ˹saying˺, "Indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.""*

In simple terms, the verse records the testament of the fathers: this was the advice of Abraham, as well as Jacob, to his children: indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in a state of full submission. The verse gives the inheritance that the prophets left, and it is not land, not wealth, not leadership — it is the faith and the command to die in it.

The verse's content is the greatest of the testaments. Abraham and Jacob, the fathers of the two lines, both gave their children the same instruction. The instruction has two parts. First: "Allah has chosen for you this faith" — the faith is not the people's invention; it is the divine choice. The religion is not a cultural preference; it is the selection of Allah, and the children of the prophets are told that the selection is their heritage. Second: "do not die except in a state of full submission" — the command that determines the whole life. The way the person lives determines the way he dies, and the way he dies determines what follows. The prophets teach their children that the final act of the life is the completion of the religion: the one who has lived in submission dies in submission.

The classical commentators (Ibn Kathir among them) explain the advice as the instruction to live the path so that death comes upon the path: the person is usually resurrected upon what he died, and he dies upon what he lived. The verse is thus the Islamic doctrine of the final moment: the believer is commanded to live the faith so completely that the death finds him in it. And the commentators note the divine promise in this: the One who helps the seeker of good remains on the path of the good — the death in submission is not the believer's achievement alone; it is the grace of the Lord of the path.

The verse is also the answer to the dispute over the inheritance. The Jews and the Christians each claimed Abraham through their line, and the verse says: the heirs of Abraham are not the claimants; they are the ones who received the testament — the faith chosen by Allah, and the life and death in the submission. The inheritance of the prophets is not the name; it is the instruction, and the heirs of the instruction are the ones who follow it.

In modern life, the verse is the criterion of the spiritual inheritance. The parent, the teacher, the founder — the inheritance they leave is not the property; it is the faith and the manner of the life. The verse's command is addressed to every child of every believer: the religion is the chosen thing, and the life is to be lived so that death finds one in it — not in the middle of a negotiation, not in the middle of a refusal, not in the state that the person has not prepared.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 133

Verse 133 says: *"Or did you witness when death came to Jacob? He asked his children, "Who will you worship after my passing?" They replied, "We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.""*

In simple terms, the verse addresses the claimants of Jacob with the question: did you witness when death came to Jacob? The question is the exposure: the Jews who claimed the inheritance of Jacob were not present at his death — the Qur'an records what happened, and the record contradicts the claim. At the moment of the death, Jacob asked his children: who will you worship after my passing? And they answered: the God of your forefathers — Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac — the One God; and to Him we all submit.

The question "did you witness" is the Qur'an's rhetorical method: the claimants had been asserting that Jacob was a Jew or a Christian — an impossible claim, since both came after him — and the verse says: you were not there; and the record of what was said at the deathbed is the answer. The record is the testament: the father's last question was not about property, not about lineage, not about the tribe — it was about worship. The religion of the deathbed is the religion of the life: the father asks, and the children answer with the Oneness of God.

The children's answer is the most important statement: "We will continue to worship your God, the God of your forefathers — Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac — the One God. And to Him we all submit." The answer includes the three forefathers — and it is worth noting that it includes Ishmael, the ancestor of the Arabs, alongside Abraham and Isaac. The Qur'an's record of the deathbed is a statement of the unity of the two lines: the religion of the children of Jacob is the religion of Abraham and Ishmael and Isaac — the One God, and to Him submission. The verse is the refutation of the claim that the heritage was exclusive to any one line.

The answer's final clause — "And to Him we all submit" — is the *islām* of the family: the submission of the children of Jacob to the One God. The Qur'an's portrait of the deathbed is the portrait of the true religion passing from the father to the children: not by blood alone, but by the testimony that the children give — the God of the fathers, the One God, the submission.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the family religion and the deathbed testament. The parent who wants the faith to continue asks the question: who will you worship after my passing? And the children's answer is the inheritance: not the name of the father, but the God of the father, the One God, and the submission to Him. The verse's lesson is that the true legacy is the one that the children can testify to at the moment of the passing — the God they will continue to worship, and the submission they will continue to live.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 134

Verse 134 says: *"That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done."*

In simple terms, the verse closes the discussion of the ancestors with the principle of individual accountability: that was a community that had already gone before; for them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned; and you will not be accountable for what they have done. The verse is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements of the doctrine that the deed is personal.

"That was a community that had already gone before" — the verse points to the fathers: Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes. They are gone; their communities are in the past. The verse's point is the separation: the ancestors are not present, and their relationship with Allah is their own. The claim of the descendants — that the standing of the fathers is transferable to the children — is destroyed by the simple fact of the passing.

"For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned" — the arithmetic of the hereafter: every community is credited with its own work. The Jews and the Christians who claimed the standing of Abraham were drawing on a credit that was not theirs; the verse says the account of Abraham is closed — for him is what he earned. The account of the claimants is open — for them is what they have earned. The standing of the ancestor cannot be inherited because the account of the ancestor is not a fund; it is a record.

"And you will not be accountable for what they have done" — the principle of the personal responsibility: no soul bears the burden of another. The Qur'an's statement here is the foundation of its whole ethics: the person who is a descendant of Abraham is not saved by the faith of Abraham; he is saved by his own faith. And the person who is not a descendant of Abraham is not denied the faith of Abraham; he can earn the same.

In modern life, the verse destroys the two errors of the inherited religion: the pride of the descendants ("I am saved by the standing of my fathers") and the despair of the outsiders ("I am not of the family"). Both are errors against the same principle: the account is personal. The believer who looks at the ancestors and claims their standing has misunderstood the record; the one who looks at the ancestors and despairs has misunderstood the record equally. The faith is not an inheritance; it is an earnings — and the earning is the person's own.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 135

Verse 135 says: *"The Jews and Christians each say, "Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided." Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.""*"

In simple terms, the verse records the invitation of the two communities — the Jews and the Christians each say: follow our faith to be rightly guided — and the answer: no! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not a polytheist. The verse is the Qur'an's refusal of the two invitations and its return to the source.

The verse's context is the debate with the People of the Book who had come to the Prophet with their claims. Each of them offered their own religion as the path of guidance: follow us, and you will be guided. And the two were mutually exclusive — the Jews required the following of the Torah of their time, the Christians required the following of the gospel of their time, and both rejected the message the Prophet brought. The Qur'an's answer is not a third religion in the sense of a new line; it is the return to the original: the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not a polytheist.

"The upright" — *ḥanīf* — is the key word of the verse. The hanif is the one who turns away from every false direction and faces the truth: the monotheist of the original religion, the one who is not a polytheist. The Qur'an uses the word for Abraham repeatedly and describes him exactly as the verse does: not a Jew, not a Christian, but upright and submissive (3:67); not of the polytheists. The answer to the two invitations is therefore not a rejection of the scriptures but a return to the source from which both descended: the faith of the one who was already a Muslim — a submitter — before either "Jew" or "Christian" existed.

The verse is also a statement of the method of the Qur'an's argument: the dispute is not settled by the claims of the later communities but by the record of the ancestor. The Jews said: follow us; the Christians said: follow us; the Qur'an says: we follow Abraham — and the one who follows Abraham is the one who follows the way of the upright, not the way of the polytheists of any age.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to every exclusive invitation: follow our path — the path of our community, our school, our nation — and you will be guided. The Qur'an's answer is the criterion: the path that leads to guidance is the path of Abraham — the upright, the non-polytheist, the submitter — and the person who follows any path that is not the path of the upright has followed the path of the exclusion. The believer's loyalty is not to the invitation of a community but to the faith of the one who was the friend of Allah, the upright, the one who was not of the polytheists.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 136

Verse 136 says: *"Say, ˹O believers,˺ "We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.""*

In simple terms, the verse gives the creed of the believers in its fullest form: we believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us, and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. The creed then adds the decisive clause: "We make no distinction between any of them." And the conclusion: to Allah we all submit.

The creed is the Qur'an's answer to the partial belief of the communities. The Jews believed in the prophets of their line and rejected Jesus and Muhammad; the Christians believed in their prophets and rejected the final messenger. The believers' creed is the complete belief: every prophet, every revelation, from the Lord. The verse names the sequence: Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants — the line of the fathers; Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets — the line of the law and the gospel. And then one phrase covers the whole: "We make no distinction between any of them."

"We make no distinction between any of them" — this is one of the most important doctrines of Islam. The religion of the believers does not select its prophets; it receives them all — his own too. The Qur'an elsewhere states the same creed (3:84), and the doctrine is the refutation of the very error the surah has been exposing: believing in part of the Book and rejecting the rest, accepting some messengers and killing others (2:87, 2:91). The believer who makes a distinction between the prophets has made a distinction between the revelations, and the distinction is the beginning of the rejection.

The creed is also the answer to the Muslim's own temptation: the temptation to believe in the Qur'an and to neglect the prophets, to accept the revelation of one age and to reject the revelation of the previous. The verse says the full belief is the belief in all: what has been revealed to us, and what was revealed to them — all from the same Lord, all to be received.

"To Allah we all submit" — the conclusion of the creed is the religion: the belief in all the prophets is not a catalogue; it is the submission to the One who sent them all. The one who believes in the prophets without the submission has accepted the history and refused the religion.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the religious community that has become partial. The community that honors its own prophets and dismisses the others, that reveres its own book and despises the ones before it — makes the distinction the verse forbids. The believer's faith is the whole line: the prophets all, the revelations all, and the Lord to whom they all called the same.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 137

Verse 137 says: *"So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺. But Allah will spare you their evil. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse states the consequence of the creed: if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be rightly guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed to the truth. And the verse closes with the assurance: Allah will spare you their evil, for He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

"If they believe in what you believe" — the condition is the same: the creed the believers just declared is the standard. The People of the Book who accept the whole line of the prophets — the line that includes the final messenger — are guided. The verse does not say they become Muslims by the change of the name; it says they believe in what the believers believe: the complete creed, the same submission. The guidance is not a label; it is the content of the belief.

"But if they turn away, they are simply opposed to the truth" — the verse's diagnosis is one of the Qur'an's sharpest words: the turning away is not a neutral disagreement; it is opposition. The person who has heard the creed, the whole line of the prophets, and the call to the submission — and who turns away — is not a seeker who has missed the truth; he is opposed to it. The Qur'an uses the same word for the opposition that the surah has exposed from the beginning: the people of the Book who recognized the messenger and rejected him (2:89), who cast the Book behind their backs (2:101), who are in dispute over the truth they know (2:139).

"But Allah will spare you their evil" — the assurance is for the believers: the opposition of the others will not harm them. The verse was revealed in a period of hostility, and the Prophet and the believers were being told: their hostility is not your concern; the One who hears their words and knows their plots will spare you their evil. The verse is not a promise of immunity from injury; it is a promise of the divine protection in the outcome: the evil they intend will not prevail against the people of the truth.

In modern life, the verse is the assurance for the community that has been opposed. The believer who has delivered the truth and been rejected is told: the rejection is not your failure; the one who turns away is opposed to the truth, and the One who is All-Hearing — who hears their objections — and All-Knowing — who knows their intentions — will spare the believers their evil. The community's task is the creed and the delivery; the outcome is with the One who hears and knows.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 138

Verse 138 says: *"This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the name of the believers' religion: this is the natural Way of Allah. The Arabic is *ṣibghat Allāh* — the dye, the baptism, the coloring of Allah. The verse asks: who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And declares: and we worship none but Him.

The classical scholars explain the word *ṣibghah* in several related ways. Ibn Abbas explained it as the religion of Allah — the pure faith of the submission that the prophets taught. Some commentators note the reference to the Christian rite of baptism, by which the converts of that community were initiated — and the Qur'an's answer: the believers' "baptism" is the baptism of Allah: not the water, but the religion; not the rite of the community, but the coloring of the faith itself. The word is also used for the dying of cloth — the coloring that gives the fabric its identity — and the metaphor is the person who has been dyed by the religion: the faith is not a garment that can be removed; it is the color that has penetrated the whole person. The believer is the one who has been "dyed" with the color of Allah — the monotheism, the submission, the uprightness.

"Who is better than Allah in ordaining a way?" — the question answers itself. The Christians claimed that their baptism was the way into the faith; the Jews claimed their law was the path; the verse says the way belongs to Allah, and no one is better than Allah in ordaining the way. The ordainer is the One who knows what the way requires: the One who has made the religion, chosen the prophets, sent the books, and given the rites. The way of Allah is the most perfect way because the ordainer is the One who knows the human being and the purpose of the human being.

"And we worship none but Him" — the conclusion is the tawhid: the way of Allah is the worship of Allah alone. The religion is not a set of claims about the prophets; it is the worship of the One who sent them all. The verse brings the whole discussion to the point: the Muslims' creed is not a position in the debate between the communities; it is the worship of the one God, without the partner of the Christians' trinity and without the polytheism of the pagans, and without the partiality of the Jews and the Christians toward their own lines.

In modern life, the verse is the definition of the identity of the believer. The identity is not in the name of the community; it is in the "color" of the faith — the depth to which the religion has penetrated the person: the monotheism that colors his worship, the submission that colors his will, and the uprightness that colors his life. The verse's question — who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? — is the believer's answer to every offer of a better religion: the way has been ordained, and the ordainer is the best of the ordainers.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 139

Verse 139 says: *"Say, "Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺.""*

In simple terms, the verse now turns the debate to its true subject: would you dispute with us about Allah — while He is our Lord and your Lord? The verse states the ground of the dispute and the boundary of the dispute: the same Lord, the separate accounts, and the devotion of the believers to Him alone.

"Would you dispute with us about Allah" — the question exposes the whole controversy. The Jews and the Christians had been disputing with the believers about religion — about Abraham, about the prophets, about the scriptures — but the dispute was really about Allah: about His choice, His grace, His messenger, His way. The believers' answer: the One who is our Lord is your Lord. There is no rival Lord; there cannot be; the quarrel about the religion of Allah is a quarrel about the One who is the Lord of both parties. The person who claims to serve the same Allah while disputing the way Allah has sent is disputing with his own Lord.

"We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours" — the verse states the principle of the separate accounts, the same principle of 2:134: the deeds are individual, and the accountability is individual. The believers do not answer for the deeds of the People of the Book, and the People of the Book do not answer for the believers. The statement is the removal of the weapon of the dispute: the believers are not responsible for the deeds of the others; the others will not be judged by the believers' claim. The accountability is the boundary, and the boundary is the judgment of Allah.

"And we are devoted to Him alone" — the final clause is the believers' position in the dispute: the devotion is not divided. The word *mukhliṣūn* — rendered "devoted" — carries the sense of the purified and the sincere: the worship that has no partner, no mixed motive, no divided loyalty. The believers are devoted to Allah alone, and that devotion is the content of their creed, the same creed that the whole section has been building: the submission to the One, the belief in all His prophets, the worship of none but Him.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the theological dispute conducted with the boundaries of the faith. The believer disputes — the verse does not forbid the dispute — but the dispute is grounded: the same Lord is the Lord of both; the accounts are individual; and the believers' devotion is to Allah alone. The verse forbids the dispute that has forgotten the common Lord, and the dispute that has forgotten the separate accounts — the dispute that judges the other's deeds as though the judgment were the believer's. The believer disputes about the religion and leaves the judgment to Allah.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 140

Verse 140 says: *"Or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians?" Say, "Who is more knowledgeable: you or Allah?" Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah? And Allah is never unaware of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse now confronts the claim directly: do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians? The question is impossible on its face — the patriarchs lived generations before either community existed — and the Qur'an answers it with two questions: who is more knowledgeable, you or Allah? And then the condemnation: who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah?

The first question exposes the absurdity of the claim. The people of the Book were claiming that the patriarchs of the ancient world were members of the religions that arose after them — a claim that even their own histories contradicted. The Qur'an's answer is: who knows better than you, or does Allah? The title of the knowledge belongs to Allah: He knows who the patriarchs were and what they worshipped, and His testimony — in the Scripture of the very claimants — states the truth. The claim of the people of the Book was not merely false; it was made against their own knowledge.

The second question is the condemnation: "Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah?" The verse names the crime: the concealment. The People of the Book had the testimony in their hands: their Scripture stated the true religion is the submission, and it stated that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes were neither Jews nor Christians — they had testified to these facts. Al-Hasan al-Basri, as recorded by the commentators, explained that they used to recite the Book which stated these things and then hid them from the people. The crime is not ignorance; it is the concealment of the known. The wrongdoer par excellence is the one who has received the testimony and hides it.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you do" — the verse closes with the warning: the concealment has been observed. The people who thought the testimony could be hidden from the public — and from the Prophet and the believers — had forgotten the One who sees the concealment. The verse's point is the whole of the section's argument: the true religion of the patriarchs is known to Allah, and the ones who have claimed them for their own religions have done so by the suppression of their own testimony.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the religious knowledge that has been received and suppressed. The person who knows the testimony and hides it has done a greater wrong than the one who does not know it: the truth is not merely unknown in his case — it is suppressed. And the verse's question — who knows better than you, or Allah? — is the ground of the believer's confidence in the truth over the claims of the authorities: the One who knows the testimony is the One whose knowledge does not change under the press of the interests and the claims of the communities.
