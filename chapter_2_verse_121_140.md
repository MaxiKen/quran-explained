## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:121

> Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it. As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers.

**Commentary:**

### Following the Book Properly

After describing people who cast the Book behind their backs, the Qur’an now praises those who follow it as it should be followed. The Arabic expression *yat­lūnahu ḥaqqa tilāwatih* carries more than the act of vocal recitation. It includes reading accurately, understanding the message, believing it, acting upon it, and conveying it faithfully. A book is not truly followed when it is recited ceremonially but ignored in matters of worship, justice, honesty, and accountability.

The verse refers first to sincere People of the Book who accepted the truth in the revelation available to them and recognized the confirmation brought by Muhammad (ﷺ). The Qur’an describes some of them as people who recognize the truth, listen with humility, and believe (5:83–85). It does not condemn every Jew or Christian, and it does not say that all members of a scriptural community respond in the same way. The decisive distinction is whether the Book is followed truthfully.

### Recitation, Understanding, and Practice

The Qur’an repeatedly joins recitation with reflection and action. “Do they not reflect upon the Qur’an?” (4:82), and “a blessed Book We have revealed, so follow it and be mindful” (6:155). Recitation is a great act of worship, but it is intended to reach the mind and heart. A person who reads the words while preserving injustice, arrogance, or dishonesty has not received the full benefit of recitation.

The expression also protects against the opposite error: acting energetically without respecting the text’s actual meaning. Following the Book requires accurate reading, sound language, context, prophetic explanation, and qualified interpretation. It is not enough to claim that one is acting according to scripture while selecting verses to support a personal agenda. True following joins the letter, meaning, purpose, and ethical demands of revelation.

For Muslims, following the Qur’an includes following the Prophet’s (ﷺ) explanation and example. Allah sent him to recite the revelations, purify people, and teach the Book and wisdom (2:151). A person cannot claim to follow the Qur’an while deliberately rejecting the authentic prophetic guidance that explains its prayer, charity, fasting, pilgrimage, and moral application.

### True Belief and the Final Revelation

The verse says that those who follow the Book properly are the ones who truly believe in it. This corrects the idea that merely possessing scripture or identifying with a scriptural community proves faith. True belief is visible in fidelity to revelation, including acceptance of the truth it contains about later guidance. When the final messenger came with a confirming Book, following earlier scripture properly should have led to recognition rather than rejection.

For those to whom Muhammad’s message has been clearly conveyed, belief in Allah includes accepting the final messenger and the Qur’an. The Qur’an commands belief in all Allah’s messengers without dividing them (2:136; 4:136). Earlier revelation is honoured, but the final revelation is the criterion for disputes about what has been preserved, altered, or misunderstood.

### The Meaning of Loss

Those who reject the Book are called the losers—*al-khāsirūn*. The loss is not limited to losing an argument or a social position. It includes losing guidance, the opportunity for repentance, moral clarity, and the reward of the Hereafter. A person may protect his reputation by rejecting a truth, but he cannot recover the eternal opportunity he surrendered if he dies upon rejection.

The Qur’an’s definition of loss is ultimately the loss of the self. “The losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection” (39:15). This is why the verse places belief and practice together. The Book is a mercy that saves the reader from this loss when it is received sincerely and followed with perseverance.

### A Mirror for Every Community

The verse is not only a judgment on earlier communities. Muslims must ask whether they follow the Qur’an as it should be followed. Do they recite without reflection, praise justice without applying it, or select promises while ignoring warnings? Do they use the Qur’an to correct themselves or only to criticize others?

Following properly does not require sinlessness. Believers fall, repent, and continue. It requires that the direction of life remain submission, that disobedience not be defended as a religious principle, and that correction be welcomed. The Book is followed when it remains the standard to which the reader returns.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:75, 2:101, and 5:83–85 on recognizing, following, and rejecting revelation.
- Qur’an 2:151 and 62:2 on recitation, purification, teaching the Book, and wisdom.
- Qur’an 4:82 and 6:155 on reflection and following revelation.
- Qur’an 2:136 and 4:136 on believing in all messengers and revelations.
- Qur’an 39:15 on the meaning of ultimate loss.
- For *ḥaqqa tilāwatih* and the relation between recitation, understanding, and practice, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:121.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:122

> O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others.

**Commentary:**

### Repeated Remembrance of Favour

Allah again addresses the Children of Israel and commands them to remember His favours and the honour He granted them. The repetition is deliberate. A blessing can become so familiar that its recipient no longer sees it as a gift. Repeated remembrance restores the connection between favour and responsibility: Allah gave them revelation, prophets, rescue, provision, and opportunities to guide humanity, so they were required to respond with gratitude and obedience.

The address is not simply a historical compliment. It recalls the period in which the Children of Israel were selected for a major prophetic mission and favoured over many peoples through revelation and deliverance. The Qur’an uses comparable language in 44:32 and 45:16. It does not establish an unconditional racial superiority or a permanent exemption from judgment. Allah’s selection is connected to a trust, and the trust can be betrayed.

The Qur’an itself distinguishes between different people within the Children of Israel and the wider People of the Book. It praises those who are upright, recite Allah’s revelations, believe in Allah, and hasten to good deeds (3:113–115). This distinction is essential. A community may be addressed collectively because of its history, while Allah judges each individual according to belief, conduct, knowledge, and intention.

### Remembering the Giver

To remember favour properly is to remember Allah as the Giver. A person may remember that he was rescued from danger but forget to worship the One who rescued him. A community may celebrate its prophetic history while neglecting the covenant that gave the history meaning. Qur’anic remembrance turns memory into present gratitude: blessings should produce prayer, justice, charity, and humility.

Ingratitude often begins when a gift is treated as entitlement. The Children of Israel had been rescued from Pharaoh, yet they later complained about provision, worshipped the calf, broke covenants, and rejected messengers. Their history illustrates that past favour does not excuse present disobedience. It increases the seriousness of disobedience because the recipient has been shown mercy and guidance.

Muslims are also commanded to remember Allah’s favours upon them. The Qur’an recalls the transformation from weakness and fear to security and community (8:26), the blessing of revelation, and the Prophet’s mission among them. Gratitude is not complete until the blessing is used in the way Allah intends. Knowledge should lead to teaching and humility; wealth to generosity; authority to justice; and safety to worship and protection of others.

### Honour Is Not Automatic Salvation

The honour mentioned in the verse is a historical favour, not an automatic ticket to Paradise. The Qur’an later says that the covenant does not extend to wrongdoers (2:124) and rejects the claims of those who rely upon communal labels. Allah judges by faith and righteousness, not by ancestry alone. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught his own family that lineage could not independently save them from Allah’s judgment (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

This corrects both pride and despair. No community should become arrogant because of its history, and no individual should believe that his ancestry makes repentance unnecessary. The honoured community is the one that fulfils its trust. When it fails, the blessing becomes evidence of what it should have done.

### A Lesson in Historical Memory

The Qur’an’s retelling of Israelite history is not an invitation to mock or hate the descendants of Israel. It is a lesson in how a community responds to revelation and power. Rescue from oppression should produce justice, not a new oppression. Receiving scripture should produce fidelity, not manipulation. Being favoured with prophets should produce humility, not rejection of the next messenger.

The believer should therefore read this verse as a mirror. What favours has Allah given me? Do I remember them only when I want more, or do I respond with gratitude? Does belonging to a religious community make me more obedient, or does it make me feel exempt? The answer shows whether remembrance is alive or merely historical.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:40, 2:47, 2:122, and 5:12 on remembering favour and fulfilling the covenant.
- Qur’an 44:32 and 45:16 on the historical favour granted to the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 2:124 and 49:13 on honour being conditional upon righteousness and taqwā.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book.
- Qur’an 8:26 and 14:7 on remembering blessings and responding with gratitude.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on personal accountability and the inability of lineage to save without faith and obedience.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:123

> And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given.

**Commentary:**

### The Warning Repeated

This verse repeats the warning previously given in 2:48. Repetition reinforces the central correction to communal privilege: no person will be saved on the Day of Judgment merely because he belongs to a favoured people. The Children of Israel were repeatedly reminded of their history and honour, but they were also repeatedly reminded that honour does not cancel personal accountability.

The repetition is not redundant. Human beings can hear a warning and then return to false security, especially when a group possesses a respected religious identity. The Qur’an brings the warning back before moving to the story of Abraham, whose true honour came through fulfilled tests and obedience—not merely through ancestry. The sequence contrasts inherited claim with earned responsibility under Allah’s covenant.

### No Soul Can Carry Another’s Burden

“No soul will be of any help to another” does not mean that people cannot intercede by Allah’s permission, share in the consequences of influence, or benefit from another’s righteous prayer. It means that no creature independently controls another person’s final account. Every soul must answer for its own belief, choices, and response to guidance.

The Qur’an says, “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164), while also teaching that those who mislead others carry a share of the burdens of those they misled (16:25). These statements are compatible. A person is not punished for another’s private sin, but he is accountable for the harm he caused by initiating, encouraging, or normalizing it. Likewise, someone who teaches good may share in the reward of those who follow.

The principle protects both justice and responsibility. A child cannot rely upon a parent’s piety, a leader cannot transfer his crimes to his followers, and a follower cannot claim that group pressure erased his own choices. At the same time, innocent people are not condemned merely because they share an ancestry or label with wrongdoers.

### Intercession and Ransom

The verse says that no intercession will be accepted and no ransom will be taken. As with 2:48, the rejected intercession is independent or pagan intercession. The Qur’an affirms that intercession may occur only by Allah’s permission and for those with whom He is pleased (2:255; 20:109). No saint, prophet, angel, ancestor, wealth, or ritual offering can compel Allah to forgive.

Ransom is equally powerless. A person may wish to offer all his wealth, family, or worldly possessions in exchange for release, but the Hereafter is not a marketplace in which punishment can be bought off. Allah’s mercy is sought through sincere faith, repentance, and righteous action before death—not through a last-minute payment after the account has been closed.

### Preparing Through Taqwā

“Guard yourselves” translates *ittaqu*, the command to cultivate taqwā. Taqwā is preparation through faith, worship, repentance, justice, and avoidance of sin. It is not the claim that one has already secured the outcome. The believer prepares because the Day is certain and because no worldly relationship can substitute for a sound heart.

The warning should produce urgency without despair. Allah has disclosed the Day so that people can prepare while deeds are still possible. A person can repent, return stolen property, reconcile with someone he harmed, establish prayer, and use his wealth for good. The door is open until death, but the believer does not presume that he will be granted unlimited time.

### The Difference Between Honour and Entitlement

The Children of Israel had been honoured, but the repeated warning shows that favour does not become entitlement. Abraham was promised leadership only after fulfilling his tests, and Allah excluded wrongdoers from the covenant (2:124). A community’s past cannot replace present obedience.

The same truth applies to Muslims. The name “Muslim,” the memory of righteous ancestors, or affiliation with a respected institution cannot save a person who knowingly rejects Allah’s commands. These identities are blessings when they lead to worship; they become dangerous when they are used to excuse injustice.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:48 and 2:255 on personal accountability and intercession by Allah’s permission.
- Qur’an 6:164 and 16:25 on burdens, influence, and individual responsibility.
- Qur’an 20:109 and 34:23 on the limits of intercession.
- Qur’an 39:47 and 70:11–14 on the futility of ransom in the Hereafter.
- Qur’an 2:124 on Abraham’s covenant and the exclusion of wrongdoers.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the Prophet’s intercession occurring only by Allah’s permission.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:124

> ˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”

**Commentary:**

### Abraham’s Tests and Fulfilment

The passage turns from the inherited claims of the Children of Israel to Abraham (Ibrāhīm, ʿalayhi al-salām), the common patriarch whom Jews, Christians, and Muslims all honour. Allah tested Abraham with commandments and he fulfilled them. The verse does not list the tests here, but the Qur’an presents his life as a series of acts of submission: rejecting idolatry, leaving his people, migrating for Allah, enduring the fire, leaving his family in the barren valley, building the Sacred House, and accepting the command concerning his son.

The tests were not intended to give Allah information about Abraham. Allah already knows His servants. They made Abraham’s sincerity visible through action and raised him to a public role model. A claim of faith becomes established in life when a person obeys under pressure, gives up what competes with Allah, and trusts the divine command when its wisdom is not immediately visible.

The word *ibtilāʾ*—testing—can involve hardship, responsibility, or a command that exposes priorities. Success is not measured by the absence of tests but by fulfilling them. Abraham did not pass because life was easy; he passed because submission remained stronger than fear, custom, and personal attachment.

### A Role Model for Humanity

Allah promised to make Abraham an *imām* for the people—a leader, exemplar, and one whose path would be followed. The honour came after fulfilment, showing that leadership in the sight of Allah is a trust built upon obedience. It is not a hereditary entitlement or a reward for public status. The true leader guides people toward Allah through knowledge, character, and sacrifice.

Abraham’s example is universal because he stood against the idolatry of his society and refused to let ancestral practice overrule truth. He was neither a Jew nor a Christian in the later communal sense; he was a ḥanīf, an upright monotheist who submitted to Allah (3:67). His example challenges communities that claim him while contradicting his worship of one God.

An exemplar is not one whose every worldly circumstance must be copied mechanically. Abraham lived in a particular time and fulfilled particular commands. What believers inherit is his orientation: truth before custom, worship before status, obedience before comfort, and mercy joined to courage.

### Abraham’s Concern for His Descendants

Abraham immediately asks about his offspring. His concern shows that righteous leadership is not selfish. He wants his descendants to share in guidance and to continue the worship of Allah. The Qur’an records his prayers for a believing community and for a messenger to be raised among them (2:128–129).

Allah’s answer corrects any assumption that lineage guarantees the covenant: “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.” Descendants can inherit a family history, a name, or a religious tradition, but they cannot inherit another person’s righteousness. The covenant belongs to those who remain faithful; wrongdoers are excluded from its promise.

This is the Qur’an’s answer to religious privilege. Abraham’s descendants include prophets and righteous people, but also people who rejected guidance. The honour of Abraham cannot be used to excuse the disobedience of later generations. Likewise, Muslims cannot rely upon being the descendants or followers of righteous people while abandoning their way.

### The Covenant and Justice

The exclusion of wrongdoers does not mean that a person who has ever sinned is permanently excluded. All human beings make mistakes, and sincere repentance restores the servant to Allah’s mercy. The term *ẓālimūn* here concerns those who persist in wrongdoing or place worship and allegiance where they do not belong. The Qur’an repeatedly opens the door to repentance while refusing to guarantee divine favour to unrepentant injustice.

Allah’s covenant is therefore both generous and morally serious. He grants leadership, guidance, and honour, but these gifts create responsibility. The covenant cannot be inherited as a legal shield against justice. It is fulfilled through faith, obedience, and the abandonment of wrongdoing.

### The Foundation of Sacred Leadership

The verse also gives a standard for leadership today. A person may be educated, charismatic, wealthy, or descended from a respected family, but leadership should be judged by trustworthiness, knowledge, justice, and obedience to Allah. Authority without taqwā becomes a means of corruption. Abraham’s leadership was established by fulfilling tests, not by demanding privilege.

The believer should ask whether he wants the status of Abraham’s heirs or the discipline of Abraham’s submission. The first is a claim; the second is a path. The next verses will show that Abraham’s covenant and the Sacred House are linked to worship, purification, prayer, and service.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:67–68 and 16:120–123 on Abraham as a ḥanīf and model of submission.
- Qur’an 21:51–70 and 37:83–113 on Abraham’s rejection of idolatry and his tests.
- Qur’an 14:35–41 and 2:125–129 on Abraham’s prayers, family, and the Sacred House.
- Qur’an 33:72 on the trust of responsibility.
- Qur’an 2:47, 2:122, and 49:13 on favour, honour, and the limits of inherited privilege.
- For the nature of Abraham’s tests and the meaning of the covenant’s exclusion of wrongdoers, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:124.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:125

> And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, “˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.” And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺.

**Commentary:**

### The Sacred House as a Centre

Allah made the Sacred House—the Kaʿbah—a place of return and a sanctuary for humanity. The Arabic *mathābah* suggests a place to which people repeatedly return, while *amn* means safety and security. The House became a centre of worship, pilgrimage, remembrance, and shared direction for believers across generations. Its centrality is spiritual and communal; it does not mean that Allah is physically located within it.

The Kaʿbah was built or raised by Abraham and Ishmael upon foundations associated with earlier worship of Allah (2:127; 22:26). Its sanctity comes from Allah’s command, not from the stone itself. Muslims face it in prayer and circumambulate it, but they worship Allah alone. The House unites people without becoming an object of divine power.

The sanctuary also establishes a principle of protected space. The Sacred Mosque and its surrounding sanctuary were not to be treated as ordinary territory for revenge, bloodshed, or oppression. Pilgrimage brings people from different languages and regions into one protected setting, where the usual markers of status are deliberately weakened.

### The Station of Abraham

Allah permits believers to take the standing-place of Abraham—*Maqām Ibrāhīm*—as a place of prayer. The station is associated with the place where Abraham stood while building the House, and Muslims pray two units after circumambulating when circumstances permit. The command honours Abraham’s labour and links later worshippers to his devotion.

The station is not worshipped, and the stone has no independent ability to benefit or harm. It is a sign that directs people to Abraham’s example. The believer stands where Abraham stood in the physical sense while trying to stand in the same spiritual position: obedience, humility, trust, and service.

The verse therefore turns history into worship. The House is not a monument to national pride or ancestral achievement. It is a living centre of tawḥīd. Pilgrims come to remember Allah, follow the rites He prescribed, and renew their submission.

### Purifying the House

Allah entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify the House for those who circumambulate, remain in retreat, and bow and prostrate. Purification includes removing physical dirt and protecting the House from idolatry, injustice, obscenity, and behaviour incompatible with worship. A sacred space must be clean in body and purpose.

The three groups mentioned—those who circumambulate, those who remain devoted in the mosque, and those who bow and prostrate—represent different forms of worship. Pilgrimage is not a single movement without meaning. It joins movement around the House, stillness in remembrance, and bodily submission in prayer. The variety of acts allows different aspects of devotion to work together.

Purification also carries a broader lesson for mosques. They should be maintained as places of prayer, teaching, remembrance, charity, and reconciliation. A mosque can be physically clean while its purpose is corrupted by factional hatred, commercial exploitation, or political intimidation. The House is purified when Allah’s name is honoured and people are protected from harm.

### Sanctuary and Human Dignity

The Sacred House was made a sanctuary “for the people,” not an exclusive symbol of one tribe’s superiority. Pilgrimage gathers people who differ in wealth, language, colour, and social rank. The rites teach that humanity’s deepest identity is servitude to Allah. The Prophet (ﷺ) declared that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab and no non-Arab to an Arab, nor one colour to another, except through taqwā (reported in Musnad Aḥmad and other collections).

The sanctuary’s peace is also a lesson for ordinary life. If Allah protects a place so that people can worship safely, believers should protect homes, neighbourhoods, and communities from needless fear. Sacredness cannot be invoked to excuse harming people who are entitled to safety.

### Abraham’s Legacy

The House connects Abraham’s faith to the final Muslim community. Abraham’s legacy is not a claim to ethnic ownership of the Kaʿbah; it is the worship of Allah alone and the service of pilgrims. The Qur’an calls Abraham and Ishmael to purify the House, showing that leadership is service rather than control.

The verse therefore asks what the believer finds at the centre of his life. Muslims physically face the Kaʿbah, but the heart must face Allah. If wealth, politics, tribe, or reputation occupies the centre, physical direction alone has not achieved the purpose of the House.

- Qur’an 2:127–129 and 22:26–29 on Abraham, Ishmael, the House, and its rites.
- Qur’an 3:96–97 on the first House established for worship and its signs.
- Qur’an 5:97 on the Kaʿbah as a support for humanity and a sanctuary.
- Qur’an 14:35–37 on Abraham’s prayer for the security and worship of the House.
- Qur’an 9:17–18 and 72:18 on maintaining mosques for Allah’s remembrance.
- For *mathābah*, *amn*, Maqām Ibrāhīm, and the purification of the House, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:125.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:126

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day.” He answered, “As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”

**Commentary:**

### Abraham’s Prayer for Mecca

Abraham prayed that the city would be secure and that its believing inhabitants would receive provision. The Qur’an later identifies the city as Makkah and records Abraham’s broader prayer for a safe sanctuary, believing families, and hearts inclined toward it (14:35–37). Security is mentioned before provision because worship, life, and social flourishing cannot develop where people live under constant fear.

The prayer shows Abraham’s concern for both spiritual and material welfare. He did not ask only for religious devotion while ignoring safety, food, and economic stability. Nor did he ask only for prosperity without faith. The two are connected: material provision should support worship, and worship should govern the use of provision.

The city’s security is a divine favour, but it also creates a human responsibility. A sanctuary must not become a place of violence, exploitation, or exclusionary pride. The Prophet (ﷺ) affirmed the sanctity of Makkah and prohibited bloodshed and injustice within it. A place made secure by Allah should be protected by those who live in and visit it.

### Provision Is Not Proof of Approval

Allah answers Abraham by saying that disbelievers will also be allowed to enjoy provision for a little while. Abraham had asked specifically for believing inhabitants, perhaps because he was concerned that the presence of disbelief would conflict with the sacred nature of the city. Allah clarifies that worldly provision is granted to believers and disbelievers alike for a limited period.

This is an important correction to the assumption that wealth, security, or success automatically proves divine approval. Allah gives worldly provision as a blessing, a test, or a temporary respite. The Qur’an says that He provides for whoever He wills without limit (2:212), and that worldly life can be made attractive to disbelievers while the mindful will rank higher in the Hereafter. Material abundance is not the final measure of honour.

The believer may enjoy lawful prosperity but must not become arrogant because of it. He may also suffer hardship without concluding that Allah has abandoned him. The final criterion is faith, righteousness, and the outcome in the Hereafter. Worldly provision ends; accountability does not.

### Enjoyment for a Little While

The phrase “for a little while” places worldly life in its true scale. Even a long human life is short compared with eternity. Disbelievers may enjoy health, wealth, influence, and pleasure, but no worldly enjoyment cancels the final return. The Qur’an says, “The enjoyment of this world is little, and the Hereafter is better for whoever is mindful” (4:77).

The temporary granting of enjoyment is not necessarily a sign that punishment has been forgotten. Allah may give time for repentance, allow a person’s choices to unfold, or test others through the person’s prosperity. The delay should not be mistaken for approval. The Qur’an warns wrongdoers not to think that respite is good for them; it may allow their burden to increase (3:178).

### Security, Hospitality, and Justice

The prayer for security has a wider ethical implication. Pilgrims, residents, visitors, and the vulnerable should be protected from violence and exploitation. Hospitality around the Sacred House should not become a commercial system that treats people as means of profit. The Qur’an calls the House a place of return and security, and security must be reflected in the conduct of those entrusted with it.

The verse does not deny the right of a community to protect its sanctuary from aggression. It distinguishes protection from injustice. Sacredness cannot be used as a cover for corruption, nor can the presence of non-believers be treated as permission to violate their rights when they are not aggressors. Allah’s judgment of disbelief is His; human beings remain bound by justice.

### The Good and Evil Destination

Allah concludes that disbelievers will be condemned to the Fire and calls it an evil destination. The contrast is between temporary worldly enjoyment and lasting final outcome. A person who has everything in this world but loses Allah’s pleasure has not truly succeeded. A person who has little but meets Allah with faith and righteousness has not truly lost.

Abraham’s prayer therefore teaches believers to ask for security, lawful provision, and faith together. They should use worldly blessings to establish worship and justice, remember that provision is temporary, and prepare for the destination that does not end.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 14:35–37 on Abraham’s prayer for a secure sanctuary, provision, and believing inhabitants.
- Qur’an 2:125 and 3:96–97 on the Sacred House as a sanctuary and centre of worship.
- Qur’an 2:212, 4:77, and 3:178 on worldly provision, temporary enjoyment, and the danger of mistaking respite for approval.
- Qur’an 28:77 on seeking the Hereafter without neglecting one’s lawful share of the world.
- Qur’an 5:97 on the Kaʿbah as a support for humanity and a sanctuary.
- For the relation between Abraham’s prayer, provision, and divine response, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:126.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:127

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ “Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### Building the House Together

The verse depicts Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundations of the Sacred House. The word “raising” can mean that they built upon existing foundations or that they raised the structure from its foundations. The Qur’an’s focus is not on settling an architectural question but on showing two generations of prophets cooperating in worship. The House was raised through labour, prayer, and submission.

Abraham was an elderly father and Ishmael a son, yet both participated. The scene is a model of family discipleship: the father does not merely give his son instructions about worship; he works beside him. Ishmael does not inherit the project as a passive descendant; he joins the obedience himself. Sacred institutions are preserved when generations share responsibility rather than treating faith as a possession handed down without effort.

The House is a physical structure, but its meaning is spiritual. It is not a dwelling place for Allah, nor does the building contain Him. It is a designated centre toward which believers turn and around which they worship Allah. Abraham’s labour therefore serves tawḥīd; the structure is a means of gathering people around the worship of the One who has no partner.

### “Our Lord, Accept from Us”

While raising the House, Abraham and Ishmael immediately pray for acceptance. Their action is great, but they do not assume that greatness guarantees acceptance. They fear that intention, execution, or hidden defects may be lacking. The prayer teaches that a believer should work hard and still depend upon Allah’s acceptance rather than upon personal admiration.

The Qur’an describes the righteous as giving what they give while their hearts are fearful because they will return to their Lord (23:60). This is not doubt in Allah’s generosity; it is humility about one’s own sincerity. A person can build a mosque, teach knowledge, give charity, or lead a family and still need to ask Allah to accept the deed.

Acceptance depends upon sincerity and conformity to revealed guidance. A beautiful project built for reputation, domination, or factional pride does not become worship simply because it carries a religious label. Abraham and Ishmael’s prayer places the project under Allah’s judgment from the beginning.

### Allah Hears and Knows

They call Allah *al-Samīʿ*—the All-Hearing—and *al-ʿAlīm*—the All-Knowing. He hears their spoken prayer and knows the hidden intention, effort, fatigue, and hope behind their work. The names reassure the servant that no prayer made during labour is lost and no sincere effort is overlooked.

Allah’s hearing is not like human hearing, which depends upon sound waves, distance, and physical organs. His attribute is affirmed in a manner befitting Him. His knowledge includes what the builders do outwardly and what they conceal inwardly. This is why the prayer for acceptance is meaningful: the One asked knows the complete reality of the offering.

The names also warn against public performance. A person may impress people with a building, speech, or donation, but Allah knows whether he sought His pleasure. The believer therefore values hidden sincerity more than visible applause.

### Sacred Work and Humility

The House was raised by prophets, yet they did not regard the task as beneath them. Physical labour can be worship when done for Allah, while seemingly religious work can be corrupted by pride. Building, cleaning, funding, teaching, hosting pilgrims, and protecting worshippers are all forms of service when they are done lawfully and sincerely.

The scene also opposes the idea that spiritual excellence requires withdrawal from practical responsibility. Abraham and Ishmael combined prayer with construction, supplication with effort, and trust with planning. The believer does not ask Allah to accept a project while refusing to do the work required to complete it.

### A Prayer for Every Deed

“Our Lord, accept from us” should accompany the believer’s worship. After prayer, he asks for acceptance; after charity, he asks for sincerity; after repentance, he asks for firmness; after teaching, he asks that knowledge benefit people. Such prayer prevents self-congratulation and keeps the heart dependent upon Allah.

The verse also teaches parents and children to cooperate in good. Families can build houses of worship, truthfulness, generosity, and mercy through repeated small acts. The physical House was raised by Abraham and Ishmael, but every believing family is called to raise a spiritual house in which Allah is remembered.

- Qur’an 3:96–97 on the first House established for worship.
- Qur’an 23:57–61 on righteous people acting while fearing that their deeds may not be accepted.
- Qur’an 18:110 on righteous action requiring sincerity and sound worship.
- Qur’an 14:35–37 on Abraham’s concern for the House and its worshippers.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:128

> Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Submission Is a Lifelong Need

Abraham and Ishmael ask Allah to make them submit to Him. This prayer comes from prophets who had already demonstrated extraordinary obedience. Their request does not mean that they were disbelievers before the prayer. It means that submission must be renewed, strengthened, and protected throughout life. No servant should assume that past faith makes future steadfastness unnecessary.

The word *muslim* means one who submits to Allah. Islam in this foundational sense is not a tribal label; it is the path of all prophets who surrendered to the One God. Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian in the later communal sense; he was a ḥanīf and a Muslim, one who turned away from idolatry and submitted to Allah (3:67). Muhammad (ﷺ) brought the final form of this submission for humanity.

The prayer also shows that even the most righteous person asks Allah for help in worship. Faith is a gift, and steadfastness is a continuing gift. The Prophet (ﷺ) frequently prayed, “O Turner of the hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion” (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2140). The believer should not rely upon his own strength or past achievements.

### A Submitting Descendant Community

Abraham and Ishmael ask for a nation from their descendants that submits to Allah. Their concern is communal and intergenerational. Righteousness is not selfish; it seeks a future in which worship, justice, and mercy continue. The prayer was answered in the establishment of a believing community connected to the Sacred House and, ultimately, the mission of Muhammad (ﷺ).

The word *ummah* refers to a community united by a direction and purpose. The prayer does not ask for a community united by race alone. Descendants can inherit a history without inheriting obedience. The Qur’an repeatedly says that Allah’s covenant does not include wrongdoers (2:124), so the honour of Abraham’s family belongs to those who follow his faith, not automatically to everyone bearing his lineage.

A believing community must therefore educate its descendants, preserve revelation, care for the vulnerable, and establish justice. Family identity can support faith, but it cannot replace personal belief. Parents can transmit language, culture, and instruction, but each child must ultimately respond to Allah.

### Learning the Rituals

“Show us our rituals” translates *arinā manāsikanā*. The prayer asks Allah to teach them the rites of pilgrimage and worship associated with the House. Even the builders of the Kaʿbah do not invent the method of worship from personal preference. They ask Allah to disclose the correct forms and meanings.

This is a principle of worship: acts of devotion are based on revelation, not on whatever a person finds emotionally impressive. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Take your rites from me,” in the context of pilgrimage (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1297). Muslims learn prayer, zakāh, fasting, and pilgrimage through the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah rather than through cultural invention.

The request for the rituals includes more than physical movements. A rite has an inward purpose: remembrance, humility, sacrifice, unity, repentance, and obedience. The believer asks not only, “What do I do?” but also, “What is this act meant to produce in me?” Ritual without meaning can become habit; meaning without revealed form can become personal invention.

### Repentance and Mercy

Abraham and Ishmael ask Allah to turn to them in grace and accept their repentance. Again, this is not evidence that prophets were guilty of the kinds of deliberate rebellion described earlier. It displays humility and the servant’s need for Allah’s continued care. The names *al-Tawwāb* and *al-Raḥīm* assure them that Allah repeatedly accepts sincere return and shows mercy.

A community also needs repentance. Building a sacred institution does not make its builders immune from error. They ask Allah to correct them, forgive their shortcomings, and preserve the work from corruption. The prayer should be recited in spirit by anyone building a family, school, mosque, charity, or public institution.

### A Model of Foundational Prayer

The prayer combines personal steadfastness, concern for descendants, correct worship, and repentance. These are the foundations of a healthy community. It does not ask only for wealth or political dominance. It asks for submission, guidance, and mercy so that material blessings will serve worship rather than replace it.

The believer should therefore pray for himself and future generations while taking practical steps to teach and embody faith. Supplication is not a substitute for education and example. Abraham and Ishmael raised the House, taught its rites, and prayed for acceptance. Action and prayer belong together.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:124–129 and 3:67–68 on Abraham’s submission, descendants, and the Sacred House.
- Qur’an 22:34 and 22:78 on worship, sacrifice, and being named Muslims.
- Qur’an 3:102 and 16:120–123 on remaining Muslim and following Abraham’s upright path.
- Qur’an 2:196 and 22:27–29 on pilgrimage rites and their purpose.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2140, on asking Allah to keep the heart firm; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1297, on learning pilgrimage rites from the Prophet.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:129

> Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. Indeed, You ˹alone˺ are the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Commentary:**

### Abraham’s Prayer for a Messenger

Among Abraham and Ishmael’s prayers was the request that Allah raise a messenger from among their descendants. The messenger would recite Allah’s revelations, teach the Book and wisdom, and purify the people. Muslims understand this prayer to have been fulfilled in Muhammad (ﷺ), who arose among the descendants of Ishmael and established the community around the Sacred House.

The prayer shows that prophethood is Allah’s choice, not a human appointment. Abraham could ask, but Allah alone selects the messenger, the time, the people, and the form of revelation. The Qur’an later says that Allah knows best where to place His message (6:124). This corrects envy and communal entitlement: no group can claim that revelation belongs to it by right.

The phrase “from among them” highlights the mercy of a messenger who shares the people’s language, human experience, and social world. A messenger remains a human servant, not a divine being, but his familiarity makes the message accessible. The Qur’an says that messengers were sent in the language of their people so that they could make the message clear (14:4).

### Four Functions of Prophetic Guidance

The first function is recitation—*yatlū ʿalayhim āyātik*. The Prophet conveys the actual revelations so that the community hears Allah’s words. The second is teaching the Book: explanation of its meanings, principles, commands, and applications. Recitation without teaching could leave people with sounds but not understanding.

The third is teaching wisdom—*al-ḥikmah*. The Sunnah is the prophetic embodiment and explanation of revelation: how to pray, give zakāh, fast, perform pilgrimage, conduct business, resolve conflict, and treat people. The Qur’an repeatedly pairs “the Book and wisdom” (2:151; 62:2), which is why Muslims study the authentic Sunnah alongside the Qur’an rather than treating them as competing authorities.

The fourth is purification—*tazkiyah*. Prophetic guidance is meant to cleanse beliefs from shirk, hearts from pride and envy, wealth from unlawful gain, tongues from falsehood, and society from injustice. Knowledge that leaves the soul arrogant has not achieved its purpose. Purification does not mean claiming sinlessness; it means continuous repentance and growth under revelation.

### Knowledge and Character Together

The order of the functions is not a rigid sequence, but it displays a complete educational method. The community hears revelation, learns its meaning, embodies wisdom, and becomes morally purified. The Prophet does not merely transmit information or merely inspire emotion. He forms people whose worship and conduct are integrated.

Sūrah al-Jumuʿah describes the same mission: reciting Allah’s verses, purifying people, and teaching them the Book and wisdom, although before that they were clearly astray (62:2). This shows that the Prophet’s mission addressed both intellectual error and moral disorder. A society can possess knowledge without purification, or religious emotion without knowledge; prophetic education joins both.

The same responsibility applies to teachers today. They should recite and explain revelation accurately, teach wisdom rather than slogans, and embody the character they seek to cultivate. Religious education must not be reduced to memorization, nor should moral activism be separated from scripture.

### The Messenger as the Answer to Abraham’s Prayer

The Qur’an presents Muhammad’s mission as connected to Abraham’s prayer and the building of the House. He called people to the same tawḥīd, restored the rites of pilgrimage, purified the sanctuary from idols, and taught a community how to worship Allah. This continuity confirms that Islam is not a new ethnic religion but the completion of the prophetic path.

The Prophet’s mission also fulfils the prayer’s concern for descendants. The request was not for power or wealth but for a messenger who would guide, teach, and purify. The greatest favour to a community is therefore not material dominance; it is clear revelation and a trustworthy teacher.

### Allah’s Might and Wisdom

The prayer ends with Allah’s names *al-ʿAzīz*—the Almighty—and *al-Ḥakīm*—the All-Wise. He has the power to send a messenger, preserve revelation, establish a community, and overcome opposition. He also knows the right time, person, language, and method. Prophethood is both an act of power and an act of wisdom.

The believer’s response is gratitude for the messenger and fidelity to the message. Love for the Prophet (ﷺ) is shown by following his Sunnah, honouring his character, and refusing to turn him into an object of worship. He is the servant who conveyed the revelation, while Allah alone is the source of guidance and purification.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:151 and 62:2 on the Prophet’s functions of recitation, teaching, wisdom, and purification.
- Qur’an 3:164 on Allah’s favour in sending a messenger from among the people.
- Qur’an 14:4 and 6:124 on messengers speaking the language of their people and Allah knowing where to place His message.
- Qur’an 33:21 on the Prophet as an excellent example.
- Qur’an 16:44 on the Prophet explaining what was revealed.
- For the connection of this prayer to Muhammad’s mission, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:129.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:130

> And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous.

**Commentary:**

### Abraham’s Faith Is the Straight Path

The Qur’an asks who would turn away from Abraham’s faith except one who has made a foolish choice. The Arabic *yarghab* can mean to turn away from or desire something instead; here the context is rejection of Abraham’s upright monotheism. The “foolish” person is not necessarily unintelligent in worldly matters. He is one whose judgment is corrupted because he prefers pride, inherited labels, or desire to the clear path of worshipping Allah alone.

Abraham’s faith is described elsewhere as *ḥanīf*—inclined away from idolatry and toward pure monotheism. He did not accept the religious customs of his people merely because they were ancient or popular. He examined the stars, moon, and sun as his people treated them as objects of worship, then rejected them because they rise and set and are created (6:74–79). His faith was intellectually serious and spiritually decisive.

The verse follows the mention of Abraham’s tests, the House, and his prayers. His faith is not a slogan detached from life. It appears in his willingness to leave false worship, endure hostility, obey Allah, serve his family, and build the sanctuary. To reject Abraham’s faith is therefore to reject a path of submission and moral courage.

### Chosen in This Life

Allah chose Abraham in this life. His selection included prophethood, intimate guidance, a reputation for truthfulness, leadership, and the honour of becoming an exemplar for humanity. The Qur’an says that Abraham was a community in himself, devoted to Allah and not a polytheist (16:120). This does not mean that he was chosen because of race or inherited privilege. His rank was connected to sincere worship and fulfilment of tests.

Abraham’s selection also demonstrates that divine honour is not measured by worldly power. He opposed a powerful society with no army or political office, yet his example outlived kings. A person may be unknown in the world and honoured by Allah, while a public ruler may be forgotten or condemned. The Qur’an’s standard is faith, truthfulness, and righteousness.

### Righteous in the Hereafter

Allah promises that Abraham will be among the righteous in the Hereafter. Righteousness—*ṣalāḥ*—includes sound belief, moral integrity, and being fit for Allah’s pleasure. Abraham’s future reward is connected to his earthly submission. He did not seek the honour of this world as an end; he sought Allah, and Allah granted him honour in both worlds.

The promise also connects the present and the future. A person’s choices now form the character with which he will meet Allah. Worship, patience, truthfulness, and sacrifice are not lost when the world fails to reward them. The Hereafter reveals the actual value of a life.

### Why Turning Away Is Foolish

Turning away from Abraham’s faith is foolish because it abandons the clearest foundation: worship of the One who created and sustains all things. It also abandons a model that joins reason, courage, family responsibility, and compassion. The person who rejects this path may gain social approval or preserve inherited identity, but he loses the truth that gives life its purpose.

The Qur’an’s use of “foolish” should not become an excuse for insulting people. The verse makes a theological judgment about rejecting tawḥīd; it does not authorize abusive speech toward individuals. The Prophet (ﷺ) called people to truth with wisdom and patience, and the Qur’an commands believers to speak in the best manner.

### Abraham Beyond Sectarian Ownership

Jews, Christians, and Muslims all claim Abraham, but the Qur’an says he was not defined by later communal labels. He lived before the Torah and Gospel were revealed and was a servant of Allah alone (3:65–67). His true heirs are those who follow his faith, including Muhammad (ﷺ) and those who believe with him (3:68).

This does not erase the historical significance of Abraham’s descendants or the legitimate differences among later communities. It rejects the idea that a group can own Abraham while contradicting his monotheism. The inheritance is spiritual and covenantal, not merely biological.

- Qur’an 3:65–68 on Abraham preceding Judaism and Christianity and the true heirs of his faith.
- Qur’an 6:74–79 on Abraham’s rejection of star, moon, and sun worship.
- Qur’an 16:120–123 on Abraham as a devoted monotheist and model.
- Qur’an 21:51–70 on Abraham’s reasoning against idolatry and his trials.
- For *safāhah*, *ḥanīf*, and Abraham’s selection, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:130.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:131

> When his Lord ordered him, “Submit ˹to My Will˺,” he responded, “I submit to the Lord of all worlds.”

**Commentary:**

### Abraham’s Immediate Submission

Allah commanded Abraham to submit, and Abraham answered without bargaining: “I submit to the Lord of all worlds.” His response is the essence of *islām*—surrendering the will to Allah. It does not mean that Abraham abandoned reason or became passive. His life shows reflection, debate with idolaters, migration, sacrifice, and practical action. Submission means that all of these are governed by Allah rather than by ego or social pressure.

The speed and clarity of the response are significant. Abraham did not say, “I will submit if the command is comfortable,” or “I will submit after my people approve.” He trusted the One who had tested and guided him. A servant may ask to understand a command, but he does not make human preference the final court over Allah’s revelation.

The title “Lord of all worlds”—*Rabb al-ʿālamīn*—makes the submission universal. Abraham did not submit to a tribal deity or a local power. He submitted to the Lord of every people, place, and created realm. This is why his faith cannot be owned by one ethnicity. The God of Abraham is the Lord of all humanity.

### Islam Before Later Labels

Abraham’s response explains why the Qur’an says he was neither a Jew nor a Christian in the later communal sense. He lived before those labels and submitted to Allah alone (3:67). The word Muslim in its universal meaning describes every prophet who surrendered to the one God. The final Sharīʿah brought through Muhammad (ﷺ) gives this submission its completed form for the last community.

This does not make religious history irrelevant. Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad each brought revelation to their communities, and the Qur’an commands Muslims to believe in them all. It does mean that later labels cannot be used to erase Abraham’s core message or to turn him into a symbol of group superiority.

### Submission and Moral Agency

Submission to Allah is sometimes misunderstood as the cancellation of human agency. The Qur’an presents the opposite. Abraham chooses to leave idolatry, speak truth, migrate, build the House, and obey the command concerning his son. His agency is not destroyed by submission; it is freed from false masters. A person enslaved to public opinion, wealth, anger, or appetite is less free than the one who submits to Allah.

The believer’s choices remain meaningful because Allah commands, forbids, rewards, and holds people accountable. Submission is a willing orientation of the heart, not the denial that human beings make choices. The Qur’an says, “There is no compulsion in religion” (2:256), while calling people to choose the path that leads to Allah.

### The Lord of Every Realm

Calling Allah the Lord of all worlds combines worship with trust. He is the Lord of human beings, angels, animals, the visible universe, and the unseen. No realm is outside His care or authority. Abraham’s submission therefore includes every part of life: worship, family, public responsibility, economics, and moral conduct.

This title also gives comfort during uncertainty. The believer may not understand every event, but the One to whom he submits is not a local power limited by circumstances. He is the Lord of every world, and His wisdom encompasses what the servant cannot see. Trust does not remove effort; it gives effort a stable direction.

### A Model of Response to Command

Abraham’s answer is a model for responding to revelation. First, know the One who commands: He is the Lord, Creator, and Sustainer. Second, seek understanding without arrogance. Third, when the truth is established, submit. Fourth, continue to act even when the command is costly. The following verses show Abraham and Jacob passing this orientation to their children.

The believer should make Abraham’s words a personal prayer. “I submit to the Lord of all worlds” means that no created person, group, ideology, or desire deserves the ultimate obedience that belongs to Allah.

- Qur’an 6:161 and 16:120–123 on Abraham’s upright monotheism.
- Qur’an 21:51–70 on Abraham’s reasoning and courageous rejection of idolatry.
- Qur’an 2:256 on the absence of compulsion and the reality of willing submission.
- Qur’an 42:13 on the unity of the religion of the prophets.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:132

> This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children, ˹saying˺, “Indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.”

**Commentary:**

### The Final Counsel of Two Prophets

The submission of Abraham was not merely a personal achievement; it became the counsel he passed to his children. Jacob, also known as Israel, gave the same counsel. The verse links the two prophets through a shared concern: preserving tawḥīd and submission in the next generation. Their most important inheritance was not land, wealth, or social status but a living relationship with Allah.

The word *waṣṣā* indicates serious advice or a binding bequest. A final counsel often reveals what a person considers most important. Abraham and Jacob did not make their children’s identity depend upon pride in ancestry. They directed them to the faith Allah had chosen and warned them to preserve it until death.

The faith chosen by Allah is submission to Him, not the later labels that communities attached to themselves. Abraham and Jacob were not asking their children to build a tribal religion. They were transmitting the religion of all prophets: worship Allah alone, obey His guidance, and remain ready to follow truth when it comes.

### “Do Not Die Except in Submission”

The command does not mean that a person can control the moment of death. It means: live continuously in submission so that when death arrives, you are found in that state. The Qur’an gives the same instruction to believers: “O believers, be mindful of Allah as He deserves and do not die except in a state of submission” (3:102).

A person cannot postpone faith and repentance until the last moment. The final state grows from repeated choices. If someone trains the heart in worship, truthfulness, repentance, and patience, he prepares to meet Allah in submission. If he repeatedly defers obedience, he may reach death without the moral readiness he assumed would appear automatically.

The command is also hopeful. Submission is not a single emotional experience that must remain at one intensity for a lifetime. It is a direction to which the servant continually returns. A believer may weaken, sin, or become confused, but he renews his submission through repentance and obedience. The goal is not a claim of perfection; it is a faithful orientation until the end.

### Chosen Faith and Personal Responsibility

Allah chose the faith for them, but the children still had to accept and live it. Divine selection of guidance does not make human response irrelevant. A parent can teach, model, and pray; he cannot believe on behalf of his child. A child can inherit a religious name; he cannot inherit another person’s obedience.

The following verse records Jacob asking his children whom they would worship after his death. Their answer demonstrates that the counsel was received as conscious faith rather than empty family tradition. This is a model of intergenerational teaching: parents give the foundation, explain its reason, and allow the next generation to affirm it.

### The Family as a School of Tawḥīd

Abraham and Jacob understood that family life shapes belief. Children learn what is truly valued by observing how parents pray, speak, use money, treat the weak, and respond to disagreement. A family that teaches tawḥīd but worships status sends a contradictory message. A family that speaks of mercy while practising cruelty undermines its own instruction.

The best parental counsel joins words with example. Abraham’s children saw his submission expressed in migration, sacrifice, hospitality, and the building of the Sacred House. Jacob’s life likewise embodied patience, trust, and concern for his family. The believer should ask Allah to make his conduct support the religious counsel he gives.

### Submission at the Point of Death

The verse also teaches that the end matters. A person may have made many mistakes but still return sincerely before death. Allah accepts repentance and changes evil deeds into good for those who believe, repent, and act righteously (25:70). The command to die in submission is therefore not a demand for a flawless life; it is a call to keep returning until the final meeting.

The prayer of Abraham and Jacob remains relevant in every household. Parents should ask not merely that their children be successful by worldly standards, but that they remain people of faith, integrity, prayer, and submission. Children should honour their parents while following Allah if a parent commands sin. The ultimate loyalty is to Allah, and family counsel is most valuable when it directs the family to Him.

- Qur’an 31:13–19 on Luqmān’s counsel concerning tawḥīd, prayer, humility, and good conduct.
- Qur’an 25:70 and 39:53 on repentance and Allah’s mercy.
- Qur’an 6:164 on individual responsibility and the limits of inherited identity.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:133

> Or did you witness when death came to Jacob? He asked his children, “Who will you worship after my passing?” They replied, “We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers—Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.”

**Commentary:**

### Jacob’s Final Concern

The verse asks whether the later claimants had witnessed Jacob’s deathbed conversation. It challenges the idea that the patriarchs belonged to later communal labels such as Judaism or Christianity. Jacob’s concern at death was not political identity or ritual branding; it was whether his children would continue to worship Allah alone.

Death often reveals a person’s deepest priorities. Jacob’s final concern was the continuity of tawḥīd in his family. He did not ask which tribe would inherit authority or how his children would preserve social status. He asked whom they would worship. The question places worship at the centre of inheritance and shows that a prophet’s greatest fear for his descendants is deviation from Allah.

The verse does not mean that Jacob knew the exact time of death in a way ordinary people do or that the Qur’an is giving a full biography of his final hours. It presents a scene that refutes a later claim about the patriarchs’ religion. Their faith was submission to Allah before later labels arose.

### The Answer of the Children

The children answer that they will worship Jacob’s God and the God of Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. They name their ancestors not to claim independent spiritual power from them but to affirm the shared faith they transmitted. Their answer preserves both continuity and tawḥīd.

Ishmael is mentioned among Jacob’s forefathers even though he was his uncle rather than his direct ancestor in the narrow genealogical sense. The expression refers to the patriarchal family line and the revered forefathers whose faith shaped the community. It does not create a technical genealogy that overrides the broader scriptural narrative.

The answer also demonstrates that family inheritance is not merely biological. To follow Abraham, Isaac, Ishmael, and Jacob is to follow their worship of the One God. A person who claims their lineage while rejecting their faith has inherited a name without inheriting the covenant.

### One God, One Direction of Submission

The phrase “the One God” is the core of the answer. The patriarchs did not worship different regional or ethnic deities. Their God was one, and to Him they submitted. This unity underlies the Qur’an’s call to Jews and Christians: come to a common word that we worship none but Allah and do not take one another as lords besides Him (3:64).

Submission to the One God does not erase differences in law between prophetic communities. Allah gave different communities laws and ways while maintaining the same foundation of tawḥīd (5:48). The final revelation confirms the foundation and provides the final law for the last community. The unity is therefore theological and spiritual, not a denial of all historical difference.

### Religious Identity and Historical Accuracy

The verse asks later communities to be honest about history. Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, and Jacob lived before the Torah and Gospel in their later forms. They cannot accurately be claimed as members of those later labels. The Qur’an says, “O People of the Book, why do you argue about Abraham when the Torah and Gospel were not revealed until after him?” (3:65).

This is not an attack upon Jews or Christians as people. It is a correction of anachronism. Historical figures should not be recruited into later sectarian disputes without regard for chronology or their actual message. The best way to honour an ancestor is to follow his truth rather than use his name to defend a contradiction.

### A Model for Family Conversation

Jacob’s question provides a model for parents and elders. He does not assume that descendants will remain faithful simply because they were raised in a religious family. He asks directly, creates space for them to affirm their belief, and makes tawḥīd the foundation of their identity. Families should discuss worship, ethics, death, and accountability openly rather than leaving children to inherit only cultural habits.

The children’s answer also shows that faith must be personally affirmed. They do not merely say, “We will follow our father’s group.” They name Allah, affirm His oneness, and declare their submission. Inherited religion becomes living faith when it is understood and chosen.

- Qur’an 31:13–19 on family counsel and the primacy of tawḥīd.
- Qur’an 6:164 on personal accountability beyond ancestry.
- For Jacob’s deathbed question, Ishmael’s mention, and the theological force of the passage, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:133.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:134

> That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done.

**Commentary:**

### The Past Cannot Be Borrowed

After recounting Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and their descendants, the Qur’an draws a boundary between past and present. They were a community that passed away; their deeds belong to them, and the deeds of later people belong to them. No generation can borrow another generation’s righteousness as a substitute for its own obedience.

This is a direct correction to religious pride based on ancestry. A person may descend from prophets or belong to a respected tradition, but he is not saved by genealogy. Abraham’s virtue cannot be claimed by someone who rejects Abraham’s monotheism, and Jacob’s counsel cannot protect someone who abandons the worship of Allah. Heritage is a responsibility to continue truth, not a certificate of immunity.

The principle applies equally to Muslims. The Companions were honoured for their faith and sacrifice, but later Muslims cannot rely upon their rank while neglecting prayer, justice, and repentance. A community’s early excellence should inspire imitation, not replace present action.

### Individual Accountability

“For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned” establishes individual accountability. Each person will be judged for his own belief, intention, and conduct. The Qur’an says, “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164), and that a person will have only what he strives for (53:39).

This does not deny that people can influence one another. A person who teaches good receives reward for those who follow, and a person who initiates evil carries a share of the burden of those he misleads (16:25; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1017). The distinction is that one is accountable for his own influence, not punished for another’s independent action that he did not cause or support.

Individual accountability protects the innocent from collective blame. It also prevents the guilty from hiding behind a group. A parent cannot carry a child’s deliberate disbelief, and a child cannot claim a parent’s righteousness as his own. Leaders and followers are each judged, with Allah knowing the degree of responsibility in every case.

### History as Guidance, Not Escape

The Qur’an preserves the stories of earlier communities so that later people can learn, not so that they can escape accountability by discussing the past. The proper question is not merely, “Were Abraham and Jacob righteous?” It is, “What does their righteousness require of me?” Their lives become guidance when the reader imitates their submission, truthfulness, courage, and concern for descendants.

The warning also applies to historical grievances. A community cannot commit injustice today and excuse it by invoking a past injury, just as it cannot demand honour because of an ancestor’s virtue while repeating the ancestor’s opponents’ behaviour. Allah’s judgment is present and personal, even though history supplies lessons and context.

### The Balance Between Continuity and Responsibility

Islam values continuity with the prophets. Muslims believe in Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad (peace be upon them), preserve their stories, and follow the final revelation. But continuity does not erase personal responsibility. Each generation must renew faith and make the truth its own through worship and action.

This balance protects against both rootlessness and ancestralism. A person without historical memory may lose the wisdom of revelation; a person obsessed with ancestry may use history to avoid reform. The Qur’an asks the reader to receive the past as a trust and answer for the present.

### A Warning Against Religious Boasting

Religious boasting often takes the form of saying, “Our founder was righteous, therefore we are safe,” or “Our ancestors received revelation, therefore our current conduct needs no examination.” This verse refuses the argument. Honourable ancestors increase the seriousness of betraying their path because their example makes the truth more visible.

The believer should therefore thank Allah for a righteous heritage by living righteously, not by using it as a shield. He prays for the prophets and the righteous while remembering that Allah will ask him about his own prayer, speech, relationships, wealth, and response to guidance.

- Qur’an 16:25 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1017, on the burden or reward of influencing others.
- Qur’an 35:18 and 39:7 on no person carrying another’s burden.
- Qur’an 12:111 on historical stories as lessons rather than inherited excuses.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:135

> The Jews and Christians each say, “Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”

**Commentary:**

### Guidance Is Not Owned by a Label

The Jews and Christians are described as inviting the Prophet and believers to follow their respective religious identities as the condition of guidance. The Qur’an answers by directing them to the faith of Abraham, the original monotheistic foundation before the later communal divisions. The response is not a rejection of Moses or Jesus; it is a rejection of the claim that a later label can monopolize the truth or redefine Abraham’s faith.

The verse speaks to the dispute’s historical context and to claims made by some members of the communities. It should not be turned into a statement that every Jew or Christian is dishonest or that Muslims are permitted to treat them unjustly. The Qur’an recognizes upright People of the Book (3:113–115) and commands respectful engagement and justice.

The issue is authority. When a group says, “Follow our label and you will be guided,” the Qur’an asks whether the label agrees with revelation. Guidance is not secured by ancestry, institutional membership, or social pressure. It is secured by sincere submission to Allah and adherence to the truth He revealed.

### Abraham the Upright Monotheist

Abraham is described as *ḥanīf*—upright, inclined away from false worship and toward pure monotheism. The Qur’an says he was not a polytheist. His faith was not a mixture of worshipping Allah and preserving parts of his people’s idolatry. He rejected the idols, challenged his community, and trusted Allah when obedience required separation from familiar customs.

The denial of polytheism is important because religious communities can claim Abraham while introducing intermediaries, divine partners, or human authorities with rights that belong only to Allah. The Qur’an repeatedly links Abraham’s faith to the command to worship Allah alone (16:120–123). His legacy is not merely genealogical; it is a way of life centred upon tawḥīd.

The word *ḥanīf* also suggests moral direction. Abraham did not drift with every social current. He turned deliberately toward Allah. A believer may live among people with different beliefs and cooperate with them in justice, but his ultimate worship and orientation remain clear.

### Islam as Continuity, Not Invention

The response “we follow the faith of Abraham” presents Islam as continuity with the prophetic tradition. Muhammad (ﷺ) did not invent a new God or a new principle of worship. He restored and completed the call of Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and all the messengers. The Qur’an commands Muslims to say that they believe in what was revealed to all of them and submit to Allah (2:136).

This continuity also explains why Muslims honour the earlier prophets. Following Muhammad does not require denigrating Abraham, Moses, or Jesus. It requires affirming their true message and accepting the final revelation that confirms and corrects what people later attributed to them. The final criterion is the Qur’an, and the final messenger is Muhammad (ﷺ).

### “No” as a Firm but Respectful Answer

The Prophet’s response begins with “No!”—a clear refusal to surrender truth to sectarian pressure. Firmness does not require abuse. A person can reject a false claim while protecting the dignity and rights of those who make it. The Qur’an commands believers to debate the People of the Book in the best manner, except with those who commit injustice (29:46).

The believer should therefore avoid two opposite errors. He should not dilute the truth to make everyone comfortable, and he should not express conviction through insult or injustice. Abraham himself spoke to his people with clarity and reasoning, while the Qur’an records his concern that they be guided.

### The Personal Challenge

The verse also asks Muslims whether they follow Abraham’s faith in reality. It is easy to claim tawḥīd while obeying wealth, tribe, political identity, or public opinion whenever these compete with Allah. The test is whether the believer follows Allah when the command challenges inherited practice or costs social approval.

Following Abraham means leaving false objects of worship, trusting Allah, fulfilling obligations, caring for family, serving the sanctuary, and remaining upright under pressure. It is not an argument for ethnic superiority. Abraham’s true heirs are those who follow his faith, regardless of their ancestry.

- Qur’an 2:130–134 and 3:65–68 on Abraham’s faith preceding later Jewish and Christian labels.
- Qur’an 6:74–79 and 16:120–123 on Abraham’s rejection of idolatry and his ḥanīf faith.
- Qur’an 2:136 and 3:84 on believing in all prophets and revelations.
- Qur’an 29:46 on respectful discussion with the People of the Book.
- Qur’an 49:13 on honour being based on taqwā, not lineage.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:136

> Say, ˹O believers,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.”

**Commentary:**

### A Complete Creed of Revelation

This verse gives the believers a concise declaration of faith. They believe in Allah, the Qur’an revealed to Muhammad (ﷺ), and the revelations granted to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, his descendants, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets from their Lord. The list is not exhaustive; it represents the entire chain of divine messengers.

The creed begins with Allah because revelation has meaning only in relation to its source. Muslims do not worship scripture, prophets, angels, or historical figures. They worship Allah and believe the guidance He sent through His messengers. The Qur’an says that every messenger called people to worship Allah and avoid false gods (16:36).

The phrase “what has been revealed to us” includes the Qur’an and the authentic prophetic guidance that explains its application. The phrase “what was revealed” to earlier prophets affirms the original divine scriptures, not every later text, interpretation, or claim attributed to them. The Qur’an confirms earlier truth and acts as a criterion over disputed or altered material (5:48).

### No Distinction Between the Prophets in Faith

“We make no distinction between any of them” means that Muslims do not accept some prophets and reject others. Jews, Christians, and polytheists had divided the messengers according to communal preference, but the Muslim creed recognizes all genuine prophets. A person who believes in Moses but rejects Jesus, or accepts Jesus while rejecting Muhammad, has not accepted the unity of the prophetic mission.

This does not mean that all prophets had exactly the same rank or that their individual missions were identical. The Qur’an says that Allah raised some messengers above others (2:253). It means that Muslims do not denigrate a prophet, deny his prophethood, or accept one while rejecting another whom Allah sent. Distinction in rank is not division in faith.

The verse also protects Muslims from creating a hierarchy of ethnic or cultural value. Allah’s messengers came from different peoples and languages, but the source of their message was one. No nation owns revelation, and no community may claim that Allah’s guidance becomes less true because it came through another people.

### The Names of the Patriarchs

The verse mentions Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants. This directly addresses the People of the Book’s attempt to claim the patriarchs exclusively. Muslims honour these prophets, but they do not turn their names into objects of worship or use their ancestry to claim automatic salvation. Their legacy is submission to Allah.

Jacob’s descendants included prophets, righteous people, and wrongdoers. The Qur’an’s use of the family list does not guarantee every descendant. It calls later communities back to the faith that animated the patriarchs. Whoever follows that faith joins the prophetic community in the spiritual sense.

### “To Allah We All Submit”

The declaration ends with submission—*wa-naḥnu lahu muslimūn*. Belief in revelation is not merely a list of historical propositions. It produces surrender to Allah. The believer hears the messages of the prophets and accepts their common call to worship, justice, repentance, and accountability.

Submission does not mean that Muslims must erase legitimate differences in law, history, or community practice. The Qur’an says that Allah gave different communities laws and ways and tests them in what He revealed (5:48). It means that the foundation of devotion is one and that the final community submits to the final revelation.

This declaration also teaches how to speak about religious disagreement. Muslims state their creed clearly without inventing false claims about other prophets or communities. They honour the truth Allah revealed, reject what contradicts it, and leave final judgment of individuals to Allah.

### The Unity of Belief and Conduct

The creed must be lived. Believing in Allah and the prophets should produce truthful speech, prayer, charity, justice, and mercy. The verse is not a formula that excuses hypocrisy. The next verse explains that if others embrace the same faith, they are guided; if they turn away, the dispute remains. The test is whether belief produces submission in action.

For Muslims, reciting this declaration should renew gratitude for the continuity of revelation. They are part of a history larger than their culture or nation. They inherit the call of Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad, and all the prophets, and they are responsible for preserving that call through sincere worship and good conduct.

- Qur’an 5:44–48 on earlier scripture, confirmation, and the Qur’an as criterion.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:137

> So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺. But Allah will spare you their evil. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### The Standard of Shared Faith

The verse tells the believers that if the People of the Book believe in what they believe—faith in Allah, all His messengers, His revelations, and the final message—then they will be guided. The standard is not conversion to an ethnic identity or submission to a political group. It is acceptance of the unified creed of revelation and the final guidance Allah sent through Muhammad (ﷺ).

The verse follows the declaration of faith in 2:136. That declaration is inclusive without being vague: it affirms Allah, the prophets, the revealed Books, and submission to Allah. It rejects the selective practice of believing in some messengers and denying others. The final revelation confirms the original truth of earlier scripture and serves as the criterion in disputes about it (5:48).

The phrase “if they turn away” describes deliberate refusal after the truth has been made clear. It does not assign the same judgment to every person who has not heard the message properly, is sincerely investigating, or is confused by inaccurate information. Allah knows the evidence each person received and judges with perfect justice. Human beings convey the message; Allah knows the hidden circumstances.

### Opposition to Truth

Those who turn away are described as being in opposition—*shiqāq*. The word conveys division, hostility, and a deliberate position against the truth. This is more than ordinary disagreement. Legitimate questions and scholarly discussion can occur without hostility, but a person who rejects clear guidance because it threatens pride or interest has chosen opposition.

The Qur’an repeatedly warns that guidance is not only a matter of intellectual access. A person may recognize the truth and still reject it because of envy, group loyalty, fear, or desire. The verse therefore connects faith with moral courage: accept truth even when it requires changing an inherited identity or admitting that one’s community was wrong.

The warning also applies to Muslims. A person may recite the creed of 2:136 and then oppose a clear command because it conflicts with culture or political interest. He may believe in all prophets verbally while rejecting the ethical demands of revelation. Genuine faith keeps the believer willing to be corrected.

### Allah Protects the Believers

Allah promises to spare the believers from the evil of those who turn away. This does not mean that no believer will ever suffer harm from an opponent. The Prophet and his Companions endured persecution, expulsion, and war. It means that Allah protects the faith, ultimate welfare, and final outcome of those who remain sincere. He can restrain enemies, expose plots, grant patience, and preserve the community from being spiritually overcome.

The promise also discourages believers from taking revenge into their own hands. They defend themselves and establish justice within lawful limits, but they trust Allah to deal with hidden hostility. The Qur’an says, “If you are patient and mindful of Allah, their plot will not harm you at all” (3:120). Patience does not mean passivity; it means refusing to let an enemy’s behaviour dictate one’s moral character.

### All-Hearing and All-Knowing

The verse closes with Allah’s names *al-Samīʿ* and *al-ʿAlīm*. He hears the believers’ prayer, the opponents’ arguments, and the hidden speech of both groups. He knows who seeks truth, who turns away from envy, who is confused, and who deliberately resists. This makes the promise of protection trustworthy.

These names also teach believers to speak with care. Allah hears the public debate and the private insult, the sincere invitation and the manipulative argument. He knows the intention behind a claim. A person should therefore not defend Islam through falsehood or injustice, believing that a good cause makes bad means acceptable.

### Clarity and Compassion

The verse offers a firm criterion while preserving compassion. Muslims should state that guidance is found in the revelation brought by Muhammad (ﷺ), but they should not treat every non-Muslim as an enemy or claim knowledge of every person’s final state. They invite with wisdom, act justly, and leave hidden judgment to Allah.

The best proof of a community’s faith is not merely its polemical confidence. It is the truthfulness, mercy, justice, worship, and integrity that the faith produces. Allah’s protection is sought through obedience, not through arrogance toward those who have not accepted the message.

- Qur’an 5:48 on the Qur’an confirming previous scripture and serving as a criterion.
- Qur’an 3:120 and 8:46 on patience, obedience, and protection from the harm of conflict.
- Qur’an 16:125 and 29:46 on inviting and debating with wisdom and good conduct.
- Qur’an 17:15 on Allah’s justice before punishment.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:138

> This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him.

**Commentary:**

### Allah’s Colour and the Natural Way

The supplied translation renders this verse as “This is the natural Way of Allah.” In the Arabic text, the expression is *ṣibghat Allāh*—literally, “the colour or dye of Allah.” Classical exegetes explained it metaphorically as Allah’s religion, the lasting mark of faith, or the spiritual colouring that submission gives to a person’s whole life. The wording suggests that genuine faith permeates the believer as dye permeates cloth; it is not a removable label worn only in public.

The verse follows the declaration that Muslims believe in all revelation and submit to Allah. Their “colour” is not ethnicity, dress, political identity, or cultural custom. It is tawḥīd, sincerity, purification, truthfulness, and obedience. This is why many translations express *ṣibghah* through the language of a way, nature, or religious pattern. The believer is formed by Allah’s guidance rather than by the competing colours of tribal pride and sectarian identity.

The metaphor also suggests permanence and visibility. Dye changes the appearance of cloth, and faith should change the person’s way of seeing and acting. It should affect prayer, speech, relationships, business, justice, and the response to hardship. A person whose faith leaves every moral habit untouched should ask whether the colour has reached the heart or remains only a claim.

### The Religion of the Fitrah

The translation’s reference to a natural way connects with the Qur’anic concept of *fiṭrah*: the disposition Allah created people upon, inclined toward recognition of their Creator and the truth (30:30). Islam does not ask human beings to become something alien to their deepest purpose. It calls them back from distortions produced by idolatry, injustice, and heedlessness.

Natural does not mean that every instinct is automatically true or that every personal desire is sacred. Human beings possess both a disposition toward truth and appetites that require guidance. Revelation protects the fitrah from being buried under culture, pride, trauma, and desire. The natural way is therefore not unrestrained instinct; it is the sound orientation of a person whose life is aligned with the Creator.

The Prophet (ﷺ) said that every child is born upon the fitrah, and that later environment shapes the religious identity into which the child is raised (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The ḥadīth does not deny personal responsibility or the influence of revelation. It explains why tawḥīd can feel like a return to something deeply familiar even when a person has been surrounded by competing beliefs.

### “Who Is Better Than Allah in Ordaining a Way?”

The rhetorical question affirms Allah’s perfect authority to prescribe a way of life. Human beings create systems according to limited knowledge, competing interests, and changing preferences. Allah knows the human being completely and guides him with wisdom. His way protects worship, family, life, wealth, honour, and moral responsibility.

This does not mean that every human institution or social arrangement outside a religious label is automatically wrong. Human beings can discover beneficial methods of administration, medicine, education, and technology. The verse concerns the ultimate path of worship and moral direction. Practical knowledge can be learned from many sources, but no created authority can replace Allah in defining ultimate truth and obligation.

The question also corrects the assumption that revelation is a burden imposed against human nature. Divine commands may restrain desires, but they protect the human being from enslavement to those desires. Fasting disciplines appetite, zakāh loosens possessiveness, prayer interrupts heedlessness, and justice restrains power. Allah’s way is better because it orders freedom toward genuine human flourishing.

### “We Worship None but Him”

The verse ends with a concise declaration of exclusive worship. The natural way is not merely a philosophy of balance or personal spirituality. It is worshipping Allah alone. The believer’s inward colour becomes visible in *ʿibādah*: prayer, reliance, love, fear, hope, obedience, and gratitude are directed ultimately to Him.

This statement also protects the metaphor from becoming a new identity idol. Muslims should not worship the name “Islam,” the community, the scholar, or their own image as religious people. They worship Allah. Their affiliation with the community is valuable only insofar as it helps them submit to Him.

### A Faith That Permeates Life

The verse invites the believer to ask what has coloured his heart. Is it Allah’s guidance, or anger, envy, wealth, nationality, sect, and public approval? Every influence leaves a mark. The Qur’an’s “colour” is renewed through recitation, prayer, repentance, truthful companionship, and obedience.

A community that carries Allah’s colour should be recognizable by justice and mercy rather than by slogans alone. It does not abandon conviction, but it also does not use conviction as a justification for oppression. Tawḥīd purifies the heart from worshipping created power and makes the believer a servant of truth.

- Qur’an 16:120–123 on Abraham’s monotheism and the command to follow his path.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:139

> Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺.

**Commentary:**

### Allah Is Lord of All

The Prophet is instructed to ask why the People of the Book dispute with the believers about Allah when He is both their Lord and the believers’ Lord. This is a powerful argument against religious tribalism. Allah is not the private possession of a community. He created, sustains, and judges all people, and no group can claim exclusive ownership of Him.

The statement does not deny that communities differ in their beliefs about Allah. It rejects the assumption that one group’s inherited claim is enough to settle the dispute. Since Allah is Lord of all, the question must be decided by revelation, evidence, and sincere submission—not by ancestry, social power, or the loudness of the argument.

The common lordship also provides a basis for respectful dialogue. Muslims can affirm that Allah is the Lord of every person while explaining that worship must be directed to Him alone. Shared creation does not mean all beliefs are correct, but it means no one has the right to deny another person’s basic dignity or to treat religious disagreement as permission for injustice.

### Each Person Bears His Own Deeds

“We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours.” The phrase establishes personal and communal responsibility without making one group responsible for another’s private choices. Each person will answer for belief, worship, speech, relationships, wealth, and conduct. An ancestor’s righteousness cannot replace the descendant’s obedience, and another community’s wrongdoing cannot become an excuse for one’s own.

The Qur’an repeatedly teaches that no bearer of burdens carries another’s burden (6:164). This does not cancel the consequences of influence. Those who teach, encourage, finance, or normalize wrongdoing may share its burden, while those who establish a good practice may share its reward. The basic principle remains that accountability follows one’s own choices and influence.

The statement also restrains religious argument. Muslims should explain their faith and distinguish truth from falsehood, but they should not pretend to possess Allah’s knowledge of every individual’s final state. They are responsible for their own sincerity and conduct; Allah will judge each person with complete knowledge.

### Devotion to Allah Alone

The believers declare that they are devoted exclusively to Allah—*mukhliṣūn*. Sincerity means purifying worship from partners, reputation, and divided allegiance. They do not worship Allah as one deity among others, nor do they serve Him only when His commands agree with their group’s desires. Their devotion is directed to Him alone.

Sincerity also means that religious debate is not a performance for victory. A person may use correct arguments while seeking applause, humiliation of an opponent, or dominance over an audience. The believer asks Allah to make the truth clear and to protect him from using truth as a tool of ego. Knowledge and speech should be acts of worship.

### A Model of Confident Dialogue

The verse models a confident but measured response. The Prophet does not surrender the truth to avoid dispute, and he does not insult the opponents. He identifies the shared Lord, clarifies distinct accountability, and states the believers’ exclusive devotion. This is the Qur’anic balance of conviction and justice.

The same pattern can guide interfaith conversation today. Muslims can explain tawḥīd, the continuity of revelation, and the finality of Muhammad’s message while treating neighbours and dialogue partners with fairness. They need not dilute their creed, and they must not turn creed into an excuse for contempt.

### The Limits of Religious Inheritance

The People of the Book claimed a special relation to Allah, but the verse redirects the discussion from group claims to actual deeds. A person’s religious heritage can be a blessing, but it does not become a substitute for worship. The believers’ claim is not “Allah belongs to us”; it is “Allah is our Lord and your Lord, and each of us will answer for what we do.”

This is also a warning to Muslims. They must not claim Allah’s support while ignoring justice, or speak of being devoted to Him while secretly serving wealth, tribe, or reputation. Exclusive worship becomes credible when it produces truthful and accountable action.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:140

> Or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians?” Say, “Who is more knowledgeable: you or Allah?” Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Commentary:**

### Anachronistic Claims About the Patriarchs

The verse challenges the claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and their descendants were Jews or Christians in the later historical sense. The patriarchs lived before the Torah and Gospel were revealed in their later forms. It is therefore anachronistic to place them inside later communal labels and then use their names to prove exclusive ownership of the faith.

The Qur’an makes the chronological argument explicit: “O People of the Book, why do you argue about Abraham when the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until after him?” (3:65). Abraham’s faith was submission to Allah and rejection of idolatry. He was neither a polytheist nor a member of a later sectarian identity (3:67).

The verse does not deny the historical connection of Jews and Christians to the prophetic tradition. It rejects the misuse of that connection. To honour Abraham is to follow his worship of the One God, not merely to claim his name while rejecting the message he embodied.

### “Who Is More Knowledgeable: You or Allah?”

The question is rhetorical and exposes the absurdity of claiming to know the religious identity of the patriarchs better than Allah, who revealed their story. Human beings may possess records, traditions, and interpretations, but Allah knows the prophets’ belief, chronology, intentions, and covenant completely.

This is also a general lesson in religious argument. When a claim concerns the unseen, the origin of revelation, or the faith of a prophet, human preference cannot overrule Allah’s disclosure. The believer should distinguish between what revelation establishes, what scholarship infers, and what later communities assume. Certainty belongs only where evidence warrants it.

The question also humbles Muslims. They cannot claim to know more than Allah about the inner state of historical figures or the final state of individuals. The Qur’an teaches what is necessary for guidance and leaves some matters unknown. Pretending to certainty beyond revelation is itself a form of speaking about Allah without knowledge.

### Hiding Testimony from Allah

The verse asks who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah. The “testimony” includes the knowledge contained in genuine revelation concerning the prophets, the unity of their message, and the signs of the final messenger. Concealing it protects a group’s status but violates a trust given for guidance.

To hide testimony is not only to refuse to speak. It can include altering a text, suppressing a relevant passage, presenting a partial narrative, or teaching a tradition in a way that prevents people from recognizing its implications. The Qur’an condemns those who conceal clear guidance after Allah has made it clear to people (2:159; 3:187).

The warning applies to every bearer of knowledge. A Muslim who hides a command because it threatens his position repeats the same sin. A teacher who suppresses evidence to preserve a faction’s reputation has made knowledge serve identity rather than truth. Sincere scholarship conveys what is established, acknowledges uncertainty, and does not manufacture certainty to protect a group.

### Allah Is Never Unaware

The closing statement affirms that Allah is never unaware of what people do. The concealment may succeed socially, and the community may preserve its preferred narrative, but Allah knows what was received, what was hidden, and why it was hidden. He also knows the sincere person who speaks truth at personal cost and the person who remains silent to avoid danger or confusion.

Allah’s knowledge makes the warning just. Human beings should not claim access to every hidden motive, but they should take clear evidence seriously. The Qur’an commands verification and justice while leaving final judgment of hearts to Allah. No reputation can erase a concealed testimony from the divine record.

### Following Abraham’s Actual Faith

The proper answer to sectarian claims is not to fight over ownership of Abraham but to follow his faith. He worshipped Allah alone, rejected idols, accepted divine tests, cared for his descendants, built the Sacred House, and prayed for a messenger who would teach and purify. These qualities are more important than a label.

The verse therefore concludes the passage on Abraham by exposing the danger of manipulating his identity. Historical figures should be approached through revelation, not recruited as symbols for claims they never made. The believer honours the patriarchs by sharing their submission and truthfulness.
