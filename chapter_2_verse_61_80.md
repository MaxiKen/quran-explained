## Verse 61

Verse 61 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when you said, "O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions." Moses scolded ˹them˺, "Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for." They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah's signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now records the complaint of the Children of Israel against the manna and quails. They said to Moses: we cannot endure the same meal every day. Ask your Lord to give us what the earth produces — herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions. Moses answered with sharp rebuke: do you exchange what is better for what is worse? Then he told them to go down to any village and they would find what they asked for. The verse then records the consequences: they were struck with disgrace and misery; they incurred Allah's displeasure for rejecting His signs and killing the prophets; and the whole outcome was the fair reward of their disobedience.

The food they asked for is striking for its ordinariness. Herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions — the staple vegetables of the earth — were the very things they had left in Egypt, where they were enslaved. The manna and quails, by contrast, were the miraculous, refined, effortless provision of Allah. The people were not asking for something better; they were asking for something *lower* — for the food of their captivity rather than the gift of their deliverance. Moses' question, "Do you exchange what is better for what is worse?" exposes the psychology of complaint: the people who already had the best were dissatisfied because they didn't have the familiar. The complaint was not about nutrition; it was about the imagination. They preferred the taste of what they knew, even at the cost of what was superior.

The deeper lesson is the one the Qur'an draws in the same verse: the exchange of "what is better for what is worse" is the fundamental pattern of human ingratitude and rebellion. The same exchange of truth for falsehood, guidance for desire, and the great for the small runs through this entire passage, and it is the essence of every sin that prefers the immediate, familiar, and pleasurable to the commanded, distant, and true. The person who trades his soul for a moment of satisfaction is doing what the Israelites did: exchanging what is better for what is worse.

The verse's final sentence is a compressed historical verdict: "They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah's signs and unjustly killing the prophets." The disgrace and misery are the consequences of their long history of rebellion — the wandering, the rejection of the prophets, the killing of those who were sent to them. The phrase "unjustly killing the prophets" refers to a pattern that runs through the history of the Children of Israel and is repeated elsewhere in the Qur'an (2:87, 2:91, 3:21, 3:112, 4:155). The killing of prophets is perhaps the most extreme example of the corruption of a people: they rejected the very messengers who came to save them. The verse ends with the phrase *dhālika bimā 'asaw wa kānū ya'tadūn* — "that is because they disobeyed and violated the limits." The punishment corresponds to the crime.

The modern application is powerful. So much of modern dissatisfaction is the same exchange: people reject what is better because it is unfamiliar and complain about what they have. The believer who has been given the gift of faith — and all the goods of the world — can find himself complaining for the same reason the Israelites complained: not because he lacks, but because he has lost the imagination of gratitude. Moses' question cuts through: are you exchanging what is better for what is worse? The verse is an invitation to measure what we have against what is truly good, and to thank the Giver rather than demand the familiar.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 62

Verse 62 says: *"Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, this verse is one of the most important statements of the Qur'an about salvation. It names four groups: the believers (the Muslims), the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians. And then it says that whoever among *any* of these truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good — will have his reward with his Lord, with no fear and no grief. The verse teaches that the criterion of salvation is not the label but the faith and the deeds.

The first thing to understand is the direction of the verse. This is *not* a verse that says every Jew, Christian, and Sabian will be saved regardless of what they believe. The verse says the opposite: it specifies the condition — *man āmana billāhi wal-yawm al-ākhir* — "whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day." A Jew who denies Allah is not saved by being a Jew. A Christian who denies the Last Day is not saved by being a Christian. A Muslim who does not do good is not saved by the label "believer." The groups are named not as categories of automatic salvation but as communities within which salvation is found by those who meet the conditions. And the conditions are explicitly stated: true belief in Allah, true belief in the Last Day, and the doing of good deeds.

The verse also contains a crucial historical qualification. The scholars (including Ibn Kathir, in his commentary which quotes the later verse 3:85) note that this verse speaks of the state of the followers of the earlier religions *before* the final message arrived — or, more precisely, it describes the criteria by which *any* person's salvation is judged: sincere monotheism, belief in accountability, and righteous deeds. After the coming of the final Prophet ﷺ, the standard is the same but the mode of reception is the message of Muhammad ﷺ. Ibn Kathir explicitly records that after this verse was revealed, Allah revealed the qualification: "Whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will never be accepted from him, and he will be one of the losers in the Hereafter" (3:85). This is not a contradiction: the Qur'an teaches that faith in Allah and the Last Day is the core, but that the *form* of that faith, in the age of the final Prophet, is Islam — the complete submission to the message of Muhammad ﷺ. The verse before us is, in its primary context, an acknowledgment of the People of the Book's genuine monotheism and a refutation of anyone who claimed that they were automatically damned for their lineage. It teaches that the judgment of individuals belongs to Allah, based on faith and deeds, not on the group label.

"Sabians" (*al-Ṣābi'ūn*) is the third group, and the identity of the Sabians is one of the many questions in the Qur'an. The classical commentators — al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir — disagree about their identity. Ibn Kathir discusses several views: some said they were a sect among the People of the Book who read the Psalms; some said they were star-worshippers; others said they were a group between the Magians and the Jews who had no specific revealed religion and lived according to their natural instinct. Mujahid's statement, reported by Ibn Kathir, is that they are neither Jews, nor Christians, nor Magians, nor polytheists. Modern scholarship commonly identifies them with the Mandaeans, a monotheistic group in Iraq associated with the prophet Yahya (John the Baptist) and the practice of baptism; other theories identify them with the star-worshippers of Harran. The truth is that the exact identity of the Sabians is uncertain, and an honest commentary should say so. What the Qur'an makes certain is their inclusion in the verse: they are a distinct religious community, and whoever among them truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good is subject to the same principle of divine justice.

The conclusion of the verse — "there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" — is the standard Qur'anic language for the people of genuine faith: no fear of the future (the Judgment), no grief over the past (the losses of this world), because they stand with the Lord who holds both. The verse is a message of hope — and a rebuke to both exclusive claims and careless assumptions: no one is saved by an identity, and no sincere truth-seeker is judged merely by the label of his community. The final judgment is Allah's, and the criteria are faith and good deeds.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 63

Verse 63 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, "Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now recounts one of the most dramatic covenant scenes in the history of the Children of Israel: the mountain was raised above them, and they were commanded to hold firmly to the Scripture that had been given to them. The scene combines fear, urgency, and obligation: the covenant was not taken verbally but visibly, with a mountain suspended over them as a witness and a warning.

The event is described in the Qur'an in several places (2:63, 2:93, 4:154, 7:171). In the biblical account, the covenant at Sinai is a grand announcement; in the Qur'anic retelling, the emphasis is different: the mountain was uprooted and suspended over the people like a canopy, and they were told: hold firmly to what We have given you, and remember what is in it. The classical commentators explain that the people had initially hesitated to accept the responsibilities of the Torah — they had seen the punishment of other nations, and the burden of the law seemed too heavy. Allah then lifted the mountain over their heads like a shadow: either accept the covenant, or the mountain will fall upon you. This was a scene of compulsion in the sense of divine urgency: the commandment was not a suggestion, and the people were to understand that their response to the revelation was not optional.

The command has two parts. First, "Hold firmly to what We have given you" — *khudhū mā ātaynakum bi-quwwah* — "take what We have given you with strength." The phrase "with strength" (*bi-quwwah*) is significant. It does not mean with physical force; it means with resolve, with seriousness, with commitment, with the whole heart and the whole will. The Qur'an uses the same phrase for the Quranic message given to the Muslims: "Take what We have given you with strength" (7:145, 7:171). The revelation is not to be received half-heartedly. It is to be grasped, mastered, and lived. The second part is "and remember what is in it" — *wa udhkurū mā fīhi* — which sums up the method: constant remembrance, constant recitation, constant application. The Scripture is not a book to be owned but a message to be lived.

The purpose is then named: *la'allakum tattaqūn* — "so perhaps you may become mindful." The Torah was taken with strength so that the people would develop taqwa — awareness of Allah, reverence, and obedience. This is the same purpose that is attached to the Qur'an and the same purpose attached to every revelation: not merely knowledge but transformation, not merely law but God-consciousness. The covenant at the mountain was not a legal paperwork; it was a formation project.

The scene also teaches a great lesson about the nature of the divine covenant. The mountain suspended over the people shows that the relationship between Allah and humanity is not a game. The obligations of revelation are real, and the consequences are real. The believer is called to take the message with strength, to remember it, and to let it produce the fear of Allah. The one who takes the Scripture loosely, who reads without living, who owns the Book without obeying it, has not kept the covenant in the way the mountain scene demands. In modern terms, the verse speaks to the seriousness of the religious life: the believer is not a consumer of spiritual comfort but a covenant-holder who has accepted a commitment that carries both weight and consequence.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 64

Verse 64 says: *"Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the covenant records the failure. After the mountain had been raised above them and they had given the covenant, they turned away. The Qur'an does not soften the betrayal: they turned away *afterwards* — after the covenant, after the command, after the mountain. And then the mercy: had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy upon them, they would have been among the losers.

The sequence is the same pattern that runs through the whole section: covenant, failure, mercy. The phrase *thumma tawallaytum* — "then you turned away" — is precise. It is not "you fell," or "you failed," or "you stumbled." The *tawallā* verb carries the sense of turning the back, going away, refusing. It is the same word used for people who turn away from the message and the warning. The Children of Israel did not merely fail to live up to the covenant; they *turned away* from it. This is a more serious description than weakness; it describes a choice to leave.

The scholars note that the verse refers to the repeated pattern of the Children of Israel's history: they accepted the covenant, then turned away — through the calf, through the repeated rebellions, through the rejection of prophets, through the corruption of the scripture. The whole history is the history of turning away. And the mercy follows: *lawlā fadl Allāhi 'alaykum wa rahmatuhu* — "had it not been for Allah's grace upon you and His mercy." The *fadl* (grace) and *rahmah* (mercy) are the two divine attributes that held them back from the final consequence: total loss. They did not dishonour Allah; Allah honoured them with patience, with delay, with repeated forgiveness, with the continuation of the prophetic line.

The verse contains a profound lesson about the difference between Allah's justice and Allah's mercy. On the strict terms of the covenant, the Children of Israel turned away, and the just consequence would have been the loss they had earned. What saved them was not their own merit, which was nonexistent, but the grace and mercy of Allah — the divine attributes that took the initiative. The same is true of every believer: nobody's salvation is earned; even the best of deeds are themselves gifts, and it is the mercy of Allah that completes what the deed begins. The believer's confidence is not in his own performance but in the infinite mercy of the One he addresses in every prayer and every prostration.

The modern application of this verse is a warning against the "second turn." A person who makes a covenant with Allah — the shahada, the commitment to prayer, the promise to leave a sin — and then turns away after the commitment is in the exact position of the Children of Israel. The verse's mercy, however, is the reason for hope: turning away is not the end, because Allah's grace and mercy are not exhausted. The believer who has broken his covenant, who has turned away from what he promised, is being told: the One who held the Children of Israel from total loss is the same One who can hold you. The verse is both a warning and a door.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 65

Verse 65 says: *"You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, "Be disgraced apes!""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now gives the most dramatic example of the Children of Israel's rebellion and its consequence: the Sabbath-breakers. The verse says: you already know about those among you who violated the Sabbath. We said to them, "Be apes, disgraced and rejected." The command was executed: the people who broke the Sabbath were transformed into apes.

The story is told in detail in the Qur'an itself, in Surah Al-A'raf, which recalls the city that was by the sea whose people transgressed the Sabbath, when their fish came to them openly on their Sabbath day but did not come on the day they had no Sabbath, and Allah made them a trial because of their rebellion (7:163). The town was a coastal settlement of the Children of Israel — classical commentators identify it as the town of Aylah (Eilat), on the shore of the Red Sea. The people had been commanded to observe the Sabbath as a day of rest and prohibition against work, including fishing. The fish, in a deliberate test, would crowd the waters on Saturday and disappear on other days. The people then resorted to a legalistic trick: they placed nets, ropes, and artificial pools before the Sabbath, so that the fish would enter on Saturday and be trapped; then they collected the fish on Sunday, claiming they had not fished on the Sabbath. The Qur'an describes it plainly: they used deceitful means to circumvent the command of Allah. The test itself was designed by Allah to expose the heart: would they obey the spirit of the Sabbath, or would they find a way around it?

The consequence is stated with terrible simplicity: "Be disgraced apes." Classical commentators (Ibn Abbas via al-'Awfi; Qatadah; al-Dahhak) explain that Allah changed their bodies into monkeys — an actual physical transformation, not merely a moral comparison. The young among them became monkeys; some accounts say the old became swine; the reports vary in detail. They lived only three days in that state and then perished, without progeny — which is why, as Ibn Kathir quotes Ibn Abbas, the apes and swine that exist now are not the descendants of those transformed; they are a different creation. The transformation was a fitting punishment: the people who had used cleverness to make sin look lawful were turned into the animal that most resembles humans in form but lacks the moral capacity that distinguishes humanity. The punishment matched the crime.

Why was the punishment so severe? Because the Sabbath was not a trivial ritual; it was the test of obedience itself. The people were not being asked to do something difficult; they were being asked to abstain from one activity on one day, and they could not even do that without finding a loophole. The lesson is that the test of faith is often in the small, specific, seemingly arbitrary command. A person who cannot obey the simple command will not obey the great ones. And the legalistic avoidance of the command is worse than open violation, because it pretends to obey while actually disobeying — it is the same sin described in verse 42 as mixing truth with falsehood.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the subtlety of self-deception in religion. The Sabbath-breakers did not say "we refuse to obey Allah"; they said "we will find a way to obey the letter while violating the spirit." This is the religious psychology of the loophole, and it is universal: the person who finds a way to indulge the sin while keeping the appearance of compliance has made his intellect a tool of his desires. The Qur'anic answer is that Allah is not deceived by loopholes, and that a religion of legalistic tricks is a religion of corruption. The verse warns the believer against treating divine commands as puzzles to be solved in one's favour rather than boundaries to be honoured.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 66

Verse 66 says: *"So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing."*

In simple terms, the verse states the purpose of the Sabbath-breakers' punishment: Allah made their fate an example — a visible warning — for those who were present at the time, those who came after them, and ultimately for all who are God-fearing. The transformation was not merely a punishment of the culprits; it was a sign, a *nakal*, a public lesson set in history.

The word *nakāl* in the verse is the key term. It means an exemplary punishment, a deterrent, a lesson taught by the fate of the punished. The Qur'an uses the same family of words for the punishment of Pharaoh ("And Allah seized him as an example of both the Hereafter and this life," 79:25) and for the revelations given to the nations. The point is that the punishment of a single community carries a message beyond its own borders: the fate of the Sabbath-breakers was written into the history of the Children of Israel as a warning for everyone who would hear the story.

The phrase "to present and future generations" — *limā bayna yadayhā wa mā khalfahā* — literally "for what was before it and what was behind it." The punishment was an example for the generations that came before (the other nations who had been punished, who had already seen the pattern) and for the generations that came after (the later Children of Israel, the contemporaries of the Prophet ﷺ, and every reader since). The construction is a way of saying: this is not a private event; it is a public sign in the river of history, pointing in both directions.

The final phrase narrows the audience: "a lesson to the God-fearing." The punishment is an example for everyone, but it is a *lesson* only for those with taqwa. This is one of the Qur'an's constant principles: the same event, the same history, is received differently by different hearts. The rebellious read the fate of the Sabbath-breakers and think only of the past; the God-fearing read it and learn: this is what happens when the commands of Allah are treated as flexible. The verse teaches that the value of history is not merely its factual content but its moral application: history is an instructor, and the only students who learn are those who fear Allah.

Why is a "lesson" tied specifically to the God-fearing? Because it is the God-fearing who are waiting to learn — who are humble enough to receive correction, who take the warnings seriously, and who are prepared to change as a result. The person who is not God-fearing reads history and finds entertainment, or indignation, or curiosity; the person who is God-fearing reads the same history and finds himself measured against it. The lesson is not read by the eyes; it is received by the heart.

The verse also carries an important message about the purpose of punishment in the Qur'anic worldview: it is educational as well as retributive. The punishment of the Sabbath-breakers was not merely the settling of an account; it was a demonstration, designed to prevent the same failure in others. In the same way, the difficulties and failures a person experiences are not merely consequences but lessons. The believer who reads this verse is invited to ask a question about his own life: what has Allah taught me through what I have suffered, and am I learning the lesson? The person who suffers without learning repeats the lesson; the person who learns from history is among the God-fearing who receive it.

The verse is also a critique of an attitude common in the modern world: the treatment of history as mere data. If the punishment of the Sabbath-breakers is read as an anthropological curiosity — a strange ancient story with no bearing on the present — then the very purpose of the narrative has been missed. The Qur'an's history is for "those in front and behind," and its primary value is not academic but spiritual: it is example, warning, and lesson.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 67

Verse 67 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, "Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow." They replied, "Are you mocking us?" Moses responded, "I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now tells the story of the cow — the story after which the entire surah is named. The event began when a man of the Children of Israel was murdered and the identity of the killer was unknown. The people asked Moses to pray for Allah to reveal the murderer. Allah's command was unexpected: sacrifice a cow. The people responded with disbelief and mockery: "Are you making fun of us?" Moses answered with the dignity of a prophet: "I seek refuge in Allah from being among the ignorant."

The occasion of the story is given in the classical tafsir. Ibn Abi Hatim recorded from 'Ubaydah al-Salmani that there was a wealthy man among the Children of Israel who had no children and whose only heir was his nephew. The nephew killed him in order to inherit his wealth, and then disposed of the body and denied any knowledge of the murder. The community was in confusion: the killer was not known, the family was divided, and justice seemed impossible. They came to Moses and asked him to discover the truth. Allah, who knows the hidden and the revealed, gave the answer — but in a form that was itself a test: sacrifice a cow.

The people's response — "Do you make fun of us?" — reveals their state: they had been given a command of enormous spiritual significance, and all they could see was an absurdity. They had seen the sea split, the manna descend, the dead revived — and they still treated the command of Allah as if it were a joke. Their question was a question of pride, not of seeking: they could not imagine that the answer to a murder might lie in the slaughter of a cow. This is the exact psychology of the person who demands that the divine wisdom match his own categories: if it doesn't fit, he assumes it is foolish. Moses' answer is a prophet's rebuke: "I seek refuge in Allah from being among the *jāhilīn*" — from being among the ignorant. The word *jāhilīn* is more than "fools"; it refers to those who act without knowledge, without respect for the limits, and without the moral seriousness required for divine commands. Moses is saying: if I mocked Allah's command, I would be one of them; my dignity is in taking the command seriously, and yours is not.

The deeper lesson of the verse is about the relationship between divine command and human categories. Human beings often receive the command of Allah as if they were entitled to understand its complete wisdom before obeying. The cow story is a training in the opposite: obedience comes first, and understanding follows. The command to sacrifice a cow was given for a specific, hidden purpose — the revelation of the murderer — but the people were not told the purpose at first. They were asked to trust. Their mockery showed that they did not. The same principle runs through the whole of Islamic law and life: many commands are obeyed before their wisdom is fully seen; the believer trusts the One who knows.

In modern life, this verse speaks to the place of trust in religion and in authority. The skeptic often enters a relationship with revelation on the condition that every command be justified to him in advance. The Qur'anic model is the reverse: the believer enters with trust, receives the command, and discovers the wisdom through obedience. The story of the cow is the story of every believer who has obeyed a command before understanding its benefit — fasting before knowing its effect, charity before knowing its reward, restraint before knowing its blessing. And it is also a warning against the pride that assumes divine wisdom is a matter of human taste.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 68

Verse 68 says: *"They said, "Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!" He replied, "Allah says, 'The cow should neither be old nor young but in between. So do as you are commanded!'""*

In simple terms, the people were not satisfied with the first command. They asked Moses to ask Allah for more specification: what type of cow? The answer came: a cow neither old nor young, but in between. And then, with a note of impatience, the command was repeated: "So do as you are commanded!"

The exchange is the beginning of a pattern that runs through the whole story: each time the people were given a simple command, they asked for more details, and each time the details increased the difficulty. Ibn Kathir's commentary, following the early authorities, makes the point explicitly: "Had they not disputed, it would have been sufficient for them to slaughter any cow. However, they disputed, and the matter was made more difficult for them, until they ended up looking for the specific cow that they were later ordered to slaughter." The same principle appears in the famous hadith of the Prophet ﷺ, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah, in which he said: "Avoid that which I forbid you to do and do that which I command you to do to the best of your capacity. Verily the people before you were destroyed only because of their excessive questioning and their disagreement with their Prophets." The people had been told: sacrifice a cow. The command was clear, and any cow would have fulfilled it. But they did not obey; they negotiated.

The answer — "neither old nor young but in between" — is a description of a mature cow, in the prime of its life. It was a fitting symbol for a community that was itself supposed to be in the middle: neither the unthinking obedience of youth nor the hardened refusal of age, but the mature response of a people who had been given the covenant. The word *'awān* in the Arabic carries the sense of a cow in its prime, fit for sacrifice. The description was not a riddle; it was an answer to their question. And the phrase "So do as you are commanded" — *fa af'alū mā tu'marūn* — is a gentle rebuke: you asked, I answered, now do what you are told.

The lesson is deep and universal: the believer who delays obedience with unnecessary questions may be doing so because the real obstacle is not a lack of information but a lack of submission. The people did not ask because they genuinely needed the details; they asked because they were resisting. Their questions created a sequence of escalating difficulty, and the cow story becomes a parable of the person who negotiates with God rather than obeys. The same pattern is visible in religious life whenever a person searches for the "specific ruling" to avoid the general one, or asks for the "clarification" in order to postpone the act.

In modern life, this verse is a challenge to the culture of infinite questioning. It is possible to seek knowledge sincerely, and it is possible to use questions as a shield against action. The Qur'anic diagnosis of the Children of Israel is that their questions were not a search for truth but a defense against it. The believer is called to the opposite: seek knowledge, but let the knowledge lead to action. The first command in the story — sacrifice a cow — was enough. Every added specification was a consequence of the resistance, and the same is true in the believer's life: obedience, when it is sincere, is simple; resistance, when it is hidden, creates complexity.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 69

Verse 69 says: *"They said, "Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour." He replied, "Allah says, 'It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.'""*

In simple terms, the people asked again: what is the colour? The answer came: a bright yellow cow, pleasant to look upon. The description is precise and vivid — a radiant yellow, a colour that pleases the eyes. The verse continues the story's pattern — the escalating questioning and the ever-finer specifications.

The word *ṣafrā'* — yellow — is the colour named. The description *fāqi'* means "bright," "clear," "deeply saturated." The scholars note that the phrase "pleasant to see" (*yasurru al-nāẓirīn*) describes a colour that gladdens the eye — perhaps a deep golden yellow, or a colour that reflects light in a way that is pleasing. The Qur'an's use of this description serves a purpose: the cow that was commanded was not merely any cow but a cow of exceptional beauty, distinguished in its colour and appearance. In the classical accounts, this specification made the cow even more difficult to find, and the people's questioning had the same effect in the area of colour as it had in the area of age: it made the command harder, not easier.

The verses also show a pattern that is important for understanding the Qur'an's method of revelation. The command was given first in general: "sacrifice a cow." The people asked, and the answer became more specific. This is not a case of Allah withholding information in order to test — it is a case of the people creating their own difficulty by refusing the first, simplest command. The Qur'an is teaching that the wisdom of obedience is lost when obedience is postponed. The general command was easy; the specifications were the result of resistance, and each specification narrowed the field until the task became almost impossible.

The phrase "pleasant to see" has a deeper spiritual meaning. The cow that was commanded was a beauty, a pleasure to the eyes — and yet the people's hearts were so hardened that the pleasure of the sight was not enough to move them. They had seen the sea split; they had seen manna descend; they had seen the dead be revived; and now they were being shown a cow of such colour that it would gladden the viewer. And they were still negotiating. The Qur'an's lesson is that the visual beauty of a sign does not by itself produce faith; the heart decides what the eyes will show. The same people who were given the most beautiful objects in the world were the people who had made their hearts as hard as stone (a condition the verses will describe in 2:74).

In modern life, the verse speaks to the psychology of endless specification. In many areas — religious, professional, and personal — people use the language of "clarification" to postpone commitment. The person who says "I will do it when I understand every detail" may actually be saying "I do not want to do it." The Qur'anic answer is the pattern of the story: the command was clear, the details were requested, and the details were given; the problem was never information, and the solution was never more data. The person who has received a clear command is told — as Moses told the people — "Do as you are commanded." The beauty of the command and the clarity of the answer are not substitutes for the obedience they require.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 70

Verse 70 says: *"Again they said, "Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow, for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.""*

In simple terms, the people asked a fourth time: make it clear to us which cow, because the cows are all alike to us. And here, for the first time, they add a phrase that changes the tone: "Then, Allah willing, we will be guided." The verse marks the point where the story moves from resistance toward reluctant compliance — but the reluctance is the point.

The phrase "all cows look the same to us" is not a statement of ignorance but a statement of refusal disguised as confusion. The cow that had been described — neither old nor young, in its prime, bright yellow, pleasing to the eyes — was not "the same as all cows." The description had already distinguished it. The people's claim that the cows were alike shows that they were using their own perception as the standard, and that they had not bothered to look for what was described. The same psychology runs through religious life: the person who says "all religions are the same" or "all paths are equal" is not making a claim of knowledge but a claim of refusal — he has not looked at the distinctions because looking would obligate him.

The phrase "Allah willing, we will be guided" is the one hopeful element in the verse. The words *in shā' Allāh* are the words of a person who is beginning to submit — who is acknowledging that guidance is not in his control but in Allah's. Up to this point, the people had spoken with the certainty of resistance: "Are you mocking us?" Now they speak with the humility of reaching: "If Allah wills, we will be guided." The change is subtle but real. The scholars note that this is the point where the story turns: the people had resisted, and the resistance was exposed; and they were beginning to concede that the path of guidance required submission.

Yet even the concession is imperfect: they said "we will be guided" *after* asking for the fourth specification, which means they still wanted one more detail. The pattern is meticulous: they were not ready to act on what they had already been told. The command to sacrifice a cow is now repeated a fourth time, and with each repetition the cow becomes more particular — age, colour, and now the work history and qualities of verse 71. The narrative is one of the most precise portraits in the Qur'an of how resistance to a simple command compounds itself.

In modern life, the verse teaches about the difference between the language of submission and the act of submission. A person can say "If Allah wills" and still be resisting, if the phrase is used as a cover for postponement. The Qur'an's answer is the story itself: the people were given a command, they negotiated through four stages, and they only acted when the command became practically impossible to refuse. The believer is being taught to recognize the pattern in himself — the habit of asking for more clarity when the answer is already clear, the tendency to accept obligation in words while postponing it in deeds. And when the resistance is finally broken, the verse's lesson is that the one who genuinely submits says, "If Allah wills, we will be guided" — and then acts on the guidance that has already come.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 71

Verse 71 says: *"He replied, "Allah says, 'It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish.'" They said, "Now you have come with the truth." Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly!"*

In simple terms, the second half of the story is the final specification. The cow should be neither old nor young, bright yellow, pleasant to see, and it should have been used neither for ploughing nor for watering — it should be wholesome and free from blemish. The people then said, "Now you have come with the truth" — but even then, they slaughtered it hesitantly, as though they were doing it reluctantly.

The final description completed the portrait of a cow of exceptional value: a mature cow in its prime, of striking colour, never worked in the fields, healthy and unblemished. In the desert economy of the Children of Israel, a cow that had never been used for labour would be a rare and precious animal — one that had been reserved, perhaps, for a purpose. The four specifications together describe a cow that is not merely fit for sacrifice but is *the* sacrifice — an animal that has been preserved and set apart. Classical commentators, notably Ibn Kathir, recount from the early tradition that the cow was ultimately found in the possession of a single man who refused to sell it except for its skin filled with gold — a statement that captures the perfection of the animal and the price the children of Israel paid for their resistance. The man was the nephew of the murdered man, and the payment for the cow, in some accounts, came from the inheritance in question. The details of the early reports vary and should be handled with care, but the pattern is consistent: an ordinary command was turned, by resistance, into an extraordinary difficulty.

"They said, 'Now you have come with the truth.'" The people recognize the truth of the description. This is an important moment: they were not unable to recognize the truth; they were able, and they said so. Their recognition, however, was not followed by immediate action. The phrase "yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly" — *wa mā kādū yaf'alūn* — literally "and they almost did not do it" — captures the final state: they did the command, but they did it as though they were barely doing it. The hesitation is the mark of a heart that obeys reluctantly, that submits after resistance, that performs the act without the spirit of submission.

The whole story is thus a portrait of the difference between obedience and compliance. Obedience is the movement of a heart that has accepted the command; compliance is the movement of a body that has run out of excuses. The Children of Israel complied because the specifications had left them no room; they did not obey because they wanted to. The verse ends with the story's conclusion — the slaughter of the cow — but the emphasis is not on the slaughter but on the *almost* — the hesitation, the reluctance.

The lesson for the believer is profound. There are two ways to do what Allah commands: with the whole heart, or with the last resistance. The believer is being trained away from the second. The person who obeys after five rounds of negotiation, who does the command only when there is no loophole left, has done the act but missed the submission. The same deed can be entirely different in value depending on the state of the heart that performs it. The cow story is a mirror: it asks the reader to examine the difference between the command being fulfilled and the heart being submitted.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the cost of delay in obedience. The people of the story could have sacrificed an ordinary cow at the beginning; by the end they were searching for a cow of extraordinary value. The same is true of every virtue postponed: the command that was easy becomes difficult, the act that was simple becomes complicated, and the believer pays the price of his own hesitation. The one who obeys early obeys cheaply; the one who obeys late pays with difficulty.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 72

Verse 72 says: *"˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed."*

In simple terms, the verse now returns to the original mystery: a man was killed, and the people disputed who had killed him. The cow had been sacrificed; the point of the sacrifice was now to be revealed. Allah had known the murderer from the beginning — the verse says "Allah revealed what you concealed" — and the whole drama of the cow was the means of bringing the hidden into the open.

The verse confirms the story's frame: the murder was the beginning, and the dispute was the middle, and the revelation was the end. The murderer had hidden the act; the people disputed in confusion and suspicion; and Allah, who knows what is concealed, designed the means by which the truth would become visible. The drama is one of the Qur'an's most striking examples of divine foreknowledge and divine intervention in human affairs. Allah does not merely know the hidden; He brings it forth, in His own time and through His own means.

"Why did Allah require a cow?" is the question the story implicitly answers. The command was not arbitrary; it was a lesson in the relationship between obedience and revelation. The people were required to do something — sacrifice a cow — and their obedience to the command was itself the condition for the revelation of the truth. The divine wisdom was hidden in the command, and the revelation of the wisdom came only after the obedience. The story is a lesson about the method of Allah: He often requires a step of obedience before the wisdom is apparent, and the step is itself the key to the wisdom. The person who obeys the command in faith discovers the purpose; the person who refuses the command never reaches the purpose.

The phrase "Allah revealed what you concealed" (*Allāhu mukhrijun mā kuntum taktumūn*) — "Allah is the One who will bring out what you were concealing" — carries a deeper theological truth. The murderer's concealment was not an escape; it was a postponement. The truth that was hidden was going to come out, because Allah is the One who brings out what is concealed. The believer is taught that concealment is not a real strategy: every hidden thing — every deed, every thought, every secret — has already been seen, and will eventually be revealed. The Qur'an says this in many places: the Day when all secrets are brought to light (86:9); He knows what you conceal and what you reveal (64:4); and the Day when all that is in the hearts is brought to light (100:10). The verse is a warning to the concealer and a comfort to the seeker of justice: nothing stays hidden from the One who knows.

In modern life, the verse is a profound statement about truth and justice. Every unresolved crime, every covered-up injustice, every secret wrongdoing has already been seen by Allah — and the verse says He will bring it out. The believer is not asked to despair when justice seems impossible; he is asked to trust the One who holds the key to every concealment. And the one who hides wrongdoing is warned: the concealment is not a fortress; it is a debt that will be collected. The story of the cow teaches that the truth can be concealed for a time, but it cannot be hidden from its revealer, and the One who reveals it is the Most Just.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 73

Verse 73 says: *"So We instructed, "Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow." This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand."*

In simple terms, the climax of the story: the dead man was struck with a piece of the cow, and he rose up, and the murderer was revealed. The verse states the point of the whole episode: this is how Allah brings the dead to life — and this is a sign shown to the people so that they may understand. The cow story is not only about a murder; it is about resurrection.

The sequence in the classical account: after the cow was slaughtered, the people were instructed to strike the body of the murdered man with a piece of the cow. When they did, the man rose, and he named the killer. The account of what happened when he rose, recorded in the tafsir (including the version reported by 'Ubaydah al-Salmani and the report that the murderer was the man's nephew and was therefore denied the inheritance), varies in detail, but the essential facts are consistent: the dead was revived, the killer was named, and the purpose of the command — the revelation of the concealed — was fulfilled.

The deeper message is in the phrase "This is how Allah brings the dead to life." The verse presents the resurrection of the murdered man as an *argument* for the resurrection of all humanity. It is one of the Qur'an's most powerful set-pieces: the God who revived a single body with a single piece of a cow — and who asked the people to take a step of obedience before the miracle occurred — is the God who will revive all bodies on the Day of Judgment. The same power shown in a small sign is the power that will be shown in the final event. The Qur'an returns to this logic repeatedly: the one who can create from nothing and revive one corpse can revive all; the first and the last are equally within His power.

The supplied translation renders the Arabic *hakadhā* — "this is how" — as "This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life," with "easily" in brackets as the translator's gloss. The gloss captures the force of the verse: the resurrection is effortless for Allah. The same point is made elsewhere: the One who originates creation is the One who repeats it, and the Qur'an says that repeating it is even easier for Him (30:27). The reader is asked to reason from the small miracle to the great one: if the resurrection of one dead man was as easy as striking him with a piece of a slaughtered cow, then the resurrection of all humanity is nothing for Allah.

The verse also teaches about the method of divine signs. Allah showed the Children of Israel the sign in order that they might understand — not merely witness. The sign was not a spectacle; it was a lesson, and the lesson was resurrection. The same pattern runs through the whole Qur'an: every miracle is a sign pointing beyond itself, and the miracle is wasted on the one who sees it without understanding. The verse's final phrase — "so that you may understand" — is the purpose clause: the sign is for understanding, and understanding is the beginning of faith.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the believer who has lost confidence in the power of Allah over the impossible. A dead man, a piece of a cow, a command — and life returns. The Qur'an is saying: the power that does this is not confined to that day; it is the power of the Lord of all, and it is present in every moment. The believer who faces what seems beyond repair — a dead relationship, a dead hope, a dead dream — is given the image of the story: Allah brings the dead to life, and He does it as easily as He does everything. The command, however, is to strike the body with the piece — to take the step of obedience — and then to wait for the One who revives.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 74

Verse 74 says: *"Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the resurrection is one of the most devastating sentences in the Qur'an. Even after a dead man was raised before their eyes, the people's hearts became hard. And not merely hard — the verse says their hearts became like stones, or even harder. Then it makes the comparison: some stones gush rivers, others split open and pour water, others crack and fall in awe of Allah. The stones respond to Allah more than the hearts do. And the verse ends: Allah is not unaware of what you do.

The comparison is a masterpiece. Stones, in the Qur'an's portrait of creation, are not the picture of hardness — they are the picture of responsiveness: some of them "gush rivers" (as in the miracle of Moses' rock), some "split and water flows out" (as in the earth that cracks for springs), and some "fall down in fear of Allah" (as in 2:74's own image, and in the verse that says "the mountains humble themselves in awe of the Qur'an," 59:21). The rocks, in other words, respond to the divine command with obedience, with flow, with falling. The Children of Israel's hearts, by contrast, received every sign — the sea, the manna, the dead revived — and responded with nothing. The heart that has become harder than a rock is a heart that has refused the natural response to the divine.

What makes a heart hard? The Qur'an gives the answer throughout this very passage: the repeated refusal of the evidence, the repeated rejection of the prophets, the repeated turning away after the covenant. The hardening is the cumulative effect of the refusal. Each time a person hears the truth and turns his back, a layer of hardness is added, and eventually the heart reaches the state described here: it sees a dead man rise, and it does not bend. The Prophet ﷺ described the same process in the hadith of the black spot on the heart (recorded by al-Tirmidhi and others): every sin leaves a mark, and repeated sin covers the heart until it can no longer perceive. The verse before us is the Qur'an's own description of that state.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you do." The final sentence is the warning: the hardness of the heart is not invisible. Allah sees the refusal, the resistance, and the state of the heart. And this is what makes the verse so serious: the hard heart is not a private psychological condition; it is a condition that has been observed, recorded, and will be answered.

The lesson for the believer is about the preservation of softness. The heart of faith is a heart that responds — to the recitation, to the sign, to the call. The believer is asked to protect that responsiveness: by remembering, by repentance, by humility, by the constant turning toward Allah. The person who lets himself become hardened through neglect — who reads the Qur'an without feeling, hears the warning without changing, sees the signs without responding — is walking the same path the verse describes. And the believer who feels his heart becoming hard has been given the cure: turn back, seek forgiveness, renew the remembrance, and let the softness of the heart be restored. The rocks of the verse are the pattern: the heart was created to respond, and the heart that no longer responds is the heart that has been covered by its own actions.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 75

Verse 75 says: *"Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?"*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now turns from the history of the Children of Israel to their present situation with the believers of Madinah. The question is direct: do you still expect them — the People of the Book — to be true to you? The reason is given: a group of them would hear the word of Allah, understand it, and then deliberately corrupt it. The verse is a warning against naivety in dealing with a people whose pattern of betrayal is documented in their own history.

The word *tuqāti'ūn* — "expect... to be true to you" — carries the sense of trusting and hoping for faithfulness. The believers had hoped, in the early period of Madinah, that the Jews and Christians would recognize the Prophet ﷺ and accept Islam, and the Qur'an describes the persistence of that hope. The verse asks: how can you expect faithfulness from a people whose own history shows that a group of them hears the word, understands it, and then deliberately corrupts it? The pattern is documented: in their own scripture, in the conduct of their scholars, in the history of the prophets they killed, in the distortion of the Torah. The Qur'an is saying: do not build your policy on an expectation that history has already contradicted.

The phrase "hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it" describes a specific method of corruption. The corruption is not accidental; it is *after* understanding (*ba'd mā 'aqalūhu*). The people heard the word, grasped its meaning, and then twisted it. This is the precise nature of the corruption: not ignorance but the deliberate distortion of known truth. The Qur'an has described the same pattern in the earlier verses — the changing of the words (2:59), the mixing of truth with falsehood (2:42), the concealment of what is known (2:146) — and here it names the psychological state: the corruption follows comprehension.

The verse also teaches the believers something about trust and prudence. In the early Madinan community, there were Muslims who continued to ask the People of the Book for religious knowledge, and who were willing to treat their claims as reliable. The Qur'an is not teaching hatred of the People of the Book — it repeatedly commands justice, kindness, and peaceful coexistence with those who do not fight the believers (60:8; 5:82-86, with its recognition of the Christians' humility). It is teaching *discernment*: know the history, verify the reports, and do not treat the claims of a community that has a documented pattern of distortion as though they were neutral. The believer is commanded to be just toward all people and wise in dealing with all people — the two are not in conflict.

The modern application is significant in an age of information and disinformation. The verse is a lesson in critical thinking grounded in knowledge of patterns: when a source has a history of distortion, verify its claims with independent evidence. It is also a lesson against naive trust and against the romanticism of the "honest opponent" — the person who assumes that because the other party is educated or religious, it is truthful. The believer's mindset is neither suspicion without evidence nor trust without evidence; it is verification based on knowledge. And the deepest lesson is about the believers themselves: the verse is a mirror for every community that hears the word and then corrupts it — for the religious leaders who twist the message for gain, and for every believer who knows the truth and chooses to conceal it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 76

Verse 76 says: *"When they meet the believers they say, "We believe." But in private they say ˹to each other˺, "Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand?""*

In simple terms, the verse describes the duplicity of a group among the People of the Book. When they meet the believers, they declare belief. But in private, they ask one another with alarm: will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand? The Qur'an exposes the lifestyle of hypocrisy: one face in public, another in private.

The verses can be read as describing a specific situation in Madinah. Some Jewish scholars had accepted Islam, or were close to it, and they related to the Muslims the things they knew from their own scripture — including the descriptions of the Prophet ﷺ and the signs of his coming. Their fellows then rebuked them: why are you disclosing to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you? Now that they know it, they will use it as argument against you on the Day of Judgment. The question "Do you not understand?" is a rebuke of the group's failure to see the consequence of what they were sharing.

The irony is pointed. The very thing the scholars had — knowledge of the Book and the prophecy of the coming Messenger — was the thing they were being told to hide. The Qur'an describes the same concealment elsewhere (2:146, 2:159-160, 3:71): a people who know the truth and suppress it. Now, in this verse, they are shown in their private consultations, debating whether to continue sharing. The verse is a portrait of a community that lives in two worlds: the public world where they claim belief, and the private world where they plan concealment.

The phrase "so that they may use it against you before your Lord" reveals what the concealers feared. They were not afraid of the believers' arguments in this world; they were afraid of the evidence in the next. The knowledge that the Muslims possessed — the prophecies, the descriptions, the signs in their own scripture — was evidence that would testify against them on the Day of Judgment. The concealment was a strategy to avoid the evidence.

This is one of the Qur'an's most penetrating diagnoses of hypocrisy: the hypocrite manages appearances in the public realm while his true intent operates in the private. The believer, by contrast, is told by the Prophet ﷺ to be the same inside and outside. The Qur'an will later name the same pattern in the hypocrites of Madinah, whose outward appearance and speech please the onlooker but who regard every cry of alarm as aimed against them (63:4). The verse before us is an early portrait of that same spiritual condition.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the danger of the double life — the person who claims belief publicly while concealing the evidence of its obligations privately; the person who behaves differently before the believers and before his own company. The Qur'an's answer is integrity: the believer is to be one person, the same in public and private, and the test of faith is not the public appearance but the private consultation. The verse also teaches the believer to be aware of the difference between profession and truth: "We believe" can be a statement of fact, an expression of hope, or a performance — and only Allah sees which.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 77

Verse 77 says: *"Do they not know that Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal?"*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the description of the private consultation is the answer: Allah knows the concealment and the revelation. The people thought their hypocrisy was safe in the privacy of the meeting; the Qur'an says the One who knows both is Allah. The concealment has no chamber that Allah cannot see.

The verse is a single, devastating sentence. The structure is deliberate: "Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal." The pair — concealment and revelation — covers the whole of human activity: what is hidden and what is shown. The Qur'an is saying there is no third category. Whatever a person does, whether in the public assembly or the private consultation, it falls into one of these two — and Allah knows both. The People of the Book could hide their scheming from the believers; they could not hide it from Allah.

The theological principle is one of the Qur'an's foundational teachings. Allah's knowledge is not partial or general; it is particular and absolute. He knows what is in the heavens and the earth, the secret and the more secret: "Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, Allah knows it" (3:29); and He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal (40:19). The knowledge of Allah is not deduced from the evidence; it is immediate, without intermediaries, complete. The verse's question, "Do they not know?", is itself the judgment: the only explanation for their conduct is that they have forgotten, or never understood, the One who sees them.

This is the same spiritual failure that the Qur'an identifies throughout the story of the Children of Israel: they knew the truth and their knowledge did not change them. Here, the pattern is even more direct: they know that Allah sees their secret. And they still behave as though the knowledge does not matter. The verse is a description of the state in which knowledge of Allah is present in the mind but absent in the conduct — the state the Qur'an calls heedlessness (*ghaflah*) and the condition of a heart that is hardened.

The lesson for the believer is about the reality of being seen. The person who is tempted to do wrong in secret — the wrong that no one will see — is told that the One who sees everything is watching, and that the "secret" was never secret. The beauty of the same truth is the other side: for the believer who fears that Allah has abandoned him in an unseen struggle, the verse is a comfort — Allah sees the struggle, the intention, the inner state that no one else can see, and the reward for that hidden obedience is His knowledge of it. The same divine knowledge that makes concealment impossible makes sincerity visible.

In modern life, the verse is a challenge to the split between private and public morality, the idea that what is done in the private realm is no one's business. The Qur'an says the private is observed: the hidden conversation, the concealed motive, the secret transaction. And the believer is called to live so that the private and the public are one — because they are both observed by the One who knows.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 78

Verse 78 says: *"And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate."*

In simple terms, the verse now distinguishes between the learned and the unlettered among the People of the Book. There is a group among them that does not know the Scripture at all — the description is stark: they know nothing about it except lies. What they have are *amānī* — fantasies, wishes, and assumptions — and nothing but speculation.

The word *ummīyūn* is critical. It refers to those who are unlettered, without knowledge of writing and scripture. The classical commentators explain it in the context: these were the common people among the Jews and Christians who did not know the actual contents of the Torah or the Gospels. They had no direct knowledge of the Book; their religion was inherited, superficial, and unexamined. What they had was *amānī* — a term that carries the double sense of "desires" and "claims." Ibn Abbas (as reported through al-Dahhak) explained the word as "false statements they utter" — things they say without grounding. They were not necessarily the intentional distorters of verse 79; they simply did not know the Scripture, and what they carried instead was a mixture of wishful claims and inherited speculation.

The phrase "they ˹wishfully˺ speculate" — *wa hum yakhruṣūn* — is the final and most important word. *Yakhruṣūn* describes the act of guessing, of making estimates without foundation — which is why the supplied translation renders it "wishfully speculate." The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere, describing those who follow nothing but conjecture and what their own souls desire — conjecture that avails nothing against the truth (53:23, 53:28). The unlettered people were not indifferent to religion; they were *confident* about religion — certain in their identity and their destiny — but their certainty was built on wishes and assumptions, not knowledge.

The Qur'an thus draws a double portrait of the People of the Book: the learned corrupters, who know the truth and hide or distort it (verses 75-77), and the unlearned who do not know the Scripture at all and are confident on the basis of their wishes (this verse). Between the two, the people had built a religion that was either falsified at the top or fantasized at the bottom. The same structure appears in every community that has drifted from its scripture: elites who distort, masses who inherit, and a religion that in both cases has lost its authority.

In modern life, the verse is a powerful critique of inherited religion without knowledge. Millions of people hold to a religious identity without knowing the content of their revealed Book — without reading it, asking about it, or examining their claims. They believe what they are told, desire what they are promised, and are confident without evidence. The difference between the illiterate of the verse and the modern believer is the accessibility of the Book: the Torah is everywhere, the Gospels are everywhere, the Qur'an is everywhere — and no one can say today that he "knows nothing about the Scripture," because the ignorance is chosen rather than inevitable. The verse is an invitation to the believer: do not be among those whose religion is wishful thinking; be among those who read, understand, and live the revelation.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 79

Verse 79 says: *"So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, "This is from Allah"—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned."*

In simple terms, the verse announces the most severe condemnation of religious corruption: those who distort the Scripture with their own hands, then attribute it to Allah, seeking a fleeting gain. The verse is a double woe: woe for what they have written, and woe for what they have earned. The scholars describe this as a reference to those among the Jews who fabricated portions of scripture in order to profit from them — who wrote a "book" themselves, attributed it to Allah, and traded it for worldly gain.

The verse's target must be understood precisely. The Qur'an was not describing a wholesale replacement of the divine scripture; the classical commentators identify the reference as a specific group among the Jews who, in their time, manufactured material and sold it, or who altered the meanings and claimed the authority of revelation. Ibn Abbas and al-Dahhak, as recorded in the tafsir, explain the verse as referring to the scholars of the Children of Israel who took worldly gain by writing books with their hands and saying "this is from Allah." The crime is the combination of three acts: distortion or forgery, false attribution, and trade. The forgery is the use of writing to create a false document; the false attribution is the claim that the document is divine; and the trade is the sale of the document for the gain of the world. Any one of these is serious; all three together are the corruption of the very source of truth.

The gain is called "fleeting" — and in the Qur'an's parallel passages it is called "a paltry price" (2:41, 3:187, 5:44, 9:9). The gain is small not because the profit is small but because the gain in the next world is infinitely greater, and the traders have lost the eternal to obtain the momentary. A few coins, a little authority, a small comfort — this is what the verse says they have sold the truth for. And the woe is doubled: for the falsehood they wrote, and for the profit they earned. Both will testify against them.

The deeper horror of the crime is that it attacks the revelation itself. The people who read the corrupted text believe they are reading the word of God. The forgery occupies the place of the truth, the false attribution gives it authority, and the trade gives it a motive. The result is that generations are misled, and the corruption of one text corrupts the religion of thousands. This is why the Qur'an reserves its harshest language for those who lead others astray while claiming divine authority.

In modern life, the verse is astonishingly contemporary. When religious authorities forge texts, fabricate sayings, invent traditions, or manufacture "scripture" for their own profit — and then sell the forgery through books, courses, and platforms for worldly gain — they are doing exactly what the verse describes. The believer is called to a fierce loyalty to the original; to verify, to examine, to refuse the manufactured authority; and to be vigilant against the tendency to create a religion of our own design. And the religious leader is warned with the verse's double woe: the falsehood will be written against him, and the profit will be collected from him.

## Verse 80

Verse 80 says: *"˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, "The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days." Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?""*

In simple terms, the verse now addresses the claim of some Jews that the Fire will not touch them except for a number of days. The claim was the basis of their confidence: they believed that whatever punishment they faced would be temporary and limited, and that their status as "the chosen people" would finally save them. The Qur'an's answer is the question — have you taken a pledge from Allah? — and the accusation: you are saying about Allah what you do not know.

The claim "the Fire will not touch us except for a number of days" was a theological position among the Jews of Madinah and their predecessors. In its historical form, the idea may have been connected to the golden calf: the report that the punishment for the calf-worshippers would last a fixed term, such as the forty days associated with that episode, or a belief that the punishment of the Children of Israel would be limited to a bounded period of the covenant's violation. Some commentators connect the claim to the idea of a limited term of atonement for the sin of the calf. The essential point of the verse is not the precise shape of the claim but its logic: the people had arrived at a comfortable conclusion — the punishment is limited and we will be saved — without any divine warrant for it.

The Qur'an's response is a masterclass in the argument against presumption. The question "Have you taken a pledge from Allah?" is devastating: if you have a pledge, produce it; if you do not, then your confidence is invention. The word behind "pledge" is *'ahd* — a covenant, a binding promise. The question is designed to distinguish between a claim based on revelation and a claim based on hope. In the Qur'anic framework, no one may make a claim about Allah — about who enters Paradise, who escapes the Fire, what is permissible and what is forbidden — without the authority of revelation. The claim about the limited duration of the punishment was not revealed; it was invented. And the verse names the sin precisely: "or are you saying about Allah what you do not know?"

This is the same human pattern the whole section has shown: building religious confidence on wishes instead of knowledge. The learned among them had corrupted the Scripture; the illiterate had only wishful speculation; and now, in this verse, a group of them claims a pledge from Allah that He never gave them. The root of the claim is the same in all cases: the desire to be saved without the conditions of salvation, to have the benefit of religion without the burden of it, to know the outcome without the submission.

The theological principle is universal: anyone — Muslim, Jew, Christian, or other — who claims a guarantee of salvation without the conditions of faith and deed is making the same error. The Qur'an's own response to a similar claim among the groups was: "The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true'" (2:111). The proof is required.

In modern life, the verse is a challenge to the religion of entitlement. The person who says "God is merciful, so I will be fine" without the mercy being reflected in faith and obedience has taken these claimants' position; the person who says "I am a Muslim, so I am saved" while rejecting the obligations of Islam is making the same presumption. The verse is not a denial of the mercy of Allah — the Qur'an is full of it — but a correction of the abuse of that mercy. History is full of communities that believed "the Fire will not touch us" and were still held to account. And Allah never breaks His word — which means if the claim of the pledge is not based on His promise, the claim is not His; it is the speaker's own invention.
