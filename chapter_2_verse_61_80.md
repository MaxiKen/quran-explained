## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:61

> And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for.” They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah’s signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations.

**Commentary:**

### Dissatisfaction After Provision

The Children of Israel had received manna and quails in the wilderness, food provided by Allah without the ordinary labour of cultivation. They then complained that they could not endure the same meal and asked Moses to pray for common agricultural produce. Their desire for herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions was not sinful merely because these foods were ordinary. The problem was their ingratitude, contempt for an extraordinary provision, and refusal to recognize the mercy already surrounding them.

The translation “garlic” reflects one interpretation of the Arabic *fūm*. Classical scholars differed over whether it means garlic, wheat, or another edible plant. The disagreement does not affect the central lesson. They wanted to exchange food sent by Allah for produce that required the ordinary conditions of a settled life, while they were still being sustained in the wilderness. Their request expressed dissatisfaction with the form of the blessing rather than a responsible response to genuine deprivation.

Moses asks, “Do you exchange what is better for what is worse?” The comparison is not a universal declaration that every simple vegetable is inferior to every other food. It concerns their moral posture and circumstances. The manna and quails were a miraculous, readily available provision suited to their journey; the requested crops represented the labour, dependence, and settlement they were impatient to recover. A lawful desire can become blameworthy when it is joined to contempt for Allah’s gift and rejection of the duty attached to one’s present situation.

### “Go Down to Any Town”

Moses tells them to go down to a town or settled place, where they will find what they request. The Arabic *miṣr* can be understood as a city or settled country; some classical reports identified it with Egypt, while others understood it generically as any urban settlement. The text’s secure point is that the food they desired was available in ordinary markets and fields. It was not a special sign of divine favour for the people in their wilderness journey.

The statement is not a condemnation of agriculture, trade, or settled life. Prophets and righteous people have eaten ordinary food and worked for their provision. It is a correction of misplaced priorities. The people were complaining about the form of provision while disregarding the unusual mercy that protected them from hunger in a harsh environment. Gratitude does not forbid seeking variety; it forbids treating gifts as worthless simply because they are familiar or because one wants what someone else has.

### Disgrace, Misery, and Divine Displeasure

The verse says that they were struck with disgrace and misery and drew upon themselves Allah’s displeasure. The terms *dhillah* and *maskanah* describe humiliation, weakness, deprivation, and a loss of honour. These consequences were not caused by eating onions or desiring vegetables. They followed a larger pattern of disobedience, rejection of signs, and violence against prophets. The verse itself gives the reasons: disobedience, transgression, disbelief in Allah’s signs, and unjust killing.

The mention of killing prophets is not a claim that every member of every generation personally committed each crime. The Qur’an often addresses a community in terms of the conduct of a representative group or the inherited moral direction of its leaders. Those who approve, defend, or continue the path of earlier criminals may share in its guilt, while innocent people are not condemned merely by ancestry. The Qur’an also states that not all People of the Book were alike (3:113–115).

The history of the Children of Israel contains repeated conflicts with prophets, including rejection, conspiracy, and killing. The Qur’an mentions that some messengers were rejected and others killed unjustly (2:87; 3:21). These accounts are warnings to every religious community: possessing scripture does not protect a people who attack truth when it challenges their status or desires.

### Provision, Contentment, and Freedom

The passage exposes a psychological pattern that appears in every age. A person can be rescued from severe hardship, receive regular provision, and still focus on what is absent. Comparison makes a blessing feel small. The cure is not refusing every desire but distinguishing between a lawful preference and an ungrateful rebellion. The believer may ask Allah for different food while remaining thankful; he must not turn preference into an accusation that Allah’s provision is inadequate.

Freedom from Pharaoh also required freedom from the mentality of entitlement. The Children of Israel had been enslaved, and their desire for control over ordinary life was understandable. Yet a community recovering from oppression must learn responsibility, patience, and trust. If every discomfort is treated as proof that Allah has failed it, liberation becomes another form of bondage—bondage to appetite and complaint.

The verse closes by calling their punishment a fair reward for their actions. Allah’s justice does not mean that every hardship is a direct punishment for a particular sin; believers can be tested and elevated through hardship. Here the text identifies a historical consequence of a settled pattern of rebellion. It teaches the general principle that ingratitude and transgression damage the dignity of individuals and communities, while gratitude preserves blessing.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:57 and 7:160 on manna, quails, and the provision in the wilderness.
- Qur’an 3:21, 2:87, and 5:70 on rejection and unjust killing of prophets.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on the upright among the People of the Book.
- Qur’an 14:7 on gratitude and increase, and 16:114 on eating good provision with thankfulness.
- Qur’an 17:4–7 on the recurring consequences of corruption and transgression among the Children of Israel.
- For the interpretations of *fūm*, *miṣr*, and the moral meaning of the complaint, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:61.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:62

> Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

### Salvation Is Not a Mere Label

This āyah states a broad principle: belonging to a named community is not, by itself, the measure of acceptance with Allah. It mentions believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians, then gives the decisive qualities: genuine belief in Allah, belief in the Last Day, and righteous action. The verse therefore rejects both ethnic privilege and empty religious identification. A person is not saved by a label that contradicts his actual faith and conduct.

The word “believers” in the immediate context can refer to those who followed Muhammad (ﷺ), while the named communities refer to people who followed their prophets sincerely before the final revelation reached them. Allah sent guidance to different peoples across history, and He does not punish a people until a messenger’s message has been established against them (17:15). Those who sincerely followed the revelation available to them, believed in Allah and the final accountability, and acted righteously are not equated with those who knowingly rejected a clear messenger.

When the final message reaches a person clearly and authentically, the Qur’an presents acceptance of Muhammad (ﷺ) as part of belief in Allah and His messengers. It says, “Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them” (3:85), and it commands belief in all the prophets without rejecting any of them (2:136). Verse 62 should therefore not be isolated from the Qur’an’s complete teaching. It is a rejection of salvation by communal name, not a denial of the obligation to respond to the final messenger after his message has become clear.

### The Meaning of the Sabians

The Sabians are mentioned several times in the Qur’an, but their exact historical identity was debated by Muslim scholars. Some classical reports connected them with a monotheistic community that followed remnants of earlier prophetic teaching; others associated them with a distinct religious group in the region of Iraq and the Levant. Because the Qur’an does not provide a full ethnographic description here, it is safer to affirm what the verse establishes without claiming certainty about every historical detail.

The inclusion of the Sabians reinforces the universality of the principle. Allah’s judgment is not based on the public name of a community alone. He knows who truly believed, who received a clear message, who acted in ignorance, and who knowingly rejected the truth. Human beings can classify communities from the outside, but only Allah can judge the complete circumstances of individuals.

### Faith and Righteous Action

The verse joins faith with righteous action. Belief in Allah is not a mere philosophical affirmation; it means worshipping Him, acknowledging His authority, and allowing that conviction to govern conduct. Belief in the Last Day gives actions eternal significance. Good deeds are not offered as a price that forces Allah to reward a person; they are the evidence of sincere faith and the means through which Allah’s mercy is sought.

The Qur’an repeatedly balances belief and action: “Those who believe and do righteous deeds will have the Gardens of Paradise as a lodging” (18:107), and “whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while being a believer, We will surely give them a good life” (16:97). A verbal claim without righteousness is exposed as hollow, while outward goodness must ultimately be directed to Allah rather than becoming self-worship or mere reputation.

The verse also establishes individual accountability. A person cannot hide behind the goodness of his ancestors or the failures of another member of his community. Every soul is judged according to what it believed, understood, intended, and did. This principle protects the sincere from collective condemnation and warns the privileged against relying on inherited status.

### No Fear and No Grief

The promise that there will be no fear and no grief is the same promise made to those who follow Allah’s guidance in the earlier passage (2:38). Fear concerns the future; grief concerns what has passed. In the Hereafter, the righteous will be protected from ultimate fear of punishment and grief over the loss of worldly life. Their earthly hardships may have been real, but they will not have the final word.

The promise is not a guarantee that a believer will never cry, worry, or suffer in this world. Prophets experienced grief, and righteous people faced persecution and loss. It means that faith gives hardship a destination and grief a context. Allah does not waste patience or righteousness, and the final meeting with Him will reveal the value of deeds that the world ignored.

### A Verse Against Both Arrogance and Relativism

This āyah restrains two opposite errors. The first is religious arrogance: claiming that a group is saved merely by its name while ignoring disbelief and injustice. The second is careless relativism: claiming that all beliefs are equally true even when the Qur’an calls people to one God and one final revelation. The verse asks us to affirm Allah’s justice, the continuity of revelation, the necessity of sincere faith, and the importance of righteous action together.

Muslims should therefore read it with humility. It is not a certificate of automatic salvation for anyone who adopts a label, and it is not a command to pronounce the final fate of individuals whose circumstances are unknown. It is a declaration that Allah rewards genuine faith and goodness, and that His judgment is more precise and just than human classifications.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:38, 2:136, and 3:84 on guidance, belief in all prophets, and the unity of revealed religion.
- Qur’an 3:85 and 5:48 on the final revelation and the Qur’an as a criterion over earlier scripture.
- Qur’an 16:97 and 18:107–108 on faith joined to righteous action and its reward.
- Qur’an 17:15 and 6:164 on Allah’s justice and individual responsibility.
- Qur’an 22:17, which also mentions the Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magians, and polytheists and reserves judgment to Allah.
- For the identity of the Sabians and the relationship between this verse and 3:85, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:62.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:63

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.”

**Commentary:**

### The Covenant at the Mountain

Allah reminds the Children of Israel of the covenant taken from them and the mountain raised above them. The scene appears elsewhere in the Qur’an in the description of Mount Sinai being lifted over them as a sign of the seriousness of the covenant (7:171). The mountain is not presented as a theatrical threat detached from guidance. It made visible the weight of the responsibility they had accepted when they received the Scripture.

A covenant joins promise and obligation. Allah gave them revelation, and they were required to hold firmly to it, believe its truth, preserve it, act upon it, and convey what it required. The command “hold firmly” suggests both intellectual adherence and practical commitment. Scripture is not held firmly by placing it in a respected building or reciting it ceremonially while rejecting its commands. It is held firmly when it governs worship, justice, speech, family life, economic conduct, and relations with others.

The covenant included the duties later summarized in this sūrah: worshipping Allah alone, kindness to parents and vulnerable people, prayer, zakāh, truthful speech, and the prohibition of bloodshed and expulsion (2:83–85). These details show that revelation cannot be reduced to private theology. A people who claim to hold the Book must allow it to regulate both the heart and public conduct.

### A Visible Sign of an Invisible Responsibility

The raised mountain was a powerful sign, but the purpose was mindfulness—*taqwā*. A sign can establish evidence, but it cannot replace willing obedience. The people could see the mountain, hear the command, and still turn away later. External pressure may produce temporary compliance, but taqwā requires the heart to remain conscious of Allah after the dramatic moment has passed.

The same lesson applies today. A person may be moved by a sermon, a crisis, a funeral, or a religious gathering, then return to old habits once the emotion fades. The covenant becomes real when the reminder is translated into consistent practice. The believer asks what the Book requires of him when no one is watching and no extraordinary sign is immediately before him.

### Revelation Is a Trust, Not a Decoration

The Children of Israel were not asked to invent their own guidance. They were given a Book. This gift relieved them of the burden of making moral truth depend on the strongest tribe, ruler, or desire. But the gift also created responsibility. A revealed standard must be learned accurately, interpreted responsibly, and obeyed with sincerity.

Muslims are included in this lesson through the Qur’an. The Prophet (ﷺ) warned that some people may recite the Qur’an without it going beyond their throats, meaning that recitation and outward religious identity do not guarantee transformation (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the descriptions of the Khawārij). The warning should make the believer ask whether the Qur’an is forming his character or merely furnishing his speech.

### Holding Firmly with Knowledge and Practice

Holding firmly does not mean refusing to think or ask questions. It means remaining anchored to the revelation while seeking sound understanding. The Qur’an praises those who follow the best of what they hear (39:18) and commands people to ask those who possess knowledge (16:43). Blind attachment to an inaccurate interpretation is not fidelity to scripture. Fidelity combines learning, humility, evidence, and obedience.

The verse also implies that the covenant is communal. Each individual is responsible, but institutions, scholars, families, and governments shape whether revelation is preserved or manipulated. A community that removes justice from its religious teaching has not held firmly to the Book, even if its public recitation is abundant. A community that obeys the Book imperfectly but repents and corrects itself remains under the possibility of mercy.

### The Mountain and the Heart

The physical mountain above them can be read as an image of the weight that revelation should have in the heart. Allah’s commands are not casual suggestions to be accepted only when convenient. They are a serious trust. The believer does not need a mountain to remain faithful, because the knowledge of Allah’s greatness and the certainty of the Hereafter create an inward seriousness.

The verse therefore calls for mindful adherence. The Children of Israel were given scripture after rescue, forgiveness, and repeated signs. Their problem was not lack of favour but failure to turn favour into commitment. The warning is equally relevant to the Muslim community: receiving the Qur’an is an honour, but honour is fulfilled only by holding firmly to it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:171 on the mountain raised above the Children of Israel and the covenant to hold firmly to the Book.
- Qur’an 2:40, 2:83–85, and 5:12 on the covenant’s content and responsibility.
- Qur’an 39:18 and 16:43 on learning, listening, and seeking sound knowledge.
- Qur’an 57:16 on hearts becoming humble before the remembrance of Allah and the revealed truth.
- Qur’an 24:51 and 2:285 on the sincere response: hearing and obeying.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on recitation without inward understanding as a warning against empty religious form.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:64

> Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers.

**Commentary:**

### Turning Away After the Covenant

After the covenant and the dramatic sign of the mountain, the Children of Israel still turned away. The verse exposes the weakness of outward promises that are not supported by sustained obedience. A person may agree when confronted by a powerful reminder, but the truth of the agreement appears later, when ordinary life resumes and the immediate pressure has gone.

Turning away can mean refusing to hold firmly to the Book, neglecting its commands, distorting its teachings, or treating the covenant as a historical ceremony rather than a living obligation. The following verses and the broader passage provide examples: calf-worship, breaking the Sabbath, killing prophets, concealing knowledge, and accepting only the parts of scripture that serve desire. The verse summarizes a pattern rather than identifying one isolated incident.

This is a warning against relying upon religious experience alone. A moving event, a miraculous sign, or an emotional pledge can begin a journey, but steadfastness requires daily choices. The Qur’an praises those who say, “Our Lord is Allah,” and then remain upright (41:30). Turning away after knowledge is more serious than never having encountered the reminder because the person has been shown the direction and then deliberately abandons it.

### Grace and Mercy Preserve the Sinner

The verse immediately adds that Allah’s grace and mercy prevented their complete loss. This balance is essential. Allah does not ignore their turning away, but He does not destroy them instantly either. He continues to provide life, revelation, opportunities for repentance, prophets, and reminders. The survival of a sinful community is itself a mercy, though it must never be mistaken for approval.

Allah’s grace includes what He gives without the servant earning it: existence, guidance, time, health, protection, and the ability to repent. His mercy includes forgiveness, concealment of faults, acceptance of return, and the multiplication of good deeds. Without this mercy, human beings would be among the losers because no one can fulfil Allah’s rights perfectly or thank Him for every blessing adequately.

The Qur’an says, “Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, not one of you would ever have been pure” (24:21). Purification requires effort, but the opportunity, ability, and acceptance of that effort are all gifts. The believer therefore acts without self-righteousness and hopes without presumption.

### The Meaning of Loss

“Losers” in the Qur’an are not merely those who experience a bad outcome in one worldly matter. They are those who lose faith, moral integrity, opportunities, relationships, and finally the soul. Sūrah al-ʿAṣr describes humanity as being in loss except those who believe, do good, uphold truth, and practise patience (103:1–3). The Children of Israel were saved from Pharaoh, but they could still lose themselves through ingratitude and rebellion.

Worldly respite can hide this loss. A person may retain wealth, health, social influence, or a respected religious name while the inner relationship with Allah deteriorates. The Qur’an warns that those who are given time to continue wrongdoing should not think the delay is good for them; it may allow the burden of sin to increase (3:178). The only safe response to respite is repentance and reform.

### Grace Does Not Cancel Responsibility

The mention of mercy does not excuse turning away. A person cannot say, “Allah is merciful,” while intentionally using mercy as permission to persist in wrongdoing. True hope produces gratitude and action. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that the wise person is one who holds himself accountable and works for what comes after death (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2459). Reliance upon Allah’s mercy means seeking it through obedience, not demanding it while rejecting the covenant.

The verse is also a message of hope for those who have turned away. As long as the person is alive, Allah’s grace can bring him back. The Qur’an calls people not to despair of Allah’s mercy and promises forgiveness to those who repent and reform (39:53; 25:70). The path back is open, but the person must stop defending the turning away.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:178 on the danger of mistaking respite for approval.
- Qur’an 24:21 on Allah’s grace and mercy as the source of purification.
- Qur’an 25:70 and 39:53 on repentance, reform, and not despairing of Allah’s mercy.
- Qur’an 41:30 and 103:1–3 on steadfastness and avoiding ultimate loss.
- Qur’an 2:52, 2:54, and 2:56 on repeated divine mercy after serious errors.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2459, on self-accountability and preparing for the Hereafter.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:65

> You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, “Be disgraced apes!”

**Commentary:**

### The Sabbath as a Covenant

Allah reminds the Children of Israel of those who violated the Sabbath. The Sabbath was a divinely appointed day of worship and rest for that community. It was a test of obedience: would they honour Allah’s command when doing so meant restraining an attractive economic opportunity? Sūrah al-Aʿrāf describes a coastal community whose fish appeared openly on the Sabbath but were less accessible on other days, leading some people to devise a scheme to take what had been prohibited (7:163–166).

The core offence was not ordinary work on a day when work happened to be difficult. It was a deliberate attempt to preserve the outward appearance of obedience while circumventing the command’s purpose. They manipulated circumstances so that they could claim technical compliance while obtaining the prohibited benefit. This is the same moral pattern criticized earlier: changing the form of a command in order to escape its meaning.

The Qur’an does not give the location or every historical detail in this āyah. The related account provides enough to understand the test, but believers should not build certainty upon every later story added to it. The secure lesson is that a revealed restriction cannot be defeated by clever rearrangement when the intention is to obtain what Allah prohibited.

### “Be Disgraced Apes”

The command “be disgraced apes” is understood by the majority of classical exegetes as a real transformation imposed upon the Sabbath-breakers. Other interpreters emphasized the metaphorical disgrace and degradation expressed by the image. The Qur’an’s wording is capable of conveying the severity of divine punishment, and the exact nature and duration of the transformation should not be expanded beyond what revelation establishes.

The image of apes is associated with imitation, loss of dignity, and a degraded condition in the moral imagination of the passage. The people wanted to imitate obedience while secretly pursuing disobedience; their punishment exposed the ugliness of that duplicity. The phrase “be disgraced” also makes clear that the punishment was not a compliment or a description of a superior form of life. It was a sign of humiliation resulting from rebellion.

This account must never be used as an ethnic insult against Jews or against any modern population. The verse concerns a specific group who committed a specific violation under a particular revealed law. The Qur’an distinguishes among the People of the Book and praises upright individuals among them (3:113–115). Applying the punishment as a blanket label to a people is itself contrary to Qur’anic justice.

### The Danger of Legal Evasion

The Sabbath-breakers illustrate the difference between genuine obedience and legal evasion. Divine law often includes both a form and a purpose. The form must be observed, but it cannot be used to destroy the purpose. A person who gives a contract a lawful name while arranging it to produce a forbidden result has not necessarily become obedient through vocabulary. A person who pays charity publicly while humiliating the recipient has preserved the outward transfer but damaged its spirit.

This does not mean that every legal device is forbidden. Islamic law recognizes legitimate contracts, concessions, and lawful planning. The decisive question is intention and substance: is the person seeking a lawful outcome through lawful means, or is he trying to obtain a forbidden outcome by disguising the route? The Sabbath story warns against the second.

### A Test of Restraint

The appearing fish represented an opportunity that seemed to reward disobedience. Temptation often becomes strongest when wrongdoing appears profitable and safe. The believer is tested precisely where he could gain by crossing a boundary. Taqwā means remembering that Allah sees the method as well as the result, and that a temporary gain cannot compensate for the loss of integrity.

The account also reassures those who obey while others appear to prosper through evasion. The Sabbath-breakers may have thought that their cleverness had defeated the command. Allah’s judgment showed that apparent success does not change the moral reality. The believer should not envy a gain purchased through disobedience or conclude that lawful restraint is foolish because the dishonest person seems to advance.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:163–166 on the Sabbath community, the fish, the attempted scheme, and the punishment.
- Qur’an 4:47 and 5:60 on the gravity of rejecting Allah’s commands and the imagery of transformation.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright people among the People of the Book.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 49:13 on justice and the prohibition of collective ethnic judgment.
- Qur’an 16:91–92 on fulfilling covenants rather than manipulating them.
- For the nature of the transformation and the moral issue of legal evasion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:65.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:66

> So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing.

**Commentary:**

### Punishment as a Public Warning

Allah made the fate of the Sabbath-breakers an example—*nakālan*—for those around them and for later generations. The punishment was not only a response to the immediate offence; it became a warning that could protect others from repeating the same pattern. Divine history functions as moral evidence. The event is preserved so that people can see the consequences of treating Allah’s commands as obstacles to be outmanoeuvred.

The phrase “present and future generations” translates an Arabic expression that can also mean those who lived at the time and those who came after them. It emphasizes that a community’s conduct can become a lesson beyond its own lifetime. The actions of leaders, scholars, institutions, and nations leave precedents. A practice of justice can benefit people long after its founders are gone; a practice of corruption can normalize harm for generations.

The Qur’an frequently presents historical accounts as *ʿibrah*—a lesson extracted from an event. “In their stories there is certainly a lesson for people of understanding” (12:111). The purpose is not entertainment, nostalgia, or superiority over an ancient people. It is to make the reader ask what kind of conduct is being praised or condemned and how the same moral structure may appear in a new setting.

### The God-Fearing Benefit from History

The final phrase identifies the people who benefit: those who are mindful of Allah. The same story that produces mockery or scepticism in one person produces caution in another. Taqwā enables the reader to see the punished community as a warning to himself rather than as an opportunity to condemn outsiders. The God-fearing person asks, “Where am I tempted to conceal disobedience beneath a lawful appearance?”

This is why Qur’anic history cannot be used responsibly as a tool for ethnic hatred. The lesson is universal. Muslims who manipulate contracts, evade obligations, exploit loopholes, or preserve outward religious respectability while pursuing forbidden gain are vulnerable to the same moral disease. The name of the community does not change the principle.

### Example and Justice

An example is effective because it connects an abstract rule to a visible consequence. The Sabbath command could have been discussed only in legal language, but the punishment made the cost of deliberate evasion unforgettable. Allah’s justice is not random: He warns, gives time, establishes proof, and then judges. The example therefore supports both fear and fairness.

The verse does not mean that every sin receives a visible punishment in this world. Many wrongdoers prosper temporarily, and many righteous people suffer. Worldly consequences are sometimes signs, sometimes tests, and sometimes delayed. The final and complete judgment is in the Hereafter. The example of the Sabbath-breakers is one historical manifestation of the general truth that Allah does not allow rebellion to escape His knowledge.

### A Lesson for Communities of Revelation

Religious communities are especially vulnerable to the misuse of sacred law. A person can become skilled at explaining why a command technically does not apply while quietly preserving the desire that the command was meant to restrain. The Sabbath-breakers show that a community can know the law and still corrupt its purpose through ingenuity.

A community protects itself by teaching both the letter and the spirit of revelation, encouraging sincere counsel, holding leaders accountable, and refusing to reward clever evasion. This does not mean abandoning legal precision. It means ensuring that precision serves obedience rather than becoming a shield for rebellion. The believer studies the law in order to obey Allah, not merely to discover the narrowest path around an obligation.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:163–166 on the Sabbath-breakers and their punishment.
- Qur’an 12:111 on historical narratives as lessons for people of understanding.
- Qur’an 3:137 and 59:2 on travelling through history and drawing instruction from past outcomes.
- Qur’an 16:90–92 on justice, covenant, and the consequences of breaking commitments.
- Qur’an 24:2 on punishment as a deterrent and public lesson under lawful authority.
- For *nakāl*, *ʿibrah*, and the scope of the example, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:66.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:67

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow.” They replied, “Are you mocking us?” Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!”

**Commentary:**

### The Story That Gives the Sūrah Its Name

The sūrah now begins the account of the cow, from which it takes its name, *al-Baqarah*. The command appears simple: sacrifice a cow. Nothing in the initial wording specifies a rare breed, special colour, age, or condition. The people could have obeyed immediately, but they answered Moses with suspicion: “Are you mocking us?” Their response reveals a damaged relationship with command and messenger. Instead of assuming that a prophetic instruction deserves serious attention, they accuse Moses of foolishness.

Moses seeks refuge in Allah from being among the ignorant—*al-jāhilīn*. Ignorance in Qur’anic language is not only lack of information. It includes reckless speech, mockery, arrogance, and acting without the moral understanding appropriate to one’s position. A prophet does not make divine commands the subject of personal amusement. The response also shows that accusations against a messenger are not harmless when they arise from contempt for revelation.

### A Plain Command Made Difficult

The command to sacrifice a cow was initially open enough to be fulfilled without unusual difficulty. Classical exegetes explain that the later details arose because the people kept asking questions instead of obeying. Each question narrowed the range of acceptable animals until the final cow became difficult and expensive to find (2:68–71). The story is therefore a case study in procrastination: an easy duty can become burdensome when the self treats obedience as a negotiation.

The verse does not condemn every request for clarification. When a command is genuinely unclear, asking is responsible. The problem is questioning as resistance—asking in order to delay, challenge, or make a simple instruction practically impossible. The Prophet (ﷺ) warned that earlier communities were destroyed by excessive questioning and disagreement with their prophets (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Clarification should serve obedience, not replace it.

### Moses’ Protection of Prophetic Dignity

Moses’ response is measured. He does not answer mockery with another insult or allow the people’s suspicion to redefine his character. He seeks refuge in Allah from foolishness, affirming that treating Allah’s command as a joke is itself ignorance. Prophetic dignity is not personal pride; it is protection of the trust of revelation.

The verse also teaches believers how to respond when accused unjustly. They should clarify the truth without becoming captive to rage. A person who is mocked for obedience may feel pressure to imitate the mocker’s language, but restraint is stronger. Moses places the matter before Allah and refuses to accept the description of a divine command as a joke.

### The Moral Meaning of Sacrifice

Sacrifice is a recurring sign of submission in the prophetic tradition. Abraham was tested with commands and fulfilled them (2:124), and Muslims were taught that neither the flesh nor blood of sacrifice reaches Allah, but taqwā reaches Him (22:37). The animal itself is not divine and has no independent power. Sacrifice expresses obedience, gratitude, and willingness to place Allah’s command above personal reluctance.

In this story, however, the people are not initially praised for spiritual eagerness. They try to turn the command into an argument about Moses’ seriousness. The irony is that the sūrah will reveal a hidden murder through the sacrificed cow, demonstrating that a command which appeared ordinary had wisdom beyond their immediate understanding. Obedience often precedes the discovery of its full benefit.

### A Warning Against Mocking Revelation

The accusation “Are you mocking us?” also reveals how people sometimes project their own irreverence onto a prophet. A person who does not want to obey may suggest that the command is unreasonable, symbolic, outdated, or not meant seriously. If the command is made to look absurd, disobedience feels justified. The Qur’an repeatedly warns against taking Allah’s signs as a joke (2:231; 9:65–66).

The believer should preserve both seriousness and balance. Not every cultural practice or personal opinion is a divine command, so claims of religious obligation must be verified. But once an authentic command is established, mockery is not a substitute for understanding. The proper approach is to ask sincerely, learn from qualified scholarship, and obey what Allah has made clear.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:68–74, the continuation and conclusion of the cow narrative.
- Qur’an 2:124 and 22:37 on testing, sacrifice, and taqwā.
- Qur’an 2:231 and 9:65–66 on not treating Allah’s revelations as objects of mockery.
- Qur’an 7:199 on responding to ignorance with forgiveness and appropriate conduct.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on excessive questioning and disagreement with prophets.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:68

> They said, “Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!” He replied, “Allah says, ‘The cow should neither be old nor young but in between. So do as you are commanded!’”

**Commentary:**

### Clarification or Evasion?

The people ask Moses to specify the age of the cow. A request for clarification can be legitimate when a command is genuinely ambiguous, but the context shows that their questioning had become a means of delay. The original command had not required a rare or exceptional animal. By repeatedly asking for additional conditions, they transformed a general obligation into a narrow search.

Moses answers that the cow should be neither old nor young, but of an age in between. The Arabic *ʿawān* refers to something mature or middle-aged. The answer is clear and sufficient. It does not invite another debate about minute classifications. The closing instruction—“So do as you are commanded”—brings the focus back from curiosity to obedience.

This is a general principle of religious learning. Not every detail is equally necessary for action. Sometimes people ask for more and more specifications because they wish to avoid making a decision. They say they need another opinion, another proof, another condition, or another explanation, when the real problem is reluctance to obey what is already clear. Knowledge can become a shelter for procrastination when it is detached from intention.

### Divine Law and the Space It Leaves Open

The initial absence of an age specification may itself be understood as a mercy. When Allah leaves a matter general, people should not voluntarily make it difficult by inventing restrictions. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Leave me as I have left you; those before you were destroyed only because of their excessive questioning and disagreement with their prophets” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). He also taught that Allah has prescribed obligations, set limits, and left some matters unmentioned as a mercy, so people should not search into them unnecessarily (Sunan al-Dāraquṭnī and other collections, with supporting narrations).

This does not mean that Islamic law lacks detail. Where Allah and His Messenger specify conditions, those conditions must be honoured. The lesson is to distinguish revealed requirements from self-imposed complications. A believer should neither ignore a clear condition nor manufacture one where Allah has left lawful room.

The people’s behaviour shows how a command can become harder through repeated demands. The later cow had to satisfy each additional specification. What began as an accessible duty became a search for an uncommon animal. The story is not an argument against asking questions; it is an argument against questioning with the purpose of escaping responsibility.

### The Middle Way

The cow is described as neither old nor young but in between. The detail also carries a broader lesson about balance. Islam repeatedly praises moderation: neither extravagance nor stinginess, neither cowardice nor reckless aggression, neither harshness nor permissiveness. The middle condition is not always the answer in every matter, but it often protects people from extremes and makes a command workable.

The prophetic method also balances clarity with practicality. A teacher should explain enough for people to obey correctly, but should not burden them with unnecessary speculation. A student should seek understanding, but should not make every decision conditional on total mastery. The objective of knowledge is guidance and action.

### Obedience Before Full Explanation

Human beings often want to know every reason before accepting a command. Reflection on divine wisdom is encouraged, but obedience cannot depend upon the servant’s complete grasp of every rationale. Parents, doctors, engineers, and legal authorities may explain a rule, but a person can still trust a reliable command when his own understanding is incomplete. Allah is All-Wise, and the limits of human insight do not limit the wisdom of revelation.

The people could have slaughtered any suitable cow and then asked about further wisdom. Instead, they asked until the command became difficult. The believer learns from this that a sincere question should make obedience clearer, not postpone it. When the truth is established and the command is within one’s ability, the proper response is to act.

- Qur’an 2:67 and 2:69–71 on the original command and the successive specifications.
- Qur’an 5:101–102 on not asking unnecessary questions that create difficulty.
- Qur’an 16:43 and 17:36 on asking knowledgeable people and not speaking without knowledge.
- Qur’an 7:31 and 25:67 on moderation and the middle way.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on excessive questioning and disagreement with prophets.
- For *ʿawān* and the exegetical lesson of the people’s repeated questions, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:68.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:69

> They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.’”

**Commentary:**

### Another Question, Another Restriction

Rather than act on Moses’ answer about the cow’s age, the people ask for its colour. The sequence shows that their questions are not arising from genuine inability to understand the command. Each answer produces another demand. A broad command becomes progressively narrower because they are treating obedience as a negotiation.

The cow is to be bright yellow—*ṣafrāʾ fāqiʿ*—a vivid, intense colour, “pleasant to see.” The description is clear enough to guide them, yet it also highlights the beauty of Allah’s creation. A lawful appreciation of beauty is not contrary to faith. Allah created adornment and good things, and the Qur’an asks who can forbid the adornment Allah has brought forth for His servants (7:32).

The problem in the story is not that the people cared about appearance. It is that they had already been given a sufficient command and continued to seek conditions that made compliance more difficult. The verse therefore distinguishes between a practical question that helps identify an object and a question that functions as resistance.

### Beauty and the Giver of Beauty

The description “pleasant to see” invites reflection on beauty as a sign of divine generosity. Colour, form, symmetry, taste, and fragrance are part of the created order through which human beings experience provision. Enjoying beauty with gratitude is permissible and can deepen awareness of Allah. But beauty is not a measure of moral worth, and an attractive object cannot make a forbidden act lawful.

The Qur’an repeatedly directs people to look at the beauty and order of creation. The sky is adorned, the earth is made fertile, and animals provide transport and nourishment. These signs are meant to lead to gratitude rather than worship of nature. The bright cow was still a creature under Allah’s command; its appearance did not grant it divine status.

The phrase also reminds the reader that a command may include both practical and aesthetic detail. Allah is not only the Lord of necessity but the Lord of beauty. Nevertheless, the people’s later hesitation shows that even a beautifully described command can be resisted when the heart prefers argument to obedience.

### When Clarification Becomes Procrastination

There is a point at which clarification stops being service and becomes delay. A person may continue asking for more detail because every additional condition creates the possibility of postponing action. The Qur’an criticizes this tendency in the Children of Israel and warns the Muslim community not to repeat it. “O believers, do not ask about things which, if made clear to you, would distress you” (5:101), while the same passage teaches that Allah has pardoned what He left unmentioned.

This does not mean that believers should accept unclear claims without investigation. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that seeking knowledge is a duty, and the Qur’an commands people to ask those who know. The balance is sincerity: ask in order to obey accurately, not to make obedience impossible. A question should reduce confusion and lead to action.

### The Significance of Colour in Revelation

The Qur’an occasionally uses colour as a sign that awakens reflection, as in the variety of plants, mountains, animals, and human complexions (35:27–28). Colour is a part of Allah’s creative diversity, not a basis for arrogance. The bright yellow cow was specified for this particular test, but no colour gives one people or person superiority over another.

The verse also challenges the human tendency to treat ordinary things as spiritually insignificant. A cow’s colour may seem mundane, yet Allah can make an ordinary creature part of a lesson about resurrection, hidden crime, obedience, and the danger of haggling. The believer does not decide that a subject is beneath reflection merely because it is familiar.

### Obedience and Aesthetic Preference

The people’s request suggests that they wanted a cow that satisfied not only the command but their own preferences. Preference is not wrong, but it becomes blameworthy when it displaces the command. In many areas of life, people ask Allah’s guidance and then attempt to modify it until it matches what they already wanted. The story teaches that revelation is not a menu from which the ego chooses only attractive items.

The proper response is to receive lawful beauty with gratitude while remaining obedient to the revealed boundary. The bright cow was a gift within a command, not a replacement for the command. The next question will show that even this specification did not end their hesitation.

- Qur’an 2:67–68 and 2:70–71 on the sequence of questions and the eventual cow.
- Qur’an 7:32 on lawful adornment and the good provision Allah has created.
- Qur’an 35:27–28 on colours and diversity as signs of Allah’s creation.
- Qur’an 5:101–102 on avoiding unnecessary questions that create hardship.
- Qur’an 16:114 and 2:172 on gratitude for good and lawful provision.
- For *ṣafrāʾ fāqiʿ* and the role of the colour specification, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:69.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:70

> Again they said, “Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow, for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.”

**Commentary:**

### “All Cows Look the Same”

The people continue their questions even after the age and colour have been specified. They say that the cows appear alike and ask for still more clarification. The statement may reflect practical difficulty, but in the context of the story it also reveals a mind that has made certainty a precondition for obedience. Instead of acting on a clear command, they demand a description so exact that no possibility of personal judgment remains.

The phrase “Allah willing” appears in their speech, but the mere use of religious language does not prove sincerity. Invoking Allah’s will should accompany genuine intention and action, not serve as a pious cover for delay. The Qur’an teaches believers to say “if Allah wills” when speaking about future plans (18:23–24), while also warning them not to use religious phrases as a substitute for fulfilling promises and obligations.

Their request illustrates a general spiritual problem. People sometimes ask for certainty not because they lack enough information, but because they want to avoid the risk and cost of obedience. They want a command so detailed that no judgment, sacrifice, or trust will be required from them. Yet divine law often leaves room for responsible reasoning within clear boundaries. The goal is not to remove every human decision but to guide it.

### The Difference Between Guidance and Paralysis

The people say, “Then, Allah willing, we will be guided.” In reality, guidance had already come through Moses’ command. They were treating guidance as something that could begin only after every uncertainty had been eliminated. The Qur’an presents a different process: a person receives the available evidence, seeks clarification where necessary, and then acts with trust. Further understanding often comes through obedience rather than preceding it entirely.

This does not mean that religion should be built on guesswork. When a matter is genuinely unclear, scholars investigate the Qur’an, Sunnah, Arabic language, historical context, and principles of law. But human beings cannot demand the kind of certainty that belongs only to Allah’s complete knowledge. A person who waits for total comprehension before obeying may never obey anything.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that what is lawful is clear, what is unlawful is clear, and between them are matters that are ambiguous to many people; whoever avoids doubtful matters protects his religion and honour (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This teaching does not eliminate scholarship or nuance. It teaches moral caution and recognizes that responsible believers must sometimes act without possessing absolute knowledge of every hidden consequence.

### “Your Lord” and the Distance of Reluctance

Their repeated phrase “call upon your Lord” may be a way of placing the responsibility upon Moses rather than taking responsibility themselves. Moses is the messenger, so asking him to convey a request is not itself improper. The problem is that they are using the request to avoid obeying a command already given. The distance between “your Lord” and their own obedience suggests a weakened relationship with Allah: they treat the command as Moses’ problem instead of their own covenant.

The believer should not make religious leaders carry a responsibility that belongs to him. Scholars can explain, teach, and issue guidance within their competence, but each person must worship, repent, fulfil trusts, and answer to Allah himself. Asking for a fatwa is not a transfer of accountability. A person cannot say on the Day of Judgment that he intended to obey but spent his life waiting for someone else to remove every difficulty.

### The Cost of Overcomplication

The story also contains a rule of communal life. Excessive demands can make simple public duties expensive and slow. When people insist on unnecessary specifications, they may exclude practical options, burden the poor, and create disputes that did not exist before. Divine guidance is wise and proportionate; human additions can become a source of hardship.

The believer therefore asks: what is required, what is recommended, and what is merely personal preference? Confusing these levels can turn a lawful choice into a false obligation or a clear duty into a matter of endless debate. Knowledge brings clarity when it distinguishes categories; it produces confusion when it multiplies conditions without evidence.

- Qur’an 5:101–102 on asking unnecessary questions and the mercy of what Allah has left unmentioned.
- Qur’an 16:43 and 17:36 on consulting people of knowledge and not following unsupported claims.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the ḥadīth of the lawful, unlawful, and doubtful matters.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:71

> He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish.’” They said, “Now you have come with the truth.” Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly!

**Commentary:**

### The Final Specifications

Moses gives the last description: the cow must not have been used for ploughing or irrigation, and it must be sound and without blemish. The Arabic terms indicate an animal kept free from agricultural labour and protected from visible defects. After this answer, the people finally acknowledge that Moses has brought the truth. Their statement is ironic: the truth was present from the first command, but they recognized it only after their questioning had made obedience difficult.

The verse shows that the later specifications were not part of the original burden in the same way. They arose through the people’s demands for clarification. Their questions did not expose a defect in revelation; they narrowed the task they were required to fulfil. The command began broadly and ended with the search for an unusually well-preserved cow. The story illustrates how resistance can increase the cost of an obligation.

This does not mean that every detail in divine law is caused by human questioning. Allah legislates with wisdom and may specify matters from the beginning. The lesson concerns cases in which people turn an open command into a difficult one through unnecessary haggling. The Qur’an warns against asking about matters that, once made clear, would create hardship (5:101).

### Wholesome and Without Blemish

The cow is described as *musallamah*—sound, safe, or free from defect—and without a mark or blemish. The requirement communicates care and seriousness in carrying out the sacrifice. Worship should not be performed with contempt or with an object deliberately chosen because it is worthless. In other passages, Allah commands believers to spend from the good things they have earned and not choose for charity what they themselves would accept only with closed eyes (2:267).

The detail also reminds the reader that outward quality can matter in worship, but outward quality does not replace inward sincerity. A flawless animal offered for reputation is not better before Allah than a modest offering given with taqwā. “Their flesh and blood do not reach Allah, but your taqwā reaches Him” (22:37). Form and quality are respected because Allah commanded them, while the heart remains the basis of acceptance.

The same principle applies beyond sacrifice. A person should fulfil a trust with sound materials, accurate information, and honest effort rather than passing defective goods to others. He should not offer the poor what he would be ashamed to accept himself. Religious language cannot sanctify negligence.

### Obedience After Delay

The people slaughtered the cow, but only “hesitantly”—*wa-mā kādū yafʿalūn*, they were scarcely able to do it. The hesitation reveals that compliance was not the same as enthusiastic submission. They acted only after exhausting their objections and narrowing the options. A person can technically fulfil an order while inwardly resenting it and seeking the smallest possible compliance.

The Qur’an does not require human beings to feel no difficulty when obeying. A believer may struggle with desire, fear, or uncertainty and still obey sincerely. The problem in the story is a pattern of deliberate resistance, not the normal effort involved in disciplining the self. The faithful response is to ask Allah for help and act, rather than to use the presence of inner reluctance as a reason for endless delay.

### The Lesson of the Rare Cow

The rarity of the final cow is a practical parable about procrastination. Each additional condition reduced the available choices and increased the burden. In personal life, delaying prayer, repayment, apology, charity, or repentance often multiplies the difficulty. The task that could have been done immediately becomes entangled with new obligations and consequences.

The story also warns leaders and communities not to create needless bureaucracy around clear duties. Rules should serve justice and clarity, not provide opportunities for evasion or control. The believer distinguishes between necessary safeguards and self-created obstacles. Obedience is not improved by needless complexity.

- Qur’an 5:101–102 on unnecessary questioning and the mercy of what Allah has not specified.
- Qur’an 2:267 and 22:37 on offering what is good and the primacy of taqwā.
- Qur’an 2:286 on Allah not burdening a soul beyond its capacity.
- Qur’an 16:91 on fulfilling commitments once they are made.
- For the linguistic detail and the effect of the successive specifications, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:71.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:72

> ˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed.

**Commentary:**

### The Hidden Murder

The verse reveals the background to the cow command: a man had been killed, and the people disputed over the identity of the killer. The murder was not openly resolved through confession or reliable testimony. Instead, a dispute arose, and the truth was concealed. Allah’s command to sacrifice the cow was therefore connected to a hidden crime and to the restoration of justice.

The Qur’an does not give every detail of the murder in this verse. Later exegetical reports describe disputes over inheritance or family rivalry, but the historical specifics are not necessary to the central lesson. The secure point is that a serious wrong had occurred, the community was arguing over responsibility, and Allah revealed what human concealment had hidden.

The connection between the people’s haggling and the murder is significant. A community that is unwilling to obey a simple command may also be unwilling to speak plainly about a crime. Both behaviours arise from a relationship with truth that has been weakened by self-interest. The cow narrative is not an isolated ritual puzzle; it is a divine means of exposing injustice.

### Allah Brings Hidden Matters to Light

The verse says that Allah revealed what they had been concealing. Nothing hidden from people is hidden from Allah. He knows the victim, the killer, the motive, the secret conversation, and the false testimony. “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, Allah knows it” (3:29). Human courts may lack evidence, but the divine record is complete.

This truth is a warning to criminals and a comfort to the wronged. A murderer may hide the weapon, manipulate witnesses, or allow suspicion to fall upon an innocent person, but Allah’s knowledge is not dependent upon human investigation. At the same time, human beings remain required to seek evidence and judge fairly. The fact that Allah knows the hidden does not give individuals permission to accuse others based on intuition or dreams.

The Qur’an commands believers to verify reports (49:6), establish testimony justly (2:282), and avoid suspicion (49:12). Divine omniscience supports human justice by making concealment morally impossible, but human judgment must still respect due process. The lesson is not to replace courts with private claims of revelation; it is to know that every court and every concealment is ultimately answerable to Allah.

### The Sanctity of Life

The background murder also prepares for the Qur’an’s insistence that human life is sacred. “Whoever kills a soul—unless for a soul or for corruption in the land—it is as if he had slain all mankind” (5:32). The verse does not erase lawful judicial distinctions, but it makes clear that killing is not a private matter without consequence. A community’s response to murder reveals whether it values justice or protects powerful interests.

The dispute over the killer may have threatened to divide the people. Unresolved bloodshed creates cycles of revenge, suspicion, and collective punishment. Allah’s revelation interrupts that cycle by exposing the concealed truth and giving the community a path to resolution. Justice protects not only the victim’s family but the wider social order.

### Truth Cannot Be Negotiated Away

The people’s dispute suggests that they were more concerned with avoiding blame than with discovering truth. This is a common pattern in institutions: when wrongdoing is exposed, people debate definitions, shift responsibility, attack witnesses, or demand impossible proof—not to establish justice but to preserve reputation. The Qur’an condemns this evasion. Truth must be pursued even when it implicates one’s own family, tribe, or leadership.

The believer therefore asks whether he is seeking truth or merely seeking a version of events that protects his interests. He must not conceal testimony, spread unverified accusations, or accept a false story because it benefits his group. Allah will reveal what people conceal, whether in this world through evidence or in the Hereafter when all secrets are exposed.

- Qur’an 2:73–74, the conclusion of the cow narrative and the hardening of hearts after the sign.
- Qur’an 3:29 and 64:4 on Allah’s knowledge of what people conceal and reveal.
- Qur’an 2:282–283 and 49:6 on testimony, verification, and trustworthy evidence.
- Qur’an 5:32 and 17:33 on the sanctity of life and the prohibition of killing without lawful right.
- Qur’an 49:12 on avoiding suspicion and investigating allegations responsibly.
- For the relationship between the murder, the cow, and the revelation of concealed truth, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:72.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:73

> So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand.

**Commentary:**

### A Sign Connected to the Cow

Allah instructed the Children of Israel to strike the dead body with a piece of the sacrificed cow. The dead person was then restored sufficiently for the truth about his killer to be revealed. The Qur’an does not identify which part of the cow was used, and it is unnecessary to build doctrine upon later speculation. The miracle’s purpose is clear: Allah exposed a hidden murder and demonstrated His power over life and death.

The event shows that the cow was not an arbitrary ritual. The command was connected to a social injustice that the community had concealed. Allah joined obedience, revelation, and justice. Had the people obeyed without haggling, the duty would have been simple; their delay made the command difficult, but the sign still brought the hidden truth to light.

The miracle is not a claim that a piece of an animal possesses the power to revive the dead. The power belongs to Allah alone. The piece of the cow was a created means chosen for that particular sign, just as Moses’ staff was a means in the parting of the sea and the springs from the rock. Created objects have no independent authority over life, death, benefit, or harm.

### Allah Brings the Dead to Life

The verse states, “This is how Allah brings the dead to life.” The immediate event was a sign within worldly life, but it also points to the greater resurrection. The One who restored a dead person for a brief purpose can restore all people for final judgment. The Qur’an repeatedly uses the revival of dead earth, the creation of the human being, and earlier miracles as evidence that resurrection is within Allah’s power (30:50; 36:78–79).

Resurrection is not a second creation that would be more difficult for Allah. “He is the One who originates creation, then repeats it, and that is easier for Him” (30:27)—with “easier” understood from the human perspective, since nothing is difficult for Allah. The Qur’an asks how people can deny Allah when they were lifeless and He gave them life, then will cause them to die and raise them again (2:28).

The restored man’s testimony also demonstrates that death does not erase truth. Human beings may bury evidence with a body, hide a weapon, intimidate witnesses, or allow time to obscure a crime, but Allah can reveal the matter by means beyond ordinary expectation. No hidden injustice escapes His knowledge.

### Signs Require Understanding

Allah says He shows His signs “so that you may understand.” The purpose of a miracle is not spectacle or entertainment. A sign should produce recognition of Allah’s power, respect for His commands, and reform in conduct. The Children of Israel had witnessed the sea part, food arrive in the wilderness, and a dead person restored, yet the following verse says that their hearts hardened. A person can see a sign and still refuse its moral implication.

Understanding in the Qur’an therefore means more than intellectual recognition. It includes allowing the sign to change one’s priorities. If a community sees that Allah can expose hidden crime, it should become more truthful. If it sees that Allah raises the dead, it should prepare for judgment. If it receives a command, it should obey before demanding unnecessary detail.

The verse also encourages reflection on ordinary signs. Most people will not witness a resurrection miracle, but everyone witnesses the revival of land after rain, the formation of life, the rhythm of sleep and waking, and the inevitability of death. These signs are sufficient for a humble heart to recognize dependence upon Allah.

### Justice and the Limits of Human Knowledge

The story does not teach that every unexplained death should be investigated through supernatural claims. Human beings must use reliable evidence, medical knowledge, testimony, and lawful judicial procedures. The miracle was a unique divine sign granted to a prophetic community. It cannot be used to justify mediums, superstition, false claims of resurrection, or private accusations.

Its moral lesson remains powerful: justice is worth pursuing even when the truth appears hidden, and Allah is able to reveal what people conceal. The believer seeks truth through lawful means while trusting that the final account is never lost. The miracle is both a proof of resurrection and a warning against treating the dead, the poor, or the powerless as though their rights can be buried without consequence.

- Qur’an 22:5–7 on the revival of the earth and the certainty of resurrection.
- Qur’an 49:6 and 2:282 on verification and responsible testimony.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:74

> Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Commentary:**

### Hardness After Repeated Signs

After the dead man was restored and the concealed murder exposed, the Qur’an says that the hearts of the Children of Israel became hard. The phrase “even then” is decisive. Their hardness developed after repeated favours, warnings, revelations, miracles, and opportunities for repentance. The problem was not lack of evidence. It was the refusal to let evidence produce humility and obedience.

A hard heart is not a physical description. It is an inward condition in which reminders no longer soften a person, repentance is postponed, suffering does not produce compassion, and clear truth is treated as an inconvenience. The Qur’an describes hearts that are diseased, sealed, rusted, or hard according to the ways people respond to Allah. These images refer to moral perception and spiritual receptivity, not to the biological organ.

Hardness usually develops gradually. A person dismisses a small reminder, justifies a small injustice, delays repentance, and becomes comfortable with a contradiction. Repetition converts an exception into a habit and a habit into a character. The Qur’an says that a stain covers the hearts because of what people earn (83:14). This is why daily remembrance, prayer, truthful companionship, and immediate repentance are protections rather than optional decorations.

### Even Rocks Respond

The Qur’an compares the hardened hearts to rocks—and says they may be harder. It then describes rocks through which rivers gush, rocks that split and release water, and rocks that fall in awe of Allah. The comparison is not a scientific classification of every type of stone; it is a rhetorical contrast. Inanimate creation responds to the patterns and commands Allah placed within it, while a human heart that possesses understanding can refuse to respond.

The Qur’an elsewhere says that if it had been sent down upon a mountain, the mountain would have humbled itself and split from fear of Allah (59:21). It also says that the heavens, earth, and mountains glorify Him, even though human beings do not understand their glorification (17:44). The point is not that rocks have human belief or legal responsibility. It is that creation, in its own manner, is subject to its Lord, while the human being can be honoured with conscious obedience and yet choose hardness.

The image of rocks releasing water also carries mercy. Even a hard surface may contain or channel life-giving water by Allah’s design. A person whose heart has become hard is not entitled to despair while life remains. Allah can soften hearts through sincere repentance, suffering rightly understood, recitation, companionship, and a renewed encounter with truth.

### Awe Is Not Weakness

Some rocks “are humbled in awe of Allah.” The Qur’anic word *khashyah* includes reverent fear grounded in knowledge. Awe is not irrational panic or humiliation before human beings. It is recognition of greatness that leads to appropriate submission. The rock imagery therefore challenges the person who considers humility beneath him. If inanimate creation responds to Allah’s greatness in its own way, why should a human being treat reverence as weakness?

The Prophet (ﷺ) warned that hearts can become rusty through sin and that they are polished through remembrance and repentance, as reported in the traditions concerning the heart. The Qur’an likewise asks believers whether it is not time for their hearts to become humble to the remembrance of Allah and the truth that has been revealed (57:16). The question is an invitation, not merely a condemnation.

### Allah Is Never Unaware

The verse closes by affirming that Allah is never unaware of what they do. Hardness may be hidden behind ritual, learning, social honour, or public loyalty, but Allah knows the heart’s response and the deeds that reveal it. He knows whether a person is genuinely unable, temporarily weak, or deliberately resistant. His judgment is precise and never based on appearance alone.

This closing statement also comforts those whose patience seems unnoticed. If an oppressor’s heart is hard and society applauds him, Allah is not unaware. If a sincere person struggles to soften his heart, Allah knows the effort. The believer continues to seek softness and does not wait for a dramatic miracle before obeying.

### A Mirror for Every Community

The passage must not be used to condemn an entire ethnicity or religion. It addresses a historical community’s repeated failures and turns those failures into a warning for every reader. Muslims can become hard-hearted through constant exposure to injustice, excessive love of wealth, religious argument without practice, or repeated rejection of clear commands. A person should fear the condition of his own heart more than he enjoys listing another community’s sins.

The cure is to return to Allah before the heart becomes comfortable with its hardness: remember blessings, recite with reflection, pray with humility, restore rights, keep company with the truthful, and repent quickly. A heart that fears its own hardness and asks to be softened is already responding differently from the heart that denies the disease.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 57:16 on the time for hearts to become humble before Allah’s remembrance.
- Qur’an 59:21 and 17:44 on mountains and creation responding to Allah in their own manner.
- Qur’an 83:14 and 6:43 on the stain and hardening caused by what people earn.
- Qur’an 39:23 on revelation causing the skin and hearts of the sincere to respond.
- Qur’an 8:2 and 13:28 on faith, remembrance, and the living heart.
- For the rock imagery, hardening, and the moral application to later communities, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:74.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:75

> Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?

**Commentary:**

### Do Not Be Naïve About Deliberate Distortion

The verse asks whether the believers should expect complete faithfulness from people among the People of the Book who heard Allah’s word, understood it, and then deliberately altered it. The question is not a command to treat every Jew or every person of scripture as untrustworthy. It identifies a group characterized by a serious betrayal of knowledge. The Qur’an itself praises upright People of the Book (3:113–115), so the criticism must remain tied to the conduct named here.

The phrase “hear the word of Allah” can refer to hearing revealed instruction through their prophets and scripture, or to hearing its recitation and explanation. “Then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it” is the aggravating circumstance. They did not merely misread an obscure passage or make an honest error. They understood and then changed, concealed, or redirected the meaning to serve an interest.

The Arabic *yuḥarrifūnahu* includes distortion, turning something away from its proper position, and altering its meaning. It may involve changing words, manipulating interpretation, selective omission, or presenting a private interest as though it were divine law. The Qur’an speaks of people who twist their tongues while reciting scripture so that listeners think the distortion is from the Book (3:78). Religious corruption often succeeds because it retains enough sacred vocabulary to appear authoritative.

### Knowledge Increases Responsibility

The verse highlights the sequence of hearing, understanding, and corrupting. Knowledge is a trust, and deliberate abuse of it is worse than simple ignorance. A person who does not know may ask, learn, and be corrected. A person who knows the truth but changes it to preserve power, wealth, or reputation harms both himself and those who depend on his authority.

This is especially serious for scholars and leaders. People entrust them with interpretation, verdicts, education, and public direction. If they conceal a command because it threatens their position or invent a religious basis for oppression, they turn the trust of knowledge into an instrument of betrayal. The Qur’an condemns those who conceal the clear proofs and guidance Allah revealed after making it clear to people (2:159).

Yet the verse also warns the listener not to assume that every disagreement is corruption. Sound scholarship may produce legitimate differences in interpretation when evidence allows more than one reading. A person may misunderstand and then revise his view. The condemned act is knowing distortion, not the mere existence of diverse opinions. Responsible interpretation identifies the strength of evidence and does not claim certainty where the text and scholarship leave room.

### Expectation, Trust, and Discernment

The believers are being taught discernment. Good character does not require abandoning judgment. A person may show courtesy, keep contracts, and deal justly with people while remaining cautious about entrusting religious or political power to those who have demonstrated deliberate betrayal. Trust in public life should be built upon evidence, consistency, and accountability, not upon flattering words or communal labels.

The Qur’an commands believers to return trusts to their owners and judge with justice (4:58), and to verify reports before harming people through ignorance (49:6). These principles prevent two extremes: naïveté that ignores evidence and suspicion that treats accusation as proof. The verse supports responsible caution about a known pattern of distortion, not prejudice against a whole population.

### The Qur’an as a Criterion

The final revelation protects believers from relying solely upon inherited claims. The Qur’an confirms the original truth of previous revelation and serves as a criterion over what people attribute to it (5:48). This does not make Muslims free to distort the Qur’an themselves. It gives the community a standard by which all religious claims—including Muslim claims—must be tested.

A community that condemns distortion in others while manipulating its own scripture repeats the disease described here. Every interpreter must ask whether he is following the text or recruiting it to defend an ideology. The Qur’an should correct the reader’s group, not merely supply proof against another group.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:78 on twisting the tongue and misrepresenting scripture.
- Qur’an 5:13–15 and 5:48 on distortion, concealment, and the Qur’an as a criterion.
- Qur’an 2:159 and 3:187 on concealing clear guidance after it has been made clear.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book and the need to avoid blanket judgments.
- Qur’an 4:58 and 49:6 on trusts, justice, and verifying reports.
- For the distinction between honest disagreement and knowing distortion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:75.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:76

> When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But in private they say ˹to each other˺, “Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand?”

**Commentary:**

### Public Profession and Private Anxiety

The verse describes a group who present themselves as believers when they meet the Muslim community, yet privately warn one another not to disclose what Allah has revealed to them. Their fear is that the believers will use this knowledge as evidence against them before Allah. The irony is severe: they recognize that the knowledge supports the truth, yet they conceal it because acknowledging it would expose their own inconsistency.

This is a more deliberate form of hypocrisy than simple confusion. They know that revelation contains signs pointing beyond their inherited position, but they want to control who may hear those signs. Knowledge is treated as property belonging to a group rather than a trust to be conveyed honestly. Their concern is not whether the truth is true; it is whether the truth will weaken their prestige and arguments.

The phrase “when they meet the believers” echoes the earlier description of the hypocrites who say “we believe” in public and mock in private (2:14). Public religious language can therefore be a form of social strategy. It may secure safety, access, or influence while concealing a contrary allegiance. The Qur’an’s concern is not private difficulty in expressing faith; it is the conscious construction of two contradictory identities.

### Knowledge Cannot Be Used to Defeat Truth

Their question—“Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you?”—shows that they know revelation is powerful evidence. Yet they imagine that hiding it will protect them. This is a futile strategy. Concealing knowledge may prevent people from hearing the truth for a time, but it cannot conceal the truth from Allah. The next verse states that Allah knows what they hide and what they reveal.

The passage exposes the temptation to control information for institutional advantage. A scholar may hide a ruling that challenges his group, a leader may suppress evidence that threatens his position, or a community may teach only the passages that support its preferred narrative. In the age of mass communication, the same conduct appears through selective quotation, algorithmic silence, edited history, and deliberate misinformation. The technology changes; the moral disease remains.

The Qur’an commands the conveyance of guidance with wisdom, not indiscriminate disclosure of every private matter. Confidentiality, protection from harm, and careful teaching are legitimate. The condemned act is concealing clear truth in order to mislead or protect wrongdoing. A responsible teacher may choose the right time and method, but he does not deny a truth because it threatens his status.

### The Fear of Being Judged by One’s Own Knowledge

Their private conversation reveals an uncomfortable reality: knowledge can become evidence against its possessor. The more clearly a person recognizes a duty, the less able he is to excuse its deliberate rejection. This is why some people prefer ignorance or discourage others from learning. If the command is not heard, they imagine they will avoid responsibility; if it is known, their refusal becomes visible.

The correct response to knowledge that exposes one’s weakness is repentance, not concealment. The Qur’an teaches that the believers say, “Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us our sins” (3:16), and that those who fear Allah return when reminded of His signs. Knowledge should lead to correction even when it is uncomfortable. A person is not required to pretend to be perfect; he is required to stop using imperfection as a reason to hide truth.

### “Do You Not Understand?”

The private speakers accuse one another of failing to understand, but their own understanding is defective. They understand the strategic value of hiding revelation, yet they fail to understand that Allah’s judgment is not limited by human secrecy. This is a recurring Qur’anic irony: the person who calls believers fools is the one who has misunderstood reality, and the person who claims to protect himself through deception is harming himself most deeply.

True understanding joins knowledge with accountability. A person who knows that Allah sees and judges him will not regard concealment as safety. He will ask how to respond honestly, repair the harm caused by earlier distortion, and teach others without manipulation. The believer’s strength is not that he has no uncomfortable knowledge; it is that he accepts correction from knowledge.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:14 and 2:75 on public profession, private contradiction, and the corruption of revealed knowledge.
- Qur’an 2:77, 3:29, and 64:4 on Allah’s knowledge of what people conceal and reveal.
- Qur’an 3:78 and 5:13 on twisting scripture and breaking the covenant.
- Qur’an 2:159 and 3:187 on concealing clear guidance after it has been revealed.
- Qur’an 3:16 and 59:18 on seeking forgiveness and preparing for the accountability of the Hereafter.
- For the historical and rhetorical context of the private conversation, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:76.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:77

> Do they not know that Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal?

**Commentary:**

### No Secret Escapes Divine Knowledge

The verse asks whether those who conceal and reveal selectively do not know that Allah is aware of both. Their strategy depends upon a limited audience. They imagine that public speech can be managed, private conversations can remain hidden, and religious knowledge can be controlled. Allah dismantles that illusion by affirming knowledge of both sides: what is declared before people and what is whispered when people are absent.

Allah’s knowledge is not like human observation. People learn through time, evidence, and testimony, and even the most careful investigation can miss a hidden motive or event. Allah knows without acquiring information. He knows the outward act, inward intention, causes, consequences, and every detail that human beings forget. “He knows the secret and what is even more hidden” (20:7). Nothing in the heart is outside His awareness.

This truth is particularly relevant to hypocrisy because hypocrisy is built on the separation of the public and private self. The hypocrite can deceive people precisely because people judge by what is visible. Allah’s judgment does not have that limitation. He knows whether a declaration of faith is sincere, whether a charity is offered for His sake, and whether a public defence of truth conceals private contempt.

### Knowledge Is a Warning and a Comfort

Allah’s complete knowledge is a warning to the dishonest and a comfort to the sincere. The person who fabricates, conceals, or betrays cannot assume that a successful public performance has erased the deed. The person who prays in secret, gives charity anonymously, resists temptation unseen, or suffers an injustice that no human recognizes can be certain that Allah has not missed it.

The Qur’an says that Allah knows the stealthy glance and what hearts conceal (40:19), and that even the weight of an atom of good or evil will be brought forth (99:7–8). This gives moral significance to small acts. The hidden apology, the private refusal of corruption, and the unpublicized act of generosity are all known to Allah, even when no human reward follows.

### Human Judgment and Divine Knowledge

The verse does not permit people to claim that they know the hidden motives of others. Allah’s knowledge is perfect; human beings are commanded to judge by reliable evidence and outward conduct. The Prophet (ﷺ) dealt with people according to what they manifested and left their inward realities to Allah. The Qur’an forbids suspicion, spying, and backbiting (49:12), while commanding verification of reports (49:6).

This balance is necessary in families, institutions, and public life. We must not ignore clear evidence of deceit simply because we cannot see the heart, but we must also not transform guesses into verdicts. Divine omniscience should produce justice and humility, not paranoia. The believer says, in effect, “Allah knows the hidden; I will fulfil my duty with the evidence available to me.”

### The Futility of Concealment

The private conversation in the preceding verse was aimed at preventing the believers from learning what the hypocrites knew. But even if every human witness had been silenced, the knowledge would remain before Allah. Concealment can alter a worldly reputation; it cannot alter the divine record. On the Day when secrets are exposed, the difference between the public story and the private truth will become clear (86:9).

This should change a person’s use of privacy. Privacy is a legitimate human need, but it is not a moral vacuum. The believer uses solitude for worship, reflection, family intimacy, and lawful rest—not as a place where Allah is imagined to be absent. The highest sincerity is consistency before the public and in private, because the true audience is Allah.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:33, 3:29, 20:7, and 67:13 on Allah’s knowledge of the hidden and the revealed.
- Qur’an 40:19 and 86:9 on concealed motives and the exposure of secrets.
- Qur’an 49:6 and 49:12 on verifying reports and avoiding suspicion, spying, and backbiting.
- Qur’an 99:7–8 on every hidden good and evil being brought to account.
- Qur’an 4:58 and 5:8 on making human judgments with justice despite limited knowledge.
- For the connection between divine knowledge and the hypocrisy described in 2:76, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 2:77.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:78

> And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate.

**Commentary:**

### Illiteracy Is Not a Moral Verdict

The verse mentions *ummiyyūn*—people described as unlettered or lacking knowledge of scripture. It does not condemn every person who cannot read or write, nor does it equate literacy with intelligence or righteousness. The Qur’an describes the Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) as *al-nabī al-ummī* in the sense that he was not trained in the reading of earlier written scriptures, yet he brought a revelation of unmatched guidance (7:157–158). The problem here is not a physical inability to read; it is a community of people who possess no reliable knowledge of the Book and yet claim religious certainty.

These people know “nothing about the Scripture except wishful tales”—*amāniyy*. The word can carry the meaning of false hopes, recitations, or hearsay. They have inherited statements about scripture but do not understand its guidance, commands, and warnings. Their religion is built on what they wish the Book would say, not what it actually says. They may repeat legends, promises of automatic forgiveness, or claims of communal superiority while remaining ignorant of repentance, justice, and obedience.

### Hearsay in Place of Knowledge

A person may be surrounded by religious language and still know little of revelation. He may quote slogans, repeat a popular preacher, or inherit a family narrative without checking whether it agrees with scripture. This is dangerous because ignorance can become confident. The person does not say, “I do not know”; he speculates and presents speculation as religion.

The Qur’an condemns following conjecture when it is offered without evidence (53:28), and it commands people to ask those who possess knowledge when they do not know (16:43). Responsible learning requires a reliable chain of teaching, attention to context, awareness of scholarly disagreement, and the humility to distinguish certainty from assumption. A person who does not know the Book should seek knowledge rather than fill the gap with desire.

This warning applies in the modern world to religious misinformation as well. Short clips, unattributed quotations, fabricated hadith, and decontextualized verses can create an illusion of scriptural knowledge. The speed of transmission does not turn an assertion into truth. The believer must verify what is attributed to Allah and His Messenger before building belief or action upon it.

### Wishful Religion

Wishful speculation is attractive because it removes the need for repentance and reform. A person may want to believe that Allah’s favour is guaranteed by ancestry, affiliation, or a verbal claim, even while he neglects justice and worship. The Qur’an later refutes the claim that the Fire will touch some people only for a few days and asks whether they have taken a covenant from Allah or are saying about Him what they do not know (2:80). Religious wishful thinking promises reward without responsibility.

The opposite error is despair. Some people invent frightening claims about Allah that contradict His mercy, while others promise unconditional forgiveness that contradicts His justice. Both are forms of speaking without knowledge. The Qur’an teaches hope and fear together: Allah forgives the repentant, rewards righteousness, and judges persistent rejection and injustice.

### The Duty to Make Knowledge Accessible

The verse also creates a responsibility for scholars and teachers. When people have little access to scripture, they become vulnerable to those who control religious information. The learned must teach clearly, honestly, and patiently rather than using complexity to preserve dependence. The Qur’an condemns concealing guidance and praises those who make truth clear to people (2:159; 3:187).

At the same time, ordinary believers must take responsibility for learning. They should not outsource every religious conviction to an influential voice without checking evidence. Asking a qualified scholar is not weakness; it is wisdom. Blind loyalty to an unqualified speaker, however, can turn hearsay into doctrine.

### Knowledge, Humility, and Action

The verse invites a healthier relationship with scripture. Read or listen with the intention of understanding, ask when uncertain, verify what is attributed to revelation, and allow knowledge to correct desire. The purpose is not to accumulate quotations but to become guided. A person who knows little but is humble and eager to learn is closer to guidance than one who knows many slogans and refuses correction.

The Children of Israel possessed a revealed Book, yet a segment of the community reduced it to wishful tales. Muslims possess the Qur’an and can repeat the same failure if recitation is separated from comprehension and obedience. The cure is sincere learning accompanied by practice.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:157–158 on the Prophet as *al-nabī al-ummī* and his universal mission.
- Qur’an 16:43 and 17:36 on asking people of knowledge and not following unsupported claims.
- Qur’an 53:28 on conjecture not replacing truth.
- Qur’an 2:79 and 2:159 on fabricating and concealing scripture.
- Qur’an 3:187 on the obligation to make revealed knowledge clear rather than hide it.
- For *ummiyyūn* and *amāniyy* and the distinction between illiteracy and moral ignorance, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:78.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:79

> So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, “This is from Allah”—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned.

**Commentary:**

### Fabricating Revelation

The verse issues a severe warning—*wayl*—to those who write a false claim and attribute it to Allah. Their offence has three parts: they produce or alter a text, falsely present it as divine, and seek worldly gain through the deception. This is not an honest interpretive disagreement or a mistaken translation. It is a deliberate manufacture of religious authority.

The phrase “with their own hands” emphasizes human agency and responsibility. They cannot blame an anonymous tradition, a careless copyist, or an inherited custom after deliberately producing the false statement. Writing can preserve truth, but it can also give a lie the appearance of permanence and authority. The written form does not make a claim sacred; its origin and content must still be tested.

The Qur’an elsewhere describes people who twist their tongues while reciting scripture so that listeners think their words are from the Book (3:78). Distortion can therefore occur through writing, speech, translation, selective quotation, or interpretation. The common element is misrepresentation: the teacher knows or should know that a claim is not from Allah but presents it as revelation.

### The Fleeting Gain

The gain may be money, influence, exemption from an obligation, protection of social status, or the ability to control a community. The Qur’an repeatedly calls such gain “little” or “fleeting” because no worldly benefit can compensate for corrupting people’s relationship with Allah. A fabricated ruling may enrich its author, but it can mislead generations, deprive people of rights, or make injustice appear sacred.

This warning does not mean every religious teacher who receives payment is corrupt. Lawful compensation for teaching, writing, or scholarly work is not the same as changing revelation for profit. The sin lies in manipulation: the person alters or invents religious content because the truth would cost him something. Honest scholarship may be paid; falsehood cannot be sanctified by a fee.

Modern forms of the same offence include fabricated hadith, false quotations attributed to the Qur’an, invented religious promises, and commercial messages that claim divine approval without evidence. A viral image or attractive booklet can spread a lie more widely than an ancient manuscript. The medium changes, but the moral responsibility remains.

### Threefold Woe

The verse repeats the warning: woe for what their hands have written and woe for what they have earned. The first relates to the fabricated text and the act of production; the second relates to the benefit, burden, and consequences acquired through it. A person is accountable for both the falsehood he creates and the advantage he takes from it.

The repetition conveys the seriousness of corrupting people’s understanding of Allah. A private lie may harm one person, but a false religious claim can shape worship, law, family relations, and public policy. Those who follow the lie may also share in the burden of those who misled them, while followers who sincerely lacked access to the truth are judged according to their circumstances. Allah’s accounting is exact.

### The Responsibility of the Reader

The verse also gives readers a duty. They should not accept every written claim merely because it is printed, shared by a respected person, or wrapped in religious vocabulary. The Qur’an commands verification, and the Sunnah established methods for distinguishing authentic reports from fabrications. A believer should consult qualified scholarship, compare claims with clear revelation, and avoid forwarding material whose source is uncertain.

This caution does not mean that ordinary people must become specialists in every discipline before learning religion. It means that they should be honest about what they know and do not know. Saying “Allah says” is a serious act. It should not be used to strengthen an argument when the speaker has only heard an unattributed claim.

### Revelation Must Not Be Bent to Desire

The underlying temptation is to make Allah’s authority serve human desire. If a command is inconvenient, a person invents an exception; if a group wants superiority, it fabricates a promise; if a leader wants obedience, he attributes his policy to revelation. The Qur’an reverses the direction: human beings must submit their desires to Allah, not recruit Allah’s name to bless their desires.

The believer therefore approaches scripture with reverence and precision. He distinguishes revelation from commentary, authenticated report from weak report, and established interpretation from personal reflection. He accepts correction when evidence proves that he spoke wrongly. Such humility protects the heart from the woe described in this verse.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:42 and 3:78 on mixing truth with falsehood and twisting scripture through speech.
- Qur’an 2:174 and 3:187 on concealing or trading revelation for worldly gain.
- Qur’an 16:116 on not falsely declaring things lawful or unlawful in Allah’s name.
- Qur’an 17:36 and 49:6 on verification and not following unsupported claims.
- Qur’an 5:44 and 5:48 on the responsibility of those entrusted with scripture and the Qur’an as a criterion.
- For *wayl*, fabrication, and the double accountability for what is written and earned, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:79.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:80

> ˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?”

**Commentary:**

### False Security Before Judgment

The verse confronts a claim made by some Jews that the Fire would touch them only for a limited number of days. The Qur’an does not attribute this claim to every Jew, nor does it erase the distinction between sincere People of the Book and those who made false religious assertions. The wording “some of” is important. Divine criticism is specific and just; it does not permit people to convert a critique of a group’s claim into contempt for an entire people.

The claim expresses a form of religious privilege: even if we are punished, the punishment will be brief and guaranteed to end. It is not based on repentance, faith, or reform, but on an assumed status before Allah. The Qur’an exposes this as wishful religion. Earlier verses condemned people who knew little of scripture except false hopes (2:78), and later verses will refute the claim that only Jews or Christians will enter Paradise (2:111).

The precise number of days is not necessary to the central lesson. Classical reports connected the claim with the period of calf-worship or with a limited punishment imagined by some members of the community. What matters is the presumption that a person can guarantee his own salvation without a covenant from Allah and without obedience.

### “Have You Taken a Pledge from Allah?”

The Prophet is instructed to ask whether they possess a pledge from Allah. A genuine promise from Allah would be true because Allah never breaks His word (3:9; 30:6). But a promise must actually come from Allah; it cannot be created by ancestry, wishful thinking, or a fabricated scripture. The challenge is therefore precise: produce the divine covenant that guarantees your claim.

This question also establishes a method for religious reasoning. When someone makes a claim about Allah’s forgiveness, punishment, law, or promise, he must provide evidence. “Allah will certainly forgive us,” “Allah has made this unlawful,” or “our group is safe regardless of conduct” are not harmless statements if they have no revealed basis. The Qur’an repeatedly asks, “Have you a knowledge, then produce it for us?” (6:148).

Allah’s promise is never broken, but humans often confuse His mercy with a promise He has not made. Allah promises forgiveness to the repentant, reward to believers who do good, and justice for those who persist in rejection. He does not promise automatic safety to a person who claims a religious identity while denying the obligations attached to it.

### Speaking About Allah Without Knowledge

The alternative in the verse is that they are saying about Allah what they do not know. This is a grave matter because religious claims direct worship and shape people’s expectations of judgment. A false promise can make a sinner complacent, while a false threat can drive a sincere person to despair. Both misrepresent Allah.

The Qur’an lists speaking about Allah without knowledge among the gravest forms of wrongdoing (7:33). It commands people not to follow conjecture and warns that assumptions cannot replace truth (53:28). Responsible scholarship therefore distinguishes between an explicit promise in revelation, a valid inference, a disputed interpretation, and a personal hope. Certainty belongs only where evidence warrants it.

### Hope Must Be Joined to Responsibility

Islam does not teach salvation through despair. Allah’s mercy is vast, and sincere repentance can erase even grave sins. But hope is not the same as guaranteed exemption. The believer hopes in Allah’s forgiveness while fearing that his actions may displease Him. This balance motivates repentance, restitution, and good works.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that a person should not rely upon lineage or status while neglecting obedience. Fatimah, his daughter, was told to save herself from the Fire because he could not avail her against Allah independently (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The statement honours personal responsibility while not denying the intercession Allah may grant by His permission.

The verse also warns communities that use history as a shield. A people may have received scripture, prophets, and past favours, but those blessings increase responsibility. The question is not merely, “Who were our ancestors?” It is, “Do we believe, obey, repent, and fulfil the covenant now?”

### A Warning for Every Religious Community

The Qur’an’s critique applies to Muslims as well. It is possible to recite promises of Paradise while neglecting prayer, justice, repentance, and the rights of people. It is possible to imagine that belonging to a nation, school, family, or movement guarantees safety. The Qur’an repeatedly rejects such complacency and makes success depend upon sincere faith and righteous action.

The proper response is to speak about Allah with reverence and evidence. We may hope for His mercy, ask for forgiveness, and rely upon His promises, but we must not manufacture a guarantee. The One who never breaks His word should be obeyed rather than quoted selectively to justify disobedience.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:62, 2:111–112, and 4:123 on salvation, religious labels, and the necessity of faith and righteous action.
- Qur’an 3:9 and 30:6 on Allah’s promise never being broken.
- Qur’an 6:148 and 7:33 on demanding evidence and the prohibition of speaking about Allah without knowledge.
- Qur’an 53:28 on conjecture not replacing truth.
- Qur’an 2:78 and 3:24 on false hopes concerning scripture and punishment.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the Prophet’s warning to his family that lineage alone cannot save anyone.
