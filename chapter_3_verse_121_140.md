## Verse 121

Verse 121 says: *˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you left your home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.*

In simple terms, the verse opens the account of the battle of Uhud: the Prophet left his home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield, and Allah is All-Hearing and All-Knowing.

The verse begins the great narrative section of the surah — the story of Uhud and its lessons, which runs with some interruptions to the end of the third main section. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "The Battle of Uhud" and states the consensus: "According to the majority of scholars, these Ayat are describing the battle of Uhud, as Ibn 'Abbas, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, As-Suddi and others said." Al-Jalalayn: "the Prophet set out with 1000 or 950 men while the idolaters numbered 3000... He had his back and that of his troops to Uhud." The verse's *ghadawta* — you left early — captures the morning departure and the careful disposition of the troops.

"when you left your home in the early morning" — *wa idh ghadawta min ahlika* — and when you went forth at dawn from your family. The *ghuduww* — the setting out in the early morning — is the time of the departure: the Prophet left his household in Madinah at dawn to meet the enemy. Al-Jalalayn: "when you went forth at dawn from your family at Medina." The *ahl* — the family, the household — is the domestic setting of the departure: the Prophet left his home, the one who would be trusted with the command, going out to the battlefield. The verse's opening with the dawn is the setting of the campaign: the *ghuduw* — the early start — is not the idle detail but the disposition of the commander who leaves the city to meet the enemy.

"to position the believers in the battlefield" — *tubawwi'u al-mu'minina maqa'ida lil-qital* — assigning the believers their positions for the fighting. The *tabwi'* — the assigning, the settling — is the disposition: the Prophet positioned the believers, each in his station, for the battle. Al-Jalalayn: "to assign the believers to have them occupy their places, stations for them to stand at for battle." The *maqa'id* — the places, the stations — is the military order: the ranks, the flanks, the archers — the arrangement of the seven hundred. Ibn Kathir: the Prophet "arranged their lines and placed a group of archers under the command of 'Abdullah bin Jubayr at the foot of the mountain and said to them, 'Defend us with your arrows in case they come up from behind us and remain here whether we are being defeated or on the verge of victory.'"

The verse sets the whole background of the battle of Uhud. Ibn Kathir recounts the occasion: after Badr, the caravan of Abu Sufyan returned safely to Makkah, and the Makkan leaders and the children of those killed at Badr demanded from him, "Spend this money on fighting Muhammad!" They spent the caravan money on warfare, mobilized their forces including the Ahabish tribes, and gathered three thousand soldiers, marching until they camped near Uhud facing Madinah. The Prophet consulted the Muslims: should they march out to meet the disbelievers or fortify themselves in Madinah? Ibn Ubayy advised staying in the city; those who had missed Badr urged going out. The Prophet went to his home, put on his shield, and came out — and when the companions hesitated, he said, "It is not for a Prophet to wear his shield for war then lay down his arms before Allah decides in his favor."

"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — *wa Allahu sami'un 'alim* — and Allah is Hearing, Knowing. The two names are the seal of the verse: the *Sami'* — the All-Hearing — hears the words of the believers and the enemies; the *'Alim* — the All-Knowing — knows the circumstances of the fight, the intentions of the hearts, and the disposition of the battle. Al-Jalalayn: "God hears what you say, knows your circumstances." The verse's closing is the surah's recurring signature: the commander's morning departure and the divine Hearing and Knowing — the human plan and the divine awareness that encompasses it.

For the reader, the verse is the historical and doctrinal frame for the whole Uhud account. The believer is taught: the campaign of Uhud begins with the arrangement — the Prophet leaving his home at dawn, positioning the believers, stationing the archers; and the account is framed by the divine Hearing and Knowing — no word of the battlefield, no intention of the heart, no detail of the arrangement is outside the divine awareness. The verse's practical lesson: the *tabwi'* — the disciplined positioning — is the obedience that prepares for the fight; the *maqa'id* — the stations — are the assignments each believer holds; and the *Sami'* and the *'Alim* are the guarantee that the campaign is not the blind history but the recounting of the One who heard and knew everything.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 122

Verse 122 says: *˹Remember˺ when two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower, then Allah reassured them. So in Allah let the believers put their trust.*

In simple terms, the verse records the moment of the wavering: two groups among the believers were about to lose courage, then Allah reassured them — so in Allah let the believers put all their trust.

The verse is the first of the lessons of Uhud within the account: the moment when the ranks nearly faltered and the divine reassurance. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn agree on the identity of the two groups: the Banu Salimah and the Banu Harithah — the two flanks of the army. Al-Jalalayn: "two parties of you, the Banu Salima and the Banu Haritha, the two flanks of the army, were about to lose heart, about to shrink from the battle and retreat after 'Abdullah bin Ubayy the hypocrite and his companions began to retreat." The verse's *hamma* — were about to, intended — is the near-miss: the intention of the retreat, the wavering of the courage, before the divine reassurance.

"two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower" — *idh hammat ta'ifatani minkum an tafshala* — when two parties of you were about to lose heart. The *fashal* — the losing heart, the cowardice, the slackening — is the temptation of the retreat: the two groups saw the withdrawal of Ibn Ubayy and his companions, heard the whisper "why should we get ourselves and our children killed?", and were about to shrink from the battle. Al-Jalalayn: Ibn Ubayy "said, 'Why should we get ourselves and our children killed?' and he also said to Abu Jabir al-Salami — who had said to him 'I implore you by God for your Prophet's sake and yours' — 'If we knew how to fight we would follow you!'" The verse's *hamma* — intended — is both the reality of the temptation and the precision of the record: the intention was there, the retreat was not carried out — the *hamma* of the near-failure and the *thabba* of the reassurance.

"then Allah reassured them" — *wa Allahu waliyyuhuma* — and Allah was their Protector, their Wali. The *wila'ah* — the protection — is the divine support: the two groups were not abandoned; Allah was their Wali. Al-Jalalayn: "God then made them — the Banu Salima and the Banu Haritha — steadfast and they did not abandon the field; and God was their Protector, their Helper." The verse's *waliyyuhuma* — their Wali — is the exact form: the *Wali* of the two groups, the Protector of the waverers — Allah, whose protection made the intention of the retreat dissolve into the steadiness of the stay. The *math'nn'a* — the reassurance — is the work of the divine: the hearts that had nearly cowered were steadied, the ranks that had nearly broken held.

"So in Allah let the believers put their trust" — *wa 'ala Allahi falyatawakkal al-mu'minun* — and upon Allah let the believers rely. The *tawakkul* — the reliance, the trust — is the command: the believers put their trust in Allah, not in the numbers, not in the flanks, not in the strength. Al-Jalalayn: "let the believers rely on God, let them place their trust in Him and none other." The verse's *falyatawakkal* — let them rely — is the imperative of the confidence: the reassurance of the two groups and the tawakkul of all believers — the one who trusts is the one who does not cower.

The verse is also the mirror of the surah's earlier command of trust (3:159 — "once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah") and the promise of the believers' confidence (3:160). Ibn Kathir frames the whole Uhud account with the lessons of the ranks: the *tabwi'* of 121, the near-cowering of 122, and the reassurance through the divine Wali. The details of the history: the army that marched with a thousand, the withdrawal of Ibn Ubayy with a third at the Shawt area, the seven hundred who remained — the *ta'ifatan* of 122 are within the seven hundred, the two tribes who wavered and held.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's honesty about the human heart and its teaching of the divine trust. The believer is taught: the ranks of the faithful can waver — the *fashal* is the real temptation, the intention of the retreat can visit even the believers; and the reassurance is the divine — Allah was the Wali, and the steadiness that followed was the work of the protection. The verse's *falyatawakkal* — let them rely — is the program: the believers of every rank, every battle, every difficulty are to place the trust in Allah — the one who is the Wali of the wavering and the strength of the cowering. The verse is the surah's first lesson of the battle: the protection before the victory, the trust before the fight, and the Wali of the two tribes of the rank.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 123

Verse 123 says: *Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered. So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful.*

In simple terms, the verse reminds the believers of the earlier victory: Allah granted them victory at Badr when they were vastly outnumbered — so be mindful of Allah, so that you may be grateful.

The verse turns from the near-failure to the earlier victory. Ibn Kathir: "And Allah has already made you victorious at Badr, when you were a weak little force. So have Taqwa of Allah that you may be grateful." The verse's *wa laqad nasarakum Allahu bi-Badr* — and Allah has certainly helped you at Badr — is the record of the divine aid; *wa antum adhillah* — while you were weak, humiliated — is the condition of the victors; and the *ittaqu* — the Taqwa — is the command of the gratitude.

"Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered" — *wa laqad nasarakum Allahu bi-Badrin wa antum adhillah* — and Allah has certainly helped you at Badr while you were weak, humiliated. The *nusr* — the victory, the help — is the divine support at Badr: the battle of Ramadan, year 2, when the believers, three hundred and thirteen, met the thousand-strong army of the Quraysh. Ibn Kathir's account: the Muslims of Badr were the *adhillah* — the weak, the few, the humiliated — the force that had been driven from Makkah, the refugees of the persecution; and the victory was the divine. Al-Jalalayn: "the Muslims received the news that Kurz bin Jabir was aiding the idolators, and this news was hard on them, so Allah revealed" — the promise of the angels of 3:124. The *adhillah* — the weak — is the point of the verse: the victory was not of the numbers, the equipment, the strength; it was of the divine help to the weak.

"So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful" — *fattaqu Allaha la'allakum tashkurun* — so fear Allah, so that you might be grateful. The *taqwa* — the mindfulness, the consciousness — is the command after the victory: the believers who were victorious at Badr are to be mindful of Allah — the memory of the victory is the cause of the gratitude. Ibn Kathir: "Allah also commands His servants to have Taqwa of Him so that they may achieve success in this life and the Hereafter." The *la'alla* — perhaps, so that — is the purpose: the Taqwa leads to the gratitude; the gratitude is the response to the favor.

The verse's position: the victory of Badr is the background of the Uhud account and the standard of the believers' confidence. The believers who nearly cowered at Uhud (122) are reminded of the victory of Badr: the one who gave the victory to the weak at Badr is the one who can give the victory at Uhud; the *nusr* of the past is the assurance of the future. Ibn Kathir's frame: the *adhillah* of Badr — the weak little force — and the *nusr* — the divine help — are the pattern: the believers who are weak in the ranks of the world and strong in the trust of Allah.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's law of the victory and the gratitude. The believer is taught: the divine help is the decisive factor — the *nusr* of the weak at Badr, the *adhillah* who were victorious; and the response is the Taqwa — the mindfulness of the One who gave the victory, without which the favor is forgotten. The verse's *la'allakum tashkurun* — so that you may be grateful — is the purpose of the reminder: the believers who remember the victory of the weak are the believers who are mindful and grateful; the *shukr* — the gratitude — is the state of the one who knows the favor's source. And the verse's *adhillah* — the weak — is the believer's confidence: the weakness of the world is not the obstacle of the victory; the *nusr* of Allah is the strength.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 124

Verse 124 says: *˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you said to the believers, “Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?”*

In simple terms, the verse recalls the Prophet's reassurance at the crisis: he said to the believers, "Is it not enough that your Lord will send down three thousand angels to reinforce you?"

The verse records the Prophet's promise of the angels. Ibn Kathir: "'Is it not enough for you that your Lord should help you with three thousand angels sent down?'" The verse's *idh taqulu lil-mu'minin* — when you said to the believers — is the speech of the reassurance; the *alan yakfiyakum* — is it not sufficient for you — is the rhetorical question of the sufficiency; and the *thalathat alaf min al-mala'ikah munzalin* — three thousand angels sent down — is the promise.

"when you said to the believers, "Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?"" — *idh taqulu lil-mu'minina alan yakfiyakum an yumiddakum rabbukum bi thalathati alafin mina al-mala'ikati munzalina* — when you said to the believers, "Is it not sufficient for you that your Lord should reinforce you with three thousand angels sent down?" The *madd* — the reinforcement, the support — is the aid of the angels; the *munzalin* — sent down — is the descent of the reinforcement. Al-Jalalayn: the verse is "an adverbial qualifier of *nasarakum* 'He gave you victory' in the previous verse" — the speech of the Prophet to the believers as the reassurance "for them." The *alan yakfiyakum* — will it not suffice you — is the question of the sufficiency: the three thousand angels are enough; the reassurance of the ranks is the divine reinforcement.

The promise is the subject of the scholars' discussion recorded by Ibn Kathir: whether the three thousand (and the five thousand of 125) refer to Badr or Uhud. View one (Al-Hasan Al-Basri, Ash-Sha'bi, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Ibn Jarir): the promise belongs to Badr — the Muslims were distressed by the news that Kurz bin Jabir was aiding the idolaters, so Allah revealed the promise; the news of the defeat reached Kurz, he did not reinforce the enemy, and thus the five thousand were not sent. Ar-Rabi': Allah supported the Muslims with one thousand (8:9), then the number reached three thousand, then five thousand — the *murdifin* — "each behind the other, following one another in succession" — of 8:9 indicates that thousands follow one another. View two: the promise belongs to Uhud — but the angels did not come to the aid of the Muslims at Uhud, because the condition of patience and Taqwa (125) was not met: the Muslims were not patient; they ran away and received no angel. Ibn Kathir concludes about 8:9 and 3:124: "the two Ayat above are similar in meaning and it appears that they both were about the battle of Badr, because the angels did fight in the battle of Badr, as the evidence indicates. Allah knows best."

The verse's reassurance: the Prophet's speech to the believers at the crisis of the ranks. The believers who were distressed by the numbers, the news, the withdrawal of the hypocrites — the Prophet's answer is the question of the sufficiency: is not the Lord's reinforcement enough? Al-Jalalayn: "you were saying to the believers, promising them as reassurance for them." The verse's *yalan yakfiyakum* — will it not suffice — is the teaching of the sufficiency: the divine aid is sufficient — the three thousand are the sufficiency, and the believers' confidence is not the numbers of the world but the reinforcement of the Lord.

For the reader, the verse is the record of the prophetic reassurance and the promise of the unseen aid. The believer is taught: the crisis of the ranks is met by the promise of the angels — the Prophet's speech discloses the unseen support, the reinforcement of the Lord. The verse's *alan yakfiyakum* — is it not enough — is the question of the sufficiency: the believer who trusts the divine reinforcement does not fear the numbers of the enemy; the *madd* of the Lord is the sufficiency. And the verse's scholarship is the honesty of the record: the promise was either the fulfilled aid at Badr or the conditioned promise at Uhud — either way, the divine reinforcement is the reality the believers' confidence rests upon. The verse teaches: the Prophet's reassurance is the method of the rank — the disclosure of the unseen support, the declaration of the sufficiency, the turning of the distressed hearts to the reinforcement of the Lord.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 125

Verse 125 says: *Most certainly, if you ˹believers˺ are firm and mindful ˹of Allah˺ and the enemy launches a sudden attack on you, Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated ˹for battle˺.*

In simple terms, the verse states the condition and the promise: if the believers remain firm and mindful of Allah, and the enemy launches a sudden attack, Allah will reinforce them with five thousand designated angels.

The verse states the conditional promise of the reinforcement. Ibn Kathir: "'But, if you hold on to patience and have Taqwa, and the enemy comes rushing at you; your Lord will help you with five thousand angels having marks (of distinction).'" The verse's *bala* — most certainly, yes — is the emphatic answer to the question of 124; the *in tasbiru wa tattaqu* — if you are patient and mindful — is the condition; the *khamsati alafin mina al-mala'ikati musawwimin* — five thousand angels marked — is the promise.

"Most certainly, if you ˹believers˺ are firm and mindful ˹of Allah˺" — *bala in tasbiru wa tattaqu* — yes, if you hold on to patience and have Taqwa. The *bala* — yes, most certainly — is the emphatic of the affirmation: the question of the sufficiency (124) is answered with the promise of the increase. The *sabr* — the patience, the firmness — and the *taqwa* — the mindfulness — are the two conditions: the patience in the fight, the mindfulness of Allah, the obedience of the command. Ibn Kathir: "means, if you observe patience while fighting the enemy, all the while fearing Me and obeying My command." The *sabr* of the battle — the holding of the rank, the endurance of the fight — and the *taqwa* of the heart — the consciousness of Allah — are the conditions of the reinforcement.

"and the enemy launches a sudden attack on you" — *wa ya'tukum min fawrihim hadha* — and they come to you from their haste, from this moment, rushing upon you. The *fawr* — the suddenness, the immediate — is the attack of the enemy: the surprise assault, the rush of the battle. Ibn Kathir (Al-Hasan, Qatadah, Ar-Rabi', As-Suddi): "they will come rushing" — instantaneously; Al-'Awfi from Ibn 'Abbas: "all at once"; another reading: before their anger subsides against the disbelievers. The verse's *min fawrihim* — from their haste — is the suddenness of the onset: the enemy launches the attack, the believers hold the rank.

"Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated ˹for battle˺" — *yumdidkum rabbukum bi khamsati alafin mina al-mala'ikati musawwimin* — your Lord will reinforce you with five thousand angels marked. The *madd* — the reinforcement — and the *musawwimin* — the marked, the designated — are the promise: the five thousand angels marked with the signs of the battle. Ibn Kathir: "Abu Ishaq As-Subay'i said from Harithah bin Mudarrib from 'Ali bin Abi Talib: 'The angels were distinguished by wearing white wool at Badr.' The angels also had special markings distinguishing their horses." The *musawwimin* — the marked — is the designation: the angels of the reinforcement, distinguished, designated for the battle.

The verse's place in the two views: if the promise refers to Badr, the condition was met and the believers received the aid (with 8:9's thousand, the three thousand, the five thousand); if it refers to Uhud, the condition was not met — the believers fled, and the reinforcement did not come. Ibn Kathir: "the angels did not come to the aid of Muslims at Uhud, because Allah made it conditional... The Muslims were not patient at Uhud. Rather, they ran away and, consequently, did not receive the support of even one angel." The verse is the promise and the condition: the *bala* of the sufficiency, the *sabr* and the *taqwa* of the condition, and the *musawwimin* of the reinforcement.

For the reader, the verse is the teaching of the conditioned aid. The believer is taught: the divine reinforcement is real — the five thousand designated angels, the promise of the Lord; and the reinforcement is conditioned — the *sabr* and the *taqwa* are the conditions: the patience of the ranks and the mindfulness of the heart. The verse's *bala* — yes — is the emphatic of the divine promise; the *in* — if — is the condition of the human response; the *musawwimin* — the designated — is the chosen reinforcement. The believer's lesson from Uhud: the *sabr* and the *taqwa* are not the passive endurance but the active conditions of the aid — the believers who hold the rank and mind Allah are the believers whom Allah reinforces; and the believers who run away lose the condition and the promise. The verse teaches: the divine support is given on the condition of the firmness and the mindfulness — the *sabr* and the *taqwa* of the believer are the channel of the reinforcement.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 126

Verse 126 says: *Allah ordained this ˹reinforcement˺ only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts. And victory comes only from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise—*

In simple terms, the verse states the purpose of the promised reinforcement: it was only good news for the believers and reassurance for their hearts — and victory comes only from Allah, the Almighty, the All-Wise.

The verse qualifies the promise of the angels. Ibn Kathir: "Allah made it not but as a message of good news for you and as an assurance to your hearts. And there is no victory except from Allah, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise." The verse's *wa ma ja'alahu Allahu illa bishra lakum* — and Allah made it not except as good news to you — is the limitation of the purpose; the *wa litatma'inna qulubukum bihi* — and so that your hearts might be at rest with it — is the intention; and the *wa ma al-nasru illa min 'indi Allahi al-'Azizi al-Hakimi* — and victory is not except from Allah, the Almighty, the All-Wise — is the doctrine.

"Allah ordained this ˹reinforcement˺ only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts" — *wa ma ja'alahu Allahu illa bishra lakum wa litatma'inna qulubukum bihi* — and Allah made it not except as good tidings to you, and so that your hearts might be reassured by it. The *bushra* — the good news, the glad tidings — is the function of the announcement: the news of the angels was not the military necessity but the *bushra* — the glad tidings. Al-Jalalayn: "as a good tiding to you of victory and that your hearts might be at peace, that they might be at rest and not be terrified by the large number of the enemy as compared to your small number." The *tuma'ninah* — the reassurance, the rest — is the purpose: the hearts of the believers, distressed at the crisis of the ranks, were to be at rest — not terrorized by the three thousand of the enemy, not terrified by the numbers of the world.

"And victory comes only from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise—" — *wa ma al-nasru illa min 'indi Allahi al-'Azizi al-Hakimi* — and victory is not except from Allah, the Almighty, the All-Wise. The *nusr* — the victory — is the exclusive: the *illa* — except — the victory only from Allah. Ibn Kathir: "Allah is the Almighty Whose power can never be undermined, and He has the perfect wisdom in His decrees and in all His decisions." The *'Aziz* — the Almighty — is the power that cannot be undermined; the *Hakim* — the All-Wise — is the wisdom of the decrees. The verse's *ma al-nasr illa* — victory is not but — is the doctrine of the surah: the victory is not of the numbers, the arrangement, the archers — the victory is the divine; the believers' battle is the obedience and the divine victory is the result. Ibn Kathir: "The Ayah means, 'Allah sent down angels and told you about their descent to encourage you and to comfort and reassure your hearts. You should know that victory only comes from Allah and that if He willed, He would have defeated your enemy without you having to fight them'" (47:4-6).

The verse's position: the announcement of the angels (124–125) is followed by the qualification of 126 — the reinforcement was the *bushra* and the *tuma'ninah*, not the cause; the *nusr* is the exclusive divine. The believers who were distressed at the crisis of the ranks are taught: the good news is for the hearts, and the victory is from Allah — the *'Aziz* who cannot be undermined and the *Hakim* who decrees with the wisdom.

For the reader, the verse is the doctrine of the victory's source and the reassurance's purpose. The believer is taught: the news of the unseen support is the *bushra* — the glad tidings — and the *tuma'ninah* — the reassurance — of the hearts; and the victory is the exclusive divine — the *illa* of the *nusr*. The verse's *'Aziz* — the Almighty — is the believer's confidence: the power that cannot be undermined; the *Hakim* — the All-Wise — is the believer's trust: the wisdom of the decrees. And the verse is the lesson of Uhud: the believers who were distressed by the numbers of the enemy are taught the *tuma'ninah* — the rest of the hearts — and the *nusr* — the victory from Allah. The *bushra* is not the cause of the victory but the glad tidings of it; the *tuma'ninah* is not the military strategy but the divine reassurance; and the *nusr* is not the human but the divine — the *'Aziz*, the *Hakim*.

## Verse 127

Verse 127 says: *to destroy a group of the disbelievers and humble the rest, causing them to withdraw in disappointment.*

In simple terms, the verse states one purpose of the fighting: to destroy a group of the disbelievers and humiliate the rest, so that they withdraw in disappointment.

The verse gives the purpose of the Jihad against the disbelievers. Ibn Kathir: "That He might cut off a part of those who disbelieve, or expose them to infamy, so that they retire frustrated." The verse's *liyaqta'a tarafan mina alladhina kafaru* — that He might cut off a part of those who disbelieve — is the first purpose; the *aw yakbitahum* — or expose them to infamy, humble them — is the second; and the *fayanqalibu kha'ibin* — so that they retire frustrated — is the result.

"to destroy a group of the disbelievers" — *liyaqta'a tarafan mina alladhina kafaru* — that He might cut off a part of those who disbelieve. The *qat'* — the cutting off, the destroying — is the annihilation of a portion: the *taraf* — the part, the side, the edge — is a group of the disbelievers. Ibn Kathir: "meaning, to cause a part of a nation to perish." The verse's *liyaqta'* — that He might cut off — is the purpose clause of the Jihad: out of His wisdom, Allah commands the believers to fight — the fight destroys a part of the disbelieving nation; the *taraf* — the portion — is the exactness of the divine: the Jihad cuts off the head of the aggression, the part, not the whole — the believers' fighting is the cutting of the part.

"and humble the rest" — *aw yakbitahum* — or expose them to infamy, humble them, disgrace them. The *kabt* — the humbling, the infamy, the disgrace — is the second purpose: the disbelievers who are not destroyed are humiliated, disgraced. Ibn Kathir: "by disgracing them and forcing them to return with only their rage, having failed in their aim to harm you." The *kabt* — the humbling — is the exposure: the disbelievers who escaped the destruction were humbled — the disgrace of the defeat, the infamy of the rout.

"causing them to withdraw in disappointment" — *fayanqalibu kha'ibin* — so that they turn back frustrated, disappointed. The *inqilab* — the turning back, the withdrawal — is the retreat; the *kha'ib* — the frustrated, the disappointed, the failed — is the state of the withdrawal. Ibn Kathir: "they retire... frustrated without achieving their aims." The verse's *kha'ibin* — disappointed — is the failure: the disbelievers withdraw — achieving no aim, gaining no victory, failing in their harm against the believers. The verse's purposes: the *qat'* — the cutting — and the *kabt* — the humbling — and the *kha'ib* — the disappointed withdrawal — are the three purposes of the divine command of the Jihad: the destruction of the part, the humiliation of the rest, the frustrated retreat.

The verse's context: the purposes of the fight (127) are the divine wisdom — the *liyaqta'* and the *aw yakbitahum* — the command of the Jihad, not the human aggression. Ibn Kathir: "out of His wisdom, He commands you to perform Jihad and to fight. Allah then mentions the various consequences of performing Jihad against the disbelievers." The verse's purposes — the *qat'* of the part, the *kabt* of the rest, the *kha'ib* of the withdrawal — are the wisdom of the fighting: the believers who were commanded to fight at Uhud (121–125) are taught the purposes of their fighting.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's statement of the purposes of the Jihad. The believer is taught: the fighting is not the aimless aggression but the divine command with the divine purposes — the *qat'* — the cutting off of the part of the disbelieving nation; the *kabt* — the humiliation of the rest; the *kha'ib* — the disappointed withdrawal. The verse's *liyaqta'* — that He might cut off — is the purpose; the *taraf* — the part — is the exactness of the command: the fight against the aggression, the cutting of the hostile part, not the lust of the slaughter. And the verse is the believers' honor: the disbelievers who fought the believers at Uhud withdrew *kha'ibin* — disappointed, failing — and the believers, despite the wound of Uhud, fought the command of the divine purposes.

## Verse 128

Verse 128 says: *You ˹O Prophet˺ have no say in the matter. It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them, for indeed they are wrongdoers.*

In simple terms, the verse corrects the Prophet's own grief at Uhud: the matter is not yours to decide — it is Allah's to forgive them or punish them, for they are indeed wrongdoers.

The verse is the correction of the Prophet's grief and the doctrine of the divine decision. Ibn Kathir: "Not for you is the decision — meaning, 'The matter is all in My Hand.'" The verse's *laysa laka mina al-amri shay'* — not for you is anything of the matter — is the limitation; the *aw yatuba 'alayhim* — or He turns to them in mercy — is the divine option; the *aw yu'adhdhibahum* — or He punishes them — is the second; and the *fa innahum zalimun* — for they are wrongdoers — is the verdict.

"You ˹O Prophet˺ have no say in the matter" — *laysa laka mina al-amri shay'* — not for you is anything of the matter, the decision. The *amr* — the matter, the decision — is the sovereignty: the decision over the disbelievers, the forgiveness or the punishment, is not the Prophet's; it is Allah's. Ibn Kathir cites the parallels: "your duty is only to convey (the Message) and on Us is the reckoning" (13:40); "Not upon you is their guidance, but Allah guides whom He wills" (2:272); "Verily, you guide not whom you like, but Allah guides whom He wills" (28:56). Muhammad bin Ishaq: "No part of the decision regarding My servants is yours, except what I command you." The verse's *laysa laka* — not for you — is the Prophet's honor and limitation: he is the messenger, the warner, the one who conveys; the decision over the guidance and the punishment is the divine.

"It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them" — *aw yatuba 'alayhim aw yu'adhdhibahum* — or He turns to them in mercy or He punishes them. The *tawbah* — the turning in mercy, the relenting — is the divine option: the forgiveness of the disbelievers through their acceptance of Islam; the *'adhab* — the punishment — is the second: the punishment of this life and the Hereafter for the disbelief. Al-Jalalayn: "until such time as He relents to them through their acceptance of Islam or chastises them." The verse's *aw* — or — is the divine alternatives: the mercy or the punishment — the two options in the Hand of Allah, not the decision of the Prophet.

"for indeed they are wrongdoers" — *fa innahum zalimun* — for they are indeed wrongdoers. The *zulm* — the wrong, the injustice — is the verdict: they are the *zalimun* — the wrongdoers — who deserve the fate. Ibn Kathir: "and thus, they deserve such a fate." The verse's *zalimun* — the wrongdoers — is the ethical verdict of the disbelievers: the *zulm* of the disbelief, the *zulm* of the harm, the *zulm* of the wrong — the wrongdoers who deserve the punishment or the mercy of the divine.

The occasion of the verse, per Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn: at Uhud, the Prophet was wounded — his head was injured, his front tooth broken — and he said, "How can a people achieve success after having injured their Prophet?" or "How does a people who have drenched the face of their Prophet in blood expect to prosper?" — and the verse was revealed: the matter is not the Prophet's; the decision is Allah's. The hadiths of the qunut: Salim bin 'Abdullah from his father — the Prophet, raising his head from bowing in the second unit of the Fajr prayer, cursed "so-and-so" — "O Allah! Curse Al-Harith bin Hisham, Suhayl bin 'Amr, Safwan bin Umayyah" — and Allah revealed the verse; "All these persons were pardoned (after they embraced Islam later on)." Abu Hurayrah: the Prophet's qunut supplications — save Al-Walid bin Al-Walid, Salamah bin Hisham, 'Ayyash bin Abi Rabi'ah and the weak believers; be hard on Mudar — and at other times "O Allah! Curse so-and-so" — "Thereafter, Allah revealed: Not for you is the decision." Anas: the Prophet was injured at Uhud and said "How can a people achieve success after having injured their Prophet?" — then the verse was revealed.

For the reader, the verse is the doctrine of the divine decision and the correction of the human grief. The believer is taught: the matter — the guidance, the forgiveness, the punishment — is not the human decision; the *amr* is the divine; the Prophet's own grief at the wound of Uhud is corrected by the verse: the decision is Allah's. The verse's *laysa laka* — not for you — is the boundary of the human: no one decides the guidance and the punishment except Allah; the *tawbah* and the *'adhab* are the divine options. And the verse's *zalimun* — the wrongdoers — is the verdict: the *zulm* of the harm and the disbelief deserves the fate — the mercy or the punishment, both in the Hand of the divine. The believer's lesson: the grief at the wound, the anger at the enemy, the wish for the revenge — corrected by the *laysa laka*: the decision is Allah's; the believer's duty is the message, and the divine's is the decision.

## Verse 129

Verse 129 says: *To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.*

In simple terms, the verse states the sovereignty and the will: everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah alone; He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills — and He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse completes the doctrine of 128: the decision over the disbelievers belongs to the One to whom everything belongs. Ibn Kathir: "And to Allah belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is in the earth. He forgives whom He wills, and punishes whom He wills. And Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful." The verse's *wa lillahi ma fi al-samawati wa ma fi al-ard* — and to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth — is the ownership; the *yaghfiru liman yasha'* — He forgives whom He wills — is the mercy; the *wa yu'adhdhibu man yasha'* — and He punishes whom He wills — is the justice; and the *Ghafur* and the *Rahim* — the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful — are the names.

"To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth" — *wa lillahi ma fi al-samawati wa ma fi al-ard* — and to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth. The ownership of 129 is the same of 3:109: the *mulkiyyah* of the whole. Ibn Kathir: "they are all His servants and His property." The verse's *ma* — whatever — is the exhaustive: the creation, the creatures, the affairs — the whole of everything is His. The ownership is the basis of the decision: the One to whom everything belongs is the One who forgives and punishes; the *amr* of 128 and the *mulq* of 129 are the two sides of the sovereignty.

"He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills" — *yaghfiru liman yasha' wa yu'adhdhibu man yasha'* — He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. The *yaghfiru* — He forgives — is the mercy of the will; the *yu'adhdhibu* — He punishes — is the justice of the will; the *yasha'* — He wills — is the sovereignty. Ibn Kathir: connecting to 128 — the disbelievers of Uhud, the wrongdoers — "He forgives whom He wills, and punishes whom He wills." The *mash'iyyah* — the will — is the divine: the forgiveness and the punishment are the will of Allah; the will is not the arbitrary but the just: the forgiveness of the repenters and the punishment of the wrongdoers.

"And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — *wa Allahu Ghafurun Rahim* — and Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The *Ghafur* — the All-Forgiving, the Oft-Forgiving — and the *Rahim* — the Most Merciful — are the names of the verse's close: the One who forgives whom He wills is the Ghafur; the One who punishes whom He wills is the Rahim — the mercy that tempers the justice, the forgiveness that is the dominant of the names. The verse's *Ghafur* and *Rahim* — the forgiveness and the mercy — are the believers' confidence: the One who owns everything and decides everything is the Ghafur and the Rahim — the forgiveness and the mercy that lie behind the decision.

The verse's place: the doctrine of the decision (128: *laysa laka*) is followed by the doctrine of the sovereignty (129: *lillahi ma fi*). The *amr* of the Prophet's correction and the *mulq* of the divine — the matter is not yours because everything belongs to Him; the *yaghfiru* and the *yu'adhdhibu* — the forgiveness and the punishment — are the will of the Owner. And the *Ghafur* and the *Rahim* — the names of the close — are the answer to the possible despair: the One who decides is the One who forgives — the *Ghafur* — and the One who punishes is the One who is Merciful — the *Rahim*.

For the reader, the verse is the doctrine of the sovereignty and the mercy. The believer is taught: everything belongs to Allah — the heavens, the earth, the affairs; the forgiveness and the punishment are His will — the *yaghfiru* and the *yu'adhdhibu*; and the names of the close — the *Ghafur* and the *Rahim* — are the mercy behind the decision. The verse's *yasha'* — whom He wills — is the sovereignty: no one dictates the decision; and the *Ghafur* — the All-Forgiving — is the hope: the mercy is the dominant — the forgiveness of the repenters, the mercy of the believers, the *Rahim* of the close. The believer's confidence: the One who owns the heavens and the earth, who forgives and punishes, is the Ghafur and the Rahim — the forgiveness and the mercy of the sovereign.

## Verse 130

Verse 130 says: *O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers to stop consuming interest — the multiplied and redoubled increase — and to be mindful of Allah so that they may prosper.

The verse turns from the battle to the economy: the prohibition of the *riba*. Ibn Kathir: "O you who believe! Do not consume Riba doubled and multiplied, but fear Allah that you may be successful." The verse's *la ta'kulu al-riba ad'afan muda'afah* — do not consume the riba, doubled and multiplied — is the prohibition; the *wattaqu Allaha* — and fear Allah — is the command; and the *la'allakum tuflihun* — so that you may prosper — is the purpose.

"Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over" — *la ta'kulu al-riba ad'afan muda'afah* — do not consume the usury, doubled and redoubled, multiplied. The *riba* — the usury, the interest — is the increase: the *ad'afan muda'afah* — doubled and multiplied — the manyfold. Ibn Kathir explains the Jahiliyyah practice: "when the time to pay a loan comes, the creditor would say to the debtor, 'Either pay now, or the loan will incur interest.' If the debtor asks for deferment of the loan, the creditor would require interest and this would occur year after year until the little capital becomes multiplied many times." Al-Jalalayn: "do not exact usury twofold and severalfold — by increasing the amount to be repaid when the loan period comes to an end and delaying the request of the loan." The verse's *ad'afan* — doubled, manyfold — is the compounding: the interest that multiplies — the small loan that becomes the many times the capital, the debt that compounds year after year. The *la ta'kulu* — do not consume — is the prohibition of the eating: the *riba* is the consuming of the increase — the unjust gain, the violation of the borrower.

"And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper" — *wattaqu Allaha la'allakum tuflihun* — and fear Allah, so that you might succeed, prosper. The *taqwa* — the mindfulness — is the command after the prohibition: the believers who abandon the *riba* are to be mindful of Allah; the *falah* — the success, the prosperity — is the purpose. Ibn Kathir: "Allah also commands His servants to have Taqwa of Him so that they may achieve success in this life and the Hereafter." The verse's *la'allakum tuflihun* — so that you might prosper — is the promise: the abandonment of the *riba* and the Taqwa lead to the *falah* — the success of this life and the Hereafter; the *tuflihun* — the prospering, the succeeding — is the *falah* of the believers.

Maududi's note on the position of the verse: "The major cause of the setback suffered at Uhud was that precisely at the moment of their victory the Muslims succumbed to the desire for worldly possessions, and turned to collecting booty rather than completing their task of crushing the enemy. Hence God thought fit to raise a barrier against this excessive adoration of money, and to urge them to give up usury which keeps man constantly absorbed in considering ways and means of amassing wealth and generally whets his appetite for money." The verse's position in the surah: after the Uhud account (121–129), the prohibition of the *riba* — the cause of the setback was the love of the world's possessions; the *riba* is the multiplication of the money, the appetite for the wealth — and the *taqwa* is the barrier.

The verse's place in the surah's section: the description of the believers' qualities (the *muttaqin* of 133–136) includes the abandonment of the *riba* (130), the fear of the Fire (131), the obedience of Allah and the Messenger (132), the hastening to the forgiveness and the Paradise (133), the spending in prosperity and adversity and the control of the anger (134), and the repentance of the sinners (135). The *riba* prohibition is the first of the qualities: the believers who are mindful are the ones who do not consume the *riba*.

For the reader, the verse is the prohibition of the unjust increase and the command of the prosperity. The believer is taught: the *riba* — the interest, the usury — is the prohibited: the doubled and multiplied increase; the *ad'afan muda'afah* — the compounding — is the practice of the Jahiliyyah: the creditor who delays the loan for the interest, the debt that multiplies year after year, the small capital that becomes the many times. And the *taqwa* — the mindfulness — is the command after the prohibition: the believers who abandon the *riba* are mindful of Allah; the *falah* — the prosperity — is the purpose: the success of the life and the Hereafter. The verse's *tuflihun* — the prospering — is the believer's prosperity: the *falah* is not the amassing of the wealth but the success of the Taqwa — the believer who abandons the *riba* and minds Allah is the one who truly prospers.

## Verse 131

Verse 131 says: *Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers to guard themselves against the Fire that has been prepared for the disbelievers.

The verse is the warning that follows the prohibition of the riba. Ibn Kathir: "And fear the Fire, which is prepared for the disbelievers." The verse's *wattaqu al-nara allati u'iddat lil-kafirin* — and fear the Fire, which is prepared for the disbelievers — is the command of the guarding; the *allati u'iddat* — which has been prepared — is the preparation; and the *lil-kafirin* — for the disbelievers — is the designation.

"Guard yourselves against the Fire" — *wattaqu al-nara* — and fear/guard against the Fire. The *taqwa* — the guarding, the fearing — is the command: the believers guard themselves against the Fire — the same *taqwa* of the verse's frame. Ibn Kathir: "Allah also threatens them with the Fire and warns them against it." The verse's *wattaqu* — and fear — is the sequence: the prohibition of the *riba* (130) is followed by the guarding against the Fire (131) — the believer who abandons the unjust gain guards against the Fire; the *taqwa* — the guarding — is the barrier.

"prepared for the disbelievers" — *allati u'iddat lil-kafirin* — which is prepared for the disbelievers. The *u'iddat* — is prepared — is the readiness: the Fire is prepared, waiting, ready for the disbelievers. Ibn Kathir connects it with the next verse: "Just as the Fire was prepared for the disbelievers" — the Fire for the disbelievers, the Paradise for the *muttaqin* (133) — the two preparations of the surah. The verse's *lil-kafirin* — for the disbelievers — is the designation: the Fire is prepared for the disbelievers; the believers guard against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers — the *taqwa* — the guarding — is the distance from the designation.

The verse's position: the personal qualities of the believers (130–136) begin with the guarding: the prohibition of the *riba* (130), the guarding of the Fire (131), the obedience of Allah and the Messenger (132), and the hastening to the forgiveness and the Paradise (133). The *wattaqu al-nar* — the guarding of the Fire — is the negative of the section: the believers who are mindful are the ones who fear the Fire; the *taqwa* of the believers includes the fear of the Fire prepared for the disbelievers. Ibn Kathir: "Allah also commands His servants to have Taqwa of Him so that they may achieve success in this life and the Hereafter."

For the reader, the verse is the warning of the Fire and the command of the guarding. The believer is taught: the Fire is prepared for the disbelievers — the *u'iddat* — the readiness of the punishment; and the believers guard themselves against it — the *wattaqu* — the guarding of the *taqwa*. The verse's *lil-kafirin* — for the disbelievers — is the designation: the believers are not the *kafirin*, but the *taqwa* is the guarding of the distance; the believer who is mindful of Allah is the one who guards against the Fire. The verse's *allati u'iddat* — which is prepared — is the reality: the Fire is not the threat of the future but the prepared reality; the *taqwa* — the guarding — is the response. And the verse's sequence: the abandonment of the *riba* (130), the guarding of the Fire (131), the obedience of the Messenger (132), the hastening to the mercy (133) — the qualities of the believer who is mindful and prosperous.

The verse is also the deepest point of the surah's moral logic. The Fire is prepared for the disbelievers — the ones who rejected the signs and the messengers; the believers are not the ones of the preparation, but they are the ones who guard — the *taqwa* is the distance from the designation, the mindfulness that keeps the believer from the path that leads to the Fire. Ibn Kathir's frame of the section is the *targhib* and the *tarhib* — the promise and the warning: the prohibition of the *riba* (130) is the negative of the world's gain; the guarding of the Fire (131) is the warning of the world's loss; the obedience of the Messenger (132) is the positive of the divine command; and the hastening to the mercy (133) is the promise of the divine reward. The verse's shortest form — *wattaqu al-nar* — and fear the Fire — is the whole of the warning: the believer who guards against the Fire is the believer who is mindful of Allah — the *taqwa* of the verse is both the fear of the punishment and the love of obedience, the *muhasabah* of the self before the standing of the reckoning.

The verse's practical reading, in the light of the hadith of the *Firdaws* (133), is the balance of the hope and the fear: the believer who asks for the Paradise is the one who guards against the Fire; the *Firdaws* of the highest and the Fire of the prepared — the two realities of the verse's section — the hope of the reward and the fear of the punishment, the *raghbah* and the *rahbah* — the desire and the awe. The believer's daily program: the *riba* abandoned (130), the Fire guarded (131), the obedience (132) and the hastening (133) — the four commands of the surah's middle — the program of the believer who is mindful and who prospers. And the verse's *allati u'iddat* — prepared — is the permanence: the Fire is not the temporary but the prepared; the guarding against it is the permanent — the *taqwa* of the entire life, the guarding of the whole path.

## Verse 132

Verse 132 says: *Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers to obey Allah and the Messenger — so that they may receive mercy.

The verse is the command of the obedience between the guarding of the Fire and the hastening to the mercy. Ibn Kathir: "And obey Allah and the Messenger that you may obtain mercy." The verse's *wa ati'u Allaha wa al-Rasul* — and obey Allah and the Messenger — is the command; the *la'allakum turhamun* — so that you may be shown mercy — is the purpose.

"Obey Allah and the Messenger" — *wa ati'u Allaha wa al-Rasula* — and obey Allah and the Messenger. The *ta'ah* — the obedience — is the two-fold: the obedience of Allah and the obedience of the Messenger — the two as one. Ibn Kathir: "And obey Allah and the Messenger." The verse's sequence: the obedience follows the guarding of the Fire (131) and precedes the hastening of the mercy (133): the believer who guards against the Fire obeys Allah and the Messenger; the *ta'ah* — the obedience — is the path of the believer. The verse's *Allah wa al-Rasul* — Allah and the Messenger — is the command of the surah: the obedience of Allah is the obedience of the Messenger; the command of the Messenger is the command of Allah (4:80).

"so you may be shown mercy" — *la'allakum turhamun* — so that you might have mercy shown to you, so that you might be granted mercy. The *rahmah* — the mercy — is the purpose: the obedience leads to the mercy. The verse's *la'allakum* — so that, perhaps — is the purpose of the command: the mercy is the result of the obedience; the *turhamun* — you might be shown mercy — is the passive: the mercy from Allah to the obedient. Ibn Kathir's sequence: the *rahmah* — the mercy — is the center of the section: the guarding of the Fire (131), the obedience for the mercy (132), the hastening to the forgiveness and the Paradise (133) — the mercy is the purpose of the obedience, and the forgiveness is the destination of the hastening.

The verse's place: the qualities of the believers (130–136). The *ta'ah* — the obedience — is the central quality: the believer who abandons the *riba*, who guards against the Fire, who obeys Allah and the Messenger — is the believer who is shown mercy. Ibn Kathir frames the section: "Allah encourages His servants to perform righteous deeds and to rush to accomplish the acts of obedience" — the obedience is the rush, the *ta'ah* is the hastening, and the mercy and the Paradise (133) are the destinations.

For the reader, the verse is the command of the obedience and the promise of the mercy. The believer is taught: obey Allah and the Messenger — the *ta'ah* of the two as one — and the mercy is the purpose: the *la'allakum turhamun* — so that you might be shown mercy. The verse's *Allah wa al-Rasul* — Allah and the Messenger — is the undivided command: the obedience of the Messenger is the obedience of Allah; the *ta'ah* is the path of the believer. And the verse's *turhamun* — the mercy — is the promise: the obedience of the believers is met with the mercy of Allah — the guarding of the Fire (131), the obedience (132), and the mercy — the sequence of the section: the believer who obeys is the one who is shown mercy.

The verse completes the chain that runs from the prohibition to the promise. Ibn Kathir's sequence in the passage: the *riba* prohibition (130), the Taqwa for the success (130), the guarding of the Fire (131), the obedience for the mercy (132), the hastening for the forgiveness (133). The obedience is the hinge: the *ta'ah* — the obedience — is the *wasilah* — the means — of the *rahmah* — the mercy; the believer who obeys Allah and the Messenger is the one who is shown mercy. The verse's *Allah wa al-Rasul* is also the surah's doctrine of the *sunnah*: the obedience of the Messenger is not the separate from the obedience of Allah; the *ta'ah* of the Message is the *ta'ah* of the Messenger — the Qur'an and the *sunnah*, the divine command and the prophetic example — one obedience, one path.

The verse's *la'allakum turhamun* — so that you might be granted mercy — is the Qur'an's purpose clause of the prophecy: the *rahman* of Allah is the destination of the obedience; the *turhamun* — the passive — is the mercy shown; the command of the obedience is the command of the mercy's reception. The verse's shortest form — *ati'u Allah wa al-Rasul* — obey Allah and the Messenger — is the whole of the religion's command: the obedience of the commands, the following of the example, the submission of the life — the *ta'ah* that the mercy answers. The believer's program: the obedience of Allah in the commands, the obedience of the Messenger in the example — the *ta'ah* of the daily life; and the mercy of the *turhamun* — the promise of the obedience.

## Verse 133

Verse 133 says: *And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers to race to the forgiveness from their Lord and to the Paradise whose breadth is as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful of Allah.

The verse is the command of the racing. Ibn Kathir: "And march forth in the way (which leads to) forgiveness from your Lord, and for Paradise as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the Muttaqin (the pious)." The verse's *wa sari'u ila maghfiratin min rabbikum* — and hasten to a forgiveness from your Lord — is the racing; the *wa jannatin 'arduha al-samawati wa al-ard* — and a Garden whose breadth is the heavens and the earth — is the destination; and the *u'iddat lil-muttaqin* — prepared for the God-fearing — is the designation.

"hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord" — *wa sari'u ila maghfiratin min rabbikum* — and race/hasten to forgiveness from your Lord. The *sari'* — the hastening, the racing — is the command: the believers race to the forgiveness; the *sar'ah* — the haste — is the speed of the obedience. Al-Jalalayn: "vie with one another hastening... to forgiveness from your Lord." The verse's *maghfirah* — the forgiveness — is the destination: the forgiveness from the Lord; the *rabbikum* — your Lord — is the source: the Lord of the believers who forgives. The *sari'u* — the haste — is the command of the section: the believers who abandon the *riba* (130), guard the Fire (131), obey the Messenger (132) — race to the forgiveness (133); the *musara'ah* — the racing — is the believer's program.

"a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth" — *wa jannatin 'arduha al-samawati wa al-ard* — and a Garden whose breadth is the heavens and the earth. The *'ard* — the breadth — is the measure: the breadth of the Paradise as the heavens and the earth. Ibn Kathir: "the meaning of Allah's statement, 'as wide as the heavens and the earth,' draws the attention to the spaciousness of Paradise. For instance, Allah said in another Ayah, while describing the couches of Paradise, 'lined with silk brocade' (55:54), so what about their outer covering? It was also said that Paradise is as wide as its length, because it is a dome under the Throne. The width and length of a dome or a circle are the same in distance." Al-Jalalayn: "as broad as both of them together if put side by side — breadth denotes ampleness." The verse's *'ard* — the breadth — is the spaciousness: the Paradise is vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the *muttaqin* — the breadth and the preparation.

"prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺" — *u'iddat lil-muttaqin* — prepared for the God-fearing, the mindful. The *u'iddat* — is prepared — is the readiness: the Paradise is prepared, waiting, ready — "Just as the Fire was prepared for the disbelievers" (131). The verse's *lil-muttaqin* — for the God-fearing — is the designation: the Paradise for the *muttaqin*, the Fire for the *kafirin* — the two preparations, the two designations, the surah's two destinations.

The hadith of the Sahih, recorded by Ibn Kathir: "When you ask Allah for Paradise, ask Him for Al-Firdaws which is the highest and best part of Paradise. From it originate the rivers of Paradise, and above it is the Throne of the Most Beneficent (Allah)." The *Firdaws* — the highest and best — is the answer to the asker. And the Al-Bazzar hadith of Abu Hurayrah: a man asked about the verse — "Where is the Fire then?" — and the Prophet said, "When the night comes, it overtakes everything, so where is the day?" — the man said, "Where Allah wants it to be" — "Similarly, the Fire is where Allah wants it to be." Ibn Kathir: the two meanings — the night not seen during the day does not mean the day is not somewhere else; the Paradise is in the utmost heights above the heavens and under the Throne, while the Fire is in the lowest of lows — the breadth of the Paradise does not contradict the place of the Fire. And the parallel: 57:21 — "Race with one another in hastening towards forgiveness from your Lord, and Paradise the width whereof is as the width of the heaven and the Earth."

For the reader, the verse is the command of the racing and the description of the destination. The believer is taught: the *sari'* — the haste — is the command: race to the forgiveness; the *maghfirah* — the forgiveness — is the destination; and the *jannah* — the Paradise whose breadth is the heavens and the earth — is the prepared destination for the *muttaqin*. The verse's *'ard* — the breadth — is the vastness: the spaciousness of the Paradise, the dome under the Throne, the expanse of the heavens and the earth; the *u'iddat* — is prepared — is the readiness: the Paradise is the prepared destination, as the Fire is prepared for the disbelievers (131). And the *Firdaws* of the hadith — the highest and best, the source of the rivers, the ceiling of the Throne — is the destination of the asker. The believer's program: the *sari'* — the racing to the forgiveness and the Paradise — the *musara'ah* of the good deeds, the *sabq* of the obedience, the haste to the destination prepared for the *muttaqin*.

## Verse 134

Verse 134 says: *˹They are˺ those who donate in prosperity and adversity, control their anger, and pardon others. And Allah loves the good-doers.*

In simple terms, the verse describes the mindful: they give in prosperity and adversity, they control their anger, and they pardon others — and Allah loves those who do good.

The verse gives the qualities of the *muttaqin* of 133. Ibn Kathir: "Those who spend (in Allah's cause) in prosperity and in adversity, who repress anger, and who pardon men; verily, Allah loves the Muhsinin (the good-doers)." The verse's *alladhina yunfiquna fi al-sarra' wa al-darra'* — those who spend in prosperity and adversity — is the giving; the *wal-kazimina al-ghayz* — and those who repress the anger — is the control; the *wal-'afina 'an al-nas* — and those who pardon the people — is the pardon; and the *wa Allahu yuhibbu al-muhsinin* — and Allah loves the good-doers — is the love.

"those who donate in prosperity and adversity" — *alladhina yunfiquna fi al-sarra' wa al-darra'* — those who spend in ease and hardship, in prosperity and adversity. The *infaq* — the spending, the donation — is the giving: the *sarr'* — the prosperity, the ease — and the *darra'* — the adversity, the hardship — are the two conditions: the giving in the prosperity and the giving in the adversity. Ibn Kathir: "in hard times and easy times, while active (or enthusiastic) and otherwise, healthy or ill, and in all conditions, just as Allah said in another Ayah, 'Those who spend their wealth (in Allah's cause) by night and day, in secret and in public' (2:274). These believers are never distracted from obeying Allah, spending on what pleases Him, being kind to His servants and their relatives, and other acts of righteousness." The verse's *wa al-darra'* — and the adversity — is the stronger: the giving in the hardship — the donation that does not cease when the condition worsens; the *infaq* of the prosperity and the adversity is the constancy of the believer's giving.

"control their anger" — *wal-kazimina al-ghayz* — and those who repress the anger, who swallow it. The *kazm* — the suppression, the swallowing — is the control: the anger is controlled, swallowed, not acted upon. Ibn Kathir: "for when they are angry, they control their anger and do not act upon it. Rather, they even forgive those who hurt them." The hadiths: Abu Hurayrah — "The strong person is not he who is able to physically overcome people. The strong person is he who overcomes his rage when he is angry" (the Two Sahihs). Ibn 'Abbas — "He who gives time to a debtor or forgives him, then Allah will save him from the heat of Jahannam... Verily, there is no dose of anything better to Allah than a dose of rage that the servant controls, and whenever the servant of Allah controls it, he will be internally filled with faith" (Imam Ahmad, good chain). Sahl bin Mu'adh from his father — "Whoever controlled rage while able to act upon it, then Allah will call him while all creation is a witness, until He gives him the choice of any of the Huris he wishes" (Abu Dawud, at-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah — hasan gharib). Ibn 'Umar — "There is not a dose of anything that the servant takes which is better than a dose of control of rage that he feels, when he does it seeking Allah's Face" (Ibn Jarir, Ibn Majah). The verse's *kazim* — the suppressors — are the strong: the control of the anger is the strength — the one who overcomes the rage is the strong one.

"and pardon others" — *wal-'afina 'an al-nas* — and those who pardon the people, who forgive them. The *'afw* — the pardon, the forgiveness — is the second of the anger's response: the one who controls the anger pardons the wrongdoer. Ibn Kathir: "They forgive those who treat them with injustice. Therefore, they do not hold any ill feelings about anyone in their hearts, and this is the most excellent conduct in this regard." The verse's *'afw* — the pardon — is the completion of the *kazm*: the control of the anger and the pardon of the wrongdoer — the *kazm* holds the anger and the *'afw* forgives the offense — the excellence of the conduct, the purging of the ill feelings from the hearts.

"And Allah loves the good-doers" — *wa Allahu yuhibbu al-muhsinin* — and Allah loves the good-doers, the Muhsinin. The *hubb* — the love — is the divine: the love of the *muhsinin* — the good-doers. Ibn Kathir: "This good conduct is a type of Ihsan [excellence in the religion]." The hadith of the three oaths: "I swear regarding three matters: no charity shall ever decrease the wealth; whenever one forgives people, then Allah will magnify his honor; and he who is humble for Allah, then Allah will raise his rank." The verse's *muhsinin* — the good-doers — is the designation: the givers, the controllers of the anger, the pardoners — the *muhsinin* whom Allah loves; the *ihsan* — the excellence — is the conduct of the qualities.

For the reader, the verse is the portrait of the mindful. The believer is taught: the *muttaqin* of 133 — those who race to the forgiveness and the Paradise — are the ones who give in prosperity and adversity, who control the anger, who pardon the others; the *infaq* of the two conditions — the giving through the ease and the hardship; the *kazm* of the anger — the strength of the control; the *'afw* of the pardon — the forgiveness of the wrongdoer. And the *hubb* of Allah — the love of the *muhsinin* — is the designation: the conduct is the *ihsan* — the excellence that Allah loves. The believer's program: the *infaq* of the prosperity and the adversity, the *kazm* of the rage, and the *'afw* of the pardon — the three qualities of the *muttaqin* — the givers and the controllers and the pardoners — the *muhsinin* whom Allah loves.

## Verse 135

Verse 135 says: *˹They are˺ those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness and do not knowingly persist in sin—and who forgives sins except Allah?*

In simple terms, the verse describes the fourth quality: when they commit an evil deed or wrong themselves, they remember Allah, seek forgiveness, and do not knowingly persist — and who forgives sins except Allah?

The verse turns from the conduct of the anger to the conduct of the sin. Ibn Kathir: "And those who, when they have committed Fahishah (immoral sin) or wronged themselves with evil, remember Allah and ask forgiveness for their sins; and none can forgive sins but Allah, and do not persist in what (wrong) they have done, while they know." The verse's *alladhina idha fa'alu fahishatan aw zalamu anfusahum* — those who, when they commit an immorality or wrong themselves — is the sin; the *dhakaru Allaha fastaghfaru lidhunubihim* — they remember Allah and seek forgiveness for their sins — is the response; the *wa man yaghfiru al-dhunuba illa Allah* — and who forgives sins except Allah — is the exclusivity; and the *wa lam yisirru 'ala ma fa'alu wa hum ya'lamun* — and do not persist in what they did while they know — is the non-persistence.

"upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves" — *idha fa'alu fahishatan aw zalamu anfusahum* — when they commit a fahishah or wrong themselves. The *fahishah* — the immorality, the indecency — is the grave sin: Al-Jalalayn — "a despicable sin such as adultery"; the *zulm* — the wronging of the self — is the lesser: Al-Jalalayn — "wrong themselves with less than that, such as a kiss." The verse's *aw* — or — is the two degrees: the fahishah and the wrong — the greater and the lesser — both the subject of the response.

"remember Allah and seek forgiveness" — *dhakaru Allaha fastaghfaru lidhunubihim* — they remember Allah and seek forgiveness for their sins. The *dhikr* — the remembrance — is the first response: Al-Jalalayn — "remember God, that is to say, His threat of punishment." The *istighfar* — the seeking of the forgiveness — is the second: they ask the forgiveness for their sins — the *dhikr* of Allah and the *istighfar* of the sins; Ibn Kathir: "if they commit an error they follow it with repentance and ask forgiveness."

"and who forgives sins except Allah?" — *wa man yaghfiru al-dhunuba illa Allah* — and who forgives the sins except Allah? The rhetorical question is the exclusivity: no one forgives sins except Allah. Ibn Kathir: "none except Allah forgives sins." The verse's *illa Allah* — except Allah — is the exclusive; the rhetorical question — *wa man* — and who — is the affirmation: the one who forgives is Allah alone. The question is the confession: the *istighfar* — the seeking of the forgiveness — is the address to the only one who can forgive.

"and do not knowingly persist in sin" — *wa lam yusirru 'ala ma fa'alu wa hum ya'lamun* — and do not persist in what they did while they know. The *israr* — the persistence, the insistence — is the negative: the believers do not persist. Ibn Kathir: "for they repent from their error, return to Allah before death, do not insist on error, and if they err again, they repent from it." The *ya'lamun* — while they know — is the knowledge: Mujahid and 'Abdullah bin 'Ubayd bin 'Umayr: "Whoever repents, then Allah will forgive him" (9:104, 4:110). The verse's *israr* — the persistence — is the distinction: the sin is the human, the persistence is the rebellion — the believer who sins and repents is the sinner of the *fahishah*; the believer who persists while knowing is the *musirr* — the one who does not belong to the description.

The hadith of the repeated repentance, recorded by Ibn Kathir (Imam Ahmad; similar in the Sahih): "A man once committed an error and said, 'O Lord! I committed an error, so forgive me.' Allah said, 'My servant committed an error and knew that he has a Lord Who forgives or punishes for the error. I have forgiven My servant.' The man committed another error and said, 'O Lord! I committed an error, so forgive me.' Allah said, 'My servant knew that he has a Lord Who forgives or punishes for the sin. I have forgiven My servant.' ... He then committed another error and said, 'O Lord! I committed an error, so forgive me.' Allah said, 'My servant knew that he has a Lord Who forgives or punishes for the error. Bear witness that I have forgiven My servant, so let him do whatever he likes.'" And Abdur-Razzaq recorded that Anas said: "I was told that when the Ayah... was revealed, Iblis (Satan) cried" — the verse's revelation was the grief of the devil: the *istighfar* of the sinners closed the door of his hope.

For the reader, the verse is the description of the repentant. The believer is taught: the sins — the *fahishah* and the *zulm* — are the human; the response is the *dhikr* and the *istighfar* — the remembrance of Allah and the seeking of the forgiveness; and the *illa Allah* — except Allah — is the exclusive: the one who forgives is Allah alone. The verse's *israr* — the persistence — is the boundary: the believer who sins and repents is the described; the believer who persists while knowing is the excluded — the *israr* while *ya'lamun* — the insistence with the knowledge — the unforgiven pattern. And the hadith of the repeated repentance: the *istighfar* — the repeated seeking — is the mercy — the forgiveness of the servant who knows the Lord who forgives; the *falyu'mal ma sha'* — "let him do whatever he likes" — is the mercy's scope: the servant who knows the forgiving Lord is the servant of the forgiveness.

## Verse 136

Verse 136 says: *Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺!*

In simple terms, the verse gives the reward of the described believers: forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens with rivers flowing beneath, staying there forever — and how excellent is the reward of the workers.

The verse closes the description of the believers. Ibn Kathir: "For such, the reward is forgiveness from their Lord, and Gardens with rivers flowing underneath (Paradise), wherein they shall abide forever. How excellent is this reward for the doers (of good)." The verse's *ula'ika jazau'uhum maghfiratun min rabbihim* — those are — for them, forgiveness from their Lord — is the reward; the *wa jannatun tajri min tahtiha al-anhar* — and Gardens under which rivers flow — is the Paradise; the *khalidina fiha* — abiding therein forever — is the permanence; and the *wa ni'ma ajru al-'amilin* — how excellent is the reward of the workers — is the praise.

"Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord" — *ula'ika jazau'uhum maghfiratun min rabbihim* — for such, their reward is forgiveness from their Lord. The *jaza'* — the reward — is the recompense: the *maghfirah* — the forgiveness — from the Lord. Ibn Kathir: "as a reward for these qualities." The verse's *min rabbihim* — from their Lord — is the source: the forgiveness from the Lord of the believers — the reward of the qualities: the giving (134), the control of the anger (134), the pardon (134), the remembrance and the forgiveness (135) — the reward of the *maghfirah* — the forgiveness from the Lord.

"and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever" — *wa jannatun tajri min tahtiha al-anharu khalidina fiha* — and Gardens under which rivers flow, abiding therein forever. The *jannat* — the Gardens — is the Paradise: the rivers flowing beneath — "carrying all kinds of drinks" (Ibn Kathir); the *khulud* — the abiding forever — is the permanence: the same *khulud* of the earlier verdicts — the abiding of the believers in the Gardens, the abiding of the deniers in the Fire. The verse's *tajri min tahtiha al-anhar* — the rivers flowing beneath — is the description of the Paradise of the surah (2:25, 3:15, 3:198) — the Gardens of the believers; the *khalidina* — abiding — is the permanence of the reward.

"How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺!" — *wa ni'ma ajru al-'amilin* — and how excellent is the reward of the workers. The *ni'ma* — how excellent, how good — is the praise of the reward; the *ajr* — the reward — is the recompense; the *'amilin* — the workers, the doers — are the ones who work — the believers who do the works of 134–135. Ibn Kathir: "Allah praises Paradise in this part of the Ayah." The verse's *ni'ma* — how excellent — is the divine commendation: the reward is excellent — the forgiveness and the Gardens and the permanence — for the workers.

The verse's place: the description of the *muttaqin* (133–136) — the *jannah* prepared for the *muttaqin* (133), the qualities of the *muttaqin* (134–135), and the reward of the *muttaqin* (136): the *maghfirah* and the *jannat* and the *khulud* — the three parts of the reward; and the *ni'ma* — the praise — the excellence of the reward of the workers. The section is the surah's portrait of the mindful: the giving, the control of the anger, the pardon, the repentance — and the reward: the forgiveness, the Gardens, the eternity.

For the reader, the verse is the reward of the workers. The believer is taught: the reward of the believers — the *maghfirah* from the Lord, the *jannat* with the rivers, the *khulud* forever — is the recompense of the qualities: the giving, the control, the pardon, the repentance. The verse's *ni'ma ajru al-'amilin* — how excellent is the reward of the workers — is the divine praise: the reward is excellent; the *'amilin* — the workers — are the ones who work the righteousness; the *ajr* — the reward — the forgiveness and the Gardens and the eternity. The believer's program: the *'amal* — the work — is the path of the reward: the *infaq* and the *kazm* and the *'afw* and the *istighfar* — the qualities of the *muttaqin* (134–135) — and the reward of the *maghfirah* and the *jannat* and the *khulud* — the excellence of the reward of the workers.

## Verse 137

Verse 137 says: *Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers.*

In simple terms, the verse turns the believers to history: similar situations have passed before them — so travel through the land and see what happened to the deniers.

The verse turns from the believers' qualities to the lessons of history. Ibn Kathir: "Many similar ways (and mishaps of life) were faced (by nations (believers and disbelievers) that have passed away) before you, so travel through the earth, and see what was the end of those who denied." The verse's *qad khalat min qablikum sunan* — similar situations have passed before you — is the history; the *fasiru fi al-ard* — so travel in the earth — is the command; and the *fanzuru kayfa kana 'aqibatu al-mukadhdhibin* — and see how was the end of the deniers — is the survey.

"Similar situations came to pass before you" — *qad khalat min qablikum sunan* — ways/situations have passed before you. The *sunan* — the ways, the patterns, the situations — is the history: the ways of Allah with the previous nations. Ibn Kathir: "the previous nations who followed their Prophets before you, they too suffered losses. However, the good end was theirs, and the ultimate defeat was for the disbelievers." Al-Jalalayn: "Ways of life have passed away before you — all manner of unbelief has preceded where they have been given respite but are then seized with punishment." The verse's *khalat* — have passed — is the past: the *sunan* — the ways — of the previous nations; the *qablikum* — before you — is the precedence: the believers' situation is not the first of the history; the losses and the victories were the patterns before.

"so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers" — *fasiru fi al-ardi fanzuru kayfa kana 'aqibatu al-mukadhdhibin* — so travel in the earth and see how was the end of the deniers. The *sayr* — the travel — is the survey: the believers travel the earth; the *nazar* — the seeing — is the contemplation: see the *'aqibah* — the end — of the *mukadhdhibin* — the deniers. Ibn Kathir: "see what was the end of those who denied." Al-Jalalayn: "travel in the land, O believers, and behold how was the end of those who denied the messengers — that is how their affair ended in destruction. So do not grieve on account of their victory; I am only giving them respite until their appointed time." The verse's *'aqibah* — the end — is the lesson: the end of the deniers is the destruction; the travel and the seeing — the historical survey — is the instruction: the believers who look at the history of the deniers see the end — the *'aqibah* — the destruction of the deniers.

The verse's composition: the believers who suffered at Uhud (the seventy dead) are taught the *sunan* — the ways of the past: the previous nations who followed their prophets also suffered losses; the good end was theirs and the ultimate defeat was the disbelievers'. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states to His believing servants who suffered losses in the battle of Uhud, including seventy dead: 'Many similar ways (and mishaps of life) were faced before you' — for the previous nations who followed their Prophets before you, they too suffered losses. However, the good end was theirs, and the ultimate defeat was for the disbelievers." The verse's *sunan* and *'aqibah* — the ways and the end — are the two: the *sunan* of the past and the *'aqibah* of the deniers — the history is the instruction, and the end is the lesson.

For the reader, the verse is the command of the historical instruction. The believer is taught: the *sunan* — the ways — of Allah with the previous nations have passed; the losses of the believers were the patterns before; and the *'aqibah* — the end — of the deniers is the destruction — the travel and the seeing of the history is the instruction. The verse's *fasiru* — so travel — is the command: the believers survey the earth and see the end of the deniers; the *fanzuru* — and see — is the contemplation: the historical sight is the lesson of the end. The believer's lesson from Uhud: the loss was not the novelty — the previous nations suffered losses and the good end was theirs; the *'aqibah* of the deniers is the destruction — the respite of the world, the seizure of the end. The verse's *sunan* — the ways — and the *'aqibah* — the end — are the believer's historical confidence: the ways of Allah are the constant and the end of the deniers is the destruction.

## Verse 138

Verse 138 says: *This is an insight to humanity —a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing.*

In simple terms, the verse states the value of the Qur'an: it is an insight for humanity, a guidance and an instruction for the God-fearing.

The verse states the nature of the recitation. Ibn Kathir: "This is a plain statement for mankind, a guidance and instruction for the Muttaqin." The verse's *hadha bayanun lil-nas* — this is a plain statement for mankind — is the declaration; the *wa hudan wa maw'izatun lil-muttaqin* — and a guidance and an instruction for the God-fearing — is the double function.

"This is an insight to humanity" — *hadha bayanun lil-nas* — this is a plain statement, an explanation for mankind. The *bayan* — the statement, the explanation, the insight — is the Qur'an: the true reality of things. Ibn Kathir: "the Qur'an explains the true reality of things and narrates how the previous nations suffered by the hands of their enemies." The verse's *lil-nas* — for mankind — is the universal address: the *bayan* is for all humanity — not the exclusive of the believers, not the private of the community — the declaration of the truth for the people. The *bayan* of the verse — the statement — is the Qur'an's explanation: the *sunan* of the past (137), the *'aqibah* of the deniers (137), the realities of the truth — the explanation of things as they are.

"a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing" — *wa hudan wa maw'izatun lil-muttaqin* — and guidance and instruction for the God-fearing. The *huda* — the guidance — and the *maw'izah* — the instruction, the admonition — are the double: the **huda** — the guidance — for the hearts; the *maw'izah* — the instruction — for the *muttaqin* — "to discourage committing the prohibited and forbidden matters" (Ibn Kathir). The verse's *lil-muttaqin* — for the God-fearing — is the function's address: the *bayan* is for all humanity, and the *huda* and the *maw'izah* are for the *muttaqin* — the ones who fear, the ones who are mindful — the guidance of the hearts and the instruction of the conduct. Ibn Kathir: "the Qur'an contains the news of the past, and guidance for your hearts, and instruction for the Muttaqin to discourage committing the prohibited and forbidden matters."

The verse's place: the *bayan* of the Qur'an — after the *sunan* of the past (137) — is the insight for humanity, the guide for the hearts, the lesson for the God-fearing. The Qur'an of the surah — the *bayan* — is the true reality: the history of the deniers (137), the guidance of the believers (138), the instruction of the *muttaqin* (138) — the three functions: the *bayan* for the people, the *huda* for the hearts, the *maw'izah* for the God-fearing.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's statement of its own value. The believer is taught: the *bayan* — the insight, the statement — is for humanity: the truth explained, the realities declared; the *huda* — the guidance — is for the hearts: the way of the truth; the *maw'izah* — the instruction — is for the *muttaqin*: the lesson of the conduct, the discouragement of the forbidden. The verse's *lil-nas* — for mankind — is the universal: the Qur'an is not the private book but the public statement; the *lil-muttaqin* — for the God-fearing — is the function: the guidance and the instruction are for the ones who fear — the *bayan* for all, the *huda* and the *maw'izah* for the mindful. The believer's response: the *bayan* — the acceptance of the statement; the *huda* — the guidance of the heart; the *maw'izah* — the lesson of the conduct — the Qur'an as the insight, the guide, and the instruction.

## Verse 139

Verse 139 says: *Do not falter or grieve, for you will have the upper hand, if you are ˹true˺ believers.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers: do not lose heart or grieve — you are the upper hand, if you are true believers.

The verse is the comfort of the believers after the loss of Uhud. Ibn Kathir: "So do not become weak, because of what you suffered, nor be sad, and you will be triumphant if you are indeed believers — for surely, the ultimate victory and triumph will be yours, O believers." The verse's *wa la tahinu* — and do not become weak — is the first negative; the *wa la tahzanu* — and do not grieve — is the second; and the *wa antum al-a'lawna in kuntum mu'minin* — and you are the upper hand, if you are believers — is the verdict.

"Do not falter or grieve" — *wa la tahinu wa la tahzanu* — and do not weaken, and do not grieve. The *wahn* — the weakness, the faltering — is the loss of the strength: the believers do not become weak because of what they suffered. Ibn Kathir: "because of what you suffered." The *huzn* — the grief, the sadness — is the loss of the hope: the believers do not grieve — the loss of Uhud is not the end of the hope; the *wahn* and the *huzn* — the weakness and the grief — are the two negatives: the believers do not falter and do not grieve — the *la* of the two commands is the prohibition of the despair.

"for you will have the upper hand" — *wa antum al-a'lawna* — and you are the upper, the supreme, the triumphant. The *a'la* — the upper, the highest, the supreme — is the position: the believers are the upper hand; the *a'lawn* — the supreme — is the believers' position: the ultimate victory and triumph is the believers', not the disbelievers'. Ibn Kathir: "for surely, the ultimate victory and triumph will be yours, O believers." Al-Jalalayn: "the final good end will be yours" — the *a'lawn* — the upper — is the verdict of the believers' position: the superiority is the believers' — the *a'lawn* is not the temporary of Uhud but the ultimate of the history.

"if you are ˹true˺ believers" — *in kuntum mu'minin* — if you are believers. The *in* — if — is the condition: the believers' upper position is conditioned on the faith; the *mu'minin* — the believers — are the ones of the *iman* — the true believers. The verse's *in* — if — is the conditional: the *a'lawn* — the upper — is the believers' position *if they are believers* — the *iman* is the condition of the upper hand; the *mu'minin* — the true believers — are the ones who hold the condition. Ibn Kathir: "you will be triumphant if you are indeed believers."

The verse's context: the comfort after the loss of Uhud (the seventy dead, the wound of the Prophet). The believers who suffered the loss are taught: do not weaken, do not grieve; the *a'lawn* — the upper — is your position, if you are believers. The loss of Uhud is not the verdict of the history; the *sunan* of the past (137) — the previous nations suffered losses and the good end was theirs; the *a'lawn* of the believers — the ultimate victory and triumph — is the believers'.

For the reader, the verse is the command against the despair. The believer is taught: the *wahn* — the weakness — and the *huzn* — the grief — are the prohibited: the believers do not falter and do not grieve — the loss is not the end, the defeat is not the verdict. And the *a'lawn* — the upper — is the believers' position: the ultimate victory and triumph is theirs, *if they are believers* — the *in* of the condition — the *iman* is the condition of the upper hand. The believer's lesson from Uhud: the weakness and the grief are the enemies of the *iman*; the *a'lawn* — the superiority — is the believers' — the ultimate, not the temporary; and the *in kuntum mu'minin* — if you are believers — is the condition: the faith is the condition of the upper hand — the believers who hold the *iman* are the ones who are the upper.

## Verse 140

Verse 140 says: *If you have suffered injuries ˹at Uḥud˺, they suffered similarly ˹at Badr˺. We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers, choose martyrs from among you—and Allah does not like the wrongdoers—*

In simple terms, the verse states the divine wisdom of the alternation: if you have suffered injuries at Uhud, they suffered similarly at Badr — We alternate these days among people so that Allah may reveal the true believers and choose martyrs from among you — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers.

The verse states the wisdom of the alternation. Ibn Kathir: "If a wound has touched you, be sure a similar wound has touched the others... And so are the days, that We give to men by turns, that Allah may know (test) those who believe, and that He may take martyrs from among you. And Allah likes not the wrongdoers." The verse's *in yamsaskum qarhun faqad massa al-qawma qarhun mithluhu* — if a wound touches you, a similar wound has touched the people — is the parallel; the *wa tilka al-ayyamu nudawiluha bayna al-nas* — and these days We alternate among people — is the alternation; the *wa liya'lama Allahu alladhina amanu* — so that Allah may know those who believe — is the revelation; the *wa yattakhidha minkum shuhada'* — and take martyrs from among you — is the selection; and the *wa Allahu la yuhibbu al-zalimin* — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers — is the verdict.

"If you have suffered injuries ˹at Uḥud˺, they suffered similarly ˹at Badr˺" — *in yamsaskum qarhun faqad massa al-qawma qarhun mithluhu* — if a wound touches you, a similar wound has touched the people, the enemy. The *qarh* — the wound, the injury — is the suffering: at Uhud the believers suffered the injuries; at Badr the disbelievers suffered the similar wound. Al-Jalalayn: "If a wound touches you, befalls you at Uhud, a like wound already at Badr has touched the other people, the disbelievers." Ibn Kathir: "if you suffered injuries and some of you were killed, then your enemies also suffered injuries and fatalities." The verse's *mithluhu* — similar to it — is the parallel: the wound of the believers and the wound of the disbelievers — the injury of Uhud and the injury of Badr — the two wounds of the two battles.

"We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people" — *wa tilka al-ayyamu nudawiluha bayna al-nas* — and these days We alternate among people, We deal them out in turn. The *dawlah* — the alternation, the turn — is the distribution: the days of the victory and the defeat are alternated among the people. Al-Jalalayn: "We dispense them among mankind, one day for one group, the next day for another." Ibn Kathir: "at times — out of wisdom — We allow the enemy to overcome you, although the final good end will be yours." The verse's *nudawiluha* — We alternate — is the divine: the *ayyam* — the days — of the victory and the defeat — the alternation among the people; the *bayna al-nas* — among the people — is the distribution: the days are not the constant of one group; the victory and the defeat are the alternation.

"so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers" — *wa liya'lama Allahu alladhina amanu* — and so that Allah may know those who believe. The *'ilm* — the knowledge — is the knowing: the *liya'lama* — so that He may know — is the revelation of the believers: Ibn Kathir — "'So that We find out who would be patient while fighting the enemies,' according to Ibn 'Abbas"; Al-Jalalayn — "that God may know through knowledge manifested outwardly those who believe who are sincere in their faith from others." The verse's *alladhina amanu* — those who believe — is the revealed: the alternation reveals the true believers — the patient, the sincere, the steadfast — the ones who are known by the test of the days.

"choose martyrs from among you" — *wa yattakhidha minkum shuhada'* — and that He may take witnesses, martyrs from among you. The *shuhada'* — the martyrs, the witnesses — are the ones killed in the cause: Ibn Kathir — "those who would be killed in Allah's cause and gladly offer their lives seeking His pleasure"; Al-Jalalayn — "honouring them with martyrdom." The verse's *yattakhidha* — He takes — is the selection: the martyrs are taken from among the believers — the ones who are chosen, honored, killed in the cause — the *shuhada'* of the alternation.

"and Allah does not like the wrongdoers" — *wa Allahu la yuhibbu al-zalimin* — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers. The *hubb* — the liking, the love — is the negative: Allah does not like the *zalimin* — the wrongdoers. Al-Jalalayn: "the evildoers, the disbelievers — that is, He will chastise them and the blessings He bestows upon them are only a means of drawing out their punishment." The verse's *la yuhibbu* — does not like — is the verdict: the wrongdoers are not the liked; the *zalimin* — the wrongdoers — the disbelievers — are the ones of the punishment — the blessings of the world are the drawing out of their punishment.

For the reader, the verse is the wisdom of the alternation. The believer is taught: the wound of Uhud — the suffering of the believers — is the parallel of the wound of Badr — the suffering of the disbelievers: the *qarh* of the two, the injuries of the two — the similar wounds. And the days — the victory and the defeat — are the alternation: the *dawlah* of the divine — the days dealt out in turn among the people; the *liya'lama* — so that He may know — the revelation of the true believers: the patients, the sincere, the steadfast — the known by the test; the *shuhada'* — the martyrs — the selected, the honored — the ones of the cause. And the *la yuhibbu al-zalimin* — the wrongdoers not liked — is the verdict: the wrongdoers are the ones of the punishment; the blessings of their world draw out the punishment. The believer's confidence: the alternation is the divine wisdom — the revelation of the believers, the selection of the martyrs, and the verdict against the wrongdoers — the days of the world are the alternation, and the good end is the believers'.
