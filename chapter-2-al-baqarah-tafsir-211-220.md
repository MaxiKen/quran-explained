# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 211–220

This batch turns from principle to the pressure of a real community. It opens by holding up the Children of Israel as a case study in what a people does with favours it has been given (211), and then names the two things that follow from that: the adornment of this life, which makes the prosperous mock the pious (212), and the long history of how one human family became many quarrelling ones — with the diagnosis that the split was never intellectual, it was envy (213). Then the cost of belonging is stated without softening: you will be shaken (214).

From there the surah answers four questions a living community actually asks. What should we spend, and on whom (215)? Why must we fight, when we hate it (216)? Was it right to fight in a sacred month, and what happens to the one who abandons the religion (217) — answered immediately by the verse of hope that follows it (218)? What about wine and gambling, which everyone around us does and which do make money (219)? And what about the orphans in our houses (220)?

Four of these ten verses begin "they ask you." That formula is worth watching across the batch: in every single case the answer quietly moves the questioner off the mechanics and onto what actually matters.

---

## Verse 211

**"Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them. And whoever trades Allah’s favour—after receiving it—˹for disbelief˺ should know that Allah is indeed severe in punishment."**

In simple terms: ask the Children of Israel how many clear proofs We gave them. And whoever exchanges God's favour — after it has come to him — then God is severe in retribution.

*Sal banī isrā'īla kam ātaynāhum min āyatin bayyinah, wa man yubaddil ni'mata Allāhi min ba'di mā jā'athu fa-inna Allāha shadīdu al-'iqāb* — "ask the Children of Israel how many a clear sign We gave them; and whoever exchanges the favour of Allah after it has come to him, then indeed Allah is severe in penalty."

**The command to ask is a rhetorical device, and it is worth pausing on, because its force depends on who is being asked.** God does not need the Children of Israel's testimony, and the Prophet ﷺ was not short of information. The instruction to *ask* means: this is not a matter on which there is any dispute. Ask them. Ask the people who were there. They will tell you how many signs were given, and they will have to tell you what they did with them.

The appeal to the People of the Book as witnesses is a consistent Qur'anic move, and it cuts two ways. It is an argument — "if you are in doubt about what We have sent down to you, ask those who have been reading the Scripture before you" (10:94) — and it is also an argument *against* them, because the same people who can confirm the signs are the ones who rejected them. "Those to whom We gave the Scripture recognise him as they recognise their own sons" (2:146). So their knowledge is being used as evidence, and their knowledge is precisely what condemns them.

***Kam*** here is not a question asking for a number. In Arabic grammar this is *kam al-khabariyyah* — the *kam* of report — which signals profusion: how *many*, how *numerous*, the signs We gave them. The point is not "count them and tell me." The point is: the quantity is beyond counting. Sign upon sign: the parting of the sea, the cloud, the manna and quails, the water from the rock, the succession of prophets, the scripture itself. A people who received a mountain of evidence and then turned on it.

**And notice the proportionality at the heart of the verse.** It does not say "they were given signs and ignored them, so they were left alone." It sets an immense favour directly next to *shadīdu al-'iqāb* — severe in penalty. The severity is not arbitrary; in the logic of the Qur'an, punishment scales with the favour spurned. That is why the Qur'an can be astonishingly gentle with the ignorant and uncompromising with those who knew. The greater the gift, the graver the ingratitude. This is the same principle that makes the hypocrites lower than the open rejecters (4:145) and makes a scholar's slip worse than a layman's.

*Wa man yubaddil ni'mata Allāh* — "whoever exchanges the favour of Allah."

***Yubaddil*** is from *badala*, to exchange or substitute — to take a thing and put something else in its place. The image is of a transaction: the favour was in the person's hand, and he swapped it. Not "lost" it, not "neglected" it. *Traded* it. The word implies deliberate dealing, and the same verb is used of those who "exchanged the favour of Allah for disbelief and led their people to the house of ruin" (14:28) — a verse so close in wording to this one that the commentators routinely read them together.

**What is the *ni'mah*?** The commentators divide, and both readings are worth holding.

The first reading is general: the favour means every gift God gave them — the signs, the deliverance from Pharaoh, the scripture, the succession of prophets, the lawful provision, the shade, the water. Whoever receives any of these and trades it away is under the threat. On this reading the verse is universal and applies to any person or people at any time, and the Children of Israel are the example rather than the limit.

The second reading is specific, and it is reported from Qatādah and others among the early commentators: the favour in question is the greatest one, which is the coming of Muhammad ﷺ — the favour whose arrival they had been awaiting and whose description they knew. On this reading 2:211 is continuous with 2:89 ("when a scripture from Allah came to them confirming what was with them — and before that they had been praying for victory over those who disbelieved — then when what they recognised came to them, they disbelieved in it") and with 2:146. The verse then carries a sharper edge: the favour being traded away is the last and greatest one, and it is being traded after it has actually arrived.

Al-Tabari reports both, and both are coherent. The specific reading gives the verse its historical bite; the general reading gives it its reach. A reader does not have to choose, but he should notice that the supplied translation's gloss — "for disbelief" — is supplying the object of the exchange from the parallel in 14:28, and that the Arabic itself leaves the object unstated. Leaving it unstated is itself the rhetorical stroke: the verse does not name what they traded it *for*, because nothing could be a fair price. Anything on the other side of that trade is loss, whatever it is.

**Now, the placement.** This verse sits immediately after 2:210, which asked what the rejecters are waiting for — whether God will come to them in the shadows of clouds. And 2:209 warned about slipping after the clear proofs have come. So 211 is the third of a sequence: the demand for a definitive sign (210), the warning about slipping (209), and now the historical demonstration that receiving signs does not save anyone. The Children of Israel are invoked here for exactly that reason — they are the proof that signs, in themselves, do not produce faith. They produce accountability.

**A word about how this verse gets misused.** It is sometimes read as a licence for general contempt toward Jews. It is not. The verse is addressed to a specific historical community in the specific context of God's covenant with them, and — more importantly — the Qur'an's own logic makes it a warning *to the Muslims first*, because the favour they had just been given was greater, not smaller. The whole point of "ask the Children of Israel" is that the listeners are meant to learn from the case, not to gloat over it. A Muslim community that reads 2:211 as a verse about someone else has already taken the first step toward being its subject. The Qur'an's own parallel makes this explicit: the command to be grateful is given to the believers too, and 16:112 warns of a town that was secure and provided for from every side, "then it denied the favours of Allah, so Allah made it taste the garment of hunger and fear."

For practice: the question the verse asks a person is not "have you received?" but "what did you do with what you received?" Guidance, provision, health, knowledge, a way out of something — the test is not in the gift but in the trade. *Kufrān al-ni'mah*, the covering-over of a favour, is not mainly a matter of saying the wrong words; it is using the gift against the Giver, or acting as though the gift were self-earned. And the verse's severity is the measure of the generosity that preceded it.

## Verse 212

**"The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers, and they mock the believers. Those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺ will rank above them on the Day of Judgment. And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit."**

In simple terms: the life of this world has been adorned for those who disbelieve, and they ridicule those who believe. But those who are mindful of God will be above them on the Day of Resurrection. And God provides for whom He wills without reckoning.

*Zuyyina li-alladhīna kafarū al-ḥayātu al-dunyā wa yaskharūna min alladhīna āmanū, wa alladhīna ittaqaw fawqahum yawma al-qiyāmah, wa Allāhu yarzuqu man yashā'u bi-ghayri ḥisāb.*

**The first word raises a question that the classical commentators fought over, and the answer matters.** *Zuyyina* is a passive verb: "it has been made fair-seeming." But made fair-seeming by whom?

The Qur'an uses this verb with different agents. Sometimes the agent is Satan: "And Satan made their deeds fair-seeming to them and turned them away from the path" (27:24); "And when Satan made their deeds fair-seeming to them and said, 'No one can overcome you today'" (8:48). Sometimes no agent is named at all, as here and at 3:14 ("the love of desires has been made fair-seeming to people — women, sons, heaped-up gold and silver, branded horses, cattle and tilled land").

So who is it? The discussion runs roughly as follows.

Al-Zamakhshari, working from Mu'tazili premises, insists the agent cannot be God, since God would not beautify disobedience; he takes it as Satan or the self. The Ash'ari and Maturidi answer — and this is the mainstream Sunni position — is a distinction rather than a flat denial: God is the creator of everything, including the adornment and the inclination, since nothing enters existence outside His creation; but the *deceptive persuasion* — the presenting of something harmful as good, the *taghlīt* — is attributed to Satan and to the self, and it is that which the servant is held to account for following. Al-Razi works through the question at length and lands on a version of this: the *zayy* (the adornment itself) is created by God, and the *taghwīr* (the delusion) is from the self and the Devil.

**What this means practically** is that the verse is not saying God made the disbelievers disbelieve. It is describing a situation: the world appears beautiful to them, and that appearance is a test, not a sentence. The believer lives in the same world and sees the same beauty — the Qur'an says so plainly at 3:14, where the same verb is used of people generally, not of disbelievers only. The difference, then, is not that the believer finds the world ugly and the disbeliever finds it beautiful. The difference is **horizon**: for one, the world's beauty is a sign pointing beyond itself; for the other, it is all there is, and so it functions as a veil.

*Wa yaskharūna min alladhīna āmanū* — "and they ridicule those who believe."

**The Qur'an's answer to ridicule is one of the most satisfying pieces of reversal in the whole text, and it is given in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn.** There: "Indeed, those who committed crimes used to laugh at those who believed, and when they passed by them they would wink at one another, and when they returned to their people they would return jesting, and when they saw them they would say, 'Indeed, these are truly astray'" (83:29–32). And then, on the Last Day: "So today those who believed are laughing at the disbelievers, upon adorned couches, looking on" (83:34–35).

Note the closing verb of that passage: *yanẓurūn* — **looking on**. The mockery is not merely inverted; it is answered with the exact structure of the original injury. They used to look at the believers and laugh. On that Day the believers look, and laugh. The Qur'an is not promising petty revenge; it is promising that the looking will finally be from the right side, which is to say that the question of who is deluded will be settled publicly and permanently.

**This is the verse for anyone mocked for their religion.** It is not a verse that tells the believer to stop caring. It tells him the timeline is longer than the joke.

*Wa alladhīna ittaqaw fawqahum yawma al-qiyāmah* — "and those who were mindful will be **above** them on the Day of Resurrection."

**"Above" is primarily rank and station, not geography.** The commentators gloss it as superiority in degree, honour and reward: the God-fearing in the Garden, the mockers in the Fire. Some reports describe a physical elevation on the Day — a barrier, a platform — and these circulate in the tafsir literature, drawing partly on 57:13 ("and a wall will be placed between them with a gate; within it is mercy, and outside of it is punishment"). But the plain and primary sense is *darajah*: rank. The verse answers the specific pain of being looked down on in this life with the promise of being raised in the next. And note that the word used for the believers is *alladhīna ittaqaw* — those who were mindful, those who had *taqwā*. Not "those who were numerous," not "those who were powerful," not "those who were clever." The surah's governing word again.

*Wa Allāhu yarzuqu man yashā'u bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — "and Allah provides for whom He wills without reckoning."

**This clause is the verse's quiet masterstroke, and it is doing two jobs at once.**

The first job is to defuse envy. If the disbelievers are prosperous and the believers are not, the natural human conclusion is that prosperity is approval. The verse denies the inference directly: provision is distributed by will, not by merit, and *bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — without reckoning, without measure, without account. God gives to whom He wills, and the giving is not a verdict. So a Muslim who sees wealth in the hands of someone who does not acknowledge God is being told: this is provision, not endorsement. And by the same logic, a Muslim who has little is being told: this is not abandonment.

The second job is to describe the Hereafter. *Bi-ghayri ḥisāb* is used elsewhere for the reward of the patient — "the patient will be given their reward in full, without reckoning" (39:10) — and for the provision that came to Mary: Zakariyyā finds provision with her in the sanctuary and asks where it came from, and she answers, "It is from Allah; indeed, Allah provides for whom He wills without account" (3:37). That cross-reference is worth holding, because it shows what unaccounted provision looks like: **unearned, undeserved, arriving from a direction you did not arrange.** Mary's provision is the emblem of the phrase. And it is the exact opposite of the mockers' situation, who have provision and no connection to the Provider.

**A caution about reading this socially.** The verse is not a call to poverty, and it is not a claim that wealth is disgraceful. The Qur'an calls wealth *khayr* — good — in places (2:180), and Solomon's dominion is described without censure. What this verse dismantles is the *inference from provision to favour*, and the *inference from deprivation to disfavour*. Both inferences are wrong, and both are epidemic.

For practice, three things follow. Do not read your statement of account as a statement of your standing with God, in either direction. Do not treat the prosperity of someone who ignores God as evidence that ignoring God works — the verse says explicitly that this is exactly what it will look like. And do not treat being ridiculed for the religion as a setback; the verse names it, predicts it, and answers it with the Day on which the looking is reversed.

## Verse 213

**"Humanity had once been one community ˹of believers before they lost faith˺. Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners, and revealed to them the Scriptures in truth to judge among people regarding their disputes. And no one disputed the Scriptures except the very people who received them after clear proofs had come to them—out of jealousy. Then Allah, by His grace, has guided the believers to the truth regarding those disputes. And Allah guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path."**

In simple terms: mankind was one community, then God sent the prophets as bearers of glad tidings and warners, and sent down with them the scripture in truth so that it might judge between people concerning that in which they differed. And none differed concerning it except those who had been given it, after the clear proofs had come to them, through envy among themselves. Then God guided those who believed to the truth of that concerning which they differed, by His permission. And God guides whom He wills to a straight path.

*Kāna al-nāsu ummatan wāḥidatan fa-ba'atha Allāhu al-nabiyyīna mubashshirīna wa mundhirīna wa anzala ma'ahumu al-kitāba bi-al-ḥaqqi li-yaḥkuma bayna al-nāsi fī-mā ikhtalafū fīhi, wa mā ikhtalafa fīhi illā alladhīna ūtūhu min ba'di mā jā'athumu al-bayyinātu baghyan baynahum, fa-hadā Allāhu alladhīna āmanū li-mā ikhtalafū fīhi min al-ḥaqqi bi-idhnih, wa Allāhu yahdī man yashā'u ilā ṣirātin mustaqīm.*

**This is one of the great anthropological verses of the Qur'an, and it opens with a sentence the commentators genuinely disagreed about.**

*Kāna al-nāsu ummatan wāḥidatan* — "mankind was one community." One community *upon what*?

**The majority reading** — reported from Ibn 'Abbās, Mujāhid, Qatādah and others among the early authorities, and followed by the majority of the commentators — is that they were one community **upon the truth**, upon one religion of pure monotheism, and that they subsequently differed, and that God sent the prophets in response to that difference. On this reading the verse describes a golden age, a fall into disagreement, and then the divine remedy.

**The second reading** — reported from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and in a variant report also from Ibn 'Abbās — is that they were one community **upon misguidance**, and that God sent the prophets to bring them out of it. On this reading the oneness is a oneness of error, and prophecy is not a repair but a first rescue.

A third strand in the literature places the unity specifically between Adam and Noah — a report to the effect that generations passed in which everyone was upon Islam, and that the first difference arose afterwards. This narration is transmitted in a number of works and its grading is discussed; it should be treated as a report, not as established fact.

**Which is right?** Al-Tabari lays out the disagreement and treats the question as open, and the honest thing is to say that the verse's own wording does not settle it: "one community" tells you they were united; it does not tell you what they were united on. **What matters is that the supplied translation has made a choice here** — its bracketed gloss "of believers before they lost faith" adopts the majority reading and makes it explicit in the translation. That is a legitimate and well-attested reading. But a reader should know that the Arabic is terser than the translation, and that the alternative exists, because the alternative changes the verse's picture of human nature: on the first reading, human beings start in truth and fall into division; on the second, they start benighted and are called out. The mainstream reading, and the one the translation follows, is the former. Both hold that division is the *problem*.

*Fa-ba'atha Allāhu al-nabiyyīna mubashshirīna wa mundhirīna* — "then Allah raised up the prophets as bearers of glad tidings and as warners."

**The pairing is constant in the Qur'an and it is a complete theory of religious address.** Glad tidings alone produces a soft religion with no accounting. Warning alone produces terror with no attraction. Every messenger is sent with both, and the Prophet ﷺ is described in exactly these terms: "Indeed, We have sent you with the truth as a bringer of glad tidings and a warner" (2:119; also 35:24: "We have sent you with the truth as a bringer of glad tidings and a warner, and there was no community but a warner passed among it"). Anyone who preaches one half of this pair has departed from the prophetic pattern.

*Wa anzala ma'ahumu al-kitāba bi-al-ḥaqqi li-yaḥkuma bayna al-nāsi fī-mā ikhtalafū fīhi* — "and He sent down with them the scripture in truth, **to judge between the people concerning that in which they differed**."

**This is the load-bearing clause of the verse, and it says something very specific about what revelation is *for*.** The scripture is not sent as private inspiration, and not as a body of optional wisdom. It is sent *to adjudicate*. Its function is to settle disputes. This is why the Islamic tradition could speak of revelation as *ḥujjah* — binding proof — and why the whole science of *uṣūl al-fiqh* is a science of deriving judgements from texts: because the premise from the outset is that God has not left the disputed matters to be settled by human consensus alone.

A small grammatical note that carries weight: the pronoun in *li-yaḥkuma* ("so that it/he may judge") could refer to the scripture, to God, or to the prophet. The closest antecedent is *al-kitāb*, and most commentators take the Book as the subject — **the scripture itself is the arbiter**. Others read it as referring to God, who judges by means of what He sent down. Both arrive at the same practical conclusion: when people differ, the reference is revelation, not the balance of opinion.

**Now the verse's sharpest sentence.**

*Wa mā ikhtalafa fīhi illā alladhīna ūtūhu min ba'di mā jā'athumu al-bayyinātu baghyan baynahum* — "and none differed concerning it except those who had been given it, after the clear proofs had come to them, out of envy among themselves."

Read the three qualifications slowly, because the verse is building a case:

1. **Not outsiders.** The ones who differed are *alladhīna ūtūhu* — the very people who **received** the scripture. Division did not come from the ignorant failing to understand. It came from the inside.
2. **Not before clarity.** *Min ba'di mā jā'athumu al-bayyināt* — **after** the clear proofs had come to them. Not in the fog of early uncertainty. After the evidence was complete.
3. **Not from doubt.** *Baghyan baynahum* — out of envy, out of the desire to dominate one another, out of transgression between themselves.

**That third clause is the diagnosis, and it is devastating.** The verse does not say they differed because the text was ambiguous, or because they lacked intelligence, or because their evidence was conflicting. It says they differed because of *baghy* — a moral disease. Religious schism is here diagnosed as a failure of character, not of intellect. Which means the remedy for sectarianism cannot be more information. You cannot argue a person out of a position he did not argue his way into.

**The parallel that clinches this is Sūrat al-Bayyinah.** There: "And those who were given the Scripture did not become divided except after the clear proof had come to them" (98:4) — the same clause, almost word for word. And immediately: "And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, sincere to Him in religion, upright, and to establish prayer and give zakāh — and that is the religion of correct judgement" (98:5). So the same verse-sequence that names the disease names the cure: *ikhlāṣ*, sincerity. Division comes from *baghy*; unity comes from *ikhlāṣ*. The one is the exact negation of the other.

*Fa-hadā Allāhu alladhīna āmanū li-mā ikhtalafū fīhi min al-ḥaqqi bi-idhnih* — "then Allah guided those who believed to the truth of that concerning which they differed, by His permission."

**Two words to hold here.**

*Bi-idhnihi* — **by His permission.** Guidance is attributed to God and conditioned on His leave. This is not a detail; it is the refutation of the idea that a person guides himself to the truth and then deserves credit for it. The believer's orthodoxy is a gift before it is an achievement, which is exactly why it cannot become a ground for pride — and pride is *baghy*, which is the disease the verse just diagnosed.

And *li-mā ikhtalafū fīhi min al-ḥaqq* — the believers were guided to **the truth of the disputed matter**. Not "they won the argument." They were shown what was actually true about the thing everyone was fighting over.

**A hadith that the commentators frequently place here, and which is well-attested.** Recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah: the Prophet ﷺ said that the Muslim community is the last in this world and the first to be judged on the Day of Resurrection, and that Friday is the day "about which those before us differed — the Jews took Saturday and the Christians took Sunday — and Allah guided us to it." That is 2:213 in a single sentence: a matter over which communities divided, and believers guided to the correct one.

**Finally: the necessary safeguard.** The verse condemns a specific kind of difference — division driven by *baghy*, after clarity, among those who received the truth. It does **not** condemn every instance of disagreement. The tradition has always distinguished *ikhtilāf madhmūm*, the blameworthy division this verse describes, from *ikhtilāf maḥmūd*, the disagreement of qualified jurists over matters the texts genuinely leave open — the kind discussed earlier in this surah, where the mujtahid who errs is still rewarded. Collapsing the two is one of the most damaging mistakes a reader can make with this verse: it would make every juristic difference a symptom of envy, which is plainly false, and it would also make every sectarian split a matter of honest reasoning, which this verse plainly denies.

For practice: whenever a community splits, ask the verse's three questions. Who is doing the splitting — those entrusted with the text, or those outside it? Did the split come after the matter was clear? And what is the actual motive — the text, or the standing of the people arguing about it? The verse gives you the diagnostic, and it is uncomfortable because it points at the most religious people in the room.

## Verse 214

**"Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, “When will Allah’s help come?” Indeed, Allah’s help is ˹always˺ near."**

In simple terms: or did you suppose that you would enter Paradise while there has not yet come to you the like of what came to those who passed away before you? Affliction and hardship touched them, and they were shaken, until the Messenger and those who believed with him said, "When is the help of Allah?" Indeed, the help of Allah is near.

*Am ḥasibtum an tadkhulū al-jannata wa lammā ya'tikum mathalu alladhīna khalaw min qablikum, massathumu al-ba'sā'u wa al-ḍarrā'u wa zulzilū ḥattā yaqūla al-rasūlu wa alladhīna āmanū ma'ahu matā naṣru Allāh, alā inna naṣra Allāhi qarīb.*

**The verse opens with a rebuke addressed at an assumption, and the assumption is worth naming: that declaring faith is the finish line.**

The parallel that makes this unmistakable is Sūrat al-'Ankabūt: "Do the people suppose that they will be left alone because they say 'we believe,' and they will not be tested? And We certainly tested those who were before them" (29:2–3). The same interrogative of rebuke, the same verb, the same reference to those before. The Qur'an is correcting a specific and very human miscalculation — that the hard part was becoming Muslim, and that what follows is the reward.

Two further parallels, both from the Madinan period and both using the same formula: "Or did you suppose that you would enter Paradise while Allah has not yet made evident those of you who strove and those who were steadfast?" (3:142), and "Or did you suppose that you would be left alone while Allah has not yet made evident those of you who strive" (9:16). The recurring word is *yet* — the test is not behind you.

*Massathumu al-ba'sā'u wa al-ḍarrā'u* — "affliction and adversity touched them."

**The two words are usually glossed as poverty and illness.** The standard reading, reported from the early commentators, is that *al-ba'sā'* is want — poverty, loss of property — and *al-ḍarrā'* is harm to the body — sickness, injury. Some reverse the pair. The difference is minor; the pairing is not. The verse names the two areas where human beings feel most exposed: **their means and their bodies.** Not their opinions. Trials of wealth and trials of health.

*Wa zulzilū* — "and they were shaken." From *zalzala*, the root of *zilzāl*, earthquake. The image is of a ground that will not hold still.

**And there is a verbatim parallel that tells us what this looked like in practice.** At the Battle of the Trench: "There the believers were tested, and shaken with a severe shaking. And when the hypocrites and those in whose hearts is a disease said, 'Allah and His Messenger did not promise us except delusion'" (33:11). The same verb — *zulzilū* — with the intensifier *shadīdan*. And two verses earlier: "when eyes shifted and hearts reached the throats, and you assumed about Allah various assumptions" (33:10).

**This is why the Battle of the Trench is reported by a number of commentators as the occasion of this verse**, and I should be careful about how much weight that bears. The verse's own grammar is about *past* communities — "the like of what came to those who passed away before you," and "the Messenger and those who believed with him" is *their* messenger. So the literal referent is the nations before. The connection to the Trench is by application and resemblance: the Prophet's community was about to be shaken in exactly this way, and the verse prepared them for it with the precedent of everyone who came before. The report linking the verse to the Trench is transmitted — it appears in the works of *asbāb al-nuzūl* — but it should be read as identifying the verse's first application rather than its literal subject.

**Now the sentence that most needs care.**

*Ḥattā yaqūla al-rasūlu wa alladhīna āmanū ma'ahu matā naṣru Allāh* — "until the Messenger and those who believed with him said, 'When is the help of Allah?'"

**The common misunderstanding is that this is a cry of doubt, and it must be answered directly, because the alternative misreading would be serious.** It is not. The commentators are emphatic on this, and the grammar is on their side: the question is *matā*, **when** — not *hal*, **whether**. It presupposes that the help is coming. It asks about the timing. This is *istibtā'* — asking for something to be brought forward — which is a form of supplication uttered in extremity, and it is the cry of people who believe the promise and are worn out waiting for it.

**And here is the verse's extraordinary mercy: the cry is recorded and not corrected.** The verse does not add "and they were wrong to say it." It reports the question and answers it. The Prophet ﷺ himself, in the hardship, said "when?" — and the Qur'an preserved it as the speech of the faithful in their darkest hour. A person whose patience finally breaks and who lets out "how much longer?" has not stepped outside the religion. He has joined a very old company, and the answer that came to them is the answer that comes to him.

*Alā inna naṣra Allāhi qarīb* — "indeed, the help of Allah is near."

**Notice what the answer does and does not do.** It does not give a date. It does not say "the help will come." It says the help **is** near — a nominal sentence, present tense, already close. The question "when?" is answered with proximity rather than with a calendar, and that is deliberate: a date would have put the believers back to counting, whereas nearness puts them back to watching. *Qarīb* is the operative word, and it is why the verse is one of the most quoted in the tradition during times of collective hardship.

**The hadith that best illustrates the verse, and it is in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*.** Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt said: we complained to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ while he was resting against his cloak in the shade of the Ka'bah. We said to him, "Will you not seek victory for us? Will you not supplicate for us?" He said: "Among those before you, a man would be taken and a pit dug for him, and he would be placed in it, and a saw would be brought and put on his head and he would be sawed in two — and that would not turn him from his religion. And a man would be combed with iron combs that tore his flesh from his bone and sinew — and that would not turn him from his religion. By Allah, this matter will be completed until a rider travels from Ṣan'ā' to Ḥaḍramawt fearing nothing but Allah, and the wolf for his sheep — **but you are impatient.**"

Two things in that hadith belong directly to this verse. The first is the comparative perspective: the Prophet ﷺ answered a complaint about present suffering with the sufferings of those before. That is precisely 2:214's method — "the like of what came to those who passed away before you." The second is the closing rebuke, *but you are impatient*, which is the same rebuke as the verse's opening question: did you suppose you would enter Paradise without this?

**The theology underneath.** The verse establishes a *sunnah* — a consistent divine pattern — that faith is tested, and that the test is not evidence of abandonment. This matters enormously for how a believer interprets his own hardship. The default human reading of suffering is "God has left me." The Qur'an's reading here is "this is the path everyone before you walked, and the ones who walked it entered the Garden." The trial is the road, not the detour.

**And note where the verse sits.** It follows 213, which described how communities split and how God guided the believers to the truth of the disputed matters. The sequence is psychologically exact: first the community is told it has been guided to the truth, and then, immediately, it is told that being guided to the truth will cost it. Guidance does not exempt anyone from the shaking. If anything, it guarantees it, because the truth has enemies (217 will say so in the plainest terms).

For practice, the verse gives permission and gives a horizon. The permission: it is not a failure of faith to feel the pressure, to be shaken, or to ask when relief is coming. The recording of the cry *is* the permission. The horizon: the answer to "when?" is "near," and the comparative perspective — look at what those before you bore — is not a way of minimising your pain but a way of resizing it. In a world where communities are displaced, imprisoned, bombed, and slandered, this verse has never been more directly applicable, and it is addressed to exactly those people.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 215

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say, “Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.”"**

In simple terms: they ask you what they should spend. Say: whatever you spend of good, it is for parents, near kin, orphans, the needy, and the wayfarer. And whatever good you do, Allah knows it.

*Yas'alūnaka mādhā yunfiqūn, qul mā anfaqtum min khayrin fa-li-al-wālidāyni wa al-aqrabīna wa al-yatāmā wa al-masākīni wa ibni al-sabīl, wa mā taf'alū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha bihi 'alīm.*

**The first thing to notice is that the question and the answer do not match, and the mismatch is the point.**

They asked *mādhā yunfiqūn* — **what** should they spend. The answer names *recipients*. Not amounts, not kinds of wealth, not percentages. **To whom.**

The commentators of rhetoric made much of this, and their explanations vary in a useful way. One says the questioner's *mā* ("what") really meant *kayfa* ("how") — that the question was never about the substance of the gift, since everyone knows you give wealth, but about the manner and destination of it. Another, sharper reading says the answer deliberately redirects: the *amount* is a matter between the servant and his Lord and admits of no single ruling, whereas the *destination* is a matter that genuinely needs specification. Tabātabā'ī surveys these and notes that the answer does touch the question indirectly through the two phrases *min khayr* ("of good") and *mā taf'alū min khayr* ("whatever good you do").

**Either way, the practical upshot is the same and it is the verse's first lesson: the important question about charity is not how much. It is where.** The person who gives a spectacular amount to the wrong place, or in the wrong spirit, or while his own mother is in want, has asked the wrong question.

**The second thing to notice is the list, because this list already appeared in this surah.** At 2:83, in the covenant taken from the Children of Israel: "worship none but Allah, and to parents do good, and to near kin, and to orphans, and to the needy." The identical sequence — parents, kin, orphans, needy — with the wayfarer added here. And a fuller version at 4:36: parents, near kin, orphans, the needy, the near neighbour, the farther neighbour, the companion at your side, and the wayfarer.

**So the Muslims are being given the same charitable charter that was given to the Children of Israel, which is itself a point about the continuity of the religion.** And note the ordering: **parents first.** Charity does not begin with the stranger. It begins at the table you were raised at.

*Min khayrin* — "of good." Not "of wealth."

**The qualifier is doing quiet work.** The verse does not say "whatever you spend of your money." It says "of *good*." Wealth handed over is not automatically a good act, and the word inserts the condition into the sentence. It also opens the door beyond money — a good *act* is covered by the closing clause, and the commentators note that the verse's final sentence widens charity to every good deed.

*Fa-li-al-wālidāyni wa al-aqrabīna* — a small grammatical note worth having: the *fa* attached to the predicate gives the sentence its conditional force. "Whatever you spend of good — it belongs to these." The *li* is entitlement and destination: this is where it is meant to go. It is not a command to divide everything equally among five categories.

**The five categories:**

*Al-wālidāyn* — the two parents. *Al-aqrabīn* — near relatives, kin. *Al-yatāmā* — orphans, meaning in Arabic a child whose father has died and who has not reached maturity; the word's root carries the sense of being alone and cut off. *Al-masākīn* — the poor, the destitute, from *sakana*, to be still: those brought low and held there. *Ibn al-sabīl* — literally "son of the road," the traveller: the person cut off from his resources while away from home, who may be wealthy at home and destitute here. That last category is a stroke of moral imagination — it establishes a claim on the stranger with no connection to you except need.

**Is this zakāh or voluntary charity?** Voluntary charity, and that is the sounder reading, for two reasons. First, the eight categories and the fixed rates of zakāh were set out later, in Sūrat al-Tawbah (9:60), and this list does not match them. Second, the commentators say so: Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān is reported as saying this verse concerns voluntary charity, and Ibn 'Abbās and Mujāhid are reported as reading the question as "how should they spend."

**Is it abrogated by zakāh?** This is reported as a genuine disagreement. Some said the ruling was superseded once zakāh was made obligatory with its defined recipients. Al-Tabari reports the claim and then argues against it, on the ground that everyone agrees a person may still give voluntary charity, give gifts, and bequeath up to a third — so the verse's guidance cannot simply have been cancelled. The stronger view, and the practical one, is that the verse continues to govern *ṣadaqah*, voluntary giving, while zakāh has its own thresholds and rates.

**On the obligation to support parents and needy kin**, the jurists are agreed on the principle and differ on the perimeter: a solvent child is obliged to maintain indigent parents, and maintenance extends to certain near relatives; the schools differ over which relatives, over whether the obligation is shared among multiple children and in what proportions, and over the conditions attached (such as whether a non-Muslim parent is included — the majority say no, with differences). The principle is established; the details are school-specific.

**A hadith that supplies the ordering.** Reported in the Sunan collections: a man asked the Prophet ﷺ who is most deserving of my good company, and he said, "Your mother"; the man asked who next, and he said, "Your mother"; who next — "Your mother"; who next — "Your father"; then the nearest and the nearest. The overwhelmingly attested version in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* is the one in which the Prophet ﷺ was asked who is most deserving of good treatment and answered "your mother," three times, then "your father," then the next nearest. This is the commentary on 2:215's opening word.

**The closing clause:** *wa mā taf'alū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha bihi 'alīm* — "and whatever good you do, Allah is knowing of it."

Note the shift from *spending* to *doing*. The verse began with money and ends with deeds, which means charity has been quietly widened to include everything a person does that is good: a word, a visit, a lift, a reconciling between two people, an hour. And the assurance attached is *'alīm* — knowing. This is the answer to the worry that most often stops people giving: does it matter? Is my small, anonymous, unremarkable contribution lost? The verse says it is known in detail. Nothing given is mislaid.

**A transmitted occasion, with its uncertainty flagged.** It is reported that the questioner was 'Amr ibn al-Jamūḥ, an elderly chief of the Anṣār, who asked about spending. Reports differ about which of the two "what should they spend" verses this occasioned — this one, or the second half of 219 — and some accounts connect it specifically to spending in the cause of the struggle. The report is transmitted in the tafsir literature and should be treated as a report rather than as firmly established.

**What this looks like now.** The verse's first application is domestic and uncomfortable: the person who gives generously and publicly while a parent is quietly short of money has inverted the list. Its second application is to the stranger — the migrant, the stranded traveller, the refugee — who has no claim on you except that he is on the road and you are not. And its third is to the anonymous good: the verse's closing promise is precisely for the giving nobody sees, which is why the tradition placed such weight on concealment in charity.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 216

**"Fighting has been made obligatory upon you ˹believers˺, though you dislike it. Perhaps you dislike something which is good for you and like something which is bad for you. Allah knows and you do not know."**

In simple terms: fighting has been prescribed for you, though it is hateful to you. And it may be that you dislike a thing while it is good for you, and it may be that you love a thing while it is bad for you. And Allah knows, and you do not know.

*Kutiba 'alaykumu al-qitālu wa huwa kurhun lakum, wa 'asā an takrahū shay'an wa huwa khayrun lakum, wa 'asā an tuḥibbū shay'an wa huwa sharrun lakum, wa Allāhu ya'lamu wa antum lā ta'lamūn.*

**The first word connects this verse to two others in this surah, and the connection is the best commentary on it.**

*Kutiba* — "it has been prescribed." The same verb, in the same form, was used for **retaliation**: "prescribed for you is legal retribution in cases of murder" (2:178). And for **fasting**: "prescribed for you is the fast, as it was prescribed for those before you" (2:183). Three things are *kutiba 'alaykum* in al-Baqarah — the lex talionis, the fast, and fighting.

**Look at what those three have in common: every one of them is something the self resists.** The soul wants revenge, not measured equivalence. The soul wants to eat and drink. The soul wants to stay home. The surah uses the verb of *prescription* for exactly the obligations that nature will not produce on its own. That is the structure of the thing: the law is where the self would not go by itself.

**And notice something unique.** This is the only one of the three where the text itself names the reluctance. 2:178 does not say "though you resent the equal retaliation." 2:183 does not say "though the fast is hateful to you." But 2:216 says, in so many words: *wa huwa kurhun lakum* — and it is hateful to you.

**That is an extraordinary piece of honesty, and it is one of the strongest internal marks of the Qur'an's reliability.** A document invented to mobilise a small community for war does not open the war-verse by telling the soldiers that the soldiers find it hateful. A scripture written to flatter its followers does not concede that God's command is distasteful to them. It says it, plainly, and then gives the reason.

*Kurhun* — a lexical note: the word is read with a *ḍamma* on the kāf. The lexicographers discuss *kurh* and *karh* as near-synonyms for what is disliked or done under compulsion, with some distinguishing *karh* (with *fatḥa*) as what one dislikes and *kurh* (with *ḍamma*) as what one is forced into. Both senses are present here: it is disliked, and it is undertaken under a compulsion that is moral rather than physical — the compulsion of a command.

*Wa 'asā an takrahū shay'an wa huwa khayrun lakum* — "and it may be that you dislike a thing while it is good for you."

**A point of creed attached to the word *'asā*.** For a human being, *'asā* expresses hope or expectation. **From God it cannot express doubt**, since doubt about the outcome is impossible for One who knows it. The commentators therefore read *'asā* and *la'alla* in God's speech as indicating something settled and near — a promise dressed in the language of possibility, which softens it and, in the view of some, keeps the servant between fear and hope rather than in complacency. Either way, the practical sense is: this will happen, and it is close.

**The verse's engine is its last clause:** *wa Allāhu ya'lamu wa antum lā ta'lamūn* — "and Allah knows, and you do not know."

This is the ground of the whole obligation, and it is the same ground the surah laid at the very beginning. The God-fearing are "those who believe in the unseen" (2:3). The angels were told, when they questioned the creation of man, "I know what you do not know" (2:30). Adam was given the names that the angels did not have (2:31–33). And God reminded the believers: "He teaches you what you did not know" (2:151). **The entire architecture of obedience in this religion rests on an asymmetry of knowledge, and 2:216 makes that asymmetry the explicit reason for a command.**

This matters because it tells you what kind of submission is being asked for. It is not "obey because it makes sense to you." It is "obey because the One commanding sees the whole board, and you see a corner of it." And the verse is careful to hold both halves: it gives a *reason* (the thing you dislike may be good for you) and then it says the reason is not the real foundation — the real foundation is who knows.

**On the ruling itself.** The obligation of fighting is, in the juristic classification, *farḍ kifāyah* — a collective obligation: if a sufficient number of the community undertake it, the rest are absolved. It becomes *farḍ 'ayn* — an individual obligation on each person able to bear it — in defined circumstances: when the ruler calls for a general mobilisation, when the enemy enters Muslim territory, or when a person is present in the ranks and the lines have met. This is the standard position of the schools, with differences in the details and in the conditions attached.

Al-Zuhrī's formulation, which the commentators quote with approval, is worth having because it closes the escape-hatch: fighting is required of every person, whether he goes out or stays behind — whoever stays behind is required to give support if support is warranted, to render aid if aid is needed, and to march out if he is commanded to; and if he is not needed, he stays. **No one is simply off the hook.**

**Two hadith that belong to this verse.** In *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, from Ibn 'Umar: the Prophet ﷺ said, "Whoever dies without having fought, and without having resolved in himself to fight, has died upon a branch of hypocrisy" — a variant wording in other transmissions reads "a death of the pre-Islamic period." Note the second clause: *and without having resolved in himself to fight*. The hadith is not only about the person who goes; it is about the person who never forms the intention, and it makes the inner resolution a matter of consequence.

And recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim: the Prophet ﷺ said on the day of the conquest of Makkah, "There is no emigration after the conquest, but struggle and intention; and if you are called to march out, then march out." The specific emigration — to Madinah — had closed. The obligation of responding had not.

**The necessary boundary, and it cannot be skipped.** This verse is not a licence for aggression, and it cannot be read in isolation. It must be read with the passage that immediately precedes it in this surah: "Fight in the cause of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress; indeed, Allah does not love transgressors" (2:190); "and fight them until there is no persecution and the religion is for Allah; but if they cease, then there is no hostility except against the wrongdoers" (2:193); and "if they incline to peace, then incline to it" (8:61). The obligation established here operates inside that framework — a restrained, limited, non-transgressive engagement. A reading of 2:216 detached from 2:190 has produced, and continues to produce, exactly the distortion the critics of the religion allege.

**And a second misunderstanding, in the opposite direction.** The verse does not praise dislike. It *names* dislike as a fact of human nature and does not ask anyone to pretend otherwise. The virtue in the verse is obedience while disliking, not the dislike itself. A person who enjoys violence has not fulfilled this verse; he has failed its premise.

**What it means for someone who is not a soldier.** The principle is far wider than its instance, and the verse's own wording invites the extension: *'asā an takrahū shay'an wa huwa khayrun lakum*. The surgery. The confrontation with a person you love. The decision to leave the job, the house, the relationship. The boundary you have to set. The apology you have to make. Almost everything a human being most needs to do is something he would rather not do, and the verse gives the reason in four words: **Allah knows; you do not.**

And at the level of a community: a people that avoids a necessary struggle because it is unpleasant frequently pays a far heavier price later. Ibn Kathīr makes exactly this point — that refraining from the struggle can lead to the enemy taking over the land and the government. The thing you refuse because it is hateful is often the thing whose avoidance is worse.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 217

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months. Say, “Fighting during these months is a great sin. But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing. And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can. And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”"**

In simple terms: they ask you about the sacred month, about fighting in it. Say: fighting in it is a great sin — but hindering from the path of Allah, and disbelief in Him, and [hindering from] the Sacred Mosque, and the expelling of its people from it, is greater in the sight of Allah. And persecution is greater than killing. And they will not cease fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they are able. And whoever of you turns back from his religion and dies as a disbeliever — those, their deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter, and those are the companions of the Fire; they abide in it forever.

*Yas'alūnaka 'an al-shahri al-ḥarāmi qitālin fīhi, qul qitālun fīhi kabīr, wa ṣaddun 'an sabīli Allāhi wa kufrun bihi wa al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa ikhrāju ahlihi minhu akbaru 'inda Allāh, wa al-fitnatu akbaru min al-qatl, wa lā yazālūna yuqātilūnakum ḥattā yaruddūkum 'an dīnikum in istaṭā'ū, wa man yartadid minkum 'an dīnihi fa-yamut wa huwa kāfirun fa-ulā'ika ḥabiṭat a'māluhum fī al-dunyā wa al-ākhirati, wa ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn.*

**This verse must be read exactly, because it is the one most often quoted out of its own grammar.**

*Qul qitālun fīhi kabīrun* — "Say: fighting in it is a great [sin]."

**The verse never says it is permitted.** Read that again. The question was "may we fight in the sacred month?" and the answer given is "fighting in it is great." The word *kabīr* stays. It is not withdrawn, not softened, not qualified out of existence. The verse then says something else is **greater** — *akbaru 'inda Allāh*.

**This is the single most important structural fact about 2:217, and it completely changes how it should be used.** The Qur'an does not sanitise the act of its own community. It concedes the gravity in the first clause, then establishes a hierarchy. A person who quotes this verse to say "Islam permits fighting in the sacred months" has skipped the first clause, which is the one that says it is a great sin.

**What are the sacred months?** Four: Rajab, Dhū al-Qa'dah, Dhū al-Ḥijjah and Muḥarram — three consecutive and one alone. Recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* from Abū Bakrah, in the report of the Farewell Pilgrimage: the Prophet ﷺ said that time has completed its cycle as it was the day God created the heavens and the earth, the year is twelve months, of which four are sacred — three in succession, Dhū al-Qa'dah, Dhū al-Ḥijjah and Muḥarram, and Rajab of Muḍar, which lies between Jumādā and Sha'bān. The sanctity predates Islam in Arab practice and was confirmed by it; 9:36 states the four sacred months as part of the religion.

**Was the prohibition of fighting in them abrogated?** Genuine disagreement. The majority of the jurists hold that the sanctity remains, so that initiating fighting in a sacred month is not lawful, while fighting in them defensively is — the reasoning being that the one who attacks you has already broken the month's sanctity, and the sanctity attached to repelling him is not less. Others, mostly among the earlier authorities, held that the prohibition was abrogated. The disagreement should be reported as a disagreement; the majority view is the one that preserves the plain sense of *qitālun fīhi kabīr*.

**Now the four crimes named as greater:**

1. *Ṣaddun 'an sabīli Allāh* — hindering people from the path of God; blocking the way to the religion.
2. *Kufrun bihi* — disbelief in Him.
3. *Al-masjidi al-ḥarām* — grammatically carried on the first item: hindering from the Sacred Mosque; blocking access to it.
4. *Wa ikhrāju ahlihi minhu* — and the driving out of its people from it: the expulsion of the Prophet ﷺ and the believers from Makkah.

*Akbaru 'inda Allāh* — **greater in the sight of Allah.** The standard of comparison is divine. This is not a utilitarian calculation performed by human beings weighing two harms; it is a revealed ordering of gravity. That distinction matters, because a "lesser of two evils" argument is always available to whoever is doing the calculating, whereas an announced divine hierarchy constrains everyone.

**And here is the verse's moral centre, which is easy to miss.** The Qur'an concedes the gravity of **its own community's** error before it names the other side's crime. It does not say "what they did was worse, so what you did was fine." It says: what you did was great, and what they did was greater. **Self-criticism precedes accusation.** The rhetorical authority of the entire verse depends on that ordering — the Qur'an can credibly name the Quraysh's crimes precisely because it has already refused to exonerate its own people.

*Wa al-fitnatu akbaru min al-qatl* — "and persecution is greater than killing."

**This clause appeared earlier in this surah in almost the same words:** "and persecution is worse than killing" (2:191), using *ashaddu* where this verse uses *akbaru*. The pairing is deliberate, and the meaning of *fitnah* here should be settled by it: **persecution, not disbelief-as-such.** This is the same conclusion reached in the earlier discussion of 2:191–193, and it is settled by the Qur'an's own usage elsewhere — "there is no compulsion in religion" (2:256) rules out the reading that would make fighting a matter of eliminating disbelief; 60:8 explicitly commands kindness and justice toward those who have not fought you on account of religion and have not driven you from your homes. *Fitnah* here is the active, organised oppression by which a people is turned away from its religion by force and pressure. And the verse's own next clause says exactly what that *fitnah* consists of.

*Wa lā yazālūna yuqātilūnakum ḥattā yaruddūkum 'an dīnikum in istaṭā'ū* — "and they will not cease fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they are able."

**Two things here.** The first is a diagnosis of the enemy's objective: this is not a border dispute and not a trade dispute. The aim is the religion itself. Which is why no territorial or economic concession would have ended it — a point of enormous practical importance, because the instinct of a beleaguered community is always to look for the price that will buy quiet. The verse says there isn't one.

The second is the four-word parenthesis: *in istaṭā'ū* — **if they are able.** The condition carries the reassurance. The determination is total; the capacity is not. The verse states the threat and, in the same breath, limits it.

*Wa man yartadid minkum 'an dīnihi fa-yamut wa huwa kāfirun* — "and whoever of you turns back from his religion **and dies while he is a disbeliever**."

**This conditional clause is where the most consequential misunderstanding sits, and it must be stated with total clarity.**

The voiding of deeds is predicated on **two** things: renouncing the religion, **and dying in that state**. The verse does not say the apostate's deeds are void at the moment of apostasy. It says they are void if he dies as a disbeliever. The condition is explicit in the text, and every school of law and theology accepts its implication: **a person who renounces the religion and then returns before his death has not met the condition.** Repentance is open. The door is not shut by this verse, and it is not shut by any verse in the Qur'an.

*Fa-ulā'ika ḥabiṭat a'māluhum fī al-dunyā wa al-ākhirati* — "those, their deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter."

*Ḥabaṭa* — the verb for a deed losing its weight, becoming null. The doctrine of *ḥubūṭ* in Islamic theology is that disbelief voids the **reward** of previously performed good. The nuance the scholars insist on: the good a disbeliever does may still be repaid in this life — in health, provision, ease, reputation — but it carries no reward in the Hereafter. The relevant hadith is the one in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from 'Ā'ishah: she asked the Prophet ﷺ about Ibn Jud'ān, who in the pre-Islamic period used to maintain ties of kinship and feed the poor, and he said: "It will not benefit him; he never said, 'My Lord, forgive me my sin on the Day of Recompense.'"

*Wa ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn* — "and those are the companions of the Fire; they abide in it forever."

**Now the boundary that has to be drawn, because it is drawn incorrectly in both directions.**

This verse says **nothing** about any punishment for apostasy in this world. Its entire statement is about the Hereafter: deeds void, companions of the Fire. The classical juristic ruling on apostasy — that the apostate is to be called to repent and executed if he does not — is derived from hadith, not from this verse. The hadith relied upon include the one in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* from Ibn 'Abbās, "Whoever changes his religion, kill him," and the one narrated by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Ibn Mas'ūd, that the blood of a Muslim is not lawful except for three: a life for a life, the married adulterer, and the one who abandons his religion and separates from the community.

That ruling, its scope, and its application today are **genuinely disputed**, and the dispute should be reported honestly rather than flattened. Among the classical points of difference: whether a female apostate is executed (the majority say she is; the Ḥanafīs hold she is imprisoned until she returns), whether repentance is always accepted (the schools accept it, with differences about whether a repeat apostate's repentance is accepted), and the conditions of capacity, coercion and sanity. Among contemporary scholars there is a substantial body of argument that the hadith-based capital ruling was tied to the political-military reality of treason and organised sedition in the early community rather than to a change of personal conviction, and that the Qur'anic treatment of apostasy is exclusively eschatological — with 2:256 and 4:137 and 3:90's conditions cited in support. Others maintain the classical ruling. **This is a live, contested question, and a commentary that presents either side as the settled consensus is not telling the truth.**

**One further boundary.** No one may use this verse to declare a named individual an inhabitant of the Fire. The verse describes a category defined by a final state known only to God; whether a given person died in it is not something anyone can establish. This has been the consistent rule throughout this commentary.

**The transmitted occasion.** The Prophet ﷺ sent 'Abdullāh ibn Jaḥsh — his paternal aunt's son — with a small party of the Emigrants, around the second year after the Hijrah, with sealed instructions to be opened after two days' march, directing them to Nakhlah, between Makkah and Ṭā'if, to bring news of the Quraysh. There they came upon a small Quraysh caravan. The day was in dispute: the men believed it to be the last day of Jumādā II; it was in fact the first of Rajab, a sacred month. They conferred, uncertain, and then attacked. One of the Quraysh — 'Amr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī — was killed by an arrow, two were taken captive, and the caravan was seized. When they returned, the Prophet ﷺ said, "I did not command you to fight in the sacred month," and he held the caravan and the captives, distributing nothing. The party despaired, believing themselves ruined. The Quraysh seized on it in their propaganda: Muhammad has violated the sacred month. Then this verse was revealed, and its revelation relieved them; Ibn Isḥāq reports that they then sought the reward of those who fight, and the Prophet ﷺ accepted the spoils, applying the fifths rule.

**This account is widely transmitted** — through Ibn Isḥāq's *sīrah* and thence into al-Tabari, Ibn Kathīr, al-Baghawī and the works of *asbāb al-nuzūl* — and it should be understood as coming primarily by that route rather than from the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s, and the details vary between transmissions: the size of the party (given variously as eight to twelve), the identity of the archer (named in different accounts as Wāqid ibn 'Abdillāh or 'Abdullāh ibn Wāqid), and the exact dating. Whatever the precise details, the episode is the setting the commentators have always read the verse against, and it explains the verse's otherwise strange structure: a community that had done something it feared was grave, being told that it was indeed grave — and greater crimes were being committed against it.

**The lesson in the structure that is easy to miss.** The Quraysh's objection was raised in bad faith: they had persecuted, blocked, and expelled. And yet the objection was *not wholly wrong* — fighting in Rajab was a grave matter, and the verse concedes it. The Qur'an here models something rare: an objection raised by a bad actor can still contain a true element, and the true element is conceded before the bad actor is answered. Intellectual honesty does not wait for a worthy opponent.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 218

**"Surely those who have believed, emigrated, and struggled in the Way of Allah—they can hope for Allah’s mercy. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: indeed, those who believed, and those who emigrated, and those who strove in the path of Allah — those hope for the mercy of Allah. And Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

*Inna alladhīna āmanū wa alladhīna hājarū wa jāhadū fī sabīli Allāhi ulā'ika yarjūna raḥmata Allāh, wa Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.*

**The verse is built as the exact counterpart of the one before it, and it must be read that way.**

2:217 closed with those who renounce their religion and die as disbelievers — their deeds void, their abode the Fire. 2:218 opens with those who believed, emigrated, and strove. Same structure, same three-part profile, opposite direction. **Two kinds of departure placed side by side:** the departure *from* the religion and the departure *for* it. One abandons the faith; the other abandons his home, his family, his property and his city because of it. The surah puts them in adjacent verses so that the reader cannot mistake one for the other.

**Notice the grammar of the opening, because it is unusual.** The verse says *alladhīna āmanū* — "those who believed" — and then, instead of simply joining the next verb to it, repeats the relative pronoun: *wa alladhīna hājarū wa jāhadū* — "and those who emigrated and strove." The repetition of *alladhīna* is not redundant. In Arabic rhetoric the repetition treats each as a distinct and independently significant attribute rather than as one undifferentiated group: **faith is a station, emigration is a station, striving is a station.** A person can have the first without the second, and the first two without the third. The verse names three separate achievements and joins them into one biography.

The same triple construction appears in Sūrat al-Anfāl (8:72 and 8:74), which is itself a sign that the formulation was established: "those who believed and emigrated and strove with their wealth and their lives in the path of Allah."

*Ulā'ika* — "those." The **distant** demonstrative. Arabic uses the far demonstrative to mark elevation and remoteness of rank, and the commentators note it here: the pronoun is not merely identifying them, it is gesturing at how high they stand.

*Yarjūna raḥmata Allāh* — "they hope for the mercy of Allah."

**This is the most theologically loaded clause in the batch, and it needs to be read slowly.**

**The verse does not say they have earned Paradise. It does not even say they will enter it. It says they *hope*.** And these are not marginal people — these are the Emigrants, the people who left Makkah with nothing, and the strivers. If ever there were a group with a claim, it was this one. And the Qur'an's description of them is: they hope.

**That is the correct Islamic posture stated in a single word, and the tradition built an entire discipline on it — the balance of *khawf* (fear) and *rajā'* (hope).** The scholars describe the servant as a bird between two wings, or a rider between two mounts: fear that drives him from disobedience, hope that draws him to obedience and keeps him from despair. Both are necessary; either alone is destructive. Fear alone produces despair and abandonment of action. Hope alone produces presumption and laxity.

**And the hadith that belongs directly here is in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, from Abū Hurayrah.** The Prophet ﷺ said: "None of you will be saved by his deeds." They said: "Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?" He said: "Not even me — unless Allah envelops me in His mercy and His grace." If the Messenger of Allah ﷺ says this about himself, then the Emigrants' hoping in 2:218 is not false modesty. It is accurate accounting.

**But — and this is the other half — the hope is not unfounded.** The verse closes *wa Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm* — "and Allah is Forgiving, Merciful." Al-Rāzī and others note the fit: the verse would not close with the two names of forgiveness and mercy after a statement of bare hope unless that hope were going to be honoured. The ending is the guarantee. **So the verse gives hope without entitlement, and it grounds the hope in God's names rather than in the servants' performance.** That is the whole of it: you are not saved by what you did, and you will not be disappointed by the One you hoped in.

The hadith that seals the practical ruling, in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from Jābir: the Prophet ﷺ said, "Let none of you die except while thinking well of Allah." And in the *ḥadīth qudsī* also recorded by Muslim: "I am as My servant thinks of Me." The scholars derived from these that the proportions of fear and hope shift across a life: fear weighs more in health and strength, when the risk is complacency; hope weighs more at the approach of death, when the risk is despair.

**Now the three verbs, and what each means now that the specific situation has passed.**

***Āmanū* — believed.** The foundation, and it is listed first and separately.

***Hājarū* — emigrated.** Historically, the migration from Makkah to Madinah. But the Prophet ﷺ himself closed that specific door and opened a wider one. Recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim: "There is no emigration after the conquest, but struggle and intention." And recorded by al-Bukhārī — in the narration of 'Abdullāh ibn 'Amr, in which the Prophet ﷺ was asked which aspect of Islam is best — he answered: "That you feed food, and give greetings of peace to those you know and those you do not know." That hadith is about the outward excellence; on *hijrah* specifically, the well-attested formulation is **"the emigrant is the one who abandons what Allah has prohibited"** (al-muhājiru man hajara mā nahā Allāhu 'anhu) — transmitted in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections and elsewhere.

**That single sentence is what keeps this verse alive for everyone.** Emigration did not end; it changed address. It is now the leaving of whatever God has forbidden: the job that cannot be done honestly, the relationship that cannot be conducted lawfully, the place where the religion cannot be practised, the habit that is destroying you. **Anyone who has left something because of God has a share in this verse's three verbs.**

***Jāhadū fī sabīli Allāh* — strove in the path of Allah.** *Sabīl Allāh* is the path that leads to Him, and *jihād* is the expenditure of effort upon it. It is broader than fighting, and the tradition is clear that it includes the struggle against one's own soul.

**A caution that must be given because it is so often got wrong.** There is a famous saying — "we have returned from the lesser struggle to the greater struggle" — that is widely quoted and that **is not established as a Prophetic hadith**; the hadith critics, including al-'Irāqī and Ibn Ḥajar, graded it weak, and al-Albānī and others treated it as very weak or fabricated. It should not be attributed to the Prophet ﷺ.

What *is* transmitted — by Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī, from Faḍālah ibn 'Ubayd — is: **"The one who strives is the one who strives against his own soul for the sake of Allah."** Al-Tirmidhī graded it *ḥasan*, and its chain is discussed by the hadith critics, with some grading it *ṣaḥīḥ* and others noting weakness. So the underlying meaning is soundly attested even though the famous wording is not, and the honest thing is to say both. There is also the sound, well-known hadith of the two rewards for the mujtahid: "When a judge gives a ruling, striving to reach the right judgement, and he is correct, he has two rewards; and if he gives a ruling and strives and errs, he has one reward" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim) — which shows *ijtihād* in the path of God as an intellectual and spiritual exertion, not only a physical one.

**The verse's last function is its placement.** 2:217 ends in the Fire; 2:218 opens with mercy. This is the surah's constant rhythm — warning and glad tidings set against each other, so that neither is read alone. It happened with 2:155–157 (testing, then blessings and mercy), and it happens here. **You are not permitted to read the threat without the promise, or the promise without the threat.**

For practice: the verse gives a person his biography in three words — believe, leave, strive — and then tells him the correct interior state while doing it. Not certainty. Not despair. Hope, resting on the Forgiving and the Merciful.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 219

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling. Say, “There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit.” They ˹also˺ ask you what they should donate. Say, “Whatever you can spare.” This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect"**

*Yas'alūnaka 'an al-khamri wa al-maysir, qul fīhimā ithmun kabīrun wa manāfi'u li-al-nāsi wa ithmuhumā akbaru min naf'ihimā, wa yas'alūnaka mādhā yunfiqūna qul al-'afw, kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāti la'allakum tatafakkarūn.*

**The first question: wine and gambling.**

*Al-khamr* comes from *khamara*, to cover or to veil — and the name is a description: it is called *khamr* because it **covers the intellect**. The same root gives *khimār*, the head-covering. The definition the tradition settled on follows the etymology rather than the substance. Recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from Ibn 'Umar: the Prophet ﷺ said, "**Every intoxicant is khamr, and every intoxicant is forbidden.**" And 'Umar, speaking from the pulpit, defined it by its effect: "Khamr is whatever covers the intellect." That definition is what allows the ruling to reach substances the seventh century had never seen.

*Al-maysir* comes from *yusr*, ease — it is the acquisition of wealth **easily**, without labour. In practice it referred to the game of the *azlām*: a camel was slaughtered and divided into portions, and lots were drawn with headless arrows; those whose arrows came up took shares, those whose arrows did not, lost their stake, and the meat was distributed to the poor. It was publicly framed as generosity and it functioned as a lottery. The commentators are unanimous that the scope is not the specific game: Ṭāwūs, 'Aṭā' and Mujāhid are reported as saying that everything involving a stake and chance is *maysir* — even, they said, boys playing with walnuts. And a report from 'Alī places backgammon and chess within it.

**Now the answer, and it is built in three movements.**

*Qul fīhimā ithmun kabīrun wa manāfi'u li-al-nāsi wa ithmuhumā akbaru min naf'ihimā* — "Say: in both there is great sin, and benefits for people — and the sin of both is greater than their benefit."

**The second movement is the one that carries the verse's persuasive genius.** The Qur'an **concedes the benefit.** It does not deny that these things produce real, felt, material good. The commentators list them without embarrassment: for wine, the pleasure, the sociability, the aiding of digestion, the profit from trading in it, and — in Ibn Kathīr's phrasing — the sharpening of some minds and the euphoria. For gambling, the acquisition of wealth without toil, and the fact that the poor were fed from it.

**Why does this matter so much?** Because the audience was a society steeped in both. A text that had begun by denying that wine is pleasant, or that gambling sometimes pays, would have been dismissed in the first sentence and would have been *false*. The Qur'an's credibility here rests on its accuracy: it tells the truth about the thing it is about to prohibit, names the genuine good in it, and *then* rules.

**And the ruling is a comparison, not a denial.** *Ithmuhumā akbaru min naf'ihimā* — the sin is greater than the benefit. Al-Qurṭubī gives the reasoning in a line: **the benefit is in this world, while the sin earns God's displeasure in the next.** Two scales, two timeframes, and the smaller one is not denied — it is outweighed.

**The third movement, and the rhetorical point: the verse hands the hearer a scale and asks him to weigh.** It does not say "do not think about it." It says: here is the sin, here is the benefit, and here is the relation between them — now judge. Which is why the verse ends with *la'allakum tatafakkarūn*, "so that you may **reflect**." God gives reasons and asks for reasoning. The method is the message.

**A qirā'ah worth knowing.** *Ithmun kabīrun* — "great sin" — is read by the majority with a *bā'*. Ḥamzah and al-Kisā'ī read it *ithmun kathīrun* — "**much** sin" — with a *thā'*. Al-Tabari prefers the *bā'* reading, on the neat argument that everyone agrees on the word *akbaru* at the end of the clause (with *bā'*), so the first word should match it. Al-Qurṭubī notes that Ibn Mas'ūd's codex had the *thā'* form in **both** places — *kathīr* and *aktharu* — and that the community subsequently set that reading aside. **Both readings describe the same reality: the sin is grave in kind and many in number.** Nothing in the practical ruling turns on the difference, and this is a good simple illustration of how the variant readings enrich rather than compete.

**Was wine prohibited by this verse?** The great majority say **no** — this is a stage. Qatādah is explicit: this verse censures them but does not prohibit them; the prohibition comes in Sūrat al-Mā'idah. Al-Qurṭubī reports this as the position of most of the commentators.

There was a minority argument, recorded and then refuted, that the verse *is* a prohibition by inference: the Qur'an has forbidden *ithm* (sin) elsewhere (7:33), and this verse declares that there is *ithm* in wine; therefore wine is forbidden. Ibn 'Aṭiyyah rejected the syllogism: the *ithm* in the verse is the sin that **arises from** wine — the quarrelling, the missed prayers, the enmity — not the substance itself. Al-Qurṭubī also notes the grammatical point that the verse says *fīhimā ithmun kabīr* — "in them is great sin" — and not *humā ithmun kabīr* — "they are a great sin."

**The four stages, as the tradition reconstructs them.** First, 16:67 — "and from the fruits of the palm trees and grapevines you take from it intoxicant and good provision" — a Makkan verse that mentions it among provisions, at a time when drinking was not yet forbidden. Second, this verse. Third, 4:43 — "do not approach prayer while you are intoxicated until you know what you are saying." Fourth, 5:90–91 — "O you who believe, intoxicants and gambling and [sacrificing to] stone altars and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan's handiwork, so avoid it, that you may be successful... will you not desist?"

**The narrations around the second and third stages are well known.** Recorded by Aḥmad and the compilers of the Sunan, from Abū Maysarah on the authority of 'Umar: 'Umar said, "O Allah, give us a clear ruling about khamr." This verse was revealed; it was recited to him, and he said again, "O Allah, give us a clear ruling about khamr." Then 4:43 was revealed; it was recited to him, and he said it again. Then the verse of al-Mā'idah was revealed, and when it was recited to him he said, "We have desisted, we have desisted." This narration is widely transmitted; its chain is discussed, and it should be cited as transmitted rather than as belonging to the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s.

And al-Baghawī and others report the incident that precipitated the third stage: after this verse was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ said that God had already given warning about the prohibition of wine, and some people left it while others continued; then 'Abd al-Raḥmān ibn 'Awf held a meal, wine was served, the guests became intoxicated, and the man who led them in the Maghrib prayer recited *Sūrat al-Kāfirūn* with the negation dropped — "Say, O disbelievers, I worship what you worship" — at which point 4:43 was revealed. This is reported in the Sunan collections, with al-Tirmidhī grading it *ḥasan*, and it is a report rather than a *ṣaḥīḥ* narration.

**Two further hadith worth having.** Recorded by Muslim and others: the Prophet ﷺ cursed wine and ten things connected to it — the one who presses it, the one for whom it is pressed, the one who drinks it, the one who carries it, the one to whom it is carried, the one who pours it, the one who sells it, the one who consumes its price, the one who buys it, and the one for whom it is bought. The point of the list is that the prohibition attaches to the whole economy, not only to the drinker. And the Prophet ﷺ said: "Whatever intoxicates in large quantity, a small quantity of it is forbidden" — reported by Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah, and graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by a number of the critics. That hadith closes the loophole that every generation rediscovers.

**The second question: what should they spend?** *Qul al-'afw* — "Say: the surplus."

**Note how this question differs from 2:215, because the two are deliberately paired.** In 2:215 the question was *mādhā yunfiqūn* and the answer named **recipients**. Here the same question is asked and the answer names an **amount**. Al-Qurṭubī makes the distinction explicit: the first was about *to whom* the spending goes, and this one is about *how much*. Two verses, the same question, two different answers — because the questioner's concern had shifted.

*Al-'afw* is from a root meaning to be effaced, to exceed, to be superfluous. Here it means the excess that remains after genuine need — what you can spare without hardship. Ibn 'Abbās, via 'Ikrimah: whatever is surplus to the needs of your family. Qatādah, 'Aṭā' and al-Suddī: the surplus beyond what you require.

**A qirā'ah here too.** The majority read *al-'afwa* in the accusative — "spend the surplus." Abū 'Amr read it in the nominative — "the surplus is what you should spend" — with a variant reported from Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ḥasan, Qatādah and Ibn Abī Isḥāq also reading the nominative. Al-Naḥḥās explains the grammar: if *mādhā* is taken as a single interrogative ("what thing?"), the accusative is preferred; if *mā* and *dhā* are taken as a relative construction ("that which"), the nominative is preferred. **Both are correct Arabic and both give the same meaning.**

**Is it abrogated by zakāh?** Reported as a disagreement. Some held that once zakāh was obligated with its defined rates, this answer was superseded. Al-Tabari reports the claim and argues against it, since the community agrees that voluntary charity remains lawful — and his argument is strong. The practical position is that this verse governs *ṣadaqah*, and zakāh has its own thresholds.

**And the practice it produced, which is the striking part.** Qatādah and others report that the Companions would earn, keep back the amount of their family's maintenance, and give away the rest in charity on the strength of this verse. Note what that implies: the "need" being deducted was **maintenance**, not lifestyle. The surplus was measured after sustenance, not after acquisition.

**On contemporary application, stated carefully.** The verse's structure transfers directly without any forcing: identify the genuine benefit honestly, identify the harm honestly, and weigh them on both the worldly and the other-worldly scale. Alcohol: public-health bodies classify it as a causal factor across a wide range of diseases and injuries, and it is implicated in a large share of violent death and road death almost everywhere. Gambling: pathological gambling is a recognised psychiatric diagnosis, and the industry has been rebuilt in the last two decades around a delivery mechanism — the smartphone — that removes every friction the older forms had. The modern forms of *maysir* include online sports betting, financial spread-betting and speculative day-trading, lottery products, and randomised purchase mechanics in games. Contemporary juristic bodies have ruled on these, and they differ among themselves — notably on commercial insurance, where cooperative or *takāful* structures are widely permitted while conventional commercial insurance remains contested, and on some forms of financial derivatives. **These modern findings are illustrative corroboration of what the verse asserts; they are not the reason for the ruling, and the verse does not need them.** The ruling is *ithmuhumā akbaru min naf'ihimā*.

**And the maqṣad, which is the deepest layer.** The objective of the law served here is the protection of the intellect — *ḥifẓ al-'aql* — and of wealth — *ḥifẓ al-māl*. And there is a reason the intellect is protected first and most strictly: **the intellect is the faculty by which the law addresses you at all.** Remove it and every other obligation becomes unperformable and unaccountable. Protecting the mind is not one item on the list of the law's concerns; it is the condition that makes the rest of the list operational. That is why the prohibition is absolute rather than graded by quantity, and why the tradition extended it to every substance that clouds the mind.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 220

**"upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you concerning orphans. Say, “Improving their condition is best. And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you ˹in faith˺. And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good. Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”"**

In simple terms: [that you may reflect] upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you about orphans. Say: setting their affairs right is best. And if you mix your affairs with theirs, then they are your brothers. And Allah knows the one who spreads corruption from the one who sets right. And if Allah had willed, He could have imposed hardship on you. Indeed, Allah is Exalted in Might, Wise.

*Fī al-dunyā wa al-ākhirati, wa yas'alūnaka 'an al-yatāmā, qul iṣlāḥun lahum khayr, wa in tukhāliṭūhum fa-ikhwānukum, wa Allāhu ya'lamu al-mufsida min al-muṣliḥ, wa law shā'a Allāhu la-a'natakum, inna Allāha 'azīzun ḥakīm.*

**First, a note for anyone building an app or a printed text, because this verse begins mid-sentence.**

*Fī al-dunyā wa al-ākhirati* — "upon this world and the Hereafter" — belongs grammatically to the end of the preceding verse: *la'allakum tatafakkarūn fī al-dunyā wa al-ākhirah* — "so that you may reflect upon this world and the Hereafter." In the verse numbering used by most printed copies of the Qur'an, the sentence is divided, with 219 ending at "reflect" and 220 opening on "upon this world and the Hereafter." The translation has preserved that division faithfully.

**But the placement is not an accident of numbering, and it is worth understanding, because the orphaned phrase turns out to be the key to 2:219.** "Reflect upon this world and the Hereafter" is exactly what you must do to see that the sin outweighs the benefit. Recall al-Qurṭubī's reasoning on the previous verse: the benefit of wine and gambling lies in this world, while the sin earns displeasure in the next. **A person who holds only one of the two worlds in view will always conclude that the benefit wins.** The weighing is only possible from the vantage of both. The phrase that got stranded at the head of this verse is the interpretive hinge of the one before it.

**Now the question: orphans.**

*Wa yas'alūnaka 'an al-yatāmā* — "and they ask you about orphans."

**The transmitted occasion, which is well known and which the commentators have always related.** After the severe verses concerning orphans' property were revealed — "give the orphans their property, and do not substitute the worthless for the good" (4:2), and "those who consume the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming fire into their bellies, and they will burn in a blaze" (4:10) — the guardians became so alarmed that they began to separate the orphans' food and drink from their own, and even their cooking vessels, fearing that anything of theirs might become mixed with anything of the orphans'. They asked the Prophet ﷺ about it, and this verse was revealed, permitting them to mix.

'A'ishah is reported to have said: "I dislike that an orphan's wealth should be in my care unless I mix my food with his food and my drink with his drink." And it is reported — by Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasā'ī, al-Ḥākim in his *Mustadrak*, and Ibn Jarīr — that when this verse was revealed, the Companions joined their food and drink with the food and drink of the orphans in their care.

**A caveat that should be stated honestly.** The chronology is not straightforward, because 4:2 and 4:10 are in Sūrat al-Nisā', generally placed after al-Baqarah's revelation. The standard account in the tafsir literature has the severe warnings producing the scruple and this verse relieving it, and that account should be reported as the transmitted explanation rather than as a securely dated sequence.

**Note what the question itself tells us.** They asked because they were afraid of wrongdoing. That fear is not weakness — it is *wara'*, scrupulousness, and the tradition regards it as a virtue. But the religion's answer is relief, not further burden. **The verse is a case study in what the law is for: it is not designed to make life unliveable for the pious.**

*Qul iṣlāḥun lahum khayr* — "Say: setting their affairs right is best."

**The word is *iṣlāḥ*, and the choice of word is the ruling.** Not "leaving their property untouched is best." Not "careful accounting is best." ***Iṣlāḥ*** — improvement, setting right, making better. The criterion established by the verse is the **welfare of the orphan**, not a formal procedure.

And the grammar reinforces it: *iṣlāḥun* is indefinite, which in this construction gives generality — **any** act of genuine improvement is better than the alternative. The verse does not prescribe a mechanism; it prescribes an outcome, and leaves the mechanism to circumstance and to juristic reasoning. This is why the jurists could permit the guardian to invest and trade the orphan's property — because freezing it would not be *iṣlāḥ* — while placing strict conditions on him precisely because *iṣlāḥ* is the test.

*Wa in tukhāliṭūhum fa-ikhwānukum* — "and if you mix your affairs with theirs, then they are your brothers."

**This is the heart of the verse.**

The question was about **property**. The answer is about **kinship**. *Fa-ikhwānukum* — "they are your brothers." Not "they are your charges," not "they are your responsibility," not "they are a trust." **Brothers.** The commentators gloss it as brothers in religion — *ikhwānukum fī al-dīn* — and the legal permission is therefore grounded in a relational reality rather than in administrative convenience. You may share a household and a table with them **because they are your brothers**, and that is what makes the arrangement lawful rather than merely tolerated.

**And this completes a pattern running through the whole batch.** Look at what the four "they ask you" verses each do. In 2:215 the question was *what*, and the answer was *whom*. In 2:219 the question was *what*, and the answer was *how much* — and the reasoning was *reflect on both worlds*. Here the question is about *property*, and the answer is about *brotherhood*. **Every one of these answers moves the questioner off the mechanics and onto the human and eternal reality underneath.** That is the Qur'an's pedagogy in miniature, and it is why these verses still work on readers who have never had to ask the original questions.

*Wa Allāhu ya'lamu al-mufsida min al-muṣliḥ* — "and Allah knows the corrupter from the one who sets right."

**The word-pair is a deliberate echo of the very beginning of this surah.** At 2:11–12 the hypocrites are told "do not spread corruption in the land," and they answer, "we are only **reformers**" (*muṣliḥūn*) — and God responds: "Truly, it is they who are the **corrupters** (*mufsidūn*), but they do not perceive." The same two participles, the same claim, the same unmasking.

**And note the participles: *al-mufsid* and *al-muṣliḥ* — the doer, not a fixed identity.** The verse does not sort people into two permanent species. It names the act. Which means a person is not locked into either column; the same guardian may set right today and corrupt tomorrow, and the verse's warning is precisely that the arrangement which makes improvement possible is the same arrangement that makes embezzlement easy.

**This is why the permission and the warning come in a single breath, and it is the verse's sharpest moment.** "Mix with them — they are your brothers. And God knows the corrupter from the reformer." The concession is granted, and the auditor is named in the same sentence. The orphan cannot audit you. The court may never audit you. Your own self-certainty will not audit you — the Qur'an has already shown, in the profile of 2:204, that a man can call God to witness his own sincerity and be lying. God knows. That is the whole of the enforcement mechanism, and it is placed exactly where the temptation is greatest.

*Wa law shā'a Allāhu la-a'natakum* — "and if Allah had willed, He could have imposed hardship on you."

*A'nata*, from *'anat*: to burden, to make difficult, to impose what strains. The meaning: He could simply have forbidden all mixing, and it would have been harder on you — and He did not. **So the concession is a gift, not a right.** This is the Qur'an's characteristic way of granting a *rukhṣah*: it never presents relief as an entitlement, but always as a mercy with the hardship that was averted held up next to it. The same logic runs through 2:185 ("Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship"), 2:178 on the retaliation, and 4:28 ("Allah wants to lighten for you, and mankind was created weak").

**And, consistent with the distinction drawn earlier in this surah on 2:205 and 2:185, this is the legislative will, not the creative will.** The verse is not saying that God's decree failed to impose hardship; it is saying that His *law*, by His wisdom and mercy, did not.

*Inna Allāha 'azīzun ḥakīm* — "indeed, Allah is Exalted in Might, Wise."

**The two names close the verse for a reason, and they are the answer to two possible misreadings of the concession.** '*Azīz* — Mighty, unopposed, subject to no pressure — which means the concession is **not** a compromise extracted by circumstance. And *Ḥakīm* — Wise — which means the concession is **not** arbitrary or soft. It is placed where it serves a purpose, and it is bounded. A lesser legislator either refuses relief to show strength or grants it so widely that the protected party loses protection. The verse grants relief and closes with the two names that say neither failure is present.

**The wider Qur'anic thread on orphans is one of the longest in the text, and it begins in Makkah before there was a state to enforce anything.** "So as for the orphan, do not oppress" (93:9). "Or the feeding on a day of severe hunger of an orphan of near relationship, or a poor person in misery" (90:15–16) — counted as part of *al-'aqabah*, the steep path that is the whole of righteousness. "That is the one who repels the orphan and does not encourage the feeding of the poor" (107:2–3) — named as the very definition of the one who denies the Recompense. And in this surah: 2:83 places good treatment of orphans in the covenant taken from the Children of Israel; 2:177 makes giving to orphans, out of love for God, part of the definition of righteousness; 2:215 names them among the recipients of charity. **From the first Makkan verses to the latest Madinan legislation, the orphan is constant — which is the surest sign that this is a test of a community's sincerity rather than a social-welfare measure.**

And a hadith in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, from Sahl ibn Sa'd: the Prophet ﷺ said, "I and the one who sponsors an orphan will be like this in Paradise," and he indicated with his index and middle fingers, holding them slightly apart.

**On the fiqh.** The guardian must act in the orphan's interest, may not give away the orphan's property, may not sell it except for genuine need or clear benefit — the schools differ on the scope, with the Ḥanafīs notably restricting the guardian's sale of an orphan's immovable property while the majority permit it where there is manifest benefit. He may not mix in any way that harms the orphan. And when the orphan reaches marriageable age and shows sound judgement, the property is handed over: "Test the orphans until they reach marriageable age; then if you perceive in them sound judgement, hand over to them their property, and do not consume it extravagantly and hastily before they grow up" (4:6). On whether a needy guardian may take from the orphan's property for his own maintenance, the transmitted reports — recorded in the Sunan collections — permit eating from it reasonably, without extravagance, without anticipating the orphan's majority, and without using it to substitute for his own wealth.

**What it looks like now.** The orphan in the extended family whose inheritance is quietly absorbed by an uncle's business. The child in state care. The refugee child with no guardian at all — a category that exists in numbers the seventh century would not have imagined, and to which this verse speaks with complete directness. The executor who "manages" an estate for a decade. The director of a charity whose budget is designated for orphans and whose salary, travel and office come out of it — the verse's *al-mufsida min al-muṣliḥ* lands on him with full force, and it is the only audit some of these cases will ever receive.

There is one contemporary point worth making, carefully and without overclaiming. Contemporary child-welfare practice has moved strongly away from institutional care and toward family-based and kinship-based placement, on the evidence that children do better in households. That is a well-established orientation in the field rather than a single finding, and it should be cited as such. **What is worth noting is the direction of fit:** this verse's instinct — integrate the orphan into a household, and ground the integration in brotherhood rather than in management — is the same instinct. The Qur'an did not place the orphan in an institution. It placed him at the table.

**And the reason the orphan is the test** is stated in the logic of the whole tradition: the orphan cannot repay you. The Prophet ﷺ was an orphan, and the Qur'an reminds him of it in the plainest terms — "Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you?" (93:6). A community's treatment of the person who has nothing to offer it in return is the cleanest available measure of whether it is serving God or itself.

## Synthesis: Verses 211–220

**The arc.** The batch opens with a case study and closes with a household. In between it covers the whole distance of what a believing community has to survive and to decide: how to read its own history (211), how to read other people's prosperity (212), why humanity keeps splitting (213), what belonging costs (214), what to do with money (215), what to do when commanded to do the thing you most want to avoid (216), how to hold a genuine moral error of your own side alongside a greater crime committed against you (217), what interior posture to have while doing all of it (218), how to judge a pleasure that really does pay (219), and how to treat the person in your house who cannot repay you (220).

**The thread that binds them is that faith is tested at the point where it is inconvenient.** 2:213 says the split was envy, not ignorance. 2:214 says you will be shaken. 2:216 says the obligation will be hateful. 2:217 says your own side committed something grave. 2:219 says the prohibited thing does have real benefits. 2:220 says the permitted arrangement is exactly where corruption becomes easy. Every one of these is a verse designed for a person who hoped the religion would be simpler than it is.

**Four verses begin "they ask you," and the pattern of the answers is the batch's most useful finding.** In each case the question is mechanical — *what*, *what*, *what*, *what about* — and in each case the answer relocates the questioner. From amount to recipient (215). From amount to surplus, with the two-world vantage supplied as the reason (219). From property to brotherhood (220). The Qur'an rarely resolves a question on the terms it was asked, because the terms are usually the problem.

**And there is a consistent refusal throughout to flatter the reader.** The Children of Israel are held up as a warning to people who have just been given something greater (211). The disbelievers' prosperity is explained in a way that denies the believer any claim to have earned his standing (212). The earliest schism in religious history is attributed to the people who had the most light (213). The believers are told they will be shaken (214). The Muslims' own conduct at Nakhlah is called *kabīr* before anyone else's is called *akbar* (217). The Emigrants — the best of them — are described as hoping, not as arriving (218). **A community taught this way is being protected from the one error that has destroyed every community before it: mistaking its own position for a verdict.**

**Two boundaries have been held throughout.** Nothing in this batch closes the door of repentance: 2:217's voiding of deeds is explicitly conditioned on *dying* in disbelief, and no verse here or elsewhere authorises declaring any named individual an inhabitant of the Fire. And where the tradition genuinely disagrees — whether the world was one community upon truth or upon error; whether fighting in the sacred months was abrogated; whether the "surplus" ruling was superseded by zakāh; whether this verse prohibited wine or prepared for its prohibition; what the ruling on apostasy in this world actually is — the disagreement has been reported rather than resolved, and the relative strength of the positions indicated where the tradition indicates it.

**The most important practical sentence in the batch is in verse 216, and it is four words long: *Allāhu ya'lamu wa antum lā ta'lamūn*.** Every obligation in these ten verses that a person finds heavy — giving when he would rather keep, fighting when he would rather not, giving up a pleasure that genuinely pays, taking on a child who is not his — rests on that single asymmetry. The verses do not mostly argue that these things are pleasant or even that they are legible. They argue that the One commanding can see the whole board.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether "one community" was upon truth or upon misguidance; whether the favour in verse 211 is general or specifically the coming of the Prophet ﷺ; who the subject of *zuyyina* is; whether fighting in the sacred months was abrogated; whether verse 219 prohibits wine or prepares for its prohibition; whether the "surplus" ruling was abrogated by zakāh; and the scope and present application of the ruling on apostasy in this world.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the expedition to Nakhlah, with its route through the sīrah literature and the variation in its details; the connection of verse 214 to the Battle of the Trench; the identification of 'Amr ibn al-Jamūḥ as the questioner about spending; the 'Umar supplication and its chain; and the incident of 'Abd al-Raḥmān ibn 'Awf's meal. One narration has been flagged as **not established**: the saying about returning from the lesser to the greater struggle, which the hadith critics have graded weak and which must not be attributed to the Prophet ﷺ.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 217: the classical juristic ruling on apostasy has been distinguished from this verse, which says nothing about any punishment in this world, and the contemporary dispute over that ruling has been reported as a live dispute rather than settled. On the same verse: the voiding of deeds has been tied explicitly to the stated condition of dying in disbelief, so that no reading of it can be taken to close the door of repentance, and the rule that no individual may be declared an inhabitant of the Fire has been restated. On verse 212: the adornment of the world has been handled without making God the author of disobedience, and without denying that He creates all things. And on verse 216: the obligation of fighting has been fenced in by 2:190 and 8:61, because a verse about prescribed fighting read without its restriction has done more damage than almost any other in the text.

The most important practical point in the batch is in verse 216 and has been stated as such: the ground of every hard obligation in these ten verses is not that the obligation is legible to you, but that the One commanding knows and you do not.
