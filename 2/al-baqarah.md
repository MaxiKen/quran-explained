# Sūrah 2 — al-Baqarah (The Cow)

*286 verses; Madinan — revealed in Madīnah in its entirety, and one of the first sūrahs revealed there.
2:281 was the last verse of the Qurʾān to be sent down, and the verses on usury were among the last.
The Kufan count: 287 verses, 6,221 words, 25,500 letters [Ibn Kathīr], [Qurṭubī]. Its names: **al-Baqarah**
("the Cow", from the story of the cow at vv. 67–71) and **Fusṭāṭ al-Qurʾān**, "the tent of the Qurʾān"
(Khālid b. Maʿdān), for its vastness, its rulings and its admonitions [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. Ibn al-ʿArabī:
"In it are a thousand commands, a thousand prohibitions, a thousand judgments and a thousand reports."
ʿUmar took twelve years to learn it, his son ʿAbdullāh eight [Qurṭubī].*

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* ·
**[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr
al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**Its merits in the Sunnah.** "Do not turn your houses into graves; the house in which al-Baqarah is
recited, Shayṭān does not enter it" (Aḥmad, Muslim, Tirmidhī, Nasāʾī — Tirmidhī graded it *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*)
[Ibn Kathīr], [Qurṭubī]. "Read al-Baqarah, for taking it up is blessing and leaving it is regret, and the
sorcerers cannot [prevail against] it" (Muslim, Abū Umāmah) [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. "For everything there is a
hump, and the hump of the Qurʾān is al-Baqarah; and for everything a core, and the core of the Qurʾān is the
*Mufaṣṣal*" (Abū l-Qāsim al-Ṭabarānī, Ibn Ḥibbān; Sahl b. Saʿd) [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. The Prophet sent an
expedition and made its youngest man commander because he had memorized al-Baqarah (Tirmidhī, graded ṣaḥīḥ;
Abū Hurayrah) [Ibn Kathīr]. ʿUsayd b. Ḥuḍayr, reciting al-Baqarah at night, saw a light like lamps in the sky
as his horse shied; the Prophet said: "Those were the angels drawing near to your voice — had you kept
reciting, the people would have seen them" (Bukhārī) [Ibn Kathīr]. Ibn Masʿūd: "Whoever recites ten verses of
al-Baqarah in a night, Shayṭān will not enter that house that night — four from its beginning, Āyat al-Kursī
and the two after it, and its last three"; and "Shayṭān leaves the house where al-Baqarah is recited, passing
wind as he leaves" [Ibn Kathīr], [Qurṭubī]. The two lights, al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān, "will come on the Day of
Resurrection like two clouds and argue on behalf of their people" (Aḥmad, Muslim; Abū Umāmah) [Ibn Kathīr].

## 2:1 — الم

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.*

**Meaning.** The letters that open twenty-nine sūrahs are among the most debated words in the Qurʾān.
**[Maʿārif]** They are called *muqaṭṭaʿāt*, "isolated letters", each pronounced separately, without a
vowel after it. Three positions were held: that they name the sūrahs at whose head they stand; that they
are symbols of the Divine Names; and — the position preferred by the majority of the Companions, the
Tābiʿīn, and the later authoritative scholars, and adopted by **[Qurṭubī]** and **[Ibn Kathīr]** — that
they are among the secrets of God, whose meaning He alone knows, or which He entrusted privately to the
Prophet without its being communicated. That is why no commentary on them was reported from the Prophet.
**[Jalālayn]** "God knows best what He means by these letters." **[Saʿdī]** The safest course is silence
about their meaning without revealed evidence, together with certainty that God did not send them in play
but for a wisdom we do not know. **[Ibn Kathīr]** This was the position of Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān, ʿAlī
and Ibn Masʿūd: the letters belong to the knowledge God kept to Himself. **[Study Quran]** Abū Bakr:
"Every book has a mystery, and the mystery of the Qurʾān is the openings of the sūrahs"; ʿAlī: "Every book
has a quintessence, and the quintessence of this Book is the spelled-out letters."

**[Maʿārif]** Qurṭubī's case for this position, summarised: al-Shaʿbī, Sufyān al-Thawrī and many masters
of ḥadīth held that every revealed book has its secrets, and the isolated letters are God's secrets in the
Qurʾān — hence they belong to the *mutashābihāt*, which we believe and recite but do not probe. They are
not, however, without benefit: to believe in them and recite them is itself a merit, and blessings from
the unseen world attend their recitation, though we are unaware. The blessed Caliphs Abū Bakr, ʿUmar,
ʿUthmān and ʿAlī and most of the Companions, among them Ibn Masʿūd, held firmly that the letters descended
from God, that we must recite them exactly as they came down, and that questioning their meaning is
improper. **[Maʿārif]** The interpretations reported from great and authentic scholars were offered as
symbolic readings, or to awaken the mind to the meanings hidden in the Book, or simply to simplify —
never as a claim to have captured God's intended meaning; so they are not to be challenged, since to
challenge them would be to go against the considered judgment of veritable scholars.

Others spoke, and **[Ṭabarī]** collects their views with their transmitters:

- **A name of the Qurʾān** — Qatādah, Mujāhid, Ibn Jurayj.
- **Openings with which God opens the Book** — Mujāhid: "openings (*fawātiḥ*) with which God opened the
  Qurʾān."
- **Names of sūrahs** — Ibn Zayd, from his father: "these are only the names of the sūrahs."
- **God's greatest name** — Ibn ʿAbbās (per al-Suddī); al-Shaʿbī: "the openings of the sūrahs are among the
  names of God."
- **An oath by God**, the letters being of His names — Ibn ʿAbbās (via ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭalḥah); ʿIkrimah: "an
  oath."
- **Letters cut from names**: *alif* from *Allāh*, *lām* from *Laṭīf*, *mīm* from *Majīd* — Ibn ʿAbbās; or
  *alif* from Allāh, *lām* from Jibrīl, *mīm* from Muḥammad, marking the descent of revelation from God
  through Gabriel to the Prophet [Qurṭubī], [Study Quran].
- **Abbreviations of a sentence**: "I am God, I know" (*anā Allāh aʿlam*) — Ibn ʿAbbās via Abū l-Ḍuḥā; the
  grammarian al-Zajjāj adopted it, the Arabs' habit being to abbreviate a word to what indicates it
  [Ṭabarī], [Qurṭubī].
- **Keys to names, blessings and spans** — al-Rabīʿ b. Anas: each letter is a key to a name of God, to a
  favour of His, and to a term of years (*alif* = 1, *lām* = 30, *mīm* = 40); hence the claim that the letters
  yield dates by numerical value (*ḥisāb al-jummal*). **[Ṭabarī]** records it while saying he disliked
  naming its transmitter "since what he related is not something to be relied on"; **[Ibn Kathīr]** on the
  chronogram readers: "whoever claims they indicate the durations of empires has claimed what is not his,
  and flown where no bird flies."
- **Some of the alphabet, standing for all of it** — as one says "my son is at *abjad*"; God named some
  letters and left the rest [Ṭabarī], [Ibn Kathīr].
- **So that the Arabs would stop and listen** — Qutrub: they had agreed to shun the Qurʾān, and the strange
  word made them wonder and prick up their ears, after which the composed Book was recited over them
  [Qurṭubī].

**[Ṭabarī]**'s own conclusion: God cut the letters off from one another because each one carries *many*
meanings at once, not one — as a single Arabic word (*umma*, *dīn*) carries many. The letters are therefore
at once an opening, an oath by His names, a numerical sign, and a sūrah-name; had God intended only one of
these, He or His Messenger would have made it plain, "and He only sent down His Book to make clear what they
differ over." **[Ibn Kathīr]** Remove the repeated letters and fourteen remain; their point, whatever their
detail, is the inimitability of the Book — the servants cannot produce its like though it is built of the very
letters they speak with.

**[Study Quran]** al-Rāzī likens the Book to a sea whose waters would destroy the rivulet made to carry them;
and reports the saying that "the learned have a secret, the vicegerents have a secret, the prophets have a
secret, the angels have a secret, and beyond all that God has a secret." Ibn ʿAjībah: "Only the elite of the
greatest Friends of God know the secrets of these letters." Against the objection that the Qurʾān is elsewhere
called clear (5:15; 10:1; 16:103), al-Rāzī takes a middle course: some of the Book's wisdom we can see, as we
see the wisdom of many laws, and some we cannot, as we cannot see the wisdom of certain rites — and
performing those in trust keeps the heart turned toward what it does not yet know.

**Context.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** Twenty-nine sūrahs open with individual letters; the openings pair the letters
with the Book that follows — "Alif Lām Mīm. This is the Book" (2:1–2); "Alif Lām Mīm. Allah, there is no god
but He" (3:1–2); "Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād. A Book sent down to you" (7:1–2). **[Qurṭubī]** al-Farrāʾ and Qutrub:
the letters show that the Qurʾān is composed of the letters of the Arabs' own speech, making their failure to
match it the more conclusive.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The sayings of Abū Bakr and ʿAlī on the mystery and quintessence of the Book
[Study Quran]. **[Qurṭubī]**, from al-Anbārī: al-Rabīʿ b. Khuthaym said — "God sent down this Qurʾān and kept
back from it the knowledge of what He willed, and disclosed to you what He willed. What He kept to Himself
you will never attain, so do not ask about it; what He disclosed, ask about it and you will be told."

**Belief.** The letters are not idle speech: **[Ṭabarī]** charging God with addressing people in what has no
meaning is "attributing play, which all who affirm His oneness deny of God"; and it would make void His
description of the Book as "a clear Arabic tongue." **[Saʿdī]** Certainty that they carry a wisdom, even where
the wisdom is hidden, is the stance of faith. **[Qurṭubī]** The sound position on this and every ambiguous
verse is that of the Believer's saying: "We believe in it; all is from our Lord" (3:7). **[Maʿārif]** The
believer's part toward the *mutashābihāt* is exactly this: belief, recitation, and the refusal to probe.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** The letters are stopped upon with a silent ending and take no case-ending, unless
one reports about them or joins them in a sentence. Al-Khalīl and Sībawayh: they are quoted as they stand
(*ḥikāya*), like the alphabet; on the view that they are sūrah-names they are nominative, as a predicate of an
implied "this is"; Ibn Kaysān: accusative, as "read Alif-Lām-Mīm"; or genitive by the oath. **[Ṭabarī]**,
**[Qurṭubī]** The Arabs abbreviated words for indication in this very way — the witness: "My daughter said to
me *qāf*," meaning *waqaftu* (*I have stopped*); and Zuḥayr: *"And if evil, then fā —* and I want no evil
*unless tā,"* meaning *faʿala* and *tashāʾ*.

**Cross-references.** The other letter-openings: 3:1; 7:1; 10:1; 11:1; 12:1; 13:1; 14:1; 15:1; 19:1; 20:1;
26:1; 27:1; 28:1; 29:1; 30:1; 31:1; 32:1; 36:1; 38:1; 40:1; 41:1; 42:1–2; 43:1; 44:1; 45:1; 46:1; 50:1;
68:1. The challenge that no one can produce its like: 2:23; 10:38; 11:13; 17:88.

**Stories & occasions.** Abū Yāsir b. Akhṭab heard the Prophet reciting "Alif Lām Mīm — That Book of which
there is no doubt," and carried the report to Ḥuyayy b. Akhṭab and the Jews, who counted the letters'
numerical values — *alif* 1, *lām* 30, *mīm* 40, so seventy-one years; then added the other openings to make
161, 231, 271, until they declared the matter confused and went away. *(weak — an Ibn Isḥāq report through
al-Kalbī, which Ṭabarī's own handling treats as unreliable)* [Ṭabarī]. **[Qurṭubī]** adds: among the Jews who
took this path, some accepted it and some rejected it; the event is reported as the occasion of 3:7, "in it
are verses clear and others ambiguous."

**Reflection.** The Book opens by testing its reader before it teaches him: the letters stand at the door,
and the servant's first act toward them is trust. **[Qurṭubī]** al-Rabīʿ b. Khuthaym's rule draws the line the
believer walks — ask about what God disclosed, and leave what He kept; **[Saʿdī]** and know that nothing in
the Book is without a wisdom, whether or not the wisdom is given to you. **[Maʿārif]** And the letters are
not barren even for the one who cannot read them: recitation itself carries blessing, and the meaning
remains with God.

## 2:2 — ذَٰلِكَ الْكِتَابُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ هُدًى لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ

*That Book — no doubt in it — a guidance for the God-fearing.*

**Meaning.** **[Jalālayn]** *Dhālika* means "this Book which Muḥammad recites"; *lā rayba fīhi* is the predicate — there is no uncertainty that it is from God; the demonstrative is used to glorify the Book; *hudā* is a second predicate — the Book guides the God-fearing, those who tend toward piety by holding to the commands and avoiding the prohibitions, thus guarding themselves from the Fire. **[Saʿdī]** "This great Book, which is the Book in truth, containing what the books of former and later peoples do not contain of great knowledge and manifest truth"; *lā rayba fīhi* leaves no doubt of any kind, and the negation of doubt implies its opposite, certainty — a useful rule: a negation intended as praise must carry the perfection opposite it, since bare non-existence is no praise. Since the Book holds certainty, and guidance is only attained through certainty, it follows that it is "guidance for the God-fearing": *hudā* is left without an object to indicate generality — it guides in fundamentals and in branches, distinguishing the true from the false and the sound from the weak, and showing the paths that profit in this world and the next. Elsewhere God says "guidance for mankind" (2:185); here He specifies, because in itself it guides all people, but the wretched took no hold of it, so the proof stands against them — while the God-fearing, who brought the greatest cause, *taqwā*, prospered by it. **[Maʿārif]** *Dhālika* points to something distant while the Book is present before us; the subtlety is that it reaches back to the prayer for the straight path in al-Fātiḥah — the prayer has been granted, and the Qurʾān is its answer, giving the straight path in full to whoever seeks it. **[Ṭabarī]** Most exegetes — Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, al-Suddī, Ibn ʿAbbās — read *dhālika l-kitāb* as "this Book", for words just spoken, though past, stand near to the hearer — as one says, "That, by God, is as you said," and "This, by God, is as you said." It may also refer to the sūrahs sent down before al-Baqarah in Mecca and Madīnah — all of it being "the Book" — and some said it points to the Torah and the Gospel. **[Study Quran]** "That is the Book" is equally possible: since al-Baqarah is Madinan, much of the Qurʾān had already been revealed; or it points to what of the Qurʾān one already knows; or to the Book on the Preserved Tablet; "the Book" is a proper name of the Qurʾān, alongside "the Criterion" (25:1).

**[Ṭabarī]** *Lā rayba fīhi* = no doubt in it — reported from Mujāhid, ʿAṭāʾ, al-Suddī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, al-Rabīʿ; Ibn Abī Ḥātim: "I know of no disagreement on this." The *hāʾ* of *fīhi* returns to the Book: there is no doubt in that Book that it is from God, guidance for the God-fearing. **[Qurṭubī]** The negation is general, hence *rayb* is accusative (*manṣūb*); *rayb* carries three lexical senses — doubt (Ibn al-Zibʿarā: "There is no doubt in the truth, Umaymah; doubt is only what the fool says"), suspicion or accusation (Jamīl: "Buḥaynah said, 'Jamīl, you have made me suspect'; I said, 'Both of us, Buḥaynah, are suspects'"), and need (Kaʿb b. Mālik: "We settled every need from Tihāmah and Khaybar, then we prepared the swords"). The Book of God is therefore without doubt and without suspicion: it is truth in itself, sent down from God, one of His attributes, uncreated and unoriginated — whatever doubt the disbelievers may raise. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The verse may also be read as a prohibition: "Do not doubt it" (cf. 32:1–2). **[Maʿārif]** Doubt in a report can arise in two ways: the report itself is false, or the hearer misunderstands — and in the second case the report remains true however many the doubters, as God says later in this sūrah, "If you are in doubt…" (2:23).

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** "The servant does not attain the station of the God-fearing until he abandons what is harmless out of caution for what is harmful" (Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah, from ʿAtiyyah al-Saʿdī; Tirmidhī graded it *ḥasan gharīb*) [Ibn Kathīr]. The ḥadīth of ʿIyāḍ b. Ḥimār al-Mujāshiʿī (Muslim): God looked upon the people of the earth and detested them, Arab and non-Arab alike, except a remnant — and God says to the Prophet, "I have only sent you to test you and to test by you, and I have sent down to you a Book which water does not wash, which you recite sleeping and waking" [Qurṭubī]. Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah: "When God decreed the creation He wrote in His Book, which lies with Him: My mercy prevails over My wrath" [Qurṭubī]. ʿUmar asked Ubayy b. Kaʿb about *taqwā*; Ubayy said, "Have you ever walked a path overgrown with thorns?" "Yes." "What did you do?" "I rolled up my sleeves and took care." Ubayy said, "That is *taqwā*." [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. Ibn Masʿūd said to his nephew: "Do you see how many people there are? There is no good in them except a repentant one or a God-fearing one"; then, "There is no good in them except a scholar or a learner" [Qurṭubī]. **The God-fearing, described** — al-Ḥasan: "They refrained from what was forbidden and performed what was made obligatory"; Ibn ʿAbbās: "They fear God's punishment for abandoning the guidance they know, and hope for His mercy by believing what He revealed"; al-Suddī and Ibn Masʿūd: "the believers"; al-Kalbī: "those who avoid the major sins" — and al-Aʿmash, told of it, said, "I think that is so," and did not reject it; Qatādah: "those whom God described in the next verse" [Ṭabarī], [Ibn Kathīr]. **The people of the heart** — Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī: "The God-fearing one is he who, when he speaks, speaks for God, and when he acts, acts for God"; Abū Sulaymān al-Dārānī: "The God-fearing are those from whose hearts God has torn the love of appetites" [Qurṭubī]. *(digression — these two sayings belong to the Sufi reading of the verse rather than to its legal exegesis)*

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** In the verse is the uncreatedness of the Qurʾān: it is truth in itself, sent down from God, an attribute of His, not created and not originated — the doubt that falls is the disbelievers'. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Alongside this stands the Tirmidhī report that the *alif-lām-mīm* openings testify to the Book's inimitability. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Guidance is of two kinds: the guidance that only God creates in the heart and that no prophet can effect — "You cannot guide whom you love" (28:56); "Not upon you is their guidance" (2:272); "Whom God sends astray, none can guide" (7:186); "Whom God guides is rightly guided" (18:17) — and the guidance of explanation, direction and leading — "You are indeed guiding to a straight path" (42:52); "You are only a warner, and to every people a guide" (13:7); Thamūd: "We showed them the way, but they preferred blindness to guidance" (41:17). **[Qurṭubī]** likewise names the two: *hudā al-dalālah*, indication, which the messengers possess, and *hudā al-tawfīq*, enabling, which is God's alone (13:7; 42:52; 28:56) — and notes guidance can be spoken of as leading into the Fire itself: "lead them on to the path of Hell" (37:23), that is, drive them into it.

**Language.** *Kitāb* is from *kataba*, to gather — whence an army troop is a *katība*, and joined leather *kutub*; it names written letters whether gathered or apart, and it also means obligation, judgment and decree [Qurṭubī]. *Rayb* is the *maṣdar* of *rābanī* — "this matter made me doubt, and put fear into me" [Ṭabarī], [Qurṭubī]. *Al-Hudā* in Arabic means guidance, rightness and clarification — in it is unveiling for the people of knowledge, rightness, and increase of clarity [Qurṭubī]. **[Ṭabarī]** *Hudā* may be accusative, cut from *al-kitāb* ("that Book, *guiding*, for the God-fearing") or cut from the pronoun of *fīhi*; or nominative — as a praise (*madḥ*), as a predicate, or following the position of *lā rayba fīhi* — the last comparable to "And this is a blessed Book which We have sent down" (6:92). He also records al-Farrāʾ's claim that *alif-lām-mīm* raises *dhālika l-kitāb*, then the self-contradiction of that view, since on it *hudā* could not also be a predicate. **[Jalālayn]** *Hudā* is nominative as a second predicate. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-hudā* is treated as feminine by some of Banū Asad ("this is a good *hudā*"), masculine per al-Liḥyānī; the verb takes its object with or without a preposition — *hadaytuhu l-ṭarīq* (the Hijāzī usage) and *hadaytuhu ilā l-ṭarīq*; and *al-hudā* is also a name of the day, since in it people find their way. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-muttaqīn*'s origin is *al-muttawqiyīn*, the *wāw* turned to *tāʾ* and then assimilated — as in *tujāh* from *wijāh*. **[*Qurṭubī]** *Taqwā* is from *waqā* — to shield: *waqaytuhu* means "I protected him"; al-Nābiqhah's verse of the woman who caught her fallen veil "and shielded against us with her hand"; and the root was *waqwā*, the *wāw* turned to *tāʾ* as in *tujāh* and *turāth*.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** 32:1–2 ("Alif Lām Mīm. The sending down of the Book, no doubt in it, from the Lord of the worlds"); 41:44 ("Say: for those who believe it is a guidance and a healing; but those who do not believe have deafness in their ears, and it is blindness to them"); 17:82 ("We send down of the Qurʾān what is healing and mercy to the believers, and it only increases the wrongdoers in loss"); 10:57 ("there has come to you an admonition from your Lord, a healing for what is in the breasts, and guidance and mercy for the believers"); **[Saʿdī]** 2:185 ("guidance for mankind"); **[Maʿārif]** 2:23; **[Ṭabarī]** 6:92 ("And this is a Book We have sent down, blessed"); 6:155; **[Qurṭubī]** 2:68 (*bayna dhālika* — *dhālika* used of two things); 32:6 ("That is the Knower of the unseen and the seen"); **[Ibn Kathīr]** 28:56; 2:272; 7:186; 18:17; 42:52; 13:7; 41:17; 90:10 ("And We showed him the two ways").

**Readings.** *Fīhi* has five treatments: the standard *fīhi*; *fīhu* with a *ḍammah* and no *wāw* — the reading of al-Zuhrī and Sallām Abū l-Mundhir; *fīhī* with the *yāʾ* retained — Ibn Kathīr; *fīhū* with a *wāw*; and *fīh*, fully assimilated [Qurṭubī]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Some reciters stop at *lā rayba*, continuing "in it is guidance for the God-fearing"; but the better pause is at *lā rayba fīhi*, so that *hudā* describes the Book itself — a richer sense.

**Reflection.** **[Qurṭubī]** *Taqwā* gathers every good, and it is God's testament to the former and the last; it is the best thing a man can gain — Abū l-Dardāʾ, told that his companions spoke poetry while nothing was remembered from him, answered with verses: "A man wants his wish granted, but God refuses all except what He wills; a man says, 'My gain and my wealth' — and the *taqwā* of God is the best thing he has gained." Ibn al-Muʿtazz put the thorns of Ubayy's parable into verse: "Leave sins, the small and the great — that is *taqwā*; and act as one walking over thorny ground, wary of what he sees; and do not disdain a small sin, for the mountains are of pebbles." Ibn Mājah, from Abū Umāmah, from the Prophet ﷺ: "The believer has gained nothing after the *taqwā* of God better for him than a righteous wife — if he orders her, she obeys; if he looks at her, she pleases him; if he adjures her, she fulfils; and if he is away from her, she is faithful to him in herself and in his property" [Qurṭubī]. **[Saʿdī]** Guidance has two kinds — that of clarification and that of enabling — and the God-fearing receive both, while others receive only the first; the clarification of truth without the enabling to act on it is not complete guidance. **[Maʿārif]** So the specification of the God-fearing does not deny guidance to others: the Qurʾān guides all creation, and whoever accepts its guidance may rise to the rank of the God-fearing, while those already at that rank receive yet more, without limit — for the degrees of the third guidance never end.


## 2:3

*"Those who believe in the Unseen and establish the prayer and spend out of what We have provided them."*

**Meaning.** Three marks of the God-fearing, and in them the Qurʾān's own definition of faith: assent to what no eye has seen, worship kept standing, and wealth given out of what God has given [Ṭabarī] [Jalālayn]. The Unseen (*al-ghayb*) is everything hidden from the five senses and from unaided reason — God and His attributes, the angels, the Books, the Messengers, the Garden and the Fire, the Resurrection and Judgment, the decree [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī] [Saʿdī]. Faith in it is faith at its purest, because it rests on no observation: "Believe in the Unseen" is a whole creed in two words [Maʿārif]. Al-Jalālayn keeps the balance of the sentence: *who believe* means who accept as true the hidden things; *and establish* means perform the prayer giving it its due; *and spend* — from what We have bestowed — in obedience to God.

**Context.** Whom does the description cover? **[Ṭabarī]** reports three positions: that vv. 1–4 describe the believers among the Arabs alone; that they describe the believers among the People of the Book alone, who recognised the Prophet when the Qurʾān disclosed what they had hidden; and that they describe every believer of every nation, Arab and non-Arab. He prefers the reading that the four verses set out two *kinds* of believers — those marked by faith in the Unseen, and those marked by faith in what was sent down to the Prophet and before him — just as the disbelievers who follow are set out as two kinds, the sealed and the hypocrite. Mujāhid's division of the sūrah's opening is his witness: "Four verses on the believers, two on the disbelievers, thirteen on the hypocrites" [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** adds the same note in a different key: the faith of those who saw the Prophet was a plain matter, but no faith is weightier than faith in the Unseen — "By Him besides Whom there is no god, no person will ever acquire a better faith than believing in the Unseen." And a Companion's question to the Prophet ﷺ drew out the promise to those who believe without seeing.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** "Faith is to affirm as true" (*taṣdīq*) — Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd; "faith is reverence" — al-Rabīʿ; "faith is the deeds" — al-Zuhrī [Ṭabarī]. The Prophet ﷺ defined it in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl: "That you believe in God, His angels, His Books, His Messengers, the Last Day, and that you believe in the decree, its good and its evil" — and the verse gathers all of it [Qurṭubī]. ***(weak)*** A report traces "the Unseen" to a chain containing al-Kalbī, which the muḥaddithūn have not upheld. From Ibn ʿUmar: Islam is built on five — testifying that there is no god but God and that Muḥammad is His Messenger, establishing the prayer, giving the alms, fasting Ramadan, and the pilgrimage [Ibn Kathīr]. Abu Jumuʿah al-Anṣārī narrated: we said, "O Messenger of God, are there people who will have greater reward than us?" He ﷺ said: "What prevents you, when the Messenger of God is among you bringing revelation from heaven? There are people who will come after you and be given a Book between two covers; they will believe in it and act on it — they have greater reward than you, twice over" [Ibn Kathīr]. And the Prophet's counsel on spending: four dinars — one in the way of God, one to free a slave, one in alms to a poor man, and one on your family — "the greatest of them in reward is the one you spend on your family" (Muslim; and from Thawbān similarly, Abū Qilāba adding: and what man's reward is greater than one who spends on small children, keeping them chaste and sufficing them?) [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings.** **(1) What faith is.** In the tongue's usage faith is *taṣdīq*, but in the Scripture's reckoning it is confirmation of the word by the deed, and a term embracing affirmation of God, His Books and His Messengers together with action upon what the tongue affirms [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]. It increases and decreases; it is not mere knowledge, for Satan knows and does not believe [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif]. **[Maʿārif]** draws the distinction of the schools: lexically *īmān* is assent in the heart, *islām* is obedience and submission; in the Sharīʿah neither is valid without the other — an inner faith unspoken, or a spoken surrender unmeant, is not the thing the Qurʾān calls faith. The two cannot be separated in the servant who has both, though the Qurʾān and the Sunnah speak of each where the other is meant. **(2) Establishing the prayer.** *Iqāma* means more than praying: to perform it completely, in its conditions, obligations and recommended parts, punctually and continuously, with the heart present [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif] [Saʿdī]. Is the *iqāma* itself an obligation or a confirmed practice? The majority hold it a Sunnah whose omission needs no repeating; al-Awzāʿī, ʿAṭāʾ, Mujāhid, Ibn Abī Laylā and the Ẓāhirīs held it obligatory and repeatable, and Ibn al-ʿArabī chose this, arguing from the Prophet's command to the Bedouin, "and establish" — as he was commanded the takbīr, the facing of the qiblah and the ablution [Qurṭubī]. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr answered with the Prophet's own "its invalidation is the takbīr": one who has not entered the prayer by the takbīr has not entered it, and what lies before the takbīr of iḥrām cannot be a cause for repeating a prayer the man has properly begun. ʿUmar ʿibn al-Khaṭṭāb: "Whoever guards it and keeps watch over it guards his religion; whoever wastes it is more wasteful of all that is besides it" [Qurṭubī]. **(3) Spending.** Ibn ʿAbbās: the obligatory alms, named alongside the prayer. Ibn Masʿūd: a man's spending on his household, before the alms were imposed. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk: charity at will, "until the seven verses of Barāʾah came down" and fixed the alms. **[Qurṭubī]** prefers the general reading: the verse praises them for spending out of the good they have been given, and everything the Sharīʿah has made due — alms, family maintenance, obligations arising at need, voluntary giving — falls under it. The report of the Qur'anic baseline stands: where the Qurʾān uses *infaq* it ordinarily covers the voluntary, and reserves *zakāh* for the obligatory [Maʿārif].

**Belief.** The verse fixes the inward order of things: faith first, because no deed is accepted without it and no rank is reached except through it [Maʿārif]; then worship, which is God's right; then spending, which is kindness to His creatures [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Qurṭubī]** marks the three shares: *faith in the Unseen* is the heart's portion, *establishing the prayer* the body's, *spending from what We provided* the wealth's. Al-Zuhrī's dictum — faith is indeed the deed, because the term covers it — is the same concern from another side [Ṭabarī]. *Ghayb* as the object of faith also names the manner of it: God is absent from the eyes in this world, not from sight and proof; the believer affirms him as a Lord who will judge deeds, and fears Him in his secrets and solitudes as in public [Qurṭubī]. Not the least part of what is unseen is the perfection of God's names and attributes; the believer affirms them, knows they are, and does not claim to fathom their "how" [Saʿdī].

**Language.** *Īmān* is *taṣdīq* — affirming another's word out of trust; it is wrongly translated "faith" if faith is taken for a mood. The Qurʾān uses it for trust between creatures ("But you will not believe us, though we speak the truth"; "He trusts in God, and trusts the believers"), and it is carried by both *bāʾ* and *lām* [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī]. *Ghayb* is from a root of absence: the sun sets, a man goes away, a hollow of ground is hidden from sight — whatever is absent from you is *ghayb* [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. *Iqāma* is from standing, and means making a thing stand firm: like a market kept going, they say the people "raised" their market when they did not leave it idle; the verb implies permanence and regularity [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. *Ṣalāh* in the speech of the Arabs is *duʿāʾ* — al-Aʿshā's wine-jar poem has "he calls down blessing over it and mutters" — and the appointed prayer takes its name because the one who prays stands exposed before God seeking the success of his request, like a suppliant [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]; *fī ṣalā* therefore means a continuing in the thing, a holding-fast [Qurṭubī]. The "We provided them" of the verse is deliberate: what is in men's hands is not by their strength but is God's provision entrusted to them — so He asks to spend *of* it, partitively, not *it* [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]. And *yuqīmūna* rather than *yafʿalūna*: the shell of the prayer is not enough; its spirit is the presence of the heart, and a servant has from his prayer only what he understood of it [Saʿdī]. **[Study Quran]** notes the movement of the verse — from a hidden inward act, to one whose act is visible but whose object is hidden, to a visible act toward a visible object — an outward progression that sets the pattern of the whole religious life.

**Cross-references.** Spoken of God's way in the same words: "They believe in the unseen… and spend out of what We provided them" (8:3; 14:31; 22:35; 28:54; 32:16) [Study Quran] [Saʿdī]. Prayer in the general and the technical senses: 33:56 (blessing on the Prophet); 19:31 (Jesus's prayer); 20:14 (Moses commanded to establish it) [Study Quran]. Fear of the Lord "in the Unseen": 67:12; 50:33; 35:28 [Ibn Kathīr]. Faith joined to deeds: 95:6; and the distinction of *islām* and *īmān*: 49:14 [Maʿārif]. The whole creed detailed at the sūrah's close, 2:285–286 [Maʿārif]. Hypocrisy as the counterfeit of faith: 4:145 — its bearers in the lowest depth of the Fire [Maʿārif].

**Stories & occasions.** A Companion of Qatāda's transmitted his word to Ibn Masʿūd's circle: "Son of Adam, if you will only come to good when you are lively, your soul is prone to boredom, dullness and weariness. The believer is he who burdens himself, the believer is he who strengthens himself, the believer is he who toughens himself; and the believers cry out to God night and day — by God, the believer does not cease saying, 'Our Lord, our Lord,' in secret and in the open until he is answered in secret and in the open" [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** Here the Qurʾān calls the path straight only after showing us its travellers, so that the seeker might join their company and take on their belief and way of life [Maʿārif]. And the verse is a whole sum of religion: faith in what we cannot see, taught by One we cannot see; worship of the One who is absent from our eyes but not from our lives; and wealth handled as a trust handed back in part — the seal of a life that is both creed and practice. "The success of the servant is his sincerity toward his Lord and his striving to benefit creation; and the misery of the servant is the absence of these two — no sincerity and no kindness" [Saʿdī].


## 2:4

*"And those who believe in what has been sent down to you and what has been sent down before you, and of the Hereafter they are certain."*

**Meaning.** More marks of the God-fearing, and this time a detailed statement of faith: they believe in the revelation given to the Prophet ﷺ and in what was revealed before him — the Torah, the Gospel and the other Scriptures — making no distinction among the Messengers and denying nothing that came from their Lord [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr]; and the Hereafter they know to be real with the knowledge that admits no doubt [Qurṭubī] [Study Quran].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** reports the Companion view through al-Suddī and Ibn ʿAbbās: these four verses describe two sorts of God-fearing believers — those who had been associators and disbelievers and then accepted Islam (v. 3), and those who had been among the People of the Book and then embraced Islam (v. 4) — and this is why the earlier Books are named here, for such believers deserve the double recompense: for believing in the earlier Books before the Qurʾān replaced them, and for believing in the Qurʾān when it came as the final Book [Maʿārif] [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Qurṭubī]** adds that some held the first verse was revealed for the believers among the Arabs and this one for the believers among the People of the Book, such as ʿAbdullāh b. Salām; and he preserves the scene of the claimants: when "those who believe in the Unseen" came down the Jews and Christians claimed it, and when "establish the prayer" and "spend" came down they claimed those too — but when "those who believe in what has been sent down to you and what has been sent down before you" came, they drew back. **[Ibn Kathīr]** keeps both readings open: the four verses may be general, embracing every believer — Arab or non-Arab, of former Scripture or not, even jinn and humans — since the attributes require one another and no one believes in the Unseen, prays and gives without believing in what the Messenger was sent with; and "the faithful among the People of the Book have a special significance here", for they believed twice.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The Prophet ﷺ counted the Books sent down: one hundred and four — fifty scrolls on Shīth, thirty on Akhnūkh, ten on Ibrāhīm, ten on Mūsā before the Torah, then the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms and the Furqān (al-Ājurrī and Abū Ḥātim) [Qurṭubī]. And when the People of the Book narrate, the Prophet ﷺ taught the middle path: "Neither reject nor affirm them; but say: We believe in what was revealed to us and what was revealed to you" [Ibn Kathīr].

**Rulings.** How is belief in all the Books possible when their rulings differ? **[Qurṭubī]** gives two answers: that one believe all of them came down from God — the position of those who hold that the earlier religious laws no longer bind; or that one believe in what was not abrogated of them — the position of those who required adherence to the earlier laws, "as will be explained, God willing". **[Maʿārif]** states the obligation as it now stands: every Muslim must believe in the earlier Books as they were originally revealed — everything God sent down in them is true, save the changes and distortions men introduced — and that those for whom they were sent were bound to act by them, but now that the earlier Books and Sharīʿahs have been abrogated, one acts by the Qurʾān alone. As for books held sacred by other traditions, one neither affirms their claim outright nor rejects the possibility offhand: what agrees with the Qurʾān may be accepted as true, and what does not is left alone — and a comparative reading of sacred books is a perilous undertaking that needs a rare aptitude and an authentic teacher.

**Belief.** The Book names this faith in the plainest terms: "The Messenger believes in what was sent down to him from his Lord, and the believers — each believes in God, His angels, His Books and His Messengers; we make no distinction among His Messengers" (2:285); "and those who believe in God and His Messengers and make no distinction among any of them" (4:152); "O you who believe, believe in God and His Messenger and the Book He sent down upon His Messenger and the Book He sent down before" (4:136). **[Maʿārif]** draws from the verse an argument for the finality of prophethood: revelation is named only as coming *before* the Prophet ﷺ — the Qurʾān says *min qablik* forty or fifty times and never once speaks of a prophet or Book coming after him; had one been intended, this definition of faith would have had to name it. **[Ibn Kathīr]** notes that the People of the Book who believed received two rewards, and says that the faith of the Arabs who believed at first hand may yet be firmer and more encompassing, and God knows best — the reward is His to apportion.

**Language.** *Al-Ākhira* is a description of the Abode: it is "the last" in relation to a first already before it, as one says of favours, "You did me one kindness after another, and you thanked me for neither the first nor the last" [Ṭabarī]; some say it is named for coming after the creation, as the world is named *dunyā* for being near [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. It is also called the Abode of Settlement, the Abode of Living, the Consequence [Maʿārif]. *Yaqīn* is knowledge and not doubt; the *wāw* in *mūqin* replaced the *yāʾ* because of the *ḍammah* before it, and the diminutive restores the original (*mūyqin*) — for diminution and plurals return words to their roots; and *ẓann* (conjecture) is at times used for *yaqīn* in the language, as in the poet's lion who "was certain" — meaning thought — "I would ransom her from him and not fight" [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 2:285; 4:136; 4:152; 4:47 ("believe in what We have revealed, confirming what is with you"); 5:68; 29:46 ("We believe in what was revealed to us and revealed to you"); 3:199 (People of the Book who believe); 98:1–8; and, for the two groups, 2:62 and 5:69 [Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]. The *min qablik* chain of verses: 16:43; 40:78; 20:47; 4:60; 39:65; 42:3; 2:183 ("fasting was decreed for you as for those before you"); 17:77 [Maʿārif]. The Hereafter's certainty elsewhere: 2:249; 27:3; 31:4; 30:60 (Study Quran's cross-references within the surah's opening) [Study Quran].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** marks the role of this doctrine in history: faith in the Hereafter was the belief that "revolutionised" the community of the Qurʾān — no penal law can reach a man in the privacy where the state cannot intrude, but the man certain that a watcher sees and a recorder writes keeps his private conduct as straight as his public conduct; from that harmony came the clean society whose manners made onlookers fall in love with Islam. Certainty of the Hereafter must follow the confession of the tongue if it is to be real. And the ordering of the verse deserves notice: the Hereafter is named after the general faith in the Unseen because it is, in a sense, the most important of the articles — the spur that turns belief into practice [Maʿārif].

## 2:5

*"These are on guidance from their Lord, and these are the successful."*

**Meaning.** The verdict on the two descriptions gone before: these — the believers in the Unseen who pray and spend, and the believers in the Books and the Hereafter — are upon a light and an uprightness from their Lord [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr], and they are the ones who attain what they sought and are saved from what they fled, in this world and the next [Ṭabarī] [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr].

**Context.** Whom does "these" gather? **[Ṭabarī]** reports three positions: the two parties of the previous verses together; the God-fearing alone as one people; or the believers of the People of the Book alone. He chooses the first — the two parties together — and gives the reason that matches God's justice: God praised both parties for their qualities, and since in justice He cannot single one out for recompense while denying the other its wage, He does not single one out for praise either, for praise is itself a kind of recompense. He then works the grammar with his usual thoroughness: on the reading that the description belongs to two groups, the relative pronoun can be *rafʿ* in four ways — joined to the "those" implied in *yuʾminūna*, or as a fresh subject whose predicate is *ulāʾika ʿalā hudā* — or *khafḍ* in two, joined to *al-muttaqīn*. **[Qurṭubī]** finds in "from their Lord" a refutation of the Qadarīs, who claim men create their own faith and guidance: had that been so, the verse would have said "from themselves"; guidance is God's granting and His enabling.

**Language.** *Al-Falāḥ* comes from *falḥ* — cutting, splitting: "iron is split by iron", the splitting of the earth in ploughing (*filāḥa*), the ploughman (*fallāḥ*), and a man whose lower lip is split is *aflaḥ* [Qurṭubī]. So the *mufliḥ* is one who has cut through the obstacles and reached what he wanted; the root also carries success (*fawz*) and permanence (*baqāʾ*), and the poets witness both senses — Labīd's "he prospers who has sense enough to hobble his camel"; "we camp in lands where all have camped before us, and we hope for *falāḥ* after ʿĀd and Ḥimyar", meaning *baqāʾ*; and the lines of ʿAbīd and al-Nābigha [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. In common usage *falāḥ* is attaining the sought and escaping the feared [Qurṭubī]. Its connection with cultivation survives in the adhān, in the caller's "Come to thriving" (*ḥayya ʿalā l-falāḥ*) [Study Quran]. *Ulāʾika* has dialect variants — the people of Najd say *alāk* and *alā laka* — and it may be used of non-rational beings, as in 17:36, "all these shall be questioned about it" [Qurṭubī].

**Readings.** Ḥamza read *ʿalayhum*, *ilayhum*, *ladayhum* — but not *min rabbuhum*, *fīhum* or *jannatayhum* — because in these three the *yāʾ* is a transformed *alif* (originally *ʿalāhum*, *ladāhum*, *ilāhum*), so the *hāʾ* is left on its *ḍammah*; al-Kisāʾī agreed with him in "upon them the abasement" and "to them the two" [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** Guidance in this verse is not information but a light, an uprightness and a soundness given by God [Ṭabarī]. The Qurʾān has opened with the Book, then shown who may ride it, then given the verdict before the road is walked: the two parties are already "on guidance" and already "the successful" — success, in the Qurʾān's language, is not a prize at the end of a long road only; it is the road itself, trodden by those whose hearts and hands and purses are in the right order.


## 2:6

*"As for the disbelievers, it is the same to them whether you warn them or do not warn them; they will not believe."*

**Meaning.** The sūrah turns from those who receive the Book to those who seal themselves against it: for this group, warning and its absence weigh the same — they do not believe [Jalālayn] [Study Quran]. "It is the same" (*sawāʾ*) means the two courses are level, equal in their case [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** weighs three reports. From Ibn ʿAbbās, the verse was sent down concerning the Jews around Madīnah who denied the prophethood of Muḥammad ﷺ while knowing he was God's Messenger to them and to all people — and he adds a second report from Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭalḥah: the Prophet ﷺ was eager that all people should believe and follow him, so God informed him that none believes except the one for whom happiness was already written, and none goes astray except the one for whom misery was already written. From al-Rabīʿ b. Anas: it was sent down concerning the leaders of the factions who were killed at Badr, "those who exchanged God's favour for denial and brought their people to the abode of ruin" (14:28–29). Ṭabarī chooses the first: the verses follow the mention of the believers of the People of the Book, and the natural sequel is the mention of the disbelievers among that same people, against whom the sūrah opens its case — the Prophet had been shown what the rabbis hid, so that they might know the One who disclosed it is the One who sent down the Torah. **[Qurṭubī]** collects the same views — the heads of the Jews, Ḥuyayy b. Akhṭab, Kaʿb b. al-Ashraf and their like; or the leaders killed at Badr — and gives his verdict: the general statement carries a specific meaning, for one who is named is only an instance of the one whose death in disbelief disclosed what was hidden from us, and that instance falls within the verse's scope. **[Jalālayn]** offers the plain example: "the like of Abū Jahl, Abū Lahab and their kind". **[Maʿārif]** frames the whole passage: the first five verses describe those who receive guidance; the next fifteen treat those who refuse it — two of them the open disbelievers, thirteen the hypocrites. And the prediction "they do not believe" is not a verdict on disbelievers as a class: it concerns those who refused to hear and whom God knew would die on their denial, for many disbelievers afterwards accepted Islam.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The word *kufr* is not only denial of God; the Prophet ﷺ, describing what he saw in the Fire, was asked, "Do they disbelieve in God?" and answered of the women: "They disbelieve their husbands and disbelieve kindness — if you were good to one of them for a whole age, and then she saw something from you, she would say, 'I never saw any good in you'" (Bukhārī). So *kufr* covers ingratitude: the covering-over of a benefaction received [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings.** *Inḍhār* is not the threat of an enemy but the warning of a friend: it is to inform of something while awakening fear, and it must leave room in time to take precaution — where there is no time to avoid, it is only notification, not warning — which is why a thief or a highwayman cannot be called a *nadhīr*; the title belongs to the prophets, who warn of the next world out of compassion, as a father warns his child of fire or snakes [Qurṭubī] [Maʿārif]. The verse's purpose is thus to console the warner, not to excuse the warned: the duty is conveyance, and the reckoning is God's — "your duty is only to convey, and on Us is the reckoning" (13:40); "you are only a warner" (11:12) [Ibn Kathīr].

**Belief.** What is written does not abolish the command: the Prophet was told that some of the arrogant recognise the truth and stubbornly persist, so that all his effort for them would bear no fruit — and he was not to consume himself in grief over them [Maʿārif] [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Maʿārif]** reads from all this a principle the Qurʾān itself states: "It is He who created you; yet some of you are disbelievers and some believers" (64:2) — mankind is divided by principle, not by birth, race, colour or land.

**Language.** The root of *kufr* is covering: the night is called *kāfir* because its darkness covers everything — "in a night whose clouds covered its stars" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]; the farmer is *kāfir* — "like rain whose vegetation pleased the tillers" (57:20) — because he covers the seed with earth; ashes are *makfūr* when the wind has swept dust over them; the sea and a great river are *kāfir*, and the far land that no one frequents is *kāfir* land [Qurṭubī]. The rabbis of Madīnah covered the Prophet's affair, though they found his description in their books [Ṭabarī]. *Sawāʾ* is from equality and balance, as in 8:58, "throw it back to them on equal terms" — that your knowledge and theirs of the state of war be level — and the poets use it of light and dark made even: "the dark-eyed mare hurries with me toward the son of Jaʿfar; night and day are the same to her"; "in a night about whose darkness a man says, 'the sound of eye and the blind are alike'" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The interrogative form "whether you warn them or not" is not a true question but marks the equality of the two courses, as one says, "It is no matter to me whether you sat or stood" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī].

**Readings.** The form *a-andhartahum* (two *hamzas*) was recited eight ways: (1) realising the first and easing the second — the reading of Madīnah, Abū ʿAmr, al-Aʿmash and ʿAbdullāh b. Abī Isḥāq, chosen by al-Khalīl and Sībawayh, and the speech of Quraysh and Saʿd b. Bakr, witnessed by the poets' *ānta* ("are you"); (2) a single *hamza* with no second, from Ibn Muḥayṣin — the interrogative *am* suffices, as in the poet's "she goes out at evening or comes at dawn"; (3) both *hamzas* realised with a separating *alif*, from Ibn Abī Isḥāq; (4) inserting the *alif* while easing the second, done often by Abū ʿAmr and Nāfiʿ; (5) realising both *hamzas* — the reading of Ḥamza, ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī, and Abū ʿUbayd's choice, though al-Khalīl found it heavy and Sībawayh likened it to *ḍaninnū*; (6) easing the first (al-Akhfash, who called it poor since easing follows difficulty, and one *hamza* already strains); (7) easing both, permitted by Abū Ḥātim; and an eighth which is not a reading, replacing the *hamza* with *hāʾ* (*hā-andhartahum*), since it departs from the written text [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 10:96–97 ("those against whom the word of your Lord is due will not believe, though every sign come to them"); 2:145 (the People of the Book would not follow your qiblah); 13:40; 11:12 [Ibn Kathīr]. Warnings of no avail: 7:193; 26:136; 36:10; 63:6 [Study Quran]; the same phrase on the lips of Noah's people, "it is all the same to us whether you admonish or are not among the admonishers" (26:136) [Qurṭubī]. The concealers: 2:159 (those who hide what We sent down) [Ṭabarī]; the exchanged favour: 14:28–29 [Ṭabarī].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** draws the line the sūrah draws: this Book is guidance, and around it men stand in two ranks — those who take it and those who turn from it. The disbelievers treated here are first the open rejecters, and then, in the thirteen verses that follow, the hypocrites who had not the honesty even of unbelief. And the warner is taught the manner of all who would reform: not to fling a message at listeners, but to speak to them with sympathy, understanding and a genuine regard for their good — the prophets' warning is that of a father at the edge of a pit, not the shout of a robber. Meanwhile the stubbornness of the sealed is given a picture in the next verse: the avenues of knowing shut, one by one.


## 2:7

*"God has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and on their eyes is a covering; and theirs is a great punishment."*

**Meaning.** The disbelievers' refusal is traced to its cause: their hearts and ears have been stamped shut, so no good enters them; their eyes are veiled, so they do not see the truth; and there awaits them a mighty, lasting chastisement [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr]. The verse describes the punishment that begins in this world — the withdrawal of the capacity to receive — before the punishment of the next [Maʿārif] [Saʿdī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** again weighs the reports on whom the description falls: Ibn ʿAbbās's rabbis of Madīnah who denied the prophethood they knew; al-Rabīʿ b. Anas's leaders of the factions killed at Badr; and he had already preferred the first. **[Ibn Kathīr]** places the two verses in the sūrah's plan: four verses described the believers, two the open disbelievers, and then thirteen would describe the hypocrites — for hypocrisy is the subtlest of the three, and the most dangerous, so God details it at length.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The keys to this verse are the Prophet's own words. From Abū Hurayrah: "When the believer commits a sin, a black spot appears in his heart. If he repents and desists and seeks forgiveness, his heart is polished; but if he sins again it increases until it closes over his heart — and that is the rust (*rān*) God speaks of: 'No! But what they earned has rusted their hearts' (83:14)" — recorded by Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī, who graded it *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*, al-Nasāʾī and Ibn Mājah [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif] [Qurṭubī]. From Ḥudhayfah: trials are offered to hearts the way straws are woven into a mat, one by one; the heart that absorbs them is marked with a black spot and the heart that rejects them with a white one, until hearts become two: white as bare rock, which no trial harms as long as the heavens and earth stand, and black like an overturned cup, knowing no good and denying no evil [Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr]. The Prophet ﷺ called the heart "a feather tossed by the winds in a wilderness" (Ibn Mājah, from Abū Mūsā) and prayed: "O You who twist the hearts, make our hearts firm in Your religion" [Qurṭubī]. And from him: "In the body is a morsel: if it is sound, the whole body is sound; if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt — it is the heart" [Qurṭubī], by which she showed the seal to be something real (*ḥaqīqī*) in the heart, God knows best.

**Rulings & Belief.** The seal (*khatm*) is the stamp: the seal and the signet of a letter secure what is inside [Ṭabarī]. Tabarī answers the sharp question — how can a heart be sealed, since one seals vessels and wrappings? — with the answer that the hearts of servants are vessels for the knowledge deposited in them, so the seal upon them and upon the hearing, through which the reports of hidden things are received, is like the seal on any vessel: faith reaches such hearts only after the seal is broken and the knot untied [Ṭabarī]. And he lays down the order of cause and effect: sins in succession close the heart, and when they have wrapped it, the seal from God comes upon it — so the punishment fits the crime, and no path of faith enters the sealed. He rejects the claim that the verse merely describes their arrogance and turning away, holding that the authentic report has settled the meaning. Where some made the sealing an excuse, he turns the verse the other way: it is among the clearest proofs of the falsity of those who deny that God imposes duties man can bear only with His help — for God tells us that He sealed those hearts and yet holds them accountable, which shows that His assistance is the servant's ground of action [Ṭabarī]. Qurṭubī's summary: the community has agreed that God has described Himself with sealing the hearts of disbelievers as a punishment for their disbelief — "Nay, God has sealed them for their disbelief" (4:155). The limbs follow the heart, yet the heart, though their king, is touched by their deeds; outward and inward are bound together [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** *Khatm* is *ṭabʿ* — stamping [Ṭabarī]. *Qalb* is from *taqallub*, turning: the container turned on its face is *qalb*, and the organ is named for the rapid thoughts that turn upon it — "the heart is only named for its turning; so beware the heart, its turning and change." The Arabs kept the *qāf* emphatic in naming the organ, to distinguish it from its root [Qurṭubī]. *Ghishāwah* is a covering — the smoke that "covered" the magnate in al-Nābigha's line; and the poet al-Ḥārith speaks of following his companion "while a covering was on my eye; and when it cleared, I cut myself with blame" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The heart may also be spoken of by *fuʾād* or *ṣadr* — "Thus, that We might strengthen your heart by it" (25:32) [Qurṭubī]. **[Study Quran]** marks the heart's role in the Qurʾān: it is the seat of sentiment, but also of knowledge and faith — the locus of revelation to the Prophet (2:97), of veiling (6:25; 17:46), of rust (83:14), of hardening (2:74) and of blindness (22:46).

**Readings.** *Ghishāwah* is recited in the nominative — "and on their eyes is a covering" — or in the accusative, "and [stamped] their eyes with a covering"; Ṭabarī prefers the nominative for two reasons: the consensus of the authorities on its soundness, and that sealing is never ascribed to the eyes in the Book, the Prophetic reports or the language — the heart and hearing are sealed, the eyes veiled, and Ibn Jurayj taught the same [Ṭabarī]. The accusative is carried by an implied "He put"; or by following the place of *samʿ*, as in the Qurʾānic sequence "with cups and ewers... and fruit... and flesh... and dark-eyed maidens" (56:17–22), where the reading follows the end of the sentence by the beginning — and as the poet says, "I fed her straw and cool water", or "I saw your husband in the battle-line wearing a sword and a spear" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. In the plural the word becomes *ghashāʾ*, with the *hāʾ* dropped — so Ibn Kaysān; al-Farrāʾ reports *ghashāwī* [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 83:14 (*al-rān* — the rust of earning) [Ṭabarī] [Maʿārif]; 4:155 (sealed for disbelief); 2:10 (increase of disease in the heart) [Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]. Sealing elsewhere: 6:46; 7:100–101; 9:87, 93; 10:74; 16:108; 18:57 [Study Quran]. And the heart's conditions: 2:74; 6:25; 17:46; 22:46 — and 8:24, "Know that God intervenes between a man and his heart" [Qurṭubī] [Study Quran]. Contrast the opening of good: 2:269 (whoever is given wisdom is given much good) and 20:114 ("My Lord, increase me in knowledge") [Study Quran].

**Stories & occasions.** Mujāhid used to show it with his hand: he would say, "They used to think the heart was like this" — the open palm — "so when a servant sinned, part of it was folded"; and he folded finger after finger with each sin until the fingers were all closed; then, he said, it was stamped with a seal — and he said that this is the *rayn* [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]. And from him too: the stain is not as severe as the stamp, nor the stamp as severe as the lock [Ibn Kathīr].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** sees here the true form of worldly punishment: the withdrawn favour that helps a man reform, so that he grows in disobedience until even the awareness of evil is lost; one punishment for an evil deed is another evil deed after it, and one reward for a good deed is another good deed after it. He also fixes the pronoun's force: "it is the same *for them*" — not for the Prophet ﷺ, who is rewarded for conveying the message whether or not it takes effect; not one verse in the Qurʾān deters him from calling even the hardest hearts. And he removes the doubt from the doctrine of the seal: read 2:7 with 83:14 and the order is plain — the rust of their own arrogance and choices closed their hearts, and God, whose way it is, produced the insensitivity after; the deed is attributed to Him as Creator, and its authorship remains theirs. **[Study Quran]** records both readings: the seal as a predestinarian decree, or as spiritual punishment and consequence rather than cause — a reminder that the Qurʾān speaks of the God-ward cause of unbelief and the human-ward cause together, and never to let one cancel the other.


## 2:8

*"And among mankind are those who say, 'We believe in God and the Last Day' — and they are not believers."*

**Meaning.** The sūrah now turns to the third class of men: those whose tongues profess what their hearts deny [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr]. *Nifāq* is to show agreement and conceal evil [Ibn Kathīr] — to say with the tongue what is not in the heart, so that God gives them the lie: "and they are not believers" [Saʿdī].

**Context.** All the commentators agree the verse concerns the hypocrites [Ṭabarī]: the Aws and Khazraj and those who behaved like them (Ibn ʿAbbās) [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]; and Mujāhid's count fixes the place — "four verses in the believers, two in the disbelievers, thirteen in the hypocrites", these thirteen beginning here [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** gives the history: hypocrisy did not exist in Mecca, for the believer there hid his faith and the disbeliever was open; it began in Madīnah when Islam gained power at Badr and men who had not embraced it saw their position exposed — among them ʿAbdullāh b. Ubayy b. Salūl, chief of Khazraj, who had been on the point of being crowned king when the revelation arrived and who then "became a Muslim" with those like him. From that day hypocrisy spread in the city and among the nomads around it; the Emigrants were free of it, since a man does not abandon home and wealth for a claim he does not believe. **[Ṭabarī]** paints the scene in detail: after the Hijrah, the rabbis of Madīnah showed their envy, and a party of the Anṣār's tribesmen who had grown up in polytheism joined them secretly; when they met the Prophet ﷺ and the believers they said, "We believe in God, His Messenger and the Resurrection", guarding themselves from the judgment due to idolaters; and when they met the Jews and those who denied the Prophet, they said, "We are with you — we are only mocking" (2:14).

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** "The sign of the hypocrite is three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks it, and when he is trusted he betrays" — and in another narration, "and when he disputes, he acts viciously" [Saʿdī]. Faith itself is defined in the reports as "knowledge in the heart, a word on the tongue, and action with the limbs" (Ibn Mājah) [Qurṭubī]. And the Prophet ﷺ absolved the Emigrants of one thing he feared might be laid to their charge: "By God, I would not mind which of you I bought from [in a pledge]: if he is a Muslim, his religion will bring it back to me; and if he is a Jew or Christian, his employer will bring it back" — no; the report intended here is the one in Muslim from Ibn Masʿūd: the creation is gathered in the mother's womb — forty days a drop, forty a clot, forty a morsel of flesh; then the angel is sent and breathes the spirit into him and writes four words: his provision, his term, his deed, and whether he is miserable or happy; "and a man will work with the works of the people of the Garden until there is between him and it only an arm's length, and then the Book overtakes him, and he works with the works of the people of the Fire and enters it" — and the reverse [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings & Belief.** The verse is a standing refutation in two directions. Against those who claimed faith is speech alone, God recounts their words and then denies that they believe, since their hearts did not affirm what their tongues said [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] — they measure out two different answers to two different audiences and call it faith. And against those who would judge men by appearances, the verse teaches the community to see through the mask; God exposed them "so that the believers would not be deceived by their outward appearance" — for to treat sinners as righteous is a great danger [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Qurṭubī]** adds a subtler doctrine: the believer may be loved by God or hated by Him — not for his present faith but for what he will end upon; the disbeliever may be punished or spared, not for his present disbelief but for his final state. So "only the ending of deeds counts", and the Book and the Prophet's reports never announce a man's place in the Fire or the Garden as a present certainty — except where God Himself does.

**Language.** *Al-Nās* is either a plural with no singular of its own form (its singular being *insān*) or the old *unās*, a general noun from motion — "he moved (*nāsa*)", as Umm Zarʿ said, "rings moved on my ears" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. Others said it is from *nisyān* — forgetting: Adam forgot God's covenant, and "he was named *insān* because he forgets", and the Qūrʾān says of him, "We had charged him before, but he forgot" (20:115); or from *uns*, intimacy — "the human is named only for his intimacy, and the heart only because it turns" [Qurṭubī]. *Al-Yawm al-Ākhir* is the last day because there is no day after it; where one objects that the Hereafter never ends, the answer is from the Arab naming of a day by the night that comes before it: the Day of Resurrection has no night after it but the night in which the resurrection dawned, and so it is the last of days — the "barren day" (22:55) [Ṭabarī]. *Munāfiq* is from the jerboa's double burrow: one opening (*nāfiqāʾ*) whose outside is dust and whose depth is hollow; the imagery was said explicitly: "the exterior of his burrow is dust, its interior a hole; and so the hypocrite: outwardly faith, inwardly denial" [Qurṭubī]. His entry and his exit are not the same, nor his presence and his absence: *Ibn Jurayj*'s description is the verse itself [Ibn Kathīr].

**Cross-references.** 63:1 ("When the hypocrites come to you they say, 'We bear witness that you are the Messenger of God' — and God bears witness that the hypocrites are liars"); 4:142 ("The hypocrites try to deceive God, but it is He who deceives them"); 58:18 (they swear to Him as they swear to you) [Ibn Kathīr]. The mocking of 2:14; and 5:85 ("God rewarded them for what they said"), the Karrāmiyya's proof text [Qurṭubī].

**Stories & occasions.** ***(weak)*** ʿAbd al-Ghanī al-Miṣrī transmitted a report from Abū Razīn al-ʿUqaylī: "You and I, Abū Razīn, will drink milk whose taste has not changed" — and when asked how God revives the dead, the Prophet ﷺ pointed to the barren land that becomes fertile and back again: "Thus is the resurrection"; and to the question, "How shall I know that I am a believer?" he answered: "None of this community — or my community — works a good deed and knows it good, or a bad deed and knows it bad, and God will repay it, or forgive it, but a believer." Qurṭubī notes the chain is not strong, though the meaning is sound, and it does not clash with the report of the "endings", for this shows him to be a believer in the present [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** finds in the hypocrites' own words a lesson in how detestable a lie is: even these men, with all their hostility, shrank from falsehood where they could — in professing Islam they would mention only faith in God and the Last Day and leave out faith in the Prophet ﷺ, for fear of lying outright. And the sūrah's ordering carries its own rebuke: the sincere were named first, the open deniers next, and the hypocrites last, joined to the deniers — the hardest of hearts are those that will not choose. The man whose tongue and heart tell the same story knows exactly where he stands; the man whose two faces have grown apart no longer does — and, as Qurṭubī's division reminds us, the only reckoning that finally matters is the one at the end.


## 2:9

*"They would deceive God and those who believe, and they deceive none but themselves — and they perceive it not."*

**Meaning.** Their show of faith was a trick: with the tongue they affirmed what the heart denied, hoping to escape the judgment their denial deserved [Ṭabarī] [Jalālayn]. They imagine they have deceived God and the believers; the truth is that the evil of the trick returns on them, and they do not feel it [Qurṭubī] [Jalālayn].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** defines their deception precisely: it is their public saying of faith, contrary to the doubt and denial inside, to ward off from themselves the judgment that would fall on the like of them had they spoken out — the sword, captivity and the like. And he answers the objection that a man can hardly mean to deceive God: the Arabs call anyone who gives with his tongue other than what is in his heart, out of fear, a deceiver of the one he escapes from — and the hypocrite escaped death, captivity and immediate punishment by what his tongue showed. **[Qurṭubī]** reports three explanations from the scholars: that they deceive God "in their own estimation and thought", since no one actually deceives Him; that the description is of their act, which is the act of a deceiver; and, from al-Ḥasan and others, that the verse has an ellipsis — they deceive the Messenger of God ﷺ and the believers, and deceiving His Messenger is deceiving God, for He called them by His message, and to deceive the believers is to deceive God. **[Maʿārif]** draws the lesson: it may be that none of them ever intended to deceive God; what they did was to try to deceive the Prophet ﷺ and the Muslims, and God counted it an attempt on Himself — so that any impertinence toward a prophet or a man of God is impertinence toward God, most of all where the greatest of creation is concerned.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** A report from the Prophet ﷺ: "Do not deceive God; for whoever deceives God, God deceives him and his own soul deceives him, if he but knew." They asked: How does one deceive God? He said: "That you do what God has commanded you, seeking by it other than Him" [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings & Belief.** The verse shows that God's "deception" is His requital in kind: He gives the hypocrites respite and lets them run on — His *imlāʾ* and *istidrāj* — and their own trick gains them nothing but the loss of the insight that would save them [Ṭabarī]. **[Ṭabarī]** also notes here a proof against those who confine God's punishment to the man who denies in open defiance after recognising the truth: God tells us of people who did not even know they were in the wrong — and yet the painful punishment is theirs. **[Qurṭubī]** fixes the hard logic of the sentence: the trick can only work on one who does not know the hidden; the One whose knowledge covers the secrets cannot be tricked, so every man who enters such a bargain tricks himself — which shows the hypocrites did not know God; had they known Him, they would have known He is not deceived.

**Language.** *Mufāʿala* in Arabic is ordinarily between two parties; some grammarians therefore said *yukhādiʿūna* here carries the sense of a one-sided *yakhdaʿūna*, as in *qātalahu Allāh* for *qatalahu*. **[Ṭabarī]** rejects this: the reciprocity is real, since they deceive God by lying and God "deceives" them by abandoning them to their own blindness and preparing for them the door in the wall, behind which is mercy — and before it, punishment (57:13). Hence the second reading: they deceive none but themselves. **[Qurṭubī]** gives the root's two meanings: corruption — "white of colour, sweet of taste, and sweet the saliva when the saliva deceives" — and concealment — the *makhdaʿ* of a house, where a thing is hidden; the Arabs say of the lizard, "it hid itself in its hole". "They perceive not" is from *shuʿūr*, to sense or notice: the poet is the *shāʿir* for noticing what others do not, and "would that I knew" is *layta shiʿrī* [Qurṭubī].

**Readings.** The second occurrence is read *yukhādiʿūna* by Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr, keeping the two forms of the verb parallel; and *yakhdaʿūna* by ʿĀṣim, Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī and Ibn ʿĀmir [Qurṭubī]. Muwarriq al-ʿIjlī read *yukhaddeʿūna* — with a doubled *dāl* — for intensity; Abū Ṭāluт ʿAbd al-Salām b. Shaddād and al-Jārūd read *yukhdaʿūna* (passive): "and they are deceived only in respect of their own souls," with the preposition dropped, as in "Moses chose his people" (7:155) for "from his people" [Qurṭubī]. **[Ṭabarī]** argues at length for the reading *yakhdaʿūna* in the second place, since it is a settled deception that has actually come to pass, while the reciprocal form only describes an attempted bargain.

**Cross-references.** 4:142 ("The hypocrites seek to deceive God, but it is He who deceives them"); 57:13 ("Return behind you and seek light") [Ṭabarī] [Study Quran]. God's plotting against the plotting of the deniers: 10:21; 13:42; 14:46; 16:26; 27:50 [Study Quran]; and 3:178, "Let not the disbelievers think that Our respite is good for them — We give them respite only that they may increase in sin" [Ṭabarī].

**Reflection.** The verse is a mirror held to every later age: the man who serves God for a name, a rank, or a reputation, and imagines himself a believer, has entered the same bargain. Ibn Jurayj's portrait of the hypocrite is the anatomy of this self-deception: his words differ from his deeds, his secret from his openness, his coming from his going, his presence from his absence [Ibn Kathīr]. Such a man imagines he has bought safety on both sides; he has bought only his own soul, and he does not notice the price.

## 2:10

*"In their hearts is a disease, and God has increased their disease; and theirs is a painful punishment because they lied."*

**Meaning.** The verse explains their blindness: their hearts are sick with doubt and hypocrisy, and so the very revelation that heals others increases them in sickness [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī]. *Marad* is used of bodies and of religion alike [Ṭabarī]; what is meant here is the sickness of their beliefs, not of their bodies [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** gathers the reports on what the disease is: doubt (Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and their companions, Qatāda, al-Rabīʿ, Ibn Zayd); hypocrisy (al-Ḍaḥḥāk); and he fixes the sense: they are neither certain with the certainty of faith nor denying with the denial of the idolater, but, as God describes them, "wavering between this and that, belonging neither to these nor to those" (4:143). The increase of disease has its parallel: whatever new ordinance or limit God sent down, the believers received as an increase of faith, and the hypocrites as an increase of doubt — "When a sūrah is sent down... as for those in whose hearts is a disease, it adds filth to their filth" (9:124–125) [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr]. So the verse is the law of increase running in both directions: the word that feeds faith starves the disease, and the word that feeds the disease increases the faith [Ibn Kathīr].

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The Prophet's own reproof of a lying heart: "A man may tell the truth and a white dot is inscribed in his heart, and a man may lie and his heart is blackened"; and from Abū Hurayrah, "a man commits a sin and his heart darkens, and if he repents, his heart is polished — and that is the rust God mentioned" [Qurṭubī]. The notorious hypocrite of the community was ʿAbdullāh b. Ubayy b. Salūl; when ʿUmar raised his case, the Prophet ﷺ said: "I would not like the Arabs to say that Muhammad kills his companions." Yet when Ibn Salūl died, the Prophet ﷺ prayed over him: "I was given the choice, and I chose"; and, in another narration, "If I knew that asking forgiveness more than seventy times would bring him forgiveness, I would ask more" [Ibn Kathīr]. When God later forbade prayer over the hypocrites, they were not to be killed for what was hidden; Ḥudhayfah received fourteen names of the men who plotted to throw the Prophet's she-camel on the way back from Tabuk, and ʿUmar used to ask him: "Am I among them?" [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings.** Why were the hypocrites not put to death, when the Prophet ﷺ knew many of them? **[Qurṭubī]** reports four positions: that he alone knew them, and a judge does not judge on his own knowledge — though the Companions agreed on this only after the revelation ceased, and the killing of al-Mujadhdhir was a case decided by revelation; that the *zindīq* is asked to repent and not killed, which Ibn al-ʿArabī rejected, since no repentance was sought of them and no one relates it; that the Prophet ﷺ spared them for the sake of uniting hearts, as his word to ʿUmar shows, and as he gave to those whose hearts were to be reconciled — and this is the Mālikī reading of his restraint; and that they were spared only because God had kept His Companions from being corrupted by them, which is not assured today — so Mālik said: "Hypocrisy at the time of the Messenger of God ﷺ is *zandaqa* among us today", and the *zindīq* is killed on proof without deferment, one of al-Shāfiʿī's two views. The Prophet's own practice remains the measure for the hidden: he judged by what was open, and left the secrets to God [Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr].

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** uses the verse to argue the reading *yakdhibūn* — "because they lied" — against *yukadhdhibūn*, "because they denied": the opening of the passage was their lying claim "we believe", and the parallel verse settles it — "God bears witness that the hypocrites are liars" (63:1), where the punishment promised is for lying, not for open denial. And **[Qurṭubī]** has Abū Ḥātim take *mā* as a *maṣdariyya*: "because of their denial of the Messengers and their rejection of the signs". The two meanings are the two halves of the same vice: the tongue lies and the heart denies.

**Language.** The root of *marad* covers every departure from soundness — a cause, a hypocrisy, a falling short in any matter; the reciters agree on the *fatḥah* of the *rāʾ*, except that al-Aṣmaʿī reports Abū ʿAmr had it *mard*, stilled [Qurṭubī]. *Alīm* is *muʾlim* — painful — like *samīʿ* for *musmiʿ*, and Dhū l-Rumma's description of the camels in a scorching heat is the witness: "we raise up from the breasts of tall she-camels; a painful blaze strikes their faces" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The "increase" (*ziyāda*) of the verse may be read as a curse spoken over them or as a report of what God does — the sick heart goes from sickness to sickness [Qurṭubī]. **[Maʿārif]** adds the physicians' analogy of a spiritual master: as bodily illness comes from an imbalance of the humours, the illnesses of the heart come from following desires — and hypocrisy is a real disease: the sufferer trembles with fear of being found out, and feeds on an envy he cannot speak.

**Cross-references.** 9:124–125 (faith increased, filth upon filth); 24:50 ("Is there a disease in their hearts?"); 33:60–61 (the hypocrites, those with disease in the heart and the spreaders of false news); 47:30 ("you will know them by the tone of their speech"); 9:101 ("the people of Madīnah who persist in hypocrisy — you know them not, We know them") [Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]. Their oaths as covers: 58:16; 63:2. And the company of the sickness with lying: "avoid the filth of idols and avoid the word of falsehood" (22:30) [Maʿārif].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** marks why the punishment here is attached to lying, when their greater crimes were unbelief and conspiracy: lying was the root out of which the rest grew. A man who lies to God first must lie about God afterwards, and then lies to himself, until he cannot tell the difference — which is the medicine of the next verses, where he is made to recognise his own portrait. The disease is named and left named; the cure the sūrah does not yet supply, because the cure was never a matter of words, but of a heart willing to be sound.


## 2:11

*"And when it is said to them, 'Do not spread corruption in the earth,' they say, 'We are only reformers.'"*

**Meaning.** The hypocrites are warned against the double-dealing by which they work ruin, and they answer with a claim of the highest public virtue: we are the ones setting things right [Jalālayn] [Maʿārif] — and note the *innamā*, "nothing but" — an exclusivity that quietly excludes the believers from being reformers at all [Maʿārif] [Saʿdī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** reports Salmān al-Fārsī's saying about this verse: "These have not yet come" — which the commentators take not as a denial but as a statement that the description is not confined to the Prophet's contemporaries but reaches everyone of that character until the resurrection [Study Quran] [Ibn Kathīr]. The majority: it was said to the hypocrites of Madīnah, and means every hypocrite after them [Ṭabarī]. Two explanations of their reply are given: from Ibn ʿAbbās, "We only want to make peace between the two parties — the believers and the People of the Book"; from Mujāhid, "When they committed an act of disobedience and were told, 'Do not do such and such', they said, 'We are upon guidance, we are reformers'" [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī]. Whichever the claim, they believed themselves right: they thought that courting the Jews and wavering over the Prophet's prophethood was statesmanship [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr].

**Belief.** Corruption (*fasād*) here is disbelief and disobedience [Ibn Kathīr] [Jalālayn]; "whoever disobeys God in the earth, or commands that He be disobeyed, has spread corruption in it, for the setting right of earth and heaven is by obedience" [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]. And God's warning covers the hidden as much as the open: "God knows the corrupter from the reconciler" (2:220) — so the maker of order must have more than good intentions; intention must be lined up with the Sharīʿah [Maʿārif]. **[Saʿdī]** sharpens the point: they combined working corruption with presenting it as reform — putting the truth upside down, and calling the act of falsehood true; these are worse than the one who sins while believing it forbidden, for that one is nearer safety and more likely to return.

**Language.** *Idhā* is a future adverb carrying the sense of the condition, and the response of the condition can be the verb, the *fāʾ*, or *idhā* itself — "when our swords grew short, their joining was our steps to our enemies, and so we strike" [Qurṭubī]. *Qīla* comes from *quwila*: the *kasrah* of the *wāw* was moved to the *qāf* and the *wāw* turned to *yāʾ*; Hudhayl and the tribes of Dubayr and Faqʿas keep it *qūla* with a silent *wāw*; al-Kisāʾī allows the *qāf*'s *ḍammah* to be suggested by a slight rounding of the lips, the speech of Qays [Qurṭubī]. *Al-Arḍ* is a collective: its singular would have been *arḍah*; they form *arḍāt* with the *tāʾ*, then *araḍūn* with *wāw* and *nūn* though it is feminine — as compensation for the *alif* and *tāʾ* they dropped — and also *arūḍ* and *arāḍī*, the last against analogy [Qurṭubī]. The word extends in the language to the lower front hooves of a beast — the blacksmith "does not turn over its earth" — to a trembling — "By God, I do not know whether the earth shook or I have an earth in me," said Ibn ʿAbbās at Baṣrah — to a cold in the head — "God gave him the earth, so he is *maʾrūḍ*" — and to the *istiʾrāḍ* of a palm shoot striking its root into the earth; a man is *arīḍ* when disposed to good [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** "Do not spread corruption in the earth after it has been set right" (7:56) [Qurṭubī]; the angels' question, "Will You place in it one who spreads corruption and sheds blood?" (2:30) [Ṭabarī]; "God knows the corrupter from the reconciler" (2:220) [Maʿārif]. Loyalty to the enemies of God is itself corruption: "And those who disbelieve are allies of one another — if you do not do this, there will be turmoil in the earth and great corruption" (8:73); "O you who believe, do not take the disbelievers as friends instead of the believers" (4:144); and their end: "the hypocrites are in the lowest depth of the Fire" (4:145) [Ibn Kathīr]. Corruption as a working of men's hands: 30:41; 47:22 [Study Quran].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** sets the criterion: it is the deed, not the label, that decides whether a man is a reformer — what thief calls himself a thief? And when a man's activity in fact produces disorder, he is called a corrupter even if he intended no such thing. The subtle danger: open crimes are known and can be policed, but the mischief that wears the garb of reform — conspiracies, the flattery of enemies, the slow dissolution of morals — has no policeman, because it works through men themselves; and no statute, no university and no bureaucracy can make a man good if the fear of God and the thought of the reckoning are gone. That is why the Prophet ﷺ spent himself on making men real men; where his teaching was lived, order came of itself.

## 2:12

*"Nay! It is they who are the corrupters — but they perceive it not."*

**Meaning.** "Behold" (*alā*) turns their verdict on its head: the claim of reform belongs to those they were excluding, and the title of corrupter to them; yet they do not feel it — the very blindness they deny is their condition [Jalālayn] [Maʿārif].

**Context.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** gives the anatomy of their mischief, following al-Ṭabarī: they disobey their Lord, persist in what He forbade, neglect His obligations, doubt His religion — which He accepts no deed without — lie to the believers with the opposite of what their hearts hold, and help the enemies of God's friends against them. And the root of it is the double face: because their outward look is a believer's, they confuse the true believers; the deceit itself is the mischief, for the harm of a plain disbeliever would be less [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Saʿdī]** states the comparison that settles the verse: "There is no corruption greater than one who disbelieves in God's signs, turns away from His way, deceives God and His friends, allies with those who fight Him and His Messenger — and then claims that this is reform. Is there, after such corruption, any corruption?"

**Belief.** The blindness is not a lack of information but a lack of useful knowledge: they know enough for God's proof to stand against them, but they do not know with a knowledge that would save them [Saʿdī]. **[Maʿārif]** distinguishes the two kinds of mischief named in the verse: those activities plainly known to be disorderly — theft, robbery, violence — from which a sensible man refrains, and those which outwardly look harmless but work unseen to destroy the morals of men, opening the door to every disorder; it is of the second that the hypocrites were guilty. And because guidance cannot be measured by private intent, God's word to His servants closed with 2:220: "God knows the corrupter from the reconciler" — so the servant aligns his thought, his deed and the Sharīʿah together, for an action done with the best of intentions may still produce disorder if it is not guided by revelation [Maʿārif]. **[Saʿdī]** adds the measure of the earthly evil: disobedience has a purchase on the created world itself, bringing blight upon the crops and fruits and plants, while the land's true cultivation is to be peopled with obedience and faith in God — for that He made men dwell in the earth and poured out provision upon them, and to act there in the opposite is to work toward its ruin.

**Language.** *Alā* is an opening of attention, the herald's "behold", and with the separate pronoun — "they, they are the corrupters" — it makes the exclusion complete: no one else is; the restriction rebounds on the claimants [Maʿārif] [Saʿdī]. *Lā yashʿurūn* is from sensation and noticing, sharper than simple ignorance of the fact and truer of these men: they do not feel the ruin they carry, as a man does not feel a cancer in its first growth.

**Cross-references.** The same warning against confusing deeds with claims and the same "God knows the corrupter from the reconciler": 2:220 [Maʿārif]. The result of disobedience in the earth: 30:41; its reduction "by the deeds of men"; and 47:22 [Study Quran]. And the end of the game of two faces: 4:145; 8:73 [Ibn Kathīr].

**Reflection.** The verse is one of the Qurʾān's severest indictments of self-satisfaction: not the man who is corrupt, but the corrupt man who is certain of his own virtue, and therefore beyond cure — for the door of reform is the feeling that something is wrong, and it has been sealed from the inside. The cure is not offered here; the sūrah moves on to their mockery, as if to show where such a heart is going. But the hint has already been given: the earth is set right by obedience, and a man is a reformer not when he is sure that he is one, but when his deed, his intention and the revealed law all point the same way.


## 2:13

*"And when it is said to them, 'Believe as the people have believed,' they say, 'Shall we believe as the fools believe?' — Nay, it is they who are the fools, but they do not know."*

**Meaning.** The call to faith is put to the hypocrites in its plainest form — believe the way those whose faith is beyond question believe — and they answer with a sneer; the verse returns their label to them [Jalālayn] [Maʿārif]. "The people" here is the community of believers, the Muhājirūn and the sincere of Yathrib [Qurṭubī]; the consensus of the commentators is that it means the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ — the only "people" who believed in the Qurʾān while it was coming down, piece by piece, from God [Maʿārif].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]**'s chain of reports all name the same target: the hypocrites meant the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ — "Do we say as the fools say?", in al-Ḍaḥḥāk's version — and they said it in hiding and mockery, until God exposed His Prophet and the believers to it and set the verdict down [Qurṭubī]. A second report from Ibn ʿAbbās makes the verse about the Jews: "Believe as the people have believed" — meaning ʿAbdullāh b. Salām and his companions — and they answered, "Shall we believe as the ignorant and the reckless?" [Qurṭubī]. Either way the sneer is the same, and its motive the same: the call demanded that they follow the very people they wished to look down upon — the road of the Companions was the one road, and that was intolerable to them.

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** draws the principle the verse is built on: the faith of the Companions is the touchstone by which the faith of every later Muslim is tried. The only *īmān* acceptable to God is one like theirs, and any belief or deed that departs from their faith and their practice, however attractive it looks or sincere it feels, is not valid under the Sharīʿah — and on this the commentators are agreed. He also draws the lesson of their insult: it has always been the way of those who go astray that whoever shows them the path appears to them a fool; but who is more foolish than the man who refuses to see clear signs?

**Rulings.** **[Maʿārif]** takes up the legal after-history of *nifāq*: in the Prophet's time a hypocrite could be identified in one of two ways — God informed him by revelation, or the man exposed himself by word or deed plainly against the creed. Revelation has ceased, but the second way stands; whoever rejects or distorts or scorns the certain doctrines of the Book, the Sunnah and the consensus is a hypocrite in the Qurʾān's sense despite his claim — the Qurʾān calls him a *mulḥid*, "one who distorts Our signs" (41:40), and the Ḥadīth calls him a *zindīq*. And since his disbelief has been established by proof, the Sharīʿah does not keep him in a class of his own but treats him as a disbeliever — which is why the scholars concluded that after the Prophet's departure the question of "hypocrites" in the technical sense lapsed: anyone who is not a real believer is a *kāfir*, and Imam Mālik's statement to this effect is reported by al-ʿAynī in his commentary on al-Bukhārī.

**Language.** *As-Safīh* is the ignorant one of weak judgment and little knowledge of what harms and benefits; the term covers women and children, which is why the majority read *al-sufahāʾ* in "do not give the foolish your property" (4:5) as those whose counsel on money is not trusted [Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]. The root means lightness and thinness: a garment is *safīh* when its weave is poor or it is worn thin, and the wind *tusaffih* a tree when it bends it — Dhū l-Rumma's line describes women walking "as spears sway when the passing of soft winds bends their upper ends"; *tasaffahtu sh-shayʾ* is to hold something cheap [Qurṭubī]. *Safah* is the opposite of *ḥilm*, and its essence is precisely what Ṭabarī assigns to them: the fool ruins what he thinks he is mending, loses what he thinks he is keeping, and injures himself while imagining he is doing himself good [Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī]. Grammar: *āminū* takes the alif of cutting; the *kāf* of "as the people believed" stands in the place of the accusative as a description of an omitted *maṣdar* — "with a faith like the faith of the people" [Qurṭubī]; and the *alif* and *lām* in "the people" and "the fools" marks a party known to those addressed, as in "those to whom the people said, 'The people have gathered against you'" (3:173) [Ṭabarī]. The double *hamza* of *al-sufahāʾ* is recited four ways, the best being to realise the first and turn the second into a pure *wāw* — the reading of Madīnah and the well-known one of Abū ʿAmr; one may lighten both, lighten the first, or realise both [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 4:5 (the *sufahāʾ* and property); 3:173 (the *alif* and *lām* for a known group) [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]; 41:40 (the *mulḥidūn* who distort the signs) [Maʿārif].

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** puts the two words on the scale: foolishness in truth is ignorance of one's own interests and effort spent on what harms one — and that fits the hypocrites exactly, who fled the faith that would have saved them and kept their places among men by a lie; while intellect and sound judgment are knowing one's interests and seeking what profits, and that fits the Companions and the believers, who gave up homes and faced a world for what they believed. The measure is descriptions and proof, not claims and empty words. And the sneer has a history: every age has its men who call the straightest road stupidity, because it costs them the position they hold in the crooked one.



## 2:14

*"And when they meet those who believe they say, 'We believe'; but when they are alone with their devils they say, 'We are with you — we were only mocking.'"*

**Meaning.** The two faces are shown plainly: faith in public, denial in private [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī]. With the believers they put on belief; when they draw aside to their *shayāṭīn* — their leaders in evil — they confess that they were only playing with the believers [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]**'s reports (Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī, Qatāda, Mujāhid, al-Rabīʿ) all turn on the same scene: when some of them met the Prophet's Companions they said, "We are on your religion"; when they went apart to their patrons, they said, "We are with you — we were mocking them." Ibn Jurayj adds the detail of timing: when the believers had ease, they said, "We are your brothers"; but in private they mocked [Ṭabarī]. **[Study Quran]**, citing Ibn Kathīr, records a specific occasion: one of them boasted to his companions, "Watch how I divert these fools from you," went out and heaped praise on some of the Companions, and told his friends, "When you see them, do as I do"; the Companions reported it to the Prophet ﷺ, and the verse came down. **[Saʿdī]** names the state the verse exposes: this is their outward and their inward conduct together — and the evil plot encircles none but its authors.

**Belief.** Who are "their devils"? The leaders in disbelief (Ibn ʿAbbās), the chiefs in evil (Qatāda), the polytheists, their companions among the hypocrites and the idolaters (Mujāhid), the jinn (al-Kalbī), the soothsayers — and the term itself decides it: *shayṭana* is remoteness from faith and good, and so covers all of them [Qurṭubī]. **[Ṭabarī]** sets out the rule that governs it everywhere: the devils of anything are its rebels and outlaws — and God has made both kinds, "devils from mankind and jinn" (6:112) [Ibn Kathīr]. The sin of the verse is therefore not single-faced unbelief but the counterfeit of faith that uses the believers' trust as its cover; its author plots against God with a language he does not believe.

**Language.** *Laqū* comes from *laqiyū*: the *ḍammah* was moved to the *qāf* and the *yāʾ* dropped because two vowelless letters met; Muḥammad b. al-Samayqaʿ al-Yamānī read it *lāqawū*, with the *wāw* kept and given the *ḍammah*, for the *fatḥah* before it makes it light — while in *laqū* the *ḍammah* before the *wāw* would make it heavy, so it was dropped [Qurṭubī]. *Khalaw ilā* is more eloquent than the usual *khalaw bi-*: the Baṣran says *khalā ilā* is for a private errand and carries no ambiguity, whereas *khalā bi-* may suggest mockery; another says *ilā* here carries the sense of "with"; the Khalīl and Sībawayh reject making *ilā* a mere substitute for *bāʾ*, and **[Ṭabarī]** prefers the Kufan reading: it means "when they turned their solitude toward their devils", so *ilā* keeps its own face, as each particle of meaning has its own face and cannot be exchanged for another without proof [Qurṭubī] [Ṭabarī]. *Istihzāʾ* is from *huzuʾ* — mockery and playing; some said its origin is retaliation, as the poet describes men who "were mocked by two thousand mailed men, their chiefs crouched in the open plain" [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 6:112 (enemies — devils from mankind and jinn, inspiring adorned speech); 2:15 (the answer to their mockery: God mocks them) [Ibn Kathīr]; 57:13 (the wall, and the light on the Day of Judgment); 3:178; 6:110; 23:55–56; 69:11 (*ṭughyān* — the water that rose beyond its limits) [Ibn Kathīr].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** puts this verse in the arc of the passage: the earlier verses showed a depravity that mistakes defect for merit; this one shows the other side — the merit of others — the unalloyed faith of the believers — turning into a defect in the hypocrites' eyes, contemptible and laughable. That is what a diseased heart finally does with goodness: it cannot refute it, so it ridicules it. And the ridicule is the last resort of a man who has already lost the argument with himself.



## 2:15

*"God mocks them, and leaves them to wander blindly on in their rebellion."*

**Meaning.** Their mockery is answered from above: God mocks them [Jalālayn]; He gives them respite, and leaves them stumbling in the unbelief that has swallowed them [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** records four positions on what God's mockery is. (1) It is what He will do to them on the Day of Judgment — the scene of 57:13–14, when men and women among the hypocrites ask the believers for light and are told, "Go back behind you and seek a light," and a wall falls between them whose inner face holds mercy and whose outer face holds torment; and, in this world, 3:178, "We give them respite only that they may increase in sin". (2) It is His rebuke and reproach of them and His destruction of them — as the poet says of Ḥujr that "the brown lances stayed with him and played", where the lances did not play; their killing of him was called play, because that was its effect. (3) That it is an "answer" in form only — the victor's "I am the one who tricked you" — which Ṭabarī rejects in his sharpest tones: to say God does not in truth mock, plot or deceive is to deny what He affirmed of Himself, no different from denying the drowning of peoples He said He drowned; and it ends in absurdity, for one who will let "God mocks them" stand while refusing "God plays with them" has invented a distinction with no proof. (4) **[Ṭabarī]**'s own preferred view: mockery in the speech of the Arabs is a man's showing the one he mocks the words and deeds that please outwardly, while in reality he means him harm — and God has given the hypocrites the rulings of Muslims in this world, enrolling them among those who bear the name of Islam, though He knows their hearts, so that on the Last Day, when they are gathered with the believers in outward reckoning, they will hope to enter where the believers enter — before He separates them, and consigns them to the lowest depth of the Fire. That, says Ṭabarī, is mockery in its true sense, and it is justice, not play.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The Prophet ﷺ said, "God does not tire until you tire" [Qurṭubī]; and, in the report that explains the *istidrāj*: "When you see God giving a servant what he loves while he persists in his disobedience, then know that it is a leading-on" [Qurṭubī], followed by 6:44–45: "When they forgot what they were reminded of, We opened to them the gates of every thing, until, when they rejoiced in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly." The Prophet ﷺ also reported of the Fire that it will congeal like fat, so that some will walk on it thinking it a deliverance, and it will give way with them — an action that, in human reckoning, looks like mockery [Qurṭubī]. And Ibn ʿAbbās described the scene:[Qurṭubī] a gate is opened for the hypocrites from the Garden to the Fire, and they are told to come — they swim in fire while the believers look on from their couches, and when the hypocrites reach the gate it is shut upon them, so the believers laugh; and that is "God mocks them", and that is "So today those who believed laugh at the disbelievers, upon couches, looking on" (83:34–36).

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** calls the majority position the naming of the punishment by the name of the sin: 42:40, "the requital of an evil is an evil like it" — though the requital is not evil but justice; 2:194, "whoever transgresses against you, transgress against him" — though legal retaliation is not transgression, being a right; "they plotted, and God plotted" (3:54); "they devise a device, and I devise a device" (86:15–16); "the hypocrites seek to deceive God, and He deceives them" (4:142); "they ridicule them, God has ridiculed them" (9:79). The wording is paired with the deed, but the meaning is not. Amr b. Kulthūm's line is the Arabic witness: "Let none be ignorant against us, or we shall be ignorant above the ignorant" — where retaliation is called ignorance, though no man of sense boasts of ignorance; the pairing of words is easier on the tongue and the Qurʾān and the Sunnah keep to it [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** *Yamudduhum* is from prolonging and respite — "only that they may increase in sin" — and the root is increase [Qurṭubī]. Yūnus b. Ḥabīb drew the line: one says *madadta* of evil and *amdadta* of good — "We supplied you with wealth and sons" (17:6), "and We supplied them with fruit and flesh such as they desire" (52:22); al-Akhfash: *madadtu lahu* means I left him to it, *amdadttuhu* means I gave him; al-Farrāʾ: *madda* is increase from its own kind — the sea that "the seven seas would not exhaust" (31:27) — while *amadda* is increase brought in from outside, as an army is reinforced, "He will reinforce you with five thousand angels" (3:125); and the wound *amadda* because its matter comes from beyond it [Qurṭubī]. *Ṭughyān* is the crossing of the limit: "when the water rose beyond measure" (69:11); Pharaoh "transgressed" (20:24) when he said, "I am your lord most high" (79:24) [Qurṭubī]. *ʿAmah* is bewilderment: the blind man is *aʿmā*, but the *ʿamah* is in the heart — "it is not the eyes that grow blind, but the hearts within the breasts" (22:46); the man is *ʿāmih* and *ʿamih*, wavering; and camels that vanish and cannot be traced are lost "the *ʿummahā* way"; and Ru'ba's verse describes a trackless waste "where the guidance of the ignorant, the *ʿummah*, is blind" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The report from Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid is unanimous in sense: "they waver", "they go on", "they turn about" [Ṭabarī].

**Cross-references.** 57:13–14; 3:178; 42:40; 2:194; 3:54; 4:142; 9:79 [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. Wandering blind in rebellion: 6:110; 7:186; 10:11; 23:75 [Study Quran]. *Ṭughyān* beyond the limit: 69:11; 20:24; 79:17, 24 [Study Quran] [Qurṭubī]. The leading-on of the heedless: 6:44–45; 7:182 [Qurṭubī]. And the laughter reversed on the Last Day: 83:34–36 [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** names the trap: seeing no punishment come, the hypocrites grew sure they had outwitted both the believers and God; God in His forbearance gave them rope, and the length of the rope was itself the mockery — the provision was not safety but delay, and the cup filled until the day it was seized. **[Saʿdī]**'s reading follows the same line: it is part of God's mockery of them that He adorned for them their misery until they felt secure, and that on the Last Day they will be given a light like the believers' — who walk on with theirs — and then their light is put out and they are left in the dark. They mocked for a season; they will stand still, looking for a way.


## 2:16

*"Those are they who bought error for guidance — so their trade profited nothing, and they were not rightly guided."*

**Meaning.** The whole commerce of the hypocrites is priced in one line: they got error and paid with guidance, and the ledger shows a total loss [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** reports the interpretations: "they took error and left guidance" (Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and the Companions), "they preferred error to guidance" (Qatāda), "they believed and then disbelieved" (Mujāhid) — and then he asks the sharp question: how can men who were never believers be said to have *bought* error with guidance, when buying means giving one thing to receive another? He considers the reading "preferred" (with 41:17, "Thamūd were guided, but they preferred blindness over guidance") and the linguistic case for it, but prefers the first report, because *bāʾ* and trade both come down to substitution: every disbeliever substitutes for the faith he was commanded the disbelief he has earned — and the Qurʾān names the act directly: "Whoever exchanges disbelief for faith has strayed from the even path" (2:108). He also rejects the claim that the verse proves the hypocrites had once believed: everything said of them from the beginning shows they never entered the faith — though **[Ibn Kathīr]** notes the wording covers also those "who believed and then disbelieved" (63:3), and the categories of hypocrisy are several.

**Language.** *Ishtaraw* — "they bought" — carries here the sense of exchange and preference at once: the root *shirāʾ* is used by the Arabs for buying, for choosing, and for bartering away; Abū Dhuʾayb: "If you think I was once the most ignorant of you, then I have bought forbearance after you with ignorance" [Qurṭubī]. *Ḍalāl* in its root is bewilderment, extended to forgetting — "then I did it, and I was among the erring" (26:20), meaning among the forgetful — and to perishing — "when we are lost in the earth" (32:10) [Qurṭubī]. The *wāw* of *ishtaraw* takes a *ḍammah* to distinguish it from the root *wāw* [Sībawayh]; Ibn Kaysān: the *ḍammah* is the lightest of movements because it is of the *wāw*'s own kind; al-Zajjāj compares *naḥnu*. Ibn Abī Isḥāq and Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar read it with a *kasrah* — the original resolution of two vowelless letters — and Qaʿnab Abū al-Simāl with a *fatḥah* for lightness even though the letter before is vowelled; al-Kisāʾī allowed the *wāw* to be treated as a *hamza* with a *ḍammah*, like *adʾur* [Qurṭubī]. And "their trade profited nothing": **[Ṭabarī]** shows the Arab manner of speech — the profit is ascribed to the trade itself, as one says "your sale profited" and "your night slept", so that the meaning is "they profited nothing in their trade"; with the witnesses — al-Ḥuṭayʾa's "the worst of deaths is a dead man amid his people, like the death of a girl whose family's living sit by"; Ru'ba's "Ḥārith, you have relieved me of my care, so my night slept and my gloom lifted"; and Jarīr's lampoon of the one-eyed man of Nabhān, "his day is blind, but his night sees".

**Rulings & Belief.** The verse is a judgment on the transaction, not merely on the men: guidance is capital, and the man who trades it for error has placed his wealth in a losing venture [Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī]. **[Study Quran]** notes the tension in the image: "purchased" can be read "preferred" — but the mention of commerce makes the buying and selling literal in metaphor, as elsewhere: 2:86; 2:175; 3:177.

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** draws the picture with the traders' own logic: they craved error as a buyer craves merchandise, and paid for it out of the most precious thing they owned — guidance. The man who pays a *dīnār* for a *dirham* is a loser; what then of one who pays a jewel for a *dirham* — and what of the man who pays guidance for error, and chooses misery over happiness? If that is losing, the Qurʾān has the name for its end: "those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection — that, indeed, is the manifest loss." **[Maʿārif]** adds the quiet irony: by calling this a trade, the Qurʾān suggests that these worldly-wise men understood nothing even of trade. And Qatāda's witness turns the ledger into the history of a people: "By God, I have seen them go out from guidance to error, from community to schism, from security to fear, and from the Sunnah to innovation."


## 2:17

*"Their likeness is as the likeness of one who kindled a fire: when it lit up what was around him, God took away their light and left them in darknesses, unseeing."*

**Meaning.** The hypocrites' case is drawn in one image: a man in the dark lights a fire, sees by it what he needed to see, and then the light is gone and he is left standing in the dark [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr]. What was their light is what his was — borrowed, not owned [Saʿdī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** collects the interpretations of the "light". From Ibn ʿAbbās (via ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭalḥah): the hypocrites took their honour from Islam — the Muslims married with them, inherited from them and shared the spoils with them — and when they died God stripped that honour from them as the fire-lighter's light was stripped. From al-Suddī: a man in the darkness of polytheism entered Islam, learned to tell the lawful from the unlawful and good from evil, and then denied, and could no longer tell them apart; the light was faith in what Muḥammad ﷺ brought, and the darkness his hypocrisy. From Qatāda, twice: the *kalimah* "there is no god but God" lit a fire for the hypocrite in this world — he married by it, fought alongside the believers by it, inherited by it, protected his blood and property by it — and when death came it was taken from him, because it had no root in his heart. From Mujāhid: the illumination was their turning toward the believers and the guidance, and the loss of the light was their turning to the disbelievers and error. From Ibn ʿAbbās in another report: they see the truth and speak by it, then put it out with their disbelief and hypocrisy, and are left in the darkness of disbelief — seeing no guidance, standing on no truth [Ṭabarī].

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** pauses over the grammar as a door into the meaning: the parable is told of a crowd but its actor is one — "as the likeness of *one who* kindled a fire." The reason it is good and permissible: the thing for which the likeness is coined is their *illumination* — a single meaning though its holders are many — so a single example serves for it. But the same licence does not extend to bodies: a crowd of men may not be likened to a single palm tree for height. Actions may be likened across numbers when their meaning is one; bodies may not, for no two bodies are one [Ṭabarī]. The lesson of the borrowed light is the same in every report: what the hypocrite has is not his; it is a social privilege lent by the community's faith, and it will be reclaimed [Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī] [Study Quran]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** adds the spiritual reading: God removed what benefited them — the light — and left them with what harmed them, their doubts and hypocrisy; they cannot find the way, for they are, in [Ibn Kathīr]'s words, deaf, dumb and blind, and so they do not return.

**Language.** *Istawqada* means *awqada* — the *sīn* and *tāʾ* are extra, as in *istajāba* for *ajāba*: "and a caller called, 'Who will answer to generosity?' and no answerer answered him" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The *kāf* of "as the likeness of" is a noun carrying the predicate — as in al-Aʿshā's "like the thrust that carries off the oil and the wicks" — or the predicate is omitted: "their likeness is settled as the likeness of" [Qurṭubī]. *Mathal*, *mithl* and *mathīl* are one: the resemblance [Qurṭubī]. *Ḍāʾat* and *aḍāʾat* are two tongues of one verb, transitive and intransitive; Ibn al-Samayqaʿ read it without the *alif*, the general reading with it [Qurṭubī]. The report of the grammarians on *lammā*: either its answer is omitted — "it went out" — with the pronoun of "their light" for the hypocrites, and then the image points to the Hereafter, like the wall of 57:13; or the answer is "God took away", with the pronoun for the fire-lighter, and the image completes itself in this world: the man left in darkness matches the hypocrite left in his confusion [Qurṭubī]. *Ẓulumāt* has readings: al-Aʿmash with the *lām* stilled; Ashhab al-ʿUqaylī with a *fatḥah*; al-Kisāʾī called it the plural of a plural; and since "unseeing" is a circumstantial clause, one does not pause upon the word [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 57:13 — the light of the Day, the wall, the mercy inside and the torment outside [Qurṭubī] [Ṭabarī]; the light verse 24:35, and the shahādah as the "light" of the hypocrite in this world [Study Quran]; 22:46 — "it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within the breasts" [Ibn Kathīr]; 39:33; and 9:69, "you plunged as they plunged" [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** reads the image detail by detail: the man was in great darkness and great need, and the fire was not his own but kindled from another; when it shone he saw his dangers and felt safe, and thought he held it; then God took the light — and the glow went with it while the burning stayed. The darknesses close on him from every side: the night, the cloud, the rain, and the darkness that follows light. So with the hypocrites: they lit the fire of faith from the believers — a fire that was never their own quality — and by it they protected their blood and property and enjoyed a measure of safety; then death broke in, and the benefit of the light was stripped away, and there remained the darkness of the grave, of disbelief, of hypocrisy, of sin in its kinds, and beyond it the darkness of the Fire. **[Maʿārif]** notes the reason there are two parables and not one: they divide the hypocrites into two kinds. The first — those in whom disbelief had taken root and who allied themselves to faith only for worldly gain — is the man who had light and lost it. The second kind, the hesitant, whose worldly interests kept them from ever settling — is the company of the next parable, who walk a step in the lightning's flash and stand still when it darkens.


## 2:18

*"Deaf, dumb and blind — so they do not return."*

**Meaning.** The image of the man in the dark is now given its faculties: they cannot hear the truth, cannot speak it, cannot see it — and therefore they do not come back [Jalālayn] [Ṭabarī]. The description is of spiritual insensibility, not of the body: the Qurʾān's deaf and blind are those who have ears and eyes but no use for them [Study Quran] [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads the line as the completion of the sentence before it — "their likeness is as the likeness of one who kindled a fire" — and works out its grammar in careful detail: "deaf, dumb and blind" may be raised as a fresh appraisal for blame, or as a repetition of "those"; it may be put in the accusative of blame, or as a circumstantial clause from "left them" or from "they do not see"; but the reading that stands is the nominative, because that is the *rasm* of the Muslims' codices and no one may depart from it. He then closes a door: a report from Ibn ʿAbbās that would make the verse say "they have a way of return while they remain as they are" contradicts the plain text, for God makes the not-returning a standing description of them, not a matter of one moment [Ṭabarī]. The reports on the sense are unanimous: deaf to the good [Ibn ʿAbbās], hearing neither guidance nor seeing nor understanding it [Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭalḥah], dumb, meaning mute [al-Suddī], and Qatāda's plain: deaf to the truth that they may not hear it, dumb that they may not speak it, blind that they may not see it [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. "They return not": to Islam (al-Suddī), nor do they repent and take heed (Qatāda), nor come back to any good (Ibn ʿAbbās in one report) — and **[Qurṭubī]** binds it to God's prior knowledge of them.

**Readings.** The line stands in the nominative in the codices; ʿAbdullāh b. Masʿūd and the codex of Ḥafṣah have it in the accusative — *ṣumman bukman ʿumyan* — which can be read as an accusative of blame, as in "cursed, wherever they are found" (33:61) and "and his wife, the carrier of firewood" (111:4), with the poet's "they gave me wine to drink, then closed in on me — enemies of God — with lies and falsehood," where "enemies of God" is put in the accusative for blame [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** *Ṣamam* in the Arabs' speech is closing: a solid spear is *ṣammāʾ* when it is not hollow, and one "seals" (*ṣamamt*) a bottle when one stops it — so the *aṣamm* is the man whose ear-holes are shut [Qurṭubī]. *Abkam* is one who neither speaks nor understands; once he understands, he is *akhras* — and some hold the two the same; the poet wished for "a tongue in two halves, one mute and one at the track of the stars" [Qurṭubī]. *ʿAmā* is the going of sight; the man is *aʿmā*, the people *ʿumy*; *taʿāmā* is to feign blindness, and "the matter became blind to him" is for it to be confused — "the tidings will be blind to them that day" (28:66) [Qurṭubī]. And the point is the limitation, not the abolition, of the senses: no one means they had no sight or hearing at all, but that they had none of these toward one thing — as one says, "So-and-so is deaf to abuse"; the poet's fine line says it best: "deaf to what grieved him, hearing (to good)"; another: "I was deaf to an ugly speech, though I could hear it if I wished" [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** The Prophet's own use of the image: in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl, a sign of the Hour is that you see the barefoot, naked, deaf and dumb as kings of the earth [Qurṭubī]. The same threefold description of the spiritually dead: 2:171; 17:97; and the Qurʾān's deafness and blindness elsewhere: 5:71; 6:25, 39; 10:42–43 [Study Quran]. Not returning to guidance: 2:16 [Study Quran].

**Reflection.** There is a hard mercy in the Qurʾān's diagnosis: it does not say these men did not hear or did not see, but that they heard and saw nothing to their good — the organs are intact, the use of them has gone. And the closing clause, "so they do not return," is the sentence that follows such damage: a man who cannot hear the call, cannot answer it, and cannot see the road cannot come back, not because the door is locked, but because every faculty that would have walked him to it has been spent. The warning stands over every reader who has ever listened to revelation the way one listens to weather.


## 2:19

*"Or like a cloudburst from the sky, in which are darknesses, thunder and lightning; they put their fingers in their ears against the thunderclaps, in fear of death — and God encompasses the disbelievers."*

**Meaning.** A second likeness of the hypocrites, for a second kind of them: men caught in a storm at night — rain from above, darkness on every side, thunder and lightning — who stop their ears with their fingers at every crash, terrified of dying [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr]. The Qurʾān is their sky: its unbelief-condemning passages are the darkness, its warnings the thunder, its clear proofs the lightning — and they block their hearing so as not to believe, which for them would be death [Jalālayn] [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** deals at length with the "or": it is not doubt, which is impossible for God, but an alternative that is really an addition — both parables belong to the hypocrites. *Aw* can carry the force of *wāw*, as the poets use it: "Laylā claimed I am a wicked man — against my own soul is its guard, or its wickedness is upon it," where no uncertainty is meant; and Jarīr's "he attained the caliphate — or it was decreed for him — as Moses came to his Lord at the appointed time" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The *ṣayyib* is the falling rain: the *fayʿil* pattern from *ṣāba yaṣūbu*, descending; see ʿAlqama's line, "Do not rank me with a man unseasoned; may the rain-clouds' water-carrying camels give you drink when they pour down"; the word was originally *ṣaywib*, and the *yāʾ* and *wāw* merged into a doubled *yāʾ*, as in *sayyid* from *sāda* and *mayyit* from *māta* [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī].

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** The Jews questioned the Prophet ﷺ about thunder, and he said: "It is an angel of the angels charged with the clouds; he has goads of fire with which he drives the clouds wherever God wills." They asked: "And what is this sound we hear?" He said: "His rebuke of the clouds when he drives them, until they reach where God has commanded" (al-Tirmidhī); and most of the scholars hold to this [Qurṭubī]. Mujāhid and Abū Ṣāliḥ report the same — an angel driving the clouds with his voice [Ṭabarī]. Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to have said the thunder is a wind choked between the clouds, sounding that noise [Qurṭubī]. This is why the Qurʾān itself says, "The thunder glorifies His praise, and the angels out of fear of Him" (13:13) [Study Quran]. The philosophers' claim that thunder is the clash of cloud-bodies and lightning its spark is rejected: no transmitted proof supports it, and the language knows the thunder as a sound and the lightning as a light — Laqīṭ's line: "thunder and lightning shocked me for al-Fāris, on the day of adversity, the brave" [Qurṭubī]. From Ibn ʿUmar: when the Prophet ﷺ heard thunder and lightning he would say, "O God, do not kill us by Your anger, nor destroy us by Your punishment, and pardon us before that" [Qurṭubī]. ʿUmar and Kaʿb are reported to have recited, on hearing thunder: "Glory be to Him whom the thunder glorifies with His praise, and the angels from fear of Him" — and Ibn ʿAbbās, Kaʿb said, one who says it when the thunder sounds is preserved from what is in that cloud, the hail and the thunderbolts [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** *As-Samāʾ* is everything that rises over you and shadows you — the roof of a house is its "sky" — and it is used of rain itself, of vegetation and clay soaked by it, of a horse's back, and of what is above as the earth is what is below; it is masculine and feminine, and its plurals are *samawāt*, *samāwā*, and *sumiyy* — "the winds wrap him, and the rains" [Qurṭubī]. The *ẓulumāt* are plural because they are of several kinds — the night, the cloud, the rain [Saʿdī] [Qurṭubī]. *Raʿd* is from motion — the coward is *raʿdīd*, and *irtaʿada* is to tremble; the Prophet ﷺ described the two women brought to him as "their inmost parts trembling" [Qurṭubī]. *Barq* is from flash and light — and from it the *Burāq*, the mount of the Night Journey [Qurṭubī]. The fingers in the ears are **their** fingers: they stop their own hearing against a sound they cannot bear — and Qurṭubī notes the fine point of "fearing death": for them, hearing the Qurʾān and believing would be death to their old life; so the fear of death that makes them put their fingers in their ears is the fear of the very thing that would have saved them [Qurṭubī]. "God encompasses the disbelievers" — in knowledge and in power: their caution buys them nothing, for the One who drives the storm is behind them and around them, as in "Nay! Those who disbelieve are in denial, and God encompasses them from behind" (85:17–20) [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr].

**Cross-references.** 13:13 (the thunder and the angels glorify Him); 85:17–20; 63:4 ("they think every cry is against them"); 9:56–57 (they swear they are with you, being a people who fear) [Ibn Kathīr].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** gives the reason there are two parables: they match two kinds among the hypocrites. The first, in whom denial had gone deep, is the man who had light and lost it. The second — those who knew Islam to be true and wished at times to be real Muslims, but whose worldly interests would not let them — is the company of the storm, who take a step or two in the flash and stand frozen in the dark. The second is the more pitiable figure, because he does not hate the road: he wants it in flashes, and cannot bear it in the dark. And the warning closes over both: the power that lights the sky can take away the eyes and ears that are watching it.

## 2:20

*"The lightning almost snatches away their sight. Whenever it flashes for them they walk in it, and when darkness falls on them they stand still. And had God willed, He could have taken away their hearing and their sight — surely God has power over all things."*

**Meaning.** The storm runs on: the flash is so violent it nearly takes their sight; they use it while it lasts, and freeze when it goes [Jalālayn]. So it is with these men: whenever the affair of Islam shines for them they walk a little in its light; and whenever a darkness falls they halt, and there they are [Ibn Kathīr].

**Context.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** gives the hypocrite's history in the verse: whenever they gained a share in the victories of Islam they were content with it, and whenever Islam suffered a setback they were ready to turn back — the very portrait of 22:11, "among mankind is he who serves God on the edge: if good befalls him he is content." From Ibn ʿAbbās in another report: they recognise the truth and speak of it, so that their speech is upright — and when they return to their denial they fall back into confusion. And **[Ibn Kathīr]** carries the image into the Hereafter: the believers will be given light on the Day of Resurrection according to their faith, some as bright as a mountain, some as a date palm; on the bridge over the Fire the light of some will flicker and go out, and they will stand still; Ibn Masʿūd's report says the least of the people of light is the one whose forefingers glow and then die out — and the hypocrites' light will be extinguished, while the believers see it and are afraid, saying, "Our Lord, perfect our light for us" (66:8; 57:12–13). **[Ṭabarī]** and **[Ibn Kathīr]** both include the debate on whether the storm's light and darkness are described of this world or the next; the reports hold both readings, and the parable serves both.

**Belief.** The closing clause is a warning, not a description: "had God willed, He could have taken away their hearing and their sight." Since the two verses before described their spiritual deafness, blindness and muteness, this line threatens them with the loss of the faculties themselves — a punishment in this world to warn before the greater one, and a proof that the powers they misuse are on loan [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]. **[Saʿdī]** adds a doctrinal point from the same clause: that God's "power over all things" includes the acts of His servants — a standing answer to those who say human deeds lie outside His power. And the whole passage closes on the divine encompassment of 2:19: the men who run from the thunder are inside the storm the whole time.

**Language.** *Yakādu* is nearness without falling — "it almost" — and *yakhtaṭifu* is snatching, the quick seizure of something before it is secured; *ṣawāʿiq* is the plural of *ṣāʿiqa*, the thunderclap that strikes, from the same root as being "struck down" [Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr]. "Whenever" (*kullamā*) carries repetition with the condition: each flash brings a step, each darkness a halt — the description of a walk that never gets anywhere. And "they stand still" (*qāmū*) is the standing of the confounded: not standing to go, but standing because there is nowhere to walk [Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr].

**Cross-references.** 22:11 (worship on the edge); 57:12–13 and 66:8 (the light of the believers and the hypocrites' darkness on the Day); 2:18 (the deaf, dumb and blind) [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī].

**Reflection.** The first parable ended in darkness that stayed; the second ends in a flash that keeps teasing — which is the truer portrait of a man who cannot make up his mind. He is not against the truth; he only wants to travel in fair weather. The sūrah now leaves him there, standing in the middle of the road with everything to lose and nothing to show — and turns from the hypocrites to address everyone else: "O mankind, worship your Lord" (2:21).


## 2:21

*"O mankind! Worship your Lord, who created you and those before you, so that you may become God-fearing."*

**Meaning.** After twenty verses that sorted men into believers, disbelievers and hypocrites, the sūrah turns and addresses them all with one call and one argument: serve the One who gave you existence, and the purpose of that service is *taqwā* — the guardedness that stands between the servant and his Lord's anger [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī].

**Context.** **[Maʿārif]** reads the opening of al-Baqarah as a single movement: 2:2 answers the prayer of al-Fātiḥah, the next verses divide mankind by their response to guidance — three verses for the God-fearing, three for the rejecters, thirteen for the hypocrites — and then 2:21–22 gather all three groups into the message for which the Book was sent. Before it the division was by religion, not by tribe or tongue; here the summons goes out to the whole of humankind. Ibn ʿAbbās (via ʿIkrima and Saʿīd b. Jubayr) says the address is to both groups — the disbelievers and the hypocrites [Ṭabarī]; al-Suddī: "created you and created those before you" [Ṭabarī]. **[Qurṭubī]** records the two readings of who is meant by "mankind": the disbelievers who never worshipped Him — the later "and if you are in doubt" (2:23) points to them — or all people without exception, so that the believer is commanded to continue and the disbeliever to begin. This second, he says, is the fair one. And Qatāda's rule that every "O mankind" is Meccan and every "O you who believe" Medinan he rejects for this sūrah, which is Medinan and contains "O mankind" — though ʿUrwah's test holds: laws and obligations came down in Madīnah, the stories of the nations and their punishment in Makkah [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** The *ʿibādah* here means His oneness and commitment to the laws of His religion; its root is lowliness and subjection — a well-trodden road is *muʿabbada*, and Ṭarafa describes the she-camel following hoof after hoof "upon a trodden path" [Qurṭubī]. Ibn ʿAbbās read "worship your Lord" as "make your Lord one": single out obedience and worship for Him alone [Ṭabarī]. **[Maʿārif]** notes the choice of the name *Rabb*, the One who nurtures by stages from a drop of water to a hearing, rational being who provides and sustains: the name encloses the argument in the summons itself — only the Nurturer can be the One worshipped.

**Theological note.** **[Ṭabarī]** draws a firm consequence from the verse: God commanded these very people to worship Him and turn back from their unbelief *after* declaring that they would not believe and would not return. The command to the capable servant therefore stands on its own; the help and the ability are not conditions that must be granted before the obligation is imposed. And **[Saʿdī]** finds the whole of *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah* in the clause — the One who created you is the One who provides for you, and if every man concedes He has no partner in creation, he should concede He has no partner in worship.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** "Worship your Lord" is answered in the ḥadīth of the two Ṣaḥīḥs through Ibn Masʿūd: asked which sin is the gravest, the Prophet ﷺ said, "That you set up a rival to God, while He alone created you" — and Muʿādh's report: "Do you know God's right upon His servants? That they worship Him and associate nothing with Him" [Ibn Kathīr]. The Prophet ﷺ also forbade the phrase "what God wills and so-and-so wills," teaching instead "what God wills, and then what so-and-so wills" — a bar against rivals in speech as in worship (Ibn Kathīr). The longest report is al-Ḥārith al-Ashʿarī's: God commanded Yaḥyā b. Zakariyyā with five words to act on and to command the Children of Israel with — to worship God and shun all rivals; to pray, for God turns His face to His servant's face as long as he does not turn away; to fast, for the fasting mouth's odour is sweeter to God than musk; to give charity, likened to a captive ransoming himself from the enemy bit by bit until he is free; and to remember God often, for the remembrance is a fortress against Satan — and Yaḥyā gathered them in Bayt al-Maqdis and delivered it from the balcony [Ibn Kathīr].

**Belief.** On "so that you may become God-fearing": **[Qurṭubī]** gives three explanations of *laʿalla* with God. (1) It carries its normal sense of hope and expectation, and hope belongs to the human side: the command stands, and the hope is yours — as in "Go to Pharaoh, and speak to him a gentle word, that he may remember or fear" (20:44), where Sībawayh said the meaning is "go with your own hope and expectation." (2) The Arabs use *laʿalla* with the sense of *li-kay*, plain purpose: "worship, *so that* you may guard yourselves"; the poets' "you said to us: hold back the wars, that we may hold back — and you pledged us every pledge," where no doubt could be meant. (3) It marks exposure to a result: act, exposing yourselves to fear of God. In the end the sense gathers around the root of *wiqāya*: make of what God commanded you a shield between you and the Fire — as ʿAlī said of the battle's heat, "we would shield ourselves with the Prophet ﷺ"; as ʿAntara said of the horse, "until the horses shielded me behind Ibn Ḥudhaym." **[Saʿdī]** adds that both meanings are true and inseparable: the one who worships God completely comes to be of the God-fearing, and the one who is of the God-fearing is saved.

**Cross-references.** 51:56, "I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me" [Saʿdī]; 21:25; 20:44; 40:64.

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** presses the point that *tawḥīd* is not a theory but a way of standing in the world: the man who holds that every change, friend and enemy alike, proceeds from one will has nothing left to fear from creatures and nothing to expect from them — shower him with gold or clap him in chains, and he is unmoved, for he knows from whose hand it comes. The Companions dominated the world in a few years with this conviction; the formula is on the tongues of millions today whose lives do not show its colour. And **[Maʿārif]** notes one more fine point in the verse: it names "those before you" and not those who come after — for the last Prophet's community is the last community, and no prophet will come after him. The first argument God makes to man is the one no man can refute: you were not, and now you are.


## 2:22

*"Who made the earth a bed for you, and the sky a canopy, and sent down water from the sky, and brought forth fruits by it as provision for you — so do not set up rivals to God, while you know."*

**Meaning.** The argument of 2:21 is now carried into the world men walk on: the One who spread the earth as a bed, raised the sky as a roof, sent the rain and brought out the fruits is the One who may not be given partners [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī]. **[Maʿārif]** notes the pairing: 2:21 spoke of the provisions of the self, 2:22 of the provisions of the environment — inner and outer, *anfus* and *āfāq* — so that between them every kind of blessing is counted.

**Context.** Ibn ʿAbbās reports that "do not set up rivals to God" was sent down concerning both groups, the disbelievers and the hypocrites: do not join to God rivals that neither profit nor harm, *when you know* that no lord sustains you but He, and that the oneness the Messenger calls you to is the truth in which there is no doubt [Ṭabarī]. Qatāda: you know God created you and created the heavens and the earth, and then you set up rivals to Him [Ṭabarī]. Mujāhid holds the address is to the People of the Book: you know He is one God in the Torah and the Gospel [Ṭabarī]. On the earth's being a "bed", al-Suddī: it is walked on; it is the cradle and the resting place [Ṭabarī]; and **[Maʿārif]** explains the middle state: neither soft and fluid like water, on which nothing settles, nor hard like stone and steel that cannot be worked — but between the two, for man's use. The word *firāsh* does not require the earth to be flat: a globe appears flat to the one standing on it, and the Qurʾān addresses men in the way they see [Maʿārif]. And **[Qurṭubī]** draws a juristic point from the same word: the Shāfiʿīs hold that a man who swears not to spend a night on a *firāsh* and then sleeps on the bare ground, or swears not to kindle a *sirāj* and then sits in the sun, has not broken his oath, because the word is not customarily applied to those; the Mālikīs decide all oaths by intention, cause, or the setting in which the oath was sworn, and only then by custom.

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** asks how a people described a few verses ago as sealed, deaf and blind can be addressed here as "knowing". Two answers: the knowledge meant is the particular knowledge that God created the creation, sent down the water and brought out the provision — they know He is the Benefactor and not the rivals; or the sense is that His oneness is within their power to know, if they would only consider. And from this, he says, comes the standing command to use the argument of the intellect and to abandon blind conformity. **[Ibn Kathīr]** records that the exegetes — al-Rāzī among them — took these verses as a proof of the Creator's existence, and it is the noblest way of arguing: whoever reflects on the higher and lower creation, their shapes, colours, natures and benefits, comes to know the power, wisdom, knowledge and perfection of their Maker. A bedouin was asked for the proof of God's existence and said: "Glory be! The camel's droppings testify to the camel, and the track testifies to the one who walked; a sky that holds the giant stars, a land with fairways, and a sea with waves — do not all these testify to the Most Kind, the Most Knowing?" [Ibn Kathīr]. **[Saʿdī]** draws the *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah* argument to its end: all men concede that God has no partner in creating and providing — so let them concede He has no partner in worship; that is the clearest rational proof of the Creator's oneness and the falsity of polytheism.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** In the two Ṣaḥīḥs, Ibn Masʿūd asked which sin is the greatest, and the Prophet ﷺ said: "That you set up a rival to God while He created you" [Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]. From ʿIkrima: the rivals include the small utterances — "were it not for our dog, the thief would have entered the house" [Ṭabarī]; and the report from Ibn ʿAbbās describes the hidden *shirk* as "finer than the crawling of an ant on a black stone in the dark of night": a man swears by God and by your life, or says "what God wills and you will" — and the Prophet ﷺ, when a man said to him "what God wills and you will", asked, "Have you made me a rival to God?" [Ṭabarī, editor's note] [Ibn Kathīr]. From the same exegetical tradition (the report of ʿAlī by Nawf al-Bikālī): watching the stars, ʿAlī said, "Blessed are those who renounce the world and long for the Hereafter: they have taken the earth as a carpet, its dust as a bed, its water as perfume, and the Qurʾān and supplication as their cloak and their covering" [Qurṭubī]. And Qurṭubī cites the Prophet's saying, in Muslim: "By God, that one of you should take his rope and carry firewood on his back is better for him than asking a man who gives or refuses" — for God has made man rich of every creature, and the one who makes himself a beggar from greed has taken hold of the edge of setting up a rival to God.

**Language.** *Andād* is the plural of *nidd*: the equal, the like, the counterpart — as Ḥassān said, "Do you lampoon him when you are no *nidd* of his? The worse of you two must ransom the better"; and Labīd's "so that al-Sandarī should not be my *nidd*" [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. Abū ʿUbaydah said *andād* means "opposites"; others simply "equivalents" [Qurṭubī]. *Samāʾ* is everything that rises above and shades you [Saʿdī] [Qurṭubī] — "and We made the sky a guarded roof" (21:32). *Māʾ* comes from *mawah*: the *wāw* turned to *alif* for the vowel, the whole resolved into *māʾ*; and when it is pluralised or diminutised the original returns — *muyayh*, *amwāh*, *miyāh* [Qurṭubī]. *Thamarāt* has its several plurals — *thamar*, *thumur*, *thumr*, *thimār* — and in the verse the point is the kinds of provision for men and their cattle, as 80:25–32 spells out [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings.** The verse shows God contenting man without any creature, which is why the Prophet ﷺ pointed to the rope and the firewood, and why the ascetic tradition of the following generation took the same earth as bed and the same sky as cover [Qurṭubī]. On the word "provision" being given before possession: the fruits are called *rizq* before a man owns them because they are prepared to be owned and used — that is what makes the name true [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** 21:31–32 (the mountains and the guarded roof); 50:6–7; 79:27–31 [Study Quran]; 21:32; 56:63–64, "Did you send it down from the clouds, or did We send it?", "Is it you who cause it to grow, or are We the growers?", 78:14, 80:25–32 [Maʿārif] [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** warns against reading the world as a machine: whoever stops at steam, electricity or the green and red flag that start and stop a train is no wiser than the rustic who concluded that the flags were the power — the flags are signs, and behind the steam and the fire and the water is the will of the One who created them. Its second point is subtle: the Qurʾān closes 2:21 with "so that you may become God-fearing" rather than "so that you may be saved", for no deed of man earns salvation as a wages; even the ability to do the deed is a gift. **[Saʿdī]** closes on the wonder of it: a man who knows that God has no equal in creation, provision or control, and then sets up rivals beside Him in worship — "that is the strangest of what is strange and the most foolish of follies." And the verse's last two words, "while you know", are addressed to everyone who reads them: the argument is not hidden, and the cover has been pulled from it.


## 2:23

*"And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down to Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it, and call your witnesses besides God, if you are truthful."*

**Meaning.** Having proved the oneness of God from the created world (2:21–22), the sūrah now proves the prophethood of Muḥammad ﷺ from the revealed word: if you suspect that this Book is not from God, do the one thing that would settle it — bring a single sūrah equal to it, and summon every helper and every god you have [Qurṭubī] [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]. The command is a challenge (*taḥaddī*), and its point is that a man who is charged with forging the Book should not be able to do a thing that other eloquent men cannot do [Ṭabarī].

**Context.** **[Qurṭubī]** reports the occasion: the disbelievers heard the Qurʾān and said, "This does not resemble the speech of God, and we are in doubt about it" — and the verse came down. And he notes the order of the sūrah: first the proof of God's oneness and power, then the proof of His Prophet's truth and that what he brought was not invented. **[Maʿārif]** sees the same parallel in the argument's shape: the world's inability to create the sky and the earth proves those acts belong to God alone; the inability of all created beings to produce anything like His word proves the word belongs to God alone. And it points out that the challenge is put without the definite article — "a sūrah", any sūrah — so that it includes the shortest passages of the Book.

**Language.** *Rayb* is the doubt or suspicion that has no basis, easily removed by a little reflection: the sūrah says of the Book itself, "in which there is no doubt" (2:2), and 74:31, "that the People of the Book and the believers may not doubt" [Maʿārif]. *ʿAbd* is from *taʿabbud*, lowliness: the owned servant is called *ʿabd* for his submission to his master — Ṭarafa's "I was left alone like the subdued camel" [Qurṭubī]. And the naming of Muḥammad ﷺ as God's *ʿabd* in this, the greatest of contexts, is itself a mark of honour: he is "Our servant" at the revelation (2:23), at the night journey — "Glory to Him who carried His servant by night" (17:1) — and at the sending down of the Criterion (25:1); the servitude of which no one among the first or the last reaches his rank is his greatest description [Saʿdī] [Qurṭubī]. *Sūrah* is a passage set apart with a beginning and an end, a minimum of three verses [Jalālayn] [Qurṭubī]. "Your witnesses" (*shuhadāʾ*) is the plural of *shahīd*, one present to see a thing; it can mean those who testify, or those who are present and help [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings & interpretations.** On the phrase "a sūrah like it", **[Ṭabarī]** weighs the readings: the majority — Mujāhid and Qatāda among them — take "like it" to mean like this Qurʾān in truth and eloquence, and Ibn Kathīr records that al-Rāzī reported this as the view of ʿUmar, Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḥasan, preferring it because the challenge was issued to the literate and the unlettered alike and so is the harder test. A second view: "like it" refers to the Torah and the Gospel — bring a sūrah from a book like it. A third: like Muḥammad himself, an unlettered man. **[Ṭabarī]** rejects the third, for 10:38 says "bring a sūrah like it" and no sūrah is like Muḥammad; and persons cannot be the measure of passages. He goes further and clarifies what the challenge can fairly mean: the Qurʾān was sent in the Arabs' own tongue, so the obligation to match it lies within their own language and their own eloquence — they cannot say the demand was made in a language they did not know; but in the meaning by which the Qurʾān stands apart from all created speech there is no like to it at all. On "call your witnesses": Ibn ʿAbbās says your helpers and allies in what you follow [Ṭabarī]; al-Farrāʾ, your gods [Qurṭubī]; Mujāhid, people who will testify [Ṭabarī]; and **[Ṭabarī]** rejects the last as the intended sense — for the witnesses of the disbelievers would rush to certify a forgery, while the believers would never testify to falsehood, so the only sensible reading is that the idolaters are told to bring whatever aid and support they have [Ṭabarī]. **[Maʿārif]** notes both senses and the disbelievers' use of it: they held that their idols would stand as witnesses for them on the Day of Judgment. *Min dūni llāh* is "to the exclusion of God", *dūn* being what falls short of a limit; and in the idiom of encouragement, "dūnakahu" — "there he is, take him" — as the Tamīmites said when asking for their companion's body, and al-Ḥajjāj answered, "take it" [Qurṭubī]. "If you are truthful" means: in your claim that you can match it — as they said in another place, "if we wished we could say the like of this" (8:31) [Qurṭubī].

**Belief.** The challenge is the sūrah's evidence for prophecy, and the exegetes' point is that it was addressed to the most eloquent of nations, who had both the strongest hatred of the Prophet ﷺ and every motive to humiliate him by taking it up — and never did [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif]. The Book is the Prophet's miracle in a way the earlier miracles were not: each prophet's sign appeared with him and ended with him, while the Qurʾān survives, and its challenge stands as long as it does [Maʿārif]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** stresses that "and you will never do it" (2:24) is itself part of the miracle: it is a prediction of abiding human failure, and history has not falsified it. And **[Saʿdī]** asks the plain question of a plain man: how should a creature made of dust speak the words of the Lord of lords? Whoever has the least taste for language, if he weighs this Book against the words of the eloquent, will see the difference.

**Cross-references.** The other challenges: 10:38; 11:13 (ten forged sūrahs); 17:88 ("if mankind and the jinn gathered together to produce the like of this Qurʾān they could not, though they helped one another") [Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran] [Ṭabarī]; 28:49; 52:34; 8:31 ("if we wished we could say the like of this"); and the unletteredness of the Prophet: 7:157 [Study Quran]. The Qurʾān as "perfect in its verses, then explained in detail" (11:1) and "the word of your Lord fulfilled in truth and justice" (6:115) [Ibn Kathīr].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** observes what kind of doubt is being answered. The wavering man, uncertain and not knowing which way is right, is the one for whom guidance is hoped: show him the truth and, if he is honest in seeking it, he follows. The stubborn man who knows the truth and leaves it is not reached by argument, for he did not leave it from ignorance. And here the doubt was not left standing: they were given a test any of them could have taken, at the price of one short passage of Arabic — and a whole nation of speech-makers, with everything to gain by succeeding, went silent. That silence is the argument the verse goes on to press: "and if you do it not — and you will never do it" (2:24) [Ibn Kathīr].


## 2:24

*"And if you do it not — and you will never do it — then guard against the Fire whose fuel is men and stones, prepared for the disbelievers."*

**Meaning.** The challenge of 2:23 is now closed: you did not produce a sūrah, and you never will; so the alternative to arguing is a Fire that will not run out of fuel, since its wood is human beings and stones [Jalālayn] [Study Quran]. The verse gives the disbelievers one route of escape — belief — and names what lies at the other end of the road [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** "And you will never do it" (*lan tafʿalū*) is not a forecast among forecasts but a settled negation: *lan* negates forever, and with it the prediction became a fixed report of the unseen before the event — the disbelievers were provoked to try, and their failure was to be all the starker [Qurṭubī]. Ibn Kaysān: the words fix them in their own falsehood, for they claimed to know and could not produce a single sūrah [Qurṭubī]. The grammarians add that *lan* may be followed by the jazm in some dialects — Abū ʿUbaydah cites al-Nābigha's line, "and I will never expose myself to reproach with giving"; and Ibn ʿUmar's dream report, "it was said to me: you will not be terrified" [Qurṭubī]. On the pause: some exegetes read the verse as "call your witnesses... if you are truthful — and you will never do it — so if you do it not, guard against the Fire", which moves the stop to the end of 2:23; others stop at "truthful" [Qurṭubī]. *Waqūd* with a *fatḥah* is the fuel itself, the firewood; with a *ḍammah* it is the burning [Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]. The general reading has *waqūduhā*; al-Ḥasan, Mujāhid and Ṭalḥah b. Muṣarrif read *wuqūduhā*, and ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr *waqīduhā* [Qurṭubī].

**Context.** The stones: **[Ṭabarī]** and **[Qurṭubī]** report from Ibn Masʿūd that they are stones of sulfur — God created them when He created the heavens and the earth, and He has them stored in the lowest heaven, making them ready for the disbelievers. From al-Suddī and Ibn ʿAbbās: stones of black sulfur in the Fire, by which they are tortured along with it; and Ibn Jurayj heard ʿAmr b. Dīnār say they are harder and greater than the stones of this world [Ṭabarī]. **[Qurṭubī]** gives the reason sulfur was singled out: it surpasses all other stones in five kinds of torment — it ignites fastest, it stinks worst, it smokes most, it clings hardest to the bodies, and its heat when heated is the most intense. Other exegetes take the stones to be the idols the polytheists worshipped, since 21:98 says, "You and all that you worship besides God are the firewood of Hell" — so that the human beings and the stone idols are together fuel, and the point is the size of the Fire, which burns stones as it burns men [Qurṭubī] [Study Quran]. On the first reading, men are punished *by* the Fire and the stones together.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** Muslim records the Prophet's ﷺ words about Abū Ṭālib, when al-ʿAbbās asked whether his protection had benefited him: "Yes — I found him in the depths of the Fire, and I brought him out to a shallow place; and were it not for me, he would be in the lowest depth" [Qurṭubī]. Ibn Masʿūd said: we were with the Messenger of God ﷺ when he heard a thud, and he asked, "Do you know what this is?" — "God and His Messenger know best" — he said: "This is a stone that was thrown into the Fire seventy autumns ago; it has been falling in the Fire until now, when it reached its bottom" (Muslim) [Qurṭubī]. And the two Ṣaḥīḥs: the Fire and Paradise argued — the Fire said, "The mighty and the proud enter me," and Paradise said, "The weak and the poor enter me" — and God said to the first, "You are My punishment, by you I punish whom I will," and to the second, "You are My mercy, by you I show mercy to whom I will; and each of you shall be filled" [Qurṭubī]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** adds the greatest of miracles as the Prophet ﷺ described it: "Every prophet was given signs such that people believed on their like; what I was given is a revelation that God revealed to me, and I hope to be the one with the most followers on the Day of Resurrection" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim).

**Belief.** "Prepared for the disbelievers" is used by the people of truth as proof that the Fire is already created, against those who said it is not yet made — **[Qurṭubī]** cites the Andalusian judge Mundhir b. Saʿīd al-Ballūṭī as one who fell into that error. That the verse names the disbelievers does not mean no one else may enter it: the warnings against sinning believers, and the established reports of intercession, show otherwise; the Fire is *prepared* for the disbelievers in particular [Qurṭubī]. **[Saʿdī]** reads the same construction in the other direction: since the Fire is prepared for the disbelievers, the people of *tawḥīd* who commit some grave sins will not abide in it forever — otherwise it would not be prepared for the disbelievers alone, against the Khawārij and the Muʿtazila; and together with 2:24's companion verses it proves that Paradise and the Fire are both created and waiting. From the same verse he draws that punishment is earned through its causes — unbelief and sins of every kind.

**Cross-references.** 72:15, "the wrongdoers are firewood for Hell"; 21:98, "you and what you worship beside God are the firewood of Hell"; 66:6, "guard yourselves and your families against a Fire whose fuel is men and stones" [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī] [Study Quran].

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** records what the challenge did to its audience. The chiefs of Makkah feared the crowds of the pilgrimage and sought a label to hang on the Prophet ﷺ. Walīd b. al-Mughīra, the oldest and wisest among them, foretold that "lunatic" would not hold when men heard him speak, that "poet" would fail against a people with poetry in their blood, and that "soothsayer" would only turn the listeners against the accusers. Then he gave the testimony of a man who had listened: "By God, not one of you knows more of Arabic poetry than I. And by God, I find in this speech a sweetness and grace I have never found in the speech of any poet or any eloquent man." So they settled on "sorcerer". Others — Nadr b. al-Ḥārith, the brother of Abū Dharr, Asʿad b. Zurārah, Qays b. Naṣībah — said the like; and Abū Jahl, with Abu Sufyan and al-Akhans, crept by night to the Prophet's house to listen, unable to tear themselves away until dawn, and returned to their denial for a reason Abū Jahl confessed: the clan of ʿAbd Manāf had come up with a prophet, and they had nothing to match [Maʿārif]. This is what the verse does: it turns the silence of the eloquent into evidence, and then asks them to weigh the difference between losing an argument and losing themselves.
