# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 251–260

This batch contains the most-quoted verse in the whole Qur'an.

2:251 completes the Ṭālūt narrative that 2:250 left hanging on a prayer. 2:252–253 draw out the lesson for the Prophet ﷺ and for the communities that follow messengers. 2:254 commands spending before a Day with no bargaining in it. **2:255 is Āyat al-Kursī — the Verse of the Footstool — which the Prophet ﷺ named as the greatest verse in the Book of God.** 2:256 follows it with *lā ikrāha fī al-dīn* — no compulsion in religion. 2:257 states the two guardianships. And 2:258–260 give three scenes: a tyrant confounded by a question about the sun, a man dead for a hundred years beside his donkey, and Abraham asking to be shown how the dead are raised.

**Three cautions before starting.** First: 2:259 is one of the most *Isrā'īliyyāt*-exposed verses in the surah — the man is not named in the text, and the identity given by the commentators is a report. Second: 2:253 and 2:255 touch the questions of ranking the messengers, of intercession, and of the divine attributes, and on all three the tradition holds positions that must be presented rather than flattened. Third: 2:256 is quoted more often than almost any verse in the Qur'an, by friends and critics alike, and in both directions it is usually quoted without its second clause. **The verse has a reason attached to it, and the reason is the verse.**

---

## Verse 251

**"So they defeated them by Allah’s Will, and David killed Goliath. And Allah blessed David with kingship and wisdom and taught him what He willed. Had Allah not repelled a group of people by ˹the might of˺ another, corruption would have dominated the earth, but Allah is Gracious to all."**

In simple terms: so they routed them by God's permission, and Dāwūd killed Jālūt. And God gave him kingship and wisdom, and taught him of what He wills. And were it not for God's repelling some people by means of others, the earth would have been corrupted — but God is full of bounty to all the worlds.

*Fa-hazamūhum bi-idhni Allāhi wa qatala Dāwūdu Jālūta wa ātāhu Allāhu al-mulka wa al-ḥikmata wa 'allamahu mimmā yashā', wa law lā daf'u Allāhi al-nāsa ba'dahum bi-ba'din la-fasadati al-arḍu wa lākinna Allāha dhū faḍlin 'alā al-'ālamīn.*

*Fa-hazamūhum **bi-idhni Allāh*** — "so they routed them **by God's permission**."

**This is the answer deferred from 2:250, and note the order.** The believing remnant stood in front of Goliath and asked for patience, firm feet, and victory. **And the answer opens with the permission, not the victory.** The routing is attributed to *idhn* before it is attributed to anyone's sword. That is the same order as 2:249's maxim: *kam min fi'atin qalīlatin ghalabat fi'atan kathīratan **bi-idhni Allāh***.

*Wa qatala **Dāwūdu** Jālūta* — "and **Dāwūd** killed Jālūt."

**And note who kills him.** Not Ṭālūt, the king they asked for. Not a commander. **Dāwūd** — who, in the transmitted accounts, was a young man not enrolled as a fighter, sent to the army with provisions, and who stepped forward when no one else would. **The pattern of 2:249 is repeated inside 2:251: the thing the world discounts is the thing God uses.**

**This is Dāwūd's first appearance in the Qur'an, and he arrives already carrying everything at once** — kingship, wisdom, and knowledge. We will meet him again at 21:78–80 (the mountains and the birds praising with him, and the making of armour), 34:10–11 and 38:17–26. **Note that the Qur'an introduces him here, in the middle of someone else's battle, and says nothing about his age, his appearance, or his rank. It says what God gave him.**

*Wa ātāhu Allāhu **al-mulka wa al-ḥikmata** wa 'allamahu **mimmā yashā'*** — "and God gave him **kingship and wisdom**, and taught him **of what He wills**."

**Three gifts, and the structure is worth noting:**
- ***Mulk*** — kingship, authority. **Power.**
- ***Ḥikmah*** — wisdom. In the tradition's usage this is knowledge joined to right action: placing things where they belong. Cross-ref 38:20: "We strengthened his kingdom and gave him wisdom and decisive speech."
- ***'Allamahu mimmā yashā'*** — "and taught him of what He wills." **Knowledge, and the qualifier that governs it.**

**And that qualifier is the same one that will close 2:255:** *wa lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shay'in min 'ilmihi **illā bi-mā shā'a*** — they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. **So the thread running from 2:251 to 2:255 is that knowledge is given, and the Giver is God.** A king is not wise because he is a king. He is wise because he was taught.

*Wa law lā **daf'u Allāhi al-nāsa ba'dahum bi-ba'din** la-fasadati al-arḍ* — "and were it not for **God's repelling some people by means of others**, the earth would have been corrupted."

**This is one of the most consequential political statements in the Qur'an, and it is usually read too quickly.**

The claim is: **what restrains corruption in the world is God's repelling of one group by another.** Not the removal of power. Not the disarming of everyone. **A check.** Some people hold back other people, and that holding-back is what keeps the world habitable.

**The jurists have read this as the scriptural basis for the necessity of defensive force and of political authority.** If the wicked were unrestrained, the earth would be ruined. **Therefore restraining them is a mercy, not a calamity** — which is the opposite of the intuition that all coercion is regrettable and force is always the failure.

**And now the checks, which the verse supplies and which must be stated every time this verse is used:**

**First: *daf'* is repulsion, not aggression.** The word means pushing back, warding off. **It sanctions the repelling of harm, not the imposition of good by force.** So this verse cannot be read as a warrant for conquest, and it operates inside the framework of 2:190–193 and 8:61 that I have held throughout.

**Second: read it with its twin at 22:40**, which is even more explicit: *"And were it not for God's repelling some people by means of others, there would have been demolished **monasteries, churches, synagogues and mosques** in which the name of God is much mentioned."* **The thing being protected by this repelling is named: the freedom to worship.** That is the purpose, and it bears directly on 2:256–257 immediately below.

**Third: the verse is used by the tradition against two errors, not one.** Against **quietism** — the claim that a believer should never hold power or use force — it says the earth would rot. And against **adventurism** — the claim that force is a good in itself — it says the purpose is *daf'*, repulsion, and the beneficiaries are named in 22:40 as houses of worship of more than one religion. **A verse whose stated beneficiaries include monasteries and synagogues is not a verse for empire.**

*Wa lākinna Allāha dhū faḍlin 'alā **al-'ālamīn*** — "but God is full of **bounty to all the worlds**."

Note *al-'ālamīn* — **the worlds, all peoples**, not the believers specifically. **The restraint of corruption is described as a universal bounty.** God's mercy to the world includes the check He places on the violent.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 252

**"These are Allah’s revelations which We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. And you are truly one of the messengers."**

In simple terms: those are the signs of God which We recite to you in truth — and indeed, you are among the messengers.

*Tilka āyātu Allāhi natlūhā 'alayka bi-al-ḥaqqi wa innaka la-mina al-mursalīn.*

**Note the sudden second person.** For four verses the surah has been narrating the Children of Israel in the third person. **Here it turns and addresses the Prophet ﷺ directly: *natlūhā 'alayka* — "We recite them to you."** That turn is the verse.

*Tilka āyātu Allāhi* — "those are the **signs of God**."

**Not "stories." Not "examples." *Āyāt* — signs.** And that is the Qur'an's own claim about its narratives, made in the middle of one: **these are evidence, not literature.** Which means the events are being asserted as having happened.

*Natlūhā 'alayka **bi-al-ḥaqq*** — "We recite them to you **in truth**."

***Bi-al-ḥaqq*** — in truth, with the truth, truly. **The recitation is true, and its content is true.** This is the Qur'an's standing claim about itself, and the commentators have always read it as a rejection of the charge that these narratives are parables or borrowings.

*Wa innaka la-mina **al-mursalīn*** — "and indeed you are **among the messengers**."

**And note the form: "among the messengers."** Not "the best of them," not "the last of them," not "the seal of the prophets" — though that is stated elsewhere (33:40). **Here he is placed in the company.** Which is both an affirmation and a consolation, and it connects directly to the next verse's ranking and to the hadith that will be cited there.

**And the consolation is the point of the placement.** The Prophet ﷺ has just been told — has just been told *directly*, in the second person — the story of a community that demanded a king, objected to the king God gave them, failed the test set for them, and despaired in front of the enemy. **And God gave them victory anyway, through the least likely person in the camp.**

**The message to him is: the doubters are not new, the failures are not new, and the outcome was never in their hands or yours.** He is addressed in the middle of a story about the Children of Israel because he is living through the same thing.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 253

**"We have chosen some of those messengers above others. Allah spoke directly to some, and raised some high in rank. To Jesus, son of Mary, We gave clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. If Allah had willed, succeeding generations would not have fought ˹among themselves˺ after receiving the clear proofs. But they differed—some believed while others disbelieved. Yet if Allah had willed, they would not have fought one another. But Allah does what He wills."**

In simple terms: those messengers — some of them We preferred over others. Among them were those to whom God spoke, and He raised some of them in rank. And We gave Jesus son of Mary the clear proofs and supported him with the Holy Spirit. And if God had willed, those after them would not have fought after the clear proofs had come to them; but they differed, some believing and some disbelieving. And if God had willed, they would not have fought one another — but God does what He wills.

*Tilka al-rusulu faḍḍalnā ba'dahum 'alā ba'd, minhum man kallama Allāhu wa rafa'a ba'dahum darajātin, wa ātaynā 'Īsā ibn Maryama al-bayyināti wa ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi al-qudus, wa law shā'a Allāhu mā iqtatala alladhīna min ba'dihim min ba'di mā jā'athumu al-bayyinātu wa lākinina ikhtalafū fa-minhum man āmana wa minhum man kafara, wa law shā'a Allāhu mā iqtatalū wa lākinna Allāha yaf'alu mā yurīd.*

*Tilka al-rusulu **faḍḍalnā ba'dahum 'alā ba'd*** — "those messengers — **some of them We preferred over others**."

**This verse is quoted more carelessly than almost any verse in the surah, and it needs two corrections up front.**

**Correction one: ranking is a fact here, and ranking as a sport is not.** The verse states a divine preference. It does **not** invite human beings to rank prophets against each other. **And the Sunnah closes that door explicitly.** In *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* from Ibn 'Abbās, the Prophet ﷺ said: **"No one should say that I am better than Yūnus ibn Mattā."** And in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s, when a man said "by Him who preferred so-and-so over so-and-so," the Prophet ﷺ discouraged the competitive comparison of prophets. **The tradition's rule: the preference belongs to God, who knows; it is not a subject for human ranking, and a Muslim's duty to every messenger is belief in him without discrimination (2:136, 2:285).**

**Correction two: the ranking is not the point of the verse. The fighting is.** Every clause here drives toward *wa law shā'a Allāhu mā iqtatalū* — which is stated **twice in a single verse.** **The verse is about what the communities did after their messengers, not about a league table of prophets.** A person who quotes this verse in order to rank is quoting its opening while ignoring its entire purpose.

*Minhum man **kallama Allāhu*** — "among them were those to whom **God spoke**."

The commentators identify this as **Mūsā**, the one called *Kalīm Allāh* — the one spoken to (4:164, 7:143). **And note the form: God spoke. Directly, without intermediary, in a manner befitting Him.** The tradition affirms *kalām* as a real attribute of God — eternal, in a manner not like the speech of creatures — and this is where the great controversy over the createdness of the Qur'an (*khalq al-Qur'ān*) was fought. **The Sunni position: God speaks truly, with a speech that is an attribute of Him, not a created thing, in a manner we do not liken to creaturely speech.** I state this here because this verse is one of its proof-texts, and the disagreement is real and historical and should not be papered over.

*Wa rafa'a ba'dahum **darajātin*** — "and He raised some of them in **ranks/degrees**."

The commentators commonly apply this to **our Prophet ﷺ**, and to those given scripture, authority and a universal mission. **The word is *darajāt* — degrees, stations.** And it is indefinite: **the verse does not name him or number them.**

*Wa ātaynā 'Īsā ibn Maryama **al-bayyināti** wa ayyadnāhu bi-**rūḥi al-qudus*** — "and We gave Jesus son of Mary the **clear proofs** and supported him with the **Holy Spirit**."

**Note what the Qur'an asserts about 'Īsā here, because this is the Qur'an's own Christology and it should be stated without either hostility or concession:**

- He is **'Īsā ibn Maryam** — Jesus, **son of Mary**. The matronymic is used throughout, and it is used deliberately: it asserts his human birth from a mother, without a father, and it is the Qur'an's standing correction of the sonship claim (19:34–36, 4:171, 5:75).
- He was given ***al-bayyināt*** — the clear proofs, the evident signs. The miracles: healing the blind and the leper, raising the dead, forming a bird from clay (3:49, 5:110).
- He was **supported with *rūḥ al-qudus***. The majority of the commentators identify *rūḥ al-qudus* as **Jibrīl** — and the phrase appears at 2:87 and 2:253, 5:110, 16:102, and in the title *Rūḥ al-Qudus* applied to Gabriel throughout the hadith. Some read it as the spirit of holiness or as the angelic support generally. Both readings held; **the identification with Jibrīl is the majority position.**
- **And he is listed among the messengers.** Not above them in kind. **The Qur'an honours him more fully than almost any other figure outside Muhammad ﷺ and Mūsā, and it does so while denying him divinity.** Both halves belong together, and a Muslim who quotes one without the other has not quoted the Qur'an.

*Wa **law shā'a Allāhu mā iqtatala** alladhīna min ba'dihim min ba'di mā jā'athumu al-bayyinātu* — "and **if God had willed, those after them would not have fought** after the clear proofs had come to them."

**And here is the famous knot, stated twice in one verse: *if God had willed, they would not have fought.***

**This is the Qur'an at its most demanding on the question of God's will and human action, and I will present the positions rather than flatten them.**

- **The plain reading** is that nothing occurs outside God's will. If the communities fought, God willed it in the sense that it did not escape His decree.
- **The Mu'tazilī reading** takes the verse as a denial that God wills evil, and reads the fighting as willed by the people and merely permitted by God.
- **The Ash'arī reading** affirms that God wills everything, good and evil, and that human acts are created by God and *acquired* (*kasb*) by the human being, who is thus genuinely accountable.
- **The Māturīdī reading** distinguishes God's *will* (*mashī'ah*) from His *good pleasure* (*riḍā*): He wills what occurs but does not approve of all of it.

**All four positions are held within the broad Sunni and Islamic theological tradition, with the Ash'arī and Māturīdī readings being the two dominant Sunni formulations. The disagreement is real and I am not going to resolve it here.** What can be said without controversy: **the verse does not present itself as a licence to stop acting, and it does not absolve the fighters.** The very next clause says *wa lākinina ikhtalafū* — **but they differed** — and places the difference with them.

**And that is the function of the verse, which is the thing most readers miss.** *Wa law shā'a Allāhu mā iqtatalū* is stated **for the consolation of the Prophet ﷺ**, continuing directly from 2:252's "you are among the messengers." **The message: the communities after their messengers fought and split. That is not a defect in your mission.** The schisms of the People of the Book are not evidence that the messengers failed.

*Wa lākinna Allāha **yaf'alu mā yurīd*** — "but God **does what He wills**."

**The closing is not a shrug.** *Yaf'alu mā yurīd* — He does what He wills. It is the Qur'an's repeated formulation for the fact that God is not answerable and not constrained, and it appears at 3:40, 5:1, 11:107, 14:27, 22:14, 22:18, 24:45, 29:20, 33:36, 85:16. **And note: it is stated immediately after the human difference has been named.** Both are true, and the verse holds them side by side without reconciling them for us.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 254

**"O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession. Those who disbelieve are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, spend from what We have provided you, before there comes a Day in which there is no trading, no friendship, and no intercession. And the disbelievers — they are the wrongdoers.

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū anfiqū mimmā razaqnākum min qabli an ya'tiya yawmun lā bay'un fīhi wa lā khullatun wa lā shafā'atun, wa al-kāfirūna hum al-ẓālimūn.*

*Anfiqū mimmā **razaqnākum*** — "spend from what **We provided you**."

**Note the possessive: *razaqnākum* — what *We* provided.** Which removes the ground of stinginess before the command is even finished. **It was never yours to begin with, so the spending asked for is a redistribution of a trust, not a loss of a possession.** Same move as 2:3, 2:245, 2:267.

*Min qabli an ya'tiya yawmun **lā bay'un fīhi wa lā khullatun wa lā shafā'atun*** — "before there comes a Day in which there is **no trading, no friendship, and no intercession**."

**Three things will be absent, and the list is carefully ordered: from the most impersonal to the most personal.**

**1. *Lā bay'* — no trading, no bargain.** You cannot buy your way out. No ransom, no settlement, no last-minute transaction. Cross-ref 2:48 and 2:123: *a Day when no soul will avail another soul at all, and no compensation will be accepted from it.* **Everything you could have spent is worthless on that Day because the currency has changed.**

**2. *Lā khullah* — no friendship.** ***Khullah*** is from *kh-l-l*, the same root as *khalīl* — the intimate friend, the one who penetrates to your inner life, the title given to Ibrāhīm (*khalīl Allāh*, 4:125). **So this is not "no acquaintances." It is: the closest human bond you have will not function on that Day.** The Qur'an says this elsewhere with terrible clarity — at 70:10–14, a man would ransom himself with his children, his wife, his brother and his whole kindred; and at 44:41–42, *a Day when no friend avails his friend at all.*

**3. *Lā shafā'ah* — no intercession.** **And this is the clause that must be handled precisely, because it looks like a flat denial and it is not one.**

***The verse says "no intercession." The very next verse says "except by His permission."*** 2:255: *man dhā alladhī yashfa'u 'indahu **illā bi-idhnih*** — who is there that can intercede with Him except by His permission. **And that is the Qur'an explaining itself by adjacency, one verse apart.** The intercession denied in 2:254 is intercession **as a claim** — as though someone had a right to it. The intercession affirmed in 2:255 is intercession **as a gift** — granted, permitted, given.

**And this is where the Sunnah's teaching on intercession sits, with no conflict at all.** The intercession of the Prophet ﷺ is established by *mutawātir* hadith — the Great Intercession (*al-shafā'ah al-'uẓmā*) on the Day of Standing is in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s, and his intercession for people of his community who entered the Fire is likewise established. **All of it is *bi-idhnih*: God gives him permission, and he intercedes.** The tradition's own formulation: intercession is real, it belongs in its origin to God, and none of it happens without His leave and His pleasure for the one on whose behalf it is made (20:109, 21:28, 34:23, 53:26).

**Which means the correct reading of 2:254 is not "there is no intercession" but "there is no intercession you can count on as a right."** And that is precisely why the verse appears in a command to spend: **do the thing now, because on that Day nothing you are relying on will work.**

*Wa **al-kāfirūna hum al-ẓālimūn*** — "and the **disbelievers** — they are the wrongdoers."

**Note the construction: subject, then an emphatic, exclusive predicate.** *Hum al-ẓālimūn* — **they, and not others, are the wrongdoers.** This is one of the proof-texts in the classical debate over whether *ẓulm* is identical with *kufr* or whether there is a lesser *ẓulm* that a believer can commit. **Both positions are held:** those who read it as exclusive cite this verse and 31:13 ("associating partners with God is a great wrong"); those who distinguish cite 6:82 ("those who believe and do not mix their faith with wrong") and the many verses that attribute *ẓulm* to believers (2:231 — *do not retain them harmfully, for whoever does that has wronged his own soul*). **The disagreement is real and is a genuine difference over scope, not over substance.**

**And note the placement: this verse stands immediately before Āyat al-Kursī.** A command to give, then the greatest statement in the Qur'an of who God is. **The reason to give is stated first as a warning, and then as a description.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 255

**"Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission? He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them, but no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺. His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him. For He is the Most High, the Greatest."**

In simple terms: God — there is no god but Him, the Living, the Self-Sustaining. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who is there that can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is before them and what is behind them, and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His Footstool encompasses the heavens and the earth, and their preservation does not weary Him. And He is the Most High, the Most Great.

*Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm, lā ta'khudhuhu sinatun wa lā nawm, lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ, man dhā alladhī yashfa'u 'indahu illā bi-idhnih, ya'lamu mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum, wa lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shay'in min 'ilmihi illā bi-mā shā'a, wasi'a kursiyyuhu al-samāwāti wa al-arḍa, wa lā ya'ūduhu ḥifẓuhumā, wa huwa al-'aliyyu al-'aẓīm.*

**This is Āyat al-Kursī. And before anything else, the report that gives it its standing.**

In *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (810), from **Ubayy ibn Ka'b**: the Messenger of God ﷺ said to him, **"O Abū al-Mundhir, do you know which verse in the Book of God that you have is the greatest?"** Ubayy said, "God and His Messenger know best." The Prophet ﷺ repeated the question, and Ubayy said: **"God — there is no god but Him, the Living, the Self-Sustaining."** And the Prophet ﷺ **struck him on the chest and said: "By God, may knowledge be pleasant for you, O Abū al-Mundhir."**

**And the second report, which every Muslim knows, is in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (2311, 3275, 5010), from Abū Hurayrah.**

Abū Hurayrah was guarding the zakāt of Ramaḍān when a figure came and began scooping up food. He seized him, and the figure said: **"Release me, and I will teach you words by which God will benefit you."** He released him. The same happened a second night, and a third. On the third night the figure said: **"When you go to your bed, recite Āyat al-Kursī — *Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm* — and a guardian from God will remain over you, and no shayṭān will come near you until morning."** When Abū Hurayrah told the Prophet ﷺ, he said: **"He told you the truth, though he is an utter liar. Do you know who you have been talking to for three nights, O Abū Hurayrah?"** He said no. He said: **"That was a shayṭān."**

**Two things about this hadith deserve to be said. First: a shayṭān taught a Companion the greatest verse in the Qur'an, and the Prophet ﷺ confirmed the teaching.** Second: *"he told you the truth, though he is a liar"* is one of the most-quoted sentences in the whole corpus, and it contains a principle — **a statement is not false because of who says it.** Truth is assessed on its own, even from the mouth of the Father of Lies.

**A third report, on the virtue of reciting it after the prayers: from Abū Umāmah al-Bāhilī, "Whoever recites Āyat al-Kursī after every prescribed prayer, nothing stands between him and entering Paradise except that he should die."** Recorded by al-Nasā'ī in *al-Sunan al-Kubrā* and by Ibn Ḥibbān in his *Ṣaḥīḥ*; **graded ṣaḥīḥ by some and ḥasan by others — the grading is disputed and I flag it as such.**

And a fourth, in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (5009) from Abū Mas'ūd: **"Whoever recites the last two verses of Sūrat al-Baqarah at night, they suffice him."**

**Now the verse itself. The tradition counts ten complete independent sentences in it, and each one is a creed in itself.**

**1. *Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa* — "God — there is no god except Him."**

Two sentences. The Name, and then the negation with its exception.

**And note what the supplied translation has done and why it matters.** *Lā ilāha* is not "there is no god at all" — that would be false, since many things have been worshipped. **It is: there is nothing that *deserves* worship except Him.** The supplied rendering "there is no god **worthy of worship** except Him" makes the ellipsis explicit. **That is the correct unpacking, and it is the difference between the Islamic *shahādah* and a bare denial of other deities.**

**This is *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah*** — the oneness of what is worshipped — and it is the sentence the entire verse exists to serve.

**2. *Al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm* — "the Ever-Living, the Self-Sustaining."**

**These are the two Names the verse is built on, and they are why it is the greatest.**

***Al-Ḥayy*** — the Living. **Not "alive" in the way a creature is alive.** A creature's life is given, borrowed, dependent, and mortal. **His life is His own: necessary, eternal, without beginning, without end, without dependence.** Every living thing other than Him is alive because He is alive.

***Al-Qayyūm*** — the Self-Subsistent who sustains everything else. The form *fay'ūl* is an intensification: **He stands by Himself, and everything else stands by Him.** The commentators gloss it *al-qā'im bi-tadbīri khalqih* — the One who upholds and manages the whole of His creation, moment by moment.

**And the pairing is the whole of *tawḥīd* in two words:** *Ḥayy* is the attribute of His essence; *Qayyūm* is the attribute of His relation to everything else. **He is alive in Himself, and everything else is alive only because of Him.**

**The tradition holds that these two Names constitute — or contain — the Greatest Name of God** (*ism Allāh al-a'ẓam*), the one which, if He is called by it, He answers. Reports place it variously in this verse, in 3:2, and in 20:111, and the reports differ. **The disagreement is genuine and I flag it.** The sound position on the Greatest Name is that it should be sought in *du'ā'* with sincerity and attention — **it is not a formula to be operated, and treating it as one is a misunderstanding of what the reports are for.**

**3. *Lā ta'khudhuhu sinatun wa lā nawm* — "neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him."**

***Sinah*** is drowsiness, the onset of sleep. ***Nawm*** is sleep. **The verse negates both, in ascending order: not even the approach of sleep, let alone sleep itself.**

**Why here?** Because everything that lives and sustains can tire, and the gods people invent are precisely gods who sleep, who rest, who lapse. **And because the claim of the previous clause depends on it.** If the Sustainer of the heavens and the earth slept for an instant, everything He sustains would cease — which is what *Qayyūm* means. **The negation of sleep is not incidental praise. It is the guarantee of the cosmos.**

**4. *Lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth."**

***Lahu*** — to Him. **Ownership, not merely governance.** And *mā* — whatever, all of it, without exception, including everything a person imagines he owns.

**Which is why the next clause lands as hard as it does.**

**5. *Man dhā alladhī yashfa'u 'indahu illā bi-idhnih* — "who is there that can intercede with Him except by His permission?"**

**Note the construction: *man dhā alladhī* — who is it that...? — the interrogative of impossibility.** The same formula as 2:245's "who is it that will lend God a good loan." **The answer is: nobody.**

**And note that this clause follows directly from "to Him belongs everything."** That is the logic. **You cannot intercede on the basis of a claim, because no one holds anything that is not already His.** Intercession is not a right. **It is a permission.**

**Which is the resolution of 2:254, one verse earlier.** 2:254: no intercession. 2:255: no intercession **except by His permission**. **The intercession that is denied is intercession as a claim; the intercession that is affirmed is intercession as a gift.** And the Prophet's ﷺ intercession, established by *mutawātir* hadith, is exactly intercession *bi-idhnih*. There is no contradiction and there never was.

***'Indahu*** — "**with Him**," in His presence. **Intercession is not a transaction conducted at a distance. It happens before Him, in front of Him, and He hears it.**

**6. *Ya'lamu mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum* — "He knows what is before them and what is behind them."**

*Mā bayna aydīhim* — what is ahead of them: the future, the Hereafter, what they are walking into. *Mā khalfahum* — what is behind them: the past, the world they have left. **Both directions, entirely.**

**And in context the "them" includes both the intercessors and those on whose behalf intercession is sought.** Which supplies the second reason intercession cannot be a claim: **He already knows the case completely.** No intercessor is bringing Him information He lacks.

**7. *Wa lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shay'in min 'ilmihi illā bi-mā shā'a* — "and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills."**

**This is the Qur'an's epistemology in one clause, and it is the most misused sentence in the verse.**

***Yuḥīṭūna*** — encompass, surround, comprehend exhaustively. **Note the precision: the verse does not say "they know nothing." It says they never *encompass*.** Created knowledge is real, genuine, and never total.

**And that clause is a permission at least as much as it is a limit.** What He wills to be known is genuinely known; **no ceiling is set on it by the text.** The verse is not a prohibition on enquiry — **it is a guarantee against totalising.** Every discipline that has ever over-reached has done so by forgetting this clause, and every honest scientist has been practising it: the map is not the territory, and the model is not the world.

**It is also the ground of the tradition's restraint about the unseen.** 31:34 names five things no one knows but Him — the Hour, the rain, what is in the wombs, what a soul will earn tomorrow, and where a soul will die. **The verse does not say knowledge is worthless. It says knowledge is given and it is bounded.**

**8. *Wasi'a kursiyyuhu al-samāwāti wa al-arḍ* — "His Footstool encompasses the heavens and the earth."**

**The word the verse is named for, and the most contested word in it. Three things must be said.**

**The transmitted statement of Ibn 'Abbās** (recorded by Wakī' in his *Tafsīr* and by al-Ḥākim in the *Mustadrak*, **stopping at Ibn 'Abbās and not raised to the Prophet ﷺ**): **"The Kursī is the place of the two feet, and no one is able to give due consideration to the Throne."** This is the position preserved and adopted by Ibn Kathīr and by the majority of the Atharī tradition.

**A minority reading, recorded by Ibn al-Jawzī among others, takes *kursī* figuratively as His knowledge or His dominion.** **Both readings are held; the first is the position of the majority of the early authorities** and is the reading that preserves the plain sense of the word.

**A reported narration** — attributed to Abū Dharr, and in other chains to Ibn 'Abbās — states: **"The seven heavens and the seven earths compared to the Kursī are but like a ring thrown into a desert plain."** **Its chain is discussed by the hadith critics, and I flag it as reported rather than established.**

**And the creedal rule: the same rule I have applied to every attribute in this surah.** The Kursī is affirmed truly, **without likening it to the thrones of kings and without denying it.** It is **not** the Throne (*'Arsh*); the Throne is greater than it; and neither is comprehended by us.

**Which is the rhetorical point of the clause.** *It is not geography. It is a measurement of the immeasurable by understating one of its parts.* **His footstool is vaster than the heavens and the earth — so what of the Throne? And what of the One who is above it?**

**9. *Wa lā ya'ūduhu ḥifẓuhumā* — "and their preservation does not weary Him."**

***Ya'ūduhu*** — from *āda*, to burden, to weary. The same root as 46:33: *"and He was not wearied by their creation."*

**And this connects back to *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* and to *lā ta'khudhuhu sinatun wa lā nawm*.** Sustaining the heavens and the earth — every particle, every instant, every heartbeat — **is not a labour to Him. It costs Him nothing.**

**And that is precisely what makes Him God and not a manager.** The head of a household grows tired. The ruler of a country grows tired. Even the caregiver who loves the work is worn out by it. **The verse says: not this One.** And the reassurance in it is personal: **He has not become weary of you, and He will not.**

**10. *Wa huwa al-'aliyyu al-'aẓīm* — "and He is the Most High, the Most Great."**

***Al-'Alī*** — the Exalted. ***Al-'Aẓīm*** — the Tremendous.

**And note the closing pair mirrors the opening pair.** The verse opened *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — His life and His sustaining. It closes *al-'Alī al-'Aẓīm* — His exaltation and His greatness. **Living and sustaining; exalted and great.** The verse is bookended by two pairs of Names.

*Al-'Alī* is affirmed **without likening His highness to spatial elevation and without denying it** — He is above His creation in His essence, His attributes, and His authority, and the *istiwā'* is as it came (2:29).

**And the whole movement of the verse, in ten steps:** exclusivity (1) → the two Names (2) → no lapse (3) → ownership (4) → no unpermitted intercession (5) → total knowledge (6) → bounded created knowledge (7) → vastness (8) → no fatigue (9) → exaltation and greatness (10). **It moves from *who*, to *what*, to *how* — and closes on praise.**

**That is why it is the greatest verse. It is a complete creed in one sentence-sequence.**

**And note its placement.** Āyat al-Kursī stands between **a command to spend before the Day** (2:254) and **a prohibition on compelling belief** (2:256). **The greatest statement of who God is sits between those two.** Which means: **the reason to give, and the reason not to force, are the same reason.** You give because everything is His. You do not force because He is *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* and does not need your recruit.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 256

**"Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: there is no compulsion in the religion — the right course has become distinct from error. So whoever rejects the *ṭāghūt* and believes in God has grasped the firmest handhold, which will never break. And God is Hearing, Knowing.

*Lā ikrāha fī al-dīni qad tabayyana al-rushdu min al-ghayyi, fa-man yakfur bi-al-ṭāghūti wa yu'min bi-Allāhi fa-qadi istamsaka bi-al-'urwati al-wuthqā lā infiṣāma lahā, wa Allāhu samī'un 'alīm.*

**This is the most quoted verse in the Qur'an outside al-Fātiḥah, and it is usually quoted without its second clause — which is the clause that makes it work.**

*Lā ikrāha fī **al-dīn*** — "there is **no compulsion** in the religion."

**Note the word: *ikrāh*, compulsion — doing a thing to someone against their will, under duress.** And *al-dīn* — **the religion**, the whole thing: belief, worship, and the conduct that follows from it. **The verse does not say "no compulsion in belief" only. It says *al-dīn*.**

**And now the reason, which is the next clause and which is routinely dropped.**

*Qad **tabayyana al-rushdu min al-ghayy*** — "**the right course has become distinct from error**."

**Read this as a causal, because it is one.** *Lā ikrāha* — no compulsion — **because** the guidance has been made clear.

**And that is the verse's argument, and it is the answer to both the critics who say Islam was spread by the sword and the apologists who quote the verse as a standalone slogan.** The reason no one is to be forced is: **the truth is already distinguishable from falsehood.** A person who is shown the distinction and still chooses has chosen; a person who is forced has not chosen anything. **Compulsion would not produce belief. It would produce compliance, which is not the thing God asked for.**

**Which is why Ibn Kathīr's gloss is the one to have:** *"Do not force anyone to become Muslim, for Islam is plain and clear and its proofs are plain and clear. Therefore there is no need to force anyone. Rather, **whoever God directs to Islam, opens his heart for it and enlightens his mind, will embrace Islam with certainty. Whoever God blinds his heart and seals his hearing and his sight — he will not benefit from being forced.**"*

**That last sentence is the point.** Forcing a person whose heart is sealed produces nothing. **The verse is not a concession to modern sensibilities. It is a statement about what belief is.**

**Now the reported occasions, which are several and which differ:**

- Reported from Ibn 'Abbās (via Sa'īd ibn Jubayr, recorded by Ibn Jarīr and by al-Wāḥidī in *Asbāb al-Nuzūl*): **a woman of the Anṣār whose children did not survive would vow that if a child of hers lived, she would raise him as a Jew.** When Banū al-Naḍīr were expelled from Madīnah, **there were children of the Anṣār among them, and the Anṣār said: "We will not leave our children."** And this verse was revealed. Sa'īd ibn Jubayr added: **"Those who wished to leave with the Jews left, and those who wished to embrace Islam embraced Islam."**
- Another report: a man of the Anṣār, Abū al-Ḥusayn, had two sons who had become Christian through traders and left Madīnah with them. He asked the Prophet ﷺ to compel them. The verse came down.
- Another: some sons of the Aws, suckled among the Jews, said "we will go out with them and follow their religion," and their families wanted to force them into Islam. The verse came down.

**Three different reports, three different occasions. Which is exactly why the uṣūlīs' rule applies — and Ibn Kathīr states it: "It was reported that the Anṣār were the reason behind revealing this verse, although **its indication is general in meaning**."** That is the principle *al-'ibrah bi-'umūm al-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣ al-sabab* — **the ruling follows the generality of the wording, not the specificity of the occasion.** The verse was revealed about a particular family in Madīnah and it is not about that family.

**Now the abrogation question, which is the one that matters most and the one people argue about.**

**A report attributed to Ibn 'Abbās (via al-Wāḥidī), and a statement attributed to Mujāhid, hold that this verse was abrogated** — the wording in al-Wāḥidī's transmission: *"This was before the Messenger of God was commanded to fight the People of the Book. But then God's saying 'there is no compulsion in religion' was abrogated, and the Prophet was commanded to fight the People of the Book in Sūrat Barā'ah."*

**The majority of the commentators and jurists hold that it is not abrogated**, and they take one of two routes:

- **That it is general and unabrogated**: the religion cannot be imposed, full stop. This is the position of the majority of later scholars and of the overwhelming weight of classical and modern *tafsīr*.
- **That it is specified rather than abrogated** (*takhṣīṣ*, not *naskh*): the verse applies to the People of the Book, who are not to be compelled into Islam so long as they pay the *jizyah* and live under the polity — reported from Qatādah and others. On this reading the ruling was narrowed to a class, not cancelled.

**And there is a third thing to say, which is decisive for me: naskh is a claim about revelation, and it requires a proof.** I have handled every claimed abrogation in this surah this way (2:106, 2:180, 2:217, 2:240) and I will not vary now. **A claim that a verse of the Qur'an has been cancelled needs a dated, sound, explicit text, and what exists here is a statement attributed to a Companion and a Successor, transmitted through a single channel, against which stands the generality of the wording and the practice of the early community.** I present the disagreement as a disagreement, and I note where the weight lies, but I will not state as established that this verse was abrogated.

*Fa-man **yakfur bi-al-ṭāghūti** wa **yu'min bi-Allāh*** — "so whoever **rejects the ṭāghūt** and **believes in God**..."

**And here is the clause that is dropped, and it is the load-bearing one.**

**Faith is described as two acts, not one: a rejection and an acceptance.** *Yakfur bi-al-ṭāghūt* — **reject, disbelieve in, renounce the ṭāghūt** — and *yu'min bi-Allāh* — believe in God. **The verse does not say "whoever believes in God." It says whoever rejects the false object of allegiance *and* believes in God.**

**Which means: believing in God while continuing to serve something else is not what this verse calls faith.** The two are one motion — turning from and turning to. **You cannot hold both, which is precisely 2:257's point in the next verse.**

***Al-ṭāghūt*** — from *ṭaghā*, to exceed all bounds, to transgress, to rebel. **The commentators' definition: everything by which a servant exceeds his proper limit** — whether it is worshipped, followed, or obeyed. It is applied to shayṭān, to idols, to soothsayers, to a ruler obeyed in disobedience to God, and — in the strongest classical statements — **to the self that commands evil.** Cross-ref 4:60 (those who seek judgement from *ṭāghūt*), 16:36, 39:17, and 60:4–6 (Ibrāhīm's dissociation).

**And that definition is the verse's contemporary edge.** The *ṭāghūt* of a place is whatever people are actually obeying. **It has taken the form of a statue, a king, a party, a market, an algorithm, and a man's own appetite.** The verse requires that it be *identified and renounced* — not merely outgrown.

*Fa-qadi istamsaka bi-**al-'urwati al-wuthqā** lā infiṣāma lahā* — "then he has grasped the **firmest handhold**, which will never break."

***Al-'urwah*** — a handle, a loop, a ring you hold onto. ***Al-wuthqā*** — the firmest, most secure. ***Lā infiṣāma lahā*** — **it has no breaking, no snapping, no separation.**

**The image: a man on a cliff, or on a wall, with nothing but a ring set in the rock.** The handhold does not break. **Which means the only way down is letting go.** And that is the verse's realism: **the grip is not the problem. The problem is ours.**

**Note also: the handhold is not "the religion" in the abstract. It is the twofold act — renouncing the false and holding to God.** That is what you grip.

*Wa Allāhu **samī'un 'alīm*** — "and God is Hearing, Knowing."

**The same closing pair as 2:244.** *Samī'* — He heard the renunciation, if it was spoken. *'Alīm* — He knows whether it was real. **The verse that forbids compulsion closes by reminding you that the one thing that cannot be compelled is also the one thing fully known.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 257

**"Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."**

In simple terms: God is the Guardian of those who believe — He brings them out of the darknesses into the light. And those who disbelieve — their guardians are the *ṭāghūt*, who bring them out of the light into the darknesses. Those are the companions of the Fire; they will abide in it forever.

*Allāhu walīyu alladhīna āmanū yukhrijuhum min al-ẓulumāti ilā al-nūr, wa alladhīna kafarū awliyā'uhumu al-ṭāghūtu yukhrijūnahum min al-nūri ilā al-ẓulumāti, ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn.*

**Note the grammatical asymmetries in this verse, because they are doing the theological work.**

**One Guardian for the believers; many guardians for the disbelievers.** *Allāhu **walīyu*** — singular. ***Awliyā'uhumu al-ṭāghūtu*** — plural. **The believer's allegiance is unified; the disbeliever's is dispersed.** The one who serves God serves one. The one who serves anything else ends up serving many, because false objects of allegiance do not agree with each other and cannot be served all at once. **That is not a poetic flourish. It is a description of what actually happens to a person without a single loyalty.**

**Many darknesses; one light.** ***Al-ẓulumāti*** — plural. ***Al-nūr*** — singular. **The same asymmetry appears throughout the Qur'an** (14:1: "a Book We sent down to you that you may bring the people out of the darknesses into the light"). **Error is multiple and scattered — there are endless ways to be wrong. Truth is one.** And that single light is also why the handhold of 2:256 is one handhold.

*Allāhu **walīyu** alladhīna āmanū* — "God is the **Guardian** of those who believe."

***Walī*** — guardian, ally, protector, patron, the one who is close to you and in charge of your affair. **It is one of the great words of the Qur'an's vocabulary of nearness,** and it carries both senses at once: **He protects, and He is close.** The tradition's mystical writing on *walāyah* grows out of this word, and the *awliyā' Allāh* of 10:62 — "the friends of God, no fear upon them and they do not grieve" — are those who have this guardianship.

**And note the sequence: first the guardianship, then the bringing out.** *Walīyu alladhīna āmanū* — then *yukhrijuhum*. **He is their Guardian first, and the rescue follows from the guardianship, not the other way round.**

*Yukhrijuhum min al-ẓulumāti ilā al-nūr* — "He brings them **out of** the darknesses **into** the light."

**And now the second half, which is the harder one.**

*Wa alladhīna kafarū awliyā'uhumu al-ṭāghūtu **yukhrijūnahum min al-nūri ilā al-ẓulumāt*** — "and those who disbelieve — their guardians are the *ṭāghūt*; **they bring them out of the light into the darknesses**."

**Both verbs are "bring out." The question is only which direction.**

**And that means the disbeliever is not depicted here as someone who was never in the light.** *Yukhrijūnahum min al-nūri* — **they bring them out of the light.** The verse's image is of a person who had light and was led out of it. **Which fits the whole Qur'anic account: every human being is born on the *fiṭrah*, the natural constitution that recognises its Lord (7:172, 30:30), and what happens afterwards is a leading-out.**

**And note who does the leading: the *ṭāghūt* they chose.** The verb is plural because the guardians are plural. **People are not dragged out of the light by a force from outside. They are led out by what they took as guardians.** That is the verse's most uncomfortable sentence and it is the reason 2:256 insisted on *yakfur bi-al-ṭāghūt* as a positive act.

*Ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā **khālidūn*** — "those are the companions of the Fire; they will abide in it forever."

**And here the standing boundary must be restated, because it is the one that matters most on a verse like this.**

**No one may declare a specific living individual an inhabitant of the Fire.** This is a rule I have held throughout this commentary and it applies here with full force. **The verse describes a category — *alladhīna kafarū* as a class — and the tradition's rule is that testimony about particular persons is withheld, except for those whom the text itself names.** A Muslim's task with this verse is to fear it for himself, not to aim it at a neighbour. **The man who is confident that someone else is going to Hell has not understood what *khālidūn* means.**

**And the verse's placement is the bridge.** 2:256: you cannot force anyone. 2:257: but you must have a guardian, and the wrong guardianship is a real loss. 2:258: a man who had everything the world offers and used it to argue with a prophet. **The arc runs: no compulsion — but a choice, and the choice has consequences.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 258

**"Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship? ˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.” He argued, “I too have the power to give life and cause death.” Abraham challenged ˹him˺, “Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west.” And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people."**

In simple terms: have you not considered the one who disputed with Abraham about his Lord, because God had given him kingship? When Abraham said, "My Lord is the One who gives life and causes death," he said, "I give life and I cause death." Abraham said, "God brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west" — and the one who disbelieved was confounded. And God does not guide the wrongdoing people.

*A-lam tara ilā alladhī ḥājja Ibrāhīma fī rabbihi an ātāhu Allāha al-mulka, idh qāla Ibrāhīmu rabbī alladhī yuḥyī wa yumītu, qāla ana uḥyī wa umītu, qāla Ibrāhīmu fa-inna Allāha ya'tī bi-al-shamsi min al-mashriqi fa'ti bihā min al-maghribi, fa-buhita alladhī kafara, wa Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn.*

*Alladhī ḥājja Ibrāhīma fī rabbihi **an ātāhu Allāha al-mulka*** — "the one who disputed with Abraham about his Lord, **because God had given him kingship**."

**Note the causal, and it is the most important word in the verse.**

***An ātāhu Allāha al-mulk*** — **because** God gave him kingship. **The kingship is named as the cause of the argument.** It was not ignorance that made him argue. **It was power.** He had been given something, and the having of it made him argue with a prophet about God.

**And note the verb: *ātāhu Allāh* — God gave it to him.** **The same verb as 2:247** (*Allāhu yu'tī mulkahu man yashā'* — God gives His kingship to whom He wills). **Which means: the power he is using to argue against God was given to him by the God he is arguing against.**

**That is one of the most withering details in the Qur'an, and it is stated in a subordinate clause, almost in passing.** Every tyrant's strength is on loan. **He is arguing with the Lender using the borrowed thing.**

**Who is he?** The commentators generally identify him as **Namrūd** (Nimrod), a king of Babylon; some reports say a king of Kan'ān, and others give other names. **The Qur'an does not name him, and the identification is transmitted, not textual.** And that anonymity is deliberate: **this is not a story about one king. It is a story about what kingship does to a man.**

*Idh qāla Ibrāhīmu **rabbī alladhī yuḥyī wa yumīt*** — "when Abraham said: **my Lord is the One who gives life and causes death**."

**Note what Ibrāhīm chose as his opening.** Not "my Lord created the heavens and the earth" — though he will use the sun in a moment. **He opens with life and death, because that is the one thing a king believes he controls.** A king decides who lives and who dies; that is what kingship *is*. **So Ibrāhīm meets him on his own claimed ground.**

*Qāla **ana uḥyī wa umīt*** — "he said: **I give life and I cause death**."

**And the reports explain the trick: he brought two men condemned to death, killed one and released the other, and said, "I gave life and I caused death."**

**And now the rhetorical lesson of the verse, which is worth stating plainly because it is one of the most useful things in the Qur'an for anyone arguing with power.**

**The king's claim was a pun.** He had quietly redefined "give life and cause death" to mean "decide whether the state executes you." **And Ibrāhīm does not argue with it.**

**That is the whole point.** To argue against a redefinition is to concede the terms. The moment you say "that is not what giving life means," you have accepted his vocabulary and are now playing on his field, and he has more soldiers. **Ibrāhīm declines.**

*Qāla Ibrāhīmu fa-inna Allāha **ya'tī bi-al-shamsi min al-mashriqi fa'ti bihā min al-maghrib*** — "Abraham said: **God brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west**."

**He changes the ground completely.**

**And note the calibration of the new demand.** He does not say "you are lying." He does not say "that is not what life means." **He asks for a demonstration that cannot be faked and cannot be reframed.** The sun rises where it rises, **in front of everyone, every single day.** There is no room for a pun. There is no room for a private definition. **And there is no way to quietly have an underling do it.**

**This is the model for disputing with power, and it has two features worth naming:**
- **It is public.** The test is one anyone can check.
- **It is impossible without being unreasonable.** Ibrāhīm is not demanding a magic trick. He is demanding the same thing God does every morning.

*Fa-**buhita** alladhī kafara* — "and the one who disbelieved was **confounded**."

***Buhita*** — struck dumb, confounded, rendered speechless. **And note carefully what the verse does NOT say.**

**It does not say he was convinced. It says he was silenced.**

**That distinction is the verse's realism, and it is the connection back to 2:256.** The man was confounded and he still did not believe. **Argument wins the argument. It does not open the heart.** And that is exactly why there is to be no compulsion in religion: **the man who has been shown the thing and still refuses cannot be moved by anything a human being can do to him.**

**And this is one of the most practically comforting verses in the Qur'an for anyone who has ever lost an argument they were clearly winning.** You can be right, be obviously right, leave the other person with nothing to say — **and still not convert them.** That is not your failure. **It is the difference between *buhita* and *āmana*.**

*Wa Allāhu **lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn*** — "and God does not **guide** the wrongdoing people."

**Note the link back to 2:254's closing: *al-kāfirūna hum al-ẓālimūn*.** The man who uses a God-given kingship to argue with a prophet about God is doing the *ẓulm* the verse names. **And guidance is withheld — not arbitrarily, but because he has placed his power where his submission belonged.**

**A caution on reading this.** "God does not guide" is not "God prevents." The tradition is careful here: **guidance is withheld from the one who has chosen wrongly, and the choosing is his.** The verse does not describe a man who wanted guidance and was refused it. **It describes a man who had a prophet standing in front of him, arguing in public, and preferred his own kingship.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 259

**"Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, “How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?” So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life. Allah asked, “How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?” He replied, “Perhaps a day or part of a day.” Allah said, “No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink— they have not spoiled. ˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity. And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!” When this was made clear to him, he declared, “˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything.”"**

In simple terms: or like the one who passed by a township lying in ruins on its roofs. He said: "How will God bring this to life after its death?" So God caused him to die for a hundred years, then raised him. He said: "How long have you tarried?" He said: "A day, or part of a day." He said: "No — you have tarried a hundred years. Look at your food and your drink: they have not staled. And look at your donkey — and that We may make you a sign for the people. And look at the bones, how We set them up and clothe them with flesh." And when it became clear to him, he said: "I know that God is powerful over everything."

*Aw ka-alladhī marra 'alā qaryatin wa hiya khāwiyatun 'alā 'urūshihā, qāla annā yuḥyī hādhihi Allāhu ba'da mawtihā, fa-amātahu Allāhu mi'ata 'āmin thumma ba'athahu, qāla kam labithta, qāla labithtu yawman aw ba'ḍa yawmin, qāla bal labithta mi'ata 'āmin, fa-unẓur ilā ṭa'āmika wa sharābika lam yatasannah, wa-unẓur ilā ḥimārika, wa li-naj'alaka āyatan li-al-nāsi, wa-unẓur ilā al-'iẓāmi kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥman, fa-lammā tabayyana lahu qāla a'lamu anna Allāha 'alā kulli shay'in qadīr.*

**A caution first, and it is a large one. The man is not named in the text.**

**The identification is a report.** Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded that **'Alī ibn Abī Ṭālib** said the verse refers to **'Uzayr**; Ibn Jarīr reported the same, and it is also reported from **Ibn 'Abbās, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, al-Suddī and Sulaymān ibn Buraydah**. Other reports name **Irmiyā (Jeremiah)**, and some name al-Khiḍr or an unnamed king. **The reports vary, and the majority of the classical commentators incline to 'Uzayr. But none of this is in the verse, and it should be held as report, not text.**

*Aw ka-alladhī marra 'alā qaryatin **wa hiya khāwiyatun 'alā 'urūshihā*** — "or like the one who passed by a township **lying hollow on its roofs**."

**Note the description, which is a single devastating image.** *Khāwiyatun 'alā 'urūshihā* — **collapsed down onto its roofs.** The roofs fell in first and came to rest on the ground, and then the walls came down on top of them. **The town went roofs-down.** It is the picture of a place not merely abandoned but inverted.

**The transmitted accounts commonly place it at Jerusalem after its destruction. Flag that as a report.**

*Qāla **annā yuḥyī hādhihi Allāhu ba'da mawtihā*** — "he said: **how** will God bring this to life after its death?"

**And the first thing to settle is: this was not disbelief.**

**Note the word: *annā* — "how," not "whether."** He did not say "will God bring this back?" He said **"*how* will God bring this back?"** The question is about the mechanism, not the possibility.

**The commentators are agreed on this, and it matters.** Ibn Kathīr and others state that he said it **because of the utter destruction he saw and the sheer implausibility of its returning to what it had been** — not because he doubted that God could. **This is the question of a man trying to picture something he already believes, and failing.**

**Which is a distinction every believer should hold onto tightly.** There is a real and legitimate question — *how?* — which is the engine of all enquiry and all awe. And there is the question — *whether?* — which is unbelief. **The verse records the first, and it is not rebuked.** Compare 2:260 below, where Ibrāhīm's request to *see* is met with a question but not with censure.

*Fa-**amātahu Allāhu mi'ata 'āmin** thumma ba'athahu* — "so **God caused him to die for a hundred years**, then raised him."

**Note the answer to his question: God does not explain the mechanism. He performs it on the questioner.**

**That is a pattern worth noticing, because it recurs.** The man asks *how*; God answers by *doing* it to him, in his own body. **He wanted to see how a dead town is revived, so he was made the dead thing that gets revived.** The lesson is not delivered as an explanation. **It is delivered as an experience.**

**And note the number: a hundred years.** Reports say the town was rebuilt seventy years after his death and that the Children of Israel returned to it — **transmitted, not textual.**

*Qāla kam labithta, qāla **labithtu yawman aw ba'ḍa yawmin*** — "He said: how long have you tarried? He said: **I have tarried a day, or part of a day**."

**Note the honesty of the answer and its uncertainty: "a day — or part of a day."** He is guessing, and he knows he is guessing. **And the guess is wrong by a factor of something like thirty-six thousand.**

**That detail is doing real work.** The man has just been shown that **his own subjective sense of duration is worthless.** A century felt like an afternoon. **Which means: whatever else this verse teaches, it teaches that you are not a reliable instrument for measuring anything outside yourself.** And that is the opener for everything that follows.

*Qāla **bal labithta mi'ata 'āmin*** — "He said: **no — you have tarried a hundred years**."

**And now the demonstration, and it comes in two parts, and the two parts are different from each other in a way that is easy to miss.**

*Fa-unẓur ilā **ṭa'āmika wa sharābika lam yatasannah*** — "look at your food and your drink: **it has not staled**."

**Sign one: preservation.**

***Lam yatasannah*** — it has not changed, not gone stale, not spoiled. (The word has a well-known grammatical discussion: its root is *sana*, the year, and the original form carries a final *hā'* that drops in the pausal reading — the point being **it has not been touched by a year, let alone a hundred.**)

**The transmitted accounts specify what he had with him — bread, pressed grapes, figs — and say he found it exactly as he had left it. Flag as report.**

**And what this sign demonstrates: God can suspend decay.** A hundred years passed over that food and did nothing to it. **Time is not an agent. It does not act on its own. It is a creature, and it does what it is told.**

*Wa-unẓur ilā **ḥimārika*** — "and look at your donkey."

**Sign two: reconstruction.**

**Because the donkey did rot.** The food was preserved; the donkey was not. **The reports describe the bones scattered to his right and his left, bleached and disarticulated.** The verse continues: *wa-unẓur ilā al-'iẓāmi **kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥman*** — "and look at the bones, **how We raise them up and then clothe them with flesh**."

***Nunshizuhā*** — We set them up, raise them, assemble them from their scattered state. ***Thumma naksūhā laḥman*** — **then We clothe them with flesh.** **Note the verb: *naksū* — We clothe.** Bones are dressed in flesh the way a body is dressed in a garment. **And the reports add the final stroke: the donkey brayed, in front of him, by God's leave. Flag as report.**

**So the two signs are deliberately complementary:**
- **The food: God can hold decay off.** Time did not touch it.
- **The donkey: God can reverse decay.** Time destroyed it completely, and He rebuilt it.

**Between them, the two signs cover both directions.** *He can stop the process, and He can run it backwards.* **That is the argument for the Resurrection, stated as a demonstration rather than a proof.** And it answers the man's *how?* exactly: **not with an explanation, but with two objects, one preserved and one remade, both in front of him.**

*Wa **li-naj'alaka āyatan li-al-nāsi*** — "and **that We may make you a sign for the people**."

**Note: *you*.** Not "and that We may show you a sign." **He is made the sign.** The man who asked *how* became the answer for everyone after him. **Which is the verse's own account of what it is doing: you are reading a sign that was a man.**

**And that is the connection to 2:243 in this batch's earlier batch** — the people who died and were raised were likewise a demonstration. **The Qur'an's argument for the Resurrection is not primarily philosophical. It is: this has happened before, in public, to people.**

*Fa-lammā tabayyana lahu qāla **a'lamu anna Allāha 'alā kulli shay'in qadīr*** — "and when it became clear to him, he said: **I know that God is powerful over everything**."

**Note the shift in his language.** At the start he said *annā yuḥyī hādhihi Allāh* — "how will God bring this to life?" At the end he says *a'lamu anna Allāha 'alā kulli shay'in qadīr* — **"I know that God is powerful over everything."**

**He asked about one town. He was answered about everything.** That is the movement the verse traces: **from a question about a ruined place to a statement about all of reality.**

**A necessary note on 'Uzayr and 9:30.** If the identification with 'Uzayr is accepted — and it is the majority report, though it is a report — then this verse is often connected to **9:30**, where the Qur'an says: **"The Jews say, 'Uzayr is the son of God,' and the Christians say, 'the Messiah is the son of God.'"** Two things must be said about this, and both are owed in fairness:

- **The classical commentators state that this was a claim of a sub-group, not of all Jews.** Ibn Kathīr transmits reports from Ibn 'Abbās that a specific small number voiced it. **No mainstream Jewish textual tradition affirms that Ezra was the son of God**, and this should be stated plainly rather than hinted at.
- **The Qur'anic idiom "the Jews say" does not universally mean "all Jews, everywhere, always."** The commentators apply the same qualifier to "the Christians say" in the same verse. **This is the tradition's own reading, not a modern softening.**

**And note the most interesting thing, if the identification holds:** the very thing for which some exalted 'Uzayr — his miraculous revival — **is narrated here in order to be attributed to God and not to him.** The story that made him extraordinary is told precisely so that the wonder lands on God. **The verse converts a possible source of exaggeration into a proof of *tawḥīd*.**

**And one last caution: whether 'Uzayr was a prophet is not stated in the Qur'an.** He is not named in any list of prophets, and the sources differ. **It should not be asserted either way.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 260

**"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead.” Allah responded, “Do you not believe?” Abraham replied, “Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured.” Allah said, “Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops. Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste. And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”"**

In simple terms: and when Abraham said: "My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead." He said: "Do you not believe?" He said: "Yes — but so that my heart may be at rest." He said: "Then take four birds and incline them to you; then place a part of them on every hill; then call them — they will come to you in haste. And know that God is Mighty, Wise."

*Wa idh qāla Ibrāhīmu rabbi arinī kayfa tuḥyī al-mawtā, qāla awa-lā tu'min, qāla balā wa lākin li-yaṭma'inna qalbī, qāla fa-khudh arba'atan mina al-ṭayri fa-ṣurhunna ilayka thumma ij'al 'alā kulli jabalin minhunna juz'an thumma id'uhunna ya'tīnaka sa'yā, wa-'lam anna Allāha 'azīzun ḥakīm.*

**This is the verse that settles, once and for all, whether asking is a sign of weak faith.**

*Rabbi **arinī kayfa tuḥyī al-mawtā*** — "My Lord: **show me how You give life to the dead**."

**Note who is speaking.** Not a doubter. Not a newcomer. **Ibrāhīm** — the one the Qur'an calls *khalīl Allāh*, the intimate friend of God (4:125); the one who was given his sound reasoning as a child (21:51–70); the one whose religion this is; the one who will be told *aslim* and answers *aslamtu li-rabbi al-'ālamīn* (2:131). **He asks.**

**And note the word again: *kayfa* — how.** The same *how* as 2:259. **Not "will You?" — "how will You?"** The question is about the manner, and it is asked by a man who has already been given certainty.

*Qāla **awa-lā tu'min*** — "He said: **do you not believe?**"

**And note: God does not rebuke him. He asks.** The question is not a reprimand; if it were, the answer would not be what it is. **The form of the question is an invitation to say out loud what the asking is for.**

*Qāla **balā** wa lākin li-**yaṭma'inna qalbī*** — "He said: **yes indeed** — but **so that my heart may be at rest**."

**This is one of the most important six words in the Qur'an.**

***Balā*** — "yes indeed," an affirmative that answers a negative question. **He does not hesitate. He does not qualify. *Balā* — yes, I believe.**

*Wa lākin **li-yaṭma'inna qalbī*** — "but so that my heart may be at rest."

**And here is the distinction the entire spiritual tradition rests on: the difference between *'ilm* and *iṭmi'nān*.**

- ***'Ilm*** — knowledge. Ibrāhīm had it. He says so: *balā*.
- ***Ṭuma'nīnah*** — tranquillity, settledness, the heart resting in what it knows. **He is asking for this.**

**And he is asking for it *in addition to* the knowledge he already has.** Which means: **knowledge and tranquillity are not the same thing, and having the first does not automatically give you the second.** A person can know a thing completely and still have an unsettled heart about it. **That is not hypocrisy. That is being human, and it is why the friend of God asked.**

**The tradition draws several things from this clause, and all of them are worth having:**

**1. Asking is not doubting.** Ibrāhīm believed and asked. **Anyone who tells you that questions are a symptom of weak faith has Ibrāhīm against him.**

**2. Certainty has degrees, and more is available.** The tradition's technical term is *ziyādat al-yaqīn* — increase in certainty. **If the friend of God could ask for more, so can you.**

**3. Seeing is a legitimate route to settledness — for some things.** The tradition is careful here: **the unseen is not to be demanded as a condition for belief** (2:118, 6:158, 2:210). What Ibrāhīm asked was to be *shown a demonstration of something he already believed* — and it was granted to a prophet, in a specific instance, by God's command. **This is not a template for demanding signs. It is a precedent for asking God to settle your heart on what you already know.**

**And now the practical application, which is the one that matters for ordinary people.** The most common version of this prayer in human experience is not "show me a miracle." It is: **"Lord, I believe this — so why is my heart not at peace about it?"** That is Ibrāhīm's question verbatim. **And the verse's answer is not an explanation. It is a demonstration — God shows him something.** Which means the settledness is not produced by an argument. **It is given.**

*Qāla fa-khudh **arba'atan mina al-ṭayri*** — "He said: then take **four birds**."

Why four? **The transmitted reports give various answers, and none is established — some say four kinds, some give a symbolic reading, and the commentators differ. The number is in the text; the reason for it is not, and it should not be invented.**

*Fa-ṣurhunna ilayka* — and here there is a **genuine disagreement over the word**, and the supplied translation has folded both readings into one rendering.

- **The majority reading takes *fa-ṣurhunna* from *ṣāra, yaṣūru* — to cut, to separate**: **"cut them into pieces."** This is the reading of Ibn 'Abbās, 'Ikrimah and others, and it is what the rest of the verse requires — parts are then distributed on hills.
- **The other reading takes it as "incline them / turn them toward you" — i.e., train them to come to you.** Some commentators hold this, and it is why several English translations (including the supplied one) render "train them to come to you" before the cutting.

**Both readings are held, and the majority position is the first. The supplied translation has combined them, which is legitimate — the sense on either reading converges on: take the birds, have them gathered to you, then divide them.**

*Thumma ij'al 'alā kulli jabalin minhunna **juz'an*** — "then place a **part of them** on every hill."

**Note the dispersal.** The parts are scattered across separate hills — **maximally separated, so that no natural explanation is available.** A bird carcass on one hill could conceivably be a bird. **A piece of a bird on each of several hills, reassembling, is not.**

*Thumma **id'uhunna ya'tīnaka sa'yā*** — "then **call them** — they will come to you **in haste**."

***Sa'yan*** — hastening, running, straining. **Not shuffling. Not reassembling slowly. Running.** The reports add that the parts flew from their separate hills and the pieces sought each other out and knit together. **Flag as report.**

**And note the structure of the demonstration: he must do the cutting, the scattering, and the calling.** God does not do it for him. **Ibrāhīm's own hands perform the destruction, the dispersal, and the summons — and then he watches the reassembly.** Which is the point: **he is placed at the exact place where the impossibility is greatest, and then he watches it undone.**

**And note the parallel with 2:259.** The man of the ruined town was made to die and was shown his donkey rebuilt. Ibrāhīm is made to cut and scatter and is shown the birds rebuilt. **In both cases the questioner is put inside the demonstration.** And in both cases the answer to *how?* is not an explanation.

**And note the parallel with 2:258, which is the pole the whole passage answers.** In 2:258 the tyrant claimed: *ana uḥyī wa umīt* — **"I give life and I cause death"** — and it was a pun. **In 2:260, God shows His friend how the dead are actually raised.** The surah answers the tyrant's boast with a prophet's demonstration. **That is the arc from 258 to 260, and it is why these verses are together.**

*Wa-'lam anna Allāha **'azīzun ḥakīm*** — "and know that God is **Mighty, Wise**."

***'Azīz*** — Mighty, unopposed, unovercome. ***Ḥakīm*** — Wise, placing everything exactly where it belongs.

**Note which two Names close this.** Not *qadīr* (Powerful) — though that is what is being demonstrated — but ***'Azīz*** and ***Ḥakīm***. **Might: nothing can resist Him. Wisdom: He does nothing without purpose.** The demonstration of power is closed with the Name of wisdom, **so that no one reads the raising of the dead as a display.**

**And the closing word is *wa-'lam* — "and know."** The same *'ilm* Ibrāhīm already had. **He is not given a new belief. He is given the settledness he asked for, and the knowledge is confirmed rather than replaced.** *Balā* — yes, I believe — *wa lākin li-yaṭma'inna qalbī.*

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis: Verses 251–260

**The arc.** 2:251 closes the Ṭālūt narrative with the victory deferred from 2:250's prayer. 2:252–253 turn to the Prophet ﷺ and to the communities that come after messengers. 2:254 commands spending before a Day with no transaction in it. **2:255 is Āyat al-Kursī.** 2:256 follows with no compulsion in religion, 2:257 with the two guardianships. And 2:258–260 give three scenes demonstrating who actually holds power.

**The centre of gravity is 2:255, and everything around it is arranged.** Immediately before it: **spend, before the Day comes** (254). Immediately after it: **do not compel** (256). **The greatest statement of who God is sits between a command to give and a prohibition on forcing — because both come from the same fact.** You give because everything is His. You do not force, because He is *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* and does not need your recruit.

**And the second half of the batch is one sustained argument about power.** 2:258: a king who thought he gave life and caused death, confounded by the direction of the sun. 2:259: a man who asked *how* a dead town could live, and was made dead for a century to find out. 2:260: the friend of God asking to be shown, and shown. **Three scenes, one conclusion: giving life and causing death belongs to God, and no one borrows it.**

**The thread that runs through the whole batch is the word *how*.** *Annā yuḥyī hādhihi Allāh* (259) — how will God bring this to life? *Kayfa tuḥyī al-mawtā* (260) — how do You give life to the dead? **And in both cases the question is not rebuked, and in both cases the answer is not an explanation but a demonstration performed on the questioner himself.** That is the Qur'an's answer to *how?* throughout: **not a mechanism, but a showing.** And in both cases the questioner is placed inside the demonstration. **He wanted to see how a dead thing is revived, so he was made the dead thing.**

**Two motifs connect back across the surah.** First: **intercession is stated flatly denied at 2:254 and qualified one verse later at 2:255** — "except by His permission." Read together, they say the intercession that is denied is intercession as a *claim*, and the intercession that is affirmed is intercession as a *gift*. Second: **"no compulsion" (256) is completed by "reject the ṭāghūt and believe in God" (256) and by the two guardianships (257).** The verse everybody quotes is not a statement of indifference. **It is a statement that faith is a two-part act — a renunciation and an allegiance — and that you cannot hold both.**

**Where the supplied translation resolves an ambiguity, it has been marked.** At 2:255, *lā ilāha* is rendered "no god **worthy of worship**," which makes explicit the ellipsis the *shahādah* requires — the denial is of anything deserving worship, not of the existence of other objects of worship. At 2:260, *fa-ṣurhunna ilayka* is rendered with both of the transmitted readings folded together ("train them to come to you, then cut them into pieces"); **the majority reading takes it as the cutting alone, and the disagreement has been noted in the commentary.** And at 2:259, "look at your food and drink — they have not spoiled" carries a spacing in the original text that has been preserved verbatim.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. **Where the scholars differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved**: whether 2:256 is abrogated, specified, or general and unabrogated; whether *kursī* means the footstool or — as a minority reads it — knowledge or dominion; whether *fa-ṣurhunna* means cutting or training; whether the man of 2:259 is 'Uzayr, Irmiyā, or another; whether 'Uzayr was a prophet; whether *rūḥ al-qudus* is Jibrīl; and how 2:253's "if God had willed" is to be held with human responsibility.

**Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout**, with particular care in 2:259, where nearly every narrative detail — the identification of the man, the location of the town, the rebuilding after seventy years, the contents of his provisions, the gathering of the donkey's bones and the braying — **is *Isrā'īliyyāt*-derived and varies between sources**. The narration attributed to Abū Dharr comparing the heavens and the earth to a ring thrown in a desert has been flagged as reported with a discussed chain. The hadith of Abū Umāmah on reciting Āyat al-Kursī after the prayers has been flagged as disputed in grading. And the identification of the disputant of 2:258 as Namrūd has been marked as transmitted rather than textual.

**Four boundaries have been observed.** On 2:255: every attribute — life, self-subsistence, the Kursī, highness, greatness — has been affirmed as it came, **without *tashbīh* and without *ta'tīl***, the same rule applied to every attribute earlier in this surah. On 2:251: *daf'* has been read as repulsion and not aggression, fenced by 2:190–193 and 8:61, and paired with 22:40, whose stated beneficiaries are monasteries, churches, synagogues and mosques. On 2:257: **no one may declare a specific living individual an inhabitant of the Fire**; the verse describes a category and the testimony about particular persons is withheld. And on 2:253: the ranking of the messengers has been held together with the hadith forbidding the competitive preference of prophets over one another, and the verse's purpose has been identified as consolation rather than classification.

The most important sentence in the batch is in 2:260, and it is six words long: ***balā — wa lākin li-yaṭma'inna qalbī.*** **"Yes indeed — but so that my heart may be at rest."** The friend of God already believed, and he asked to be shown. **Which settles, permanently, that a question is not a doubt — and that wanting your heart to catch up with what you know is a prayer God answers.**
