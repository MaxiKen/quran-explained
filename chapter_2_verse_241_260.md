## Verse 241

Verse 241 says: *"Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺."*

The verse's *al-mut'ah* — the provision — the known good — the measure of the divorce's decency. ## Verse 243

Verse 243 says: *"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death? Allah said to them, "Die!" then He gave them life. Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful."*

In simple terms, the verse tells of those who fled their homes in their thousands, fearing death — and Allah said "Die!", then revived them — the lesson that no escape can avert destiny, and that Allah's bounty is met by the ingratitude.

"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death" — the verse's story. Ibn Kathir (from Waki' ibn Jarrah, from Ibn 'Abbas): they were four thousand persons who escaped the plague that broke out in their land — they said, "We should go to a land free of death!" When they reached a certain area, Allah said to them, "Die!" — and they all died. Afterwards, one of the Prophets passed by them, supplicated to Allah, and Allah brought them back to life. Several scholars of the Salaf: these people were the residents of a city in the time of the Children of Israel — an epidemic broke out; they fled to the wilderness; they came to a fertile valley and filled it; Allah sent two angels, one from the lower side, one from the upper — the angels screamed once and all the people died instantly, as the death of one man. Ibn Kathir: this story indicates that no caution can avert destiny — there is no refuge from Allah but to Allah Himself — these people departed fleeing the epidemic, seeking long life, and earned the opposite of what they sought — death came quickly and instantaneously and seized them all. Maududi: the story's lesson — the fear of death, the attempt to escape, and the divine decree.

"Allah said to them, "Die!" then He gave them life" — the verse's power: the Word — "Die!" — and they died; and the will — the life — and they lived. The verse's note: the resurrection of this community is the sign of the resurrection of all — the One who said "Die!" to the thousands is the One who will call all from the graves.

Ibn Kathir: "Allah is Bountiful" — in what He shows them of the manifest signs, the decisive proofs, the conclusive evidences; "but most people do not show gratitude" — they do not give thanks for what Allah has bestowed, in their religion and their worldly affairs. The verse's frame: the bounty of the revival — the mercy of the second life — and the ingratitude of the majority.

The verse's place: after the family law — the story of the fleeing thousands — the medicine of the heart: the fear of death, the trust in Allah, the gratitude. And the reader's part: the refuge from Allah is only to Allah; the death will come where you are; and the bounty of the life after the death deserves the thanks.

Yet if Allah had willed, they would not have fought one another. But Allah does what He wills."*

In simple terms, the verse speaks of the messengers' ranks: Allah spoke directly to some, raised some in degree, gave Jesus the clear proofs and the holy spirit; and the people who came after differed and fought — by Allah's will, not His compulsion.

"We have chosen some of those messengers above others" — the verse's *tafdil* — the preference — the raising of some above others. Ibn Kathir: we preferred some of them to others. The verse's frame: the messengers differ in the manner of revelation, in the laws, in the degrees — the differences are Allah's choice.

"Allah spoke directly to some" — the verse's *taklim* — the direct speech — Musa (cf. 4:164: "Allah spoke to Musa directly"). Ibn Kathir: "to some of them Allah spoke (directly)" — Musa and Muhammad ﷺ — and also Adam, according to a hadith recorded in Sahih Ibn Hibban from Abu Dharr. The verse's frame: the direct speech is the rank — the word without the intermediary.

"and raised some high in rank" — the verse's *darajat* — the degrees. Ibn Kathir: others He raised to degrees of honor. The verse's frame: the rank above the rank — the Prophet's night journey (Al-Isra), the chosen — the degrees of the divine grace.

"To Jesus, son of Mary, We gave clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit" — the verse's 'Isa. Ibn Kathir: "We gave 'Isa, the son of Maryam, clear signs" — the proofs and the unequivocal evidences that testify to the truth that 'Isa delivered to the Children of Israel — testifying that he was Allah's servant and His Messenger to them. And "the holy spirit" (Ruh al-Qudus) — Jibril (Gabriel) — who accompanied him wherever he went (Al-Jalalayn). The clear proofs: reviving the dead, healing the blind and the lepers, creating the bird from clay, by Allah's will. The verse's frame: the clear signs and the support — the confirmation of the servant and the Messenger.

"If Allah had willed, succeeding generations would not have fought ˹among themselves˺ after receiving the clear proofs" — the verse's divine will. Ibn Kathir: if Allah had willed, those who came after them — their communities — would not have fought against one another after the clear proofs had come to them. The verse's frame: the fighting after the proofs is the human choice — the will of Allah is the decree, not the compulsion: He willed the freedom, and they chose the fight.

"But they differed—some believed while others disbelieved" — the verse's division: the differing — the believing and the disbelieving. The verse's frame: the proofs came, and the difference came — the human response to the clear signs.

"Yet if Allah had willed, they would not have fought one another" — the verse's repetition of the will: the if — the could-have — the decree's openness. Ibn Kathir: but they fell into variance, as He willed — and some of them believed and some disbelieved. The balance: Allah does not force; Allah's will encompasses; the human's responsibility stands.

"But Allah does what He wills" — the verse's close: *innallaha yaf'alu ma yurid* — Allah does what He wills — the decree's execution, the wisdom's unfolding. The verse's frame: the will is not the constraint; the will is the justice and the wisdom — Allah does what He wills, and the humans bear the choices.

The verse's lesson: the ranks of the messengers are Allah's; the fighting after the proofs is the people's choice; and Allah does what He wills. And the reader's part: the proofs are the messengers' legacy, and the responding is the human's responsibility.

## Verse 254

Verse 254 says: *"O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession. Those who disbelieve are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the spending before the Day: the Day of no bargaining, no friendship, no intercession — and the disbelievers are the wrongdoers.

"O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you" — the verse's *anfaqu* — the spending — from what Allah provided — the charity, the donation, the seeking of the Hereafter. Ibn Kathir: spend from what We have given you — before the Day. The verse's frame: the spending is the investing in the Hereafter — where nothing benefits except the righteous deeds.

"before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession" — the verse's Day: the la bay' — the no-buying, the no-bargaining — the no trade that yields profit; the la khullah — the no-friendship that saves; the la shafa'ah — the no-intercession that avails. Ibn Kathir: the Day when no trade, no friendship, no intercession — the investing: the righteous deed. The note: the no-intercession is the no-intercession-without-permission: on that Day, intercession exists only by Allah's permission (cf. 20:109: "no intercession shall avail except for the one for whom the Most Gracious has given permission"). The verse's frame: the Day's commerce: no bargains, no friends, no intercessors — only the deeds.

"Those who disbelieve are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers" — the verse's close: the kafirun — the disbelievers — the zalimun — the wrongdoers. Ibn Kathir: the wrongdoers — because they placed things in the wrong places: the disbelief in the place of the faith, the wealth in the place of the deed. The verse's frame: the wrong is the refusal — the one who does not spend for the Day of no-bargaining has wronged the self.

The verse's lesson: the spending before the Day is the wise trade; the Day's economy is the deed only. And the reader's part: the donations are the purchase of the Hereafter — the trade of the believers — and the ones who refuse are the wrongdoers of the self.

## Verse 255

Verse 255 says: *"Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission? He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them, but no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺. His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him. For He is the Most High, the Greatest."*

In simple terms, the verse is the Ayat al-Kursi: the Ever-Living, the All-Sustaining; no drowsiness or sleep; the heavens and the earth are His; intercession only by His permission; His knowledge is beyond grasp; His Seat encompasses all; and He is the Most High, the Greatest.

"Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining" — the verse's foundation: the ilah — the only — al-Hayy — the Ever-Living — al-Qayyum — the All-Sustaining, the Self-Subsisting — the One by Whom all subsists. Ibn Kathir: Allah — there is no deity except Him — the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all existence. The verse's frame: the two names: the Life and the Sustenance — the One who lives and the One who keeps all alive.

"Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him" — the verse's negation: the sinah — the drowsiness — the nawm — the sleep — the senses' lapse — not on Him. Ibn Kathir: neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep. The verse's frame: the keepers of the worlds sleep not; the Guardian of the heavens and the earth is awake forever.

"To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth" — the verse's ownership: all — the heavens and the earth. Ibn Kathir: to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.

"Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?" — the verse's intercession: the shafa'ah — only by His permission. Ibn Kathir: who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission? The verse's frame: the intercession is the mercy's gate — the permission is the gate's key — no one steps before the decree without the leave.

"He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them" — the verse's knowledge: the ahead — the present, the future — the behind — the past. Ibn Kathir: He knows what is before them and what is after them.

"but no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺" — the verse's limit: the encompasses — no one encompasses His knowledge — except what He wills. Ibn Kathir: they encompass not a thing of His knowledge except for what He wills. The verse's frame: the knowledge is the sea — the creatures are given the cups of the will.

"His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him" — the verse's *Kursi* — the Seat. Ibn Kathir (and the classical notes): His Kursi extends over the heavens and the earth — Ibn 'Abbas and the Salaf: the Kursi is the footstool of the Throne ('Arsh), encompassing all creation — the 'Arsh is far greater — the narration: the Kursi in relation to the 'Arsh is like a ring thrown in a vast desert; the preservation of both does not tire Him — the qayyum's endless keeping. The verse's frame: the Seat encompasses — the heavens and the earth are within the seat of the Most High — and the preservation is effortless.

"For He is the Most High, the Greatest" — the verse's close: al-'Aliyy — the Most High — al-'Adhim — the Greatest. Ibn Kathir: He is the Most High — above all — the Greatest — above all measure.

The verse's verse of the Book's summit: Ubayy ibn Ka'b's hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim): the Prophet asked, "O Abu Mundhir, do you know which verse in the Book of Allah with you is greatest?" Ubayy recited the Verse of the Throne (2:255) — and the Prophet tapped his chest and said, "May knowledge delight you, Abu Mundhir!" The verse's frame: the greatest verse — the summary of the faith: the One, the Living, the Sustaining — the protector's verse. And the reader's part: the Ayat al-Kursi is the believer's shield and the faith's summary.

## Verse 256

Verse 256 says: *"Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse rules on the religion's acceptance: no compulsion in religion — the truth is distinct from falsehood — whoever rejects the false gods and believes in Allah has grasped the firmest, unfailing handhold.

"Let there be no compulsion in religion" — the verse's principle: la ikrah fi al-din. Ibn Kathir: "Do not force anyone to become Muslim, for Islam is plain and clear, and its proofs and evidence are plain and clear — therefore, there is no need to force anyone to embrace Islam. Rather, whoever Allah directs to Islam, opens his heart for it and enlightens his mind, will embrace Islam with certainty; whoever Allah blinds his heart and seals his hearing and sight, then he will not benefit from being forced to embrace Islam." The verse's frame: the religion is the faith of the heart — the compulsion produces the appearance, not the belief.

"for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood" — the verse's clarity: al-rushd — the right course — min al-ghayy — from the wrong. Ibn Kathir: the right course has become distinct from the wrong path. The verse's frame: the clarity is the reason: the path is plain — the choice is the human's.

"So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold" — the verse's *taghut* — the false gods — the idols, the false deities, the false leaders, the Satan's allies — the disbelieve-in; and the *'urwat al-wuthqa* — the firmest, unfailing handhold — the no-break. Ibn Kathir: who disbelieves in taghut and believes in Allah — has grasped the most trustworthy handhold that will never break — the strongest grip, built solid, its handle firmly connected — the firmest handhold. Ma'arif: "He will have hold of the true religion with the strongest grasp." The verse's frame: the two acts: the renouncing of the false gods and the believing in Allah — the handhold is the two acts' grip.

"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — the verse's close: the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. Ibn Kathir: the All-Hearer of the statements, the All-Knower of the intentions.

The verse's occasion (Ibn Jarir, from Ibn 'Abbas): before Islam, when an Ansar woman's children would not live, she would vow that if a child survived, she would raise him as a Jew; when Banu an-Nadir were evacuated from al-Madinah, some of the children of the Ansar were among them — the Ansar said, "We will not abandon our children" — Allah revealed, "There is no compulsion in religion." (Sa'id ibn Jubayr: those who wished to leave with the Jews left, and those who wished to embrace Islam embraced Islam.) The verse's frame: the occasion is specific, the principle is general — the religion is not compelled — the Jews' leaving and the children's choosing.

The verse's place: after the Ayat al-Kursi — the greatest verse of the faith — then the verse of the no-compulsion: the faith's greatest verse is not the faith's coercion; the clear truth is the freedom's ground. And the reader's part: the truth stands out clearly — the handhold is the firmest — and the religion is the heart's affair, not the force's.

## Verse 257

Verse 257 says: *"Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."*

In simple terms, the verse speaks of the two guardianships: Allah is the Guardian of the believers, bringing them from darkness to light; the disbelievers' guardians are the false gods, leading them from light to darkness — the residents of the Fire, forever.

"Allah is the Guardian of the believers" — the verse's *wali* — the Guardian, the Protector, the Ally. Ibn Kathir: Allah is the Wali (Protector or Guardian) of those who believe. The verse's frame: the wali — the one who protects, who defends, who aids, who guides — the believers' Guardian is Allah.

"He brings them out of darkness and into light" — the verse's light: the darknesses (the plural) — the doubts, the falsehoods, the disbelief — the light (the singular) — the truth. Ibn Kathir: He brings them out from darknesses into light — the light of the truth is one; the darknesses of the falsehood are many. Ma'arif: Allah mentioned the light in the singular and the darkness in the plural, because truth is one, while disbelief comes as several types, all of which are false. The verse's frame: the several dark exits and the single light's entrance.

"As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness" — the verse's reversal: the taghut — the false deities, the false leaders — the awliya' — the guardians — the Shaitan and his allies. Ibn Kathir: their Awliya' (supporters and helpers) are Taghut (false deities and false leaders) — they bring them out from light into darknesses. The verse's frame: the fitrah's light — the children enter the world on the light of the nature — and the taghut's guardians lead them into the darkness of the false.

"It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever" — the verse's close: the dwellers of the Fire — the khulud — the eternity. Ibn Kathir: those are the dwellers of the Fire, and they will abide therein forever.

The verse's lesson: the belief makes Allah the Guardian; the disbelief makes the false gods the leaders — the light's path and the darkness's path — and the destinations: the eternity of the Fire and the light of the believers. And the reader's part: the guardianship is the choice's fruit — Allah's guardianship for the believers, the taghut's path for the disbelievers.

## Verse 258

Verse 258 says: *"Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship? ˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death." He argued, "I too have the power to give life and cause death." Abraham challenged ˹him˺, "Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west." And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people."*

In simple terms, the verse tells of the argument with Abraham: the king granted the kingdom argued that he too gives life and death; Abraham challenged him with the sun from the east — make it rise from the west — and the disbeliever was dumbstruck.

"The one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship" — the verse's arguer: the king — Nimrod. Ibn Kathir: the king who disputed with Ibrahim was King Nimrod, son of Canaan, son of Kush, son of Sam, son of Noah, as Mujahid stated — it was also said he was Nimrod, son of Falikh, son of 'Abir, son of Shalikh, son of Arfakhshand, son of Sam, son of Noah. Mujahid: the kings who ruled the eastern and western parts of the world are four — two believers and two disbelievers — the believers: Sulayman bin Dawud and Dhul-Qarnayn; the disbelievers: Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar. The verse's frame: the kingship — the grant of Allah — used against the Lord: the "because" — the granted king argued about the Grantor.

"When Abraham said, "My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death"" — the verse's first proof: the life and the death — the Lord's authority. Ibn Kathir: it appears that Nimrod asked Ibrahim to produce proof that Allah exists — Ibrahim replied: "My Lord is He Who gives life and causes death."

"He argued, "I too have the power to give life and cause death"" — the verse's reply: the empty claim. Ibn Kathir: Nimrod arrogantly and defiantly claimed that he was the creator and pretended that it was he who brings life and death — later on Fir'awn imitated him and announced (28:38): "I know not that you have a god other than me." Al-Jalalayn (in the classical notes): he said "I give life" if he pardoned the man condemned, and "I cause death" if he killed him. The verse's frame: the claiming of the Lord's mark — the kingship's delusion.

"Abraham challenged ˹him˺, "Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west."" — the verse's second proof: the sun's course. Ibn Kathir: "You claim that it is you who brings life and death. He who brings life and death controls the existence and creates whatever is in it, including controlling its planets and their movements. For instance, the sun rises every day from the east. Therefore, if you were a god, as you claimed, bringing life and death, then bring the sun from the west." The verse's frame: the argument of the common sign: the sun — the daily miracle — the challenge: the reversal of the known course.

"And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck" — the verse's defeat: the futiha — the stunned, the defeated — the idle, the silent. Ibn Kathir: since the king was aware of his weakness and inadequacy and that he was not able to reply to Ibrahim's request, he was idle, silent and unable to comment — the proof was established against him.

"And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people" — the verse's close: the wrongdoers — the zalimin — even after the proof: the clear proof, and the not-guiding: the guidance is the heart's opening — the one who insists on the wrong is not guided. Ibn Kathir: Allah guides not the people who are wrongdoers.

The verse's occasion (As-Suddi): the debate between Ibrahim and Nimrod occurred after Ibrahim was thrown into the fire — Ibrahim did not meet the king before that day. The verse's frame: the fire's survivor confronting the fire's tyrant with the sun's course.

The verse's lesson: the kingship is Allah's grant; the world's signs are Allah's proofs; the arguer is dumbstruck, and the insistence on the wrong blocks the guidance. And the reader's part: the common signs — the sun, the life, the death — are the testimonies against the claims of the lords besides Allah.

## Verse 259

Verse 259 says: *"Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, "How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?" So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life. Allah asked, "How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?" He replied, "Perhaps a day or part of a day." Allah said, "No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink— they have not spoiled. ˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity. And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!" "Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins" — the verse's passer: the man of the ruined city. Ibn Kathir (and the classical notes): the one who passed by the city — the city in ruins — its walls fallen, its people gone — the city whose falling is the destruction — he wondered. The classical identification: 'Uzayr (Ezra) — per most of the commentators — the one who passed by Bayt al-Maqdis (Jerusalem) after its destruction. The verse's frame: the ruins of the city and the question of the revival: how could the ruins be alive again?

"He wondered, "How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?"" — the verse's wonder: the question of the doubt? — or the question of the longing? Ibn Kathir: he wondered — the question of the astonished, not the denier: the heart's question about the how of the resurrection. The verse's frame: the wonder about the how, and the divine answer: the showing.

"So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life" — the verse's death and life: the hundred years. Ibn Kathir: Allah caused him to die for a hundred years — the night and the day of the long sleep — then raised him. "Allah asked, "How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?" "˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity" — the verse's second sign: the donkey — the bones — and the man: the sign for humanity. "When this was made clear to him, he declared, "˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything"" — the verse's close: the affirmation: "I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything" — after the sight, the knowledge. Ibn Kathir: the proof was established — he declared the knowledge: the capability over everything.

The verse's lesson: the wonder's question is answered by the sight; the hundred years and the preserved food and the gathered bones — the resurrection's evidence. And the reader's part: the ruins' question is the resurrection's preface — the answer is the died-and-lived: Allah is Most Capable of everything.

## Verse 260

Verse 260 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead." Allah responded, "Do you not believe?" Abraham replied, "Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured." Allah said, "Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops. Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste. And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.""*

In simple terms, the verse tells of Abraham's request: show me how You give life to the dead — for the heart's reassurance — and Allah commanded the four birds: cut them, scatter them on the hilltops, call them — and they came in haste — the demonstration of the Almighty, the All-Wise.

"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead"" — the verse's request: the show — the how of the resurrection. Ibn Kathir: Ibrahim said: "My Lord! Show me how You give life to the dead" — the request of the eye-witnessing — the heart's addition to the faith. The verse's frame: the request of the knowing: not the doubt's question but the certainty's long.

"Allah responded, "Do you not believe?"" — the verse's question: the divine check: do you not believe? Ibn Kathir: "Do you not believe?" — the correction of the request's appearance.

"Abraham replied, "Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured"" — the verse's answer: the belief — and the reassurance: *li-yatma'inna qalbi* — the heart's tranquility — the eye's sight added to the mind's certainty. Ibn Kathir: "Yes (I believe), but to be stronger in faith" — the heart's rest. The verse's frame: the belief is present; the request is the heart's calm — the faith's deepening: the eye-witness above the report.

"Allah said, "Then bring four birds, train them to come to you"" — the verse's command: the four birds — the taming, the inclining them to himself. Ibn Kathir: "Take four birds, then cause them to incline towards you" — the scholars of tafsir disagreed over the type of birds — the Qur'an did not mention it. Al-Jalalayn: a peacock, an eagle, a raven and a cock; other notes: a cockerel, a raven, a duck and a peacock; Ibn 'Abbas (in the classical tradition): four kinds — the peacock, the rooster, the pigeon, the crow. The verse's frame: the species are not the point; the command is the test: the train them — the familiar call — the return of the known.

"˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops" — the verse's cutting and scattering. Ibn Kathir: "fasarhunna ilayka" — the cutting to pieces — the explanation of Ibn 'Abbas, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Abu Malik, Abu al-Aswad ad-Dili, Wahb bin Munabbih, al-Hasan and As-Suddi. Ibrahim caught four birds, slaughtered them, removed the feathers, tore them to pieces and mixed the pieces together — he placed parts on four or seven hills, and kept the heads in his hand (Ibn 'Abbas). The verse's frame: the scattering and the mixing: the death's dispersion, the resurrection's reassembly.

"Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste" — the verse's call and the flight. Ibn Kathir (from Ibn 'Abbas): Allah commanded Ibrahim to call the birds; he did; Ibrahim witnessed the feathers, blood and flesh fly to each other, the parts flew each to their bodies, until every bird came back to life and came walking at a fast pace towards him — each bird came to collect its head from Ibrahim's hand; if he gave the bird another head, the bird refused; when he gave each bird its own head, the head was placed on the body by Allah's leave and power. The verse's frame: the call — the known voice — and the fly: the resurrection at the command.

"And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise" — the verse's close: the Almighty — over the resurrection — the All-Wise — in the demonstration. Ibn Kathir: "And know that Allah is All-Mighty, All-Wise" — no one can overwhelm or resist Him. The verse's frame: the might of the command and the wisdom of the sign.

The verse's lesson: the heart's reassurance is granted; the belief's deepening is the divine gift; the scattered birds are the resurrection's demonstration. And the reader's part: even the prophets' hearts long for the sight — the eye-witness of the resurrection is the faith's rest — and Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.
