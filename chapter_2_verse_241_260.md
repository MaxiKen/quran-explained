## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:241

> And for divorced women there is a suitable provision—a duty upon the mindful.

**Commentary:**

### A General Right of Kind Treatment

After detailing dowries, waiting periods, reconciliation, and widowhood, the Qur’an gives a broad ethical command concerning divorced women: they are entitled to a suitable provision—*matāʿ bi-l-maʿrūf*. The verse reminds the community that divorce does not erase the woman’s dignity or leave her responsible for all the financial and emotional costs of a marriage’s end.

The provision can include a compensatory gift, maintenance owed during the relevant waiting period, housing, clothing, and practical support according to the circumstances and the specific legal category of the divorce. The detailed obligations differ depending on whether the marriage was consummated, whether a dowry was specified, whether the divorce is revocable, and whether there are children. A qualified scholar and legal adviser should examine the case rather than applying one amount to every woman.

### Maʿrūf and Ability

*Maʿrūf* means what is fair, recognized as good, and consistent with revelation and sound custom. A wealthy man should not offer a token gift while keeping wealth for himself, and a poor man is not required to give what would destroy his ability to survive or support dependants. Fairness considers the woman’s needs, the husband’s means, the length of the marriage, and the rights already established by contract and law.

The command is not a payment for the woman’s silence. It does not allow a husband to purchase permission to insult, abuse, or conceal wrongdoing. It is an expression of responsibility as the relationship ends. If the couple have children, support for the children remains a separate obligation and must not be counted as a favour to the mother.

### Divorce Does Not Cancel Humanity

A marriage may end, but the former spouses remain human beings with rights. The husband must not expose private matters, manipulate children, withhold documents, or use financial dependence to continue control. The wife must also avoid false claims and unlawful seizure, but the presence of conflict never removes the moral requirement of justice.

The Prophet ﷺ said that the best people are those best in character to their families. That character is tested not only while affection is strong but when disappointment and resentment appear. A dignified provision can prevent poverty, reduce hostility, and help a woman rebuild her life without being treated as discarded property.

### A Duty upon the Mindful

The verse calls the provision a duty upon the *muttaqūn*. Taqwā makes a person fulfil rights even when a court cannot compel him and when relatives will praise him for doing less. It also prevents the recipient from turning a lawful claim into revenge or public humiliation. Both parties remember that Allah sees the settlement and knows whether it was voluntary, fair, and sufficient.

The mindful person settles accounts transparently, pays what is due promptly, and does not force the other spouse to beg. He may give more than the minimum because he remembers the years of shared life and the favour that once existed. Generosity is especially valuable when the marriage ends without a clear legal remedy for every loss.

### Continuity with Earlier Verses

This command echoes 2:236, where a woman divorced before consummation and without a fixed dowry is given a suitable gift, and 2:237, where generosity is called closer to taqwā. It also completes the warning in 2:231 against retaining a woman to harm her. The Qur’an’s family law repeatedly joins legal precision with ethical excellence.

The later verses of Sūrah al-Ṭalāq explain maintenance and housing according to ability. These passages should be read together. A Muslim cannot cite one verse to minimize a right while ignoring the wider Qur’anic requirement of kindness.

### Community Support

Sometimes a former husband cannot provide enough, or he has died, disappeared, or become abusive. The community, relatives, zakāh institutions, and lawful public systems should help divorced women and children. The Qur’anic ethic is not satisfied by telling a vulnerable woman to be patient while those with means refuse support.

Social support should preserve dignity. It can include housing, legal assistance, education, childcare, counselling, and protection from harassment. The aim is to restore independence rather than create a new form of dependence.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:236–242 on provision, divorce, family rights, and gracious treatment.
- Qur’an 4:19, 4:20, and 65:6–7 on kindness, maintenance, housing, and ability according to means.
- Qur’an 2:231 and 2:237 on not harming a divorced woman and not forgetting graciousness.
- Qur’an 24:22 and 59:9 on forgiveness, generosity, and caring for vulnerable people.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the Prophet’s family conduct, maintenance, and the virtue of caring for widows and the poor.
- For *mutʿah*, *maʿrūf*, *taqwā*, and the general provision due to divorced women, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:241.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:242

> Thus Allah makes His revelations clear to you so that you may understand.

**Commentary:**

### Clarity in Family Law

This verse concludes the connected passage on divorce, waiting periods, dowry, maintenance, breastfeeding, widowhood, and fairness. Allah says that He makes His revelations clear—*yubayyinu Allāhu lakum āyātihi*—because family rights are too important to be left to anger, custom, or the power of the strongest person. Clear guidance protects people when emotions make judgement difficult.

The Qur’an’s detail is itself a mercy. It addresses situations that families may prefer to avoid discussing: separation before consummation, a widow’s waiting period, breastfeeding, financial support, reconciliation, and the finality of divorce. By bringing these matters into revelation, Allah gives vulnerable people language and rights that private customs might deny them.

### Understanding Requires Reflection

The purpose is “so that you may understand”—*taʿqilūn*. Understanding is more than memorizing legal phrases. It means recognizing the wisdom, limits, purposes, and moral consequences of the rulings. A person who knows the waiting period but uses it to harass his former wife has not understood. A person who knows the maintenance rule but refuses to support children has missed the command’s purpose.

The Qur’anic *ʿaql* connects evidence with action. It asks how a rule protects dignity, prevents harm, and directs people toward taqwā. It also requires humility: complex cases should be referred to qualified scholars and legal authorities rather than settled by personal confidence.

### Clarity Does Not Mean Simplicity in Every Case

Allah’s revelations are clear in their principles, but applying them can require knowledge. Menstrual cycles may be irregular, a divorce may be uttered in anger, assets may be disputed, and parents may disagree about a child’s care. Clear revelation does not remove the need for careful interpretation, evidence, consultation, and due process.

The believer distinguishes a clear command from a difficult application. He does not claim that every question has a one-line answer, and he does not use complexity to avoid an obvious duty. The legal schools developed detailed methods precisely because they respected the text’s clarity and the diversity of circumstances.

### Law and Mercy Together

The preceding laws contain boundaries, but they also contain concessions and opportunities for generosity. Divorce is limited, yet reconciliation is possible. Dowry is protected, yet voluntary waiver is praised. Breastfeeding has a suggested period, yet parents may wean through consultation. Widowhood has a defined waiting period, yet the woman retains agency afterward.

Understanding means seeing these provisions together. A person should not select the harshest interpretation when Allah has provided ease, nor the easiest interpretation when it destroys another’s rights. Justice and mercy are not opposing values in the Qur’an.

### A Warning against Customary Injustice

Human customs can be useful when consistent with revelation, but they can also make oppression appear normal. A family may treat a widow as property, force a divorced woman to surrender her dowry, or prevent remarriage to preserve social status. The Qur’an makes its guidance clear so that people can measure custom by justice rather than justice by custom.

The same applies to modern practices. Legal paperwork, family courts, and social expectations should be used to protect rights, not to hide wealth or exclude the vulnerable. The person of understanding asks whether an arrangement fulfils Allah’s purpose.

### Understanding and Accountability

The verse makes knowledge a responsibility. A person who understands the rule must apply it honestly and teach it accurately. He should not use selective quotations to win a family dispute. If he discovers that he misunderstood, he corrects himself and repairs the harm.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the best people are those who learn the Qur’an and teach it, and he demonstrated that teaching includes practical mercy. Knowledge is a trust, especially when it affects marriage, children, property, and personal safety.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:228–242 on detailed family law and the need for clear understanding.
- Qur’an 4:35 and 65:1–7 on mediation, procedure, housing, and maintenance.
- Qur’an 16:43 and 17:36 on asking qualified people and avoiding claims without knowledge.
- Qur’an 3:7 and 39:18 on reflecting upon revelation and following the best of it.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, the hadith on learning and teaching the Qur’an; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on family rights, justice, and consulting qualified people.
- For *bayān*, *āyāt*, *ʿaql*, and the relation between legal clarity and moral understanding, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:242.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:243

> Have you not considered those who left their homes in the thousands, fearing death? Allah said to them, “Die,” then He restored them to life. Surely Allah is full of bounty to people, but most people are ungrateful.

**Commentary:**

### A Community Fleeing Death

The verse recalls a large group who left their homes in fear of death. The Qur’an does not identify the town or give every historical detail. Classical exegetes transmitted reports connecting the event to a plague or another deadly danger among Banū Isrāʾīl, but those reports differ in detail. What is certain from the revelation is that thousands fled, Allah caused them to die, and then restored them to life as a sign of His power.

The story is not a condemnation of every attempt to escape danger. Islam permits seeking safety, treatment, shelter, and protection. The issue is the heart’s assumption that flight or worldly means can escape Allah’s decree. A person takes means while knowing that life and death remain in Allah’s hand.

### Life and Death Belong to Allah

The command “Die” and the restoration to life display Allah’s absolute authority. Human beings may plan, build walls, travel, develop medicine, and protect themselves, but none can guarantee life. The verse prepares the reader for the following command to fight in Allah’s cause and to spend for the community: fear of death must not become a reason to abandon every duty.

At the same time, the passage does not glorify death or demand reckless exposure. The Prophet ﷺ taught people to avoid plague-stricken areas and not to enter them, while one already inside should not flee, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This shows that avoiding contagion and taking precautions are compatible with belief in decree.

### Resurrection Is Possible

The restoration of the dead is a sign of the resurrection. The One who returned life to this group can raise all humanity after death. The event is also a direct lesson in Allah’s ability to reverse what people consider final. Death is not a barrier to divine power, and the Hereafter is not an irrational idea.

The Qur’an tells such stories not to satisfy curiosity about supernatural spectacle but to establish accountability. If Allah can restore life, then people will return to Him and answer for fear, courage, gratitude, and the use of their life.

### Fear and Responsibility

Fear of death is natural. A believer may feel fear and still act courageously. Courage is not the absence of the survival instinct; it is refusing to let fear cancel an obligation when lawful protection of others requires action. The story warns against collective cowardice that leaves a community defenceless or abandons the vulnerable.

The following verses call for spending and speak of the leaders of Banū Isrāʾīl seeking a king to fight. The sequence suggests that life must be used in obedience and service, not merely preserved for its own sake. A long life wasted in fear is not automatically better than a shorter life lived with faith and benefit.

### Divine Bounty and Ingratitude

“Allah is full of bounty to people.” Life itself is a bounty, and restoration is a further sign. Guidance, health, safety, family, knowledge, and opportunities to serve are also gifts. Ingratitude—*kufr al-niʿmah*—occurs when people receive these gifts but attribute them solely to themselves or use them against Allah’s purposes.

The verse says most people are ungrateful, reminding believers that gratitude cannot be assumed from survival. A person who escapes a disease should not merely return to old negligence. He thanks Allah through obedience, care for others, and responsible use of renewed opportunity.

### Avoiding Superstition and False Certainty

Because the Qur’an leaves the town unnamed, believers should not build doctrine on uncertain details. The story’s lesson remains valid without knowing whether the event involved a particular plague, town, or tribe. This is a Qur’anic discipline: take the guidance that revelation confirms, and avoid turning transmitted possibilities into certainty.

The same restraint applies to contemporary disasters. No person should confidently declare that a particular victim was punished or that a catastrophe has one hidden meaning known only to him. Allah knows the wisdom of events. People respond with repentance, practical care, and compassion.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:243–252 on life, death, spending, leadership, and the struggle of Banū Isrāʾīl.
- Qur’an 3:145 and 3:154–156 on death by Allah’s permission and not fleeing from decree.
- Qur’an 40:68 and 75:36–40 on Allah’s power to give life and restore the dead.
- Qur’an 64:15 and 67:2 on life as a test and wealth and family as trials.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on plague quarantine, which demonstrates both reliance on Allah and taking protective means.
- For the identity of the people, the meaning of “thousands,” their death and restoration, and the limits of historical reports, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:243.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:244

> Fight in Allah’s cause, and know that Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### Courage after the Lesson of Death

After recalling the people who fled in fear of death, the Qur’an commands the believers to fight in Allah’s cause. The verse is a call to courage when defence and the protection of the community are required. It does not praise violence for its own sake or command individuals to attack people who have not attacked them. Its meaning is governed by the limits already given: fight those who fight, do not transgress, accept cessation, and keep the objective of ending persecution (2:190–193).

The story of those who fled death teaches that preserving life is not the only human duty. If fear makes a community abandon every responsibility, the vulnerable may be left to aggressors. Courage means taking a lawful risk to protect others when there is a reasonable duty and capacity to do so.

### “In Allah’s Cause”

*Fī sabīl Allāh* refers to striving for a cause approved by Allah. A conflict motivated by revenge, tribal pride, conquest, wealth, or personal ambition cannot become holy merely through religious language. The cause must be just, the authority legitimate, the means proportionate, and the conduct within the revealed limits.

The broader meaning of striving includes resisting one’s own desires, seeking knowledge, supporting the oppressed, spending for public welfare, and speaking truth to power. Armed fighting is a restricted form that requires conditions and cannot be separated from the Qur’an’s protection of life and its prohibition of transgression.

### Allah Hears and Knows

Allah is *Samīʿ* and *ʿAlīm*. He hears the leader’s call, the soldier’s fear, the victim’s cry, and the peace proposal that a powerful person tries to conceal. He knows whether the fighter seeks Allah’s pleasure or fame, whether a commander is protecting civilians or using a cause for ambition, and whether a person is refusing duty out of wisdom or cowardice.

This ending prevents both hypocrisy and despair. A person may appear brave to people while being corrupt before Allah, and a frightened person may possess great sincerity. Divine judgement reaches the hidden intention and the actual conduct.

### Authority and Discipline

Fighting is not an individual’s private decision. It requires a legitimate community or state authority, consultation, evidence, and a clear objective. The chaos of self-appointed groups is itself destructive. Individuals who declare others unbelievers and attack civilians violate the command rather than applying it.

The community must also provide discipline and accountability. Orders that violate Allah’s limits cannot be obeyed, and commanders must investigate abuses. Those who kill non-combatants, torture prisoners, betray safe conduct, or destroy without necessity are transgressors even if they claim a righteous cause.

### Fear Does Not Excuse Injustice

The verse does not demand that every person fight physically. Capacity, skill, health, family responsibilities, and the nature of the threat matter. A person may support a just defence through medical care, food, shelter, communication, diplomacy, or financial assistance. The obligation is applied according to ability and lawful authority.

Nor does fear justify abandoning people who depend upon one’s protection. A person may feel afraid and still take the next responsible step. Prayer, consultation, training, and solidarity help turn fear into disciplined courage.

### The Moral Aim of Defence

The aim is to restore safety, freedom from persecution, and the ability to worship Allah. When that aim is achieved, fighting must stop. The Prophet ﷺ accepted treaties and safe conduct and commanded mercy to non-combatants. Victory over an enemy does not give permission to humiliate or destroy his family.

The believer remembers that all parties will return to Allah. Fighting may be necessary, but it is never outside moral accountability. The one who begins a war for ego and the one who restrains himself for Allah are not equal, even if both claim courage.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:190–194 and 2:243–244 on fear, defence, limits, and Allah’s knowledge of intention.
- Qur’an 4:71–77 and 8:60–61 on preparation, defence, peace, and the protection of the oppressed.
- Qur’an 22:39–40 on permission to fight those who are attacked and expelled.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 60:8–9 on justice toward enemies and peaceful non-Muslims.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on lawful defence, obedience within what is right, and the prohibition of killing non-combatants and violating treaties.
- For *qitāl fī sabīl Allāh*, *samʿ*, *ʿilm*, intention, and lawful authority, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:244.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:245

> Who is it that will lend Allah a good loan so that He may multiply it for him many times? Allah withholds and gives abundantly, and to Him you will be returned.

**Commentary:**

### The Metaphor of a Loan

Allah is free of all need, yet He calls charitable spending a “good loan”—*qarḍ ḥasan*. The metaphor honours the giver by promising that what is offered for Allah’s sake will be returned with multiplication. A loan normally expects repayment, but Allah does not need the servant’s wealth. The expression teaches that charity is secure with Him and that no sincere gift is lost.

A *qarḍ ḥasan* is given from lawful wealth, with sincere intention, without humiliating the recipient, and without demanding an unfair return. It can include financial support, food, education, medical care, shelter, and resources for a just public cause. The “goodness” lies in the source, intention, method, and benefit.

### Multiplication without Calculation

Allah multiplies the reward many times, as explained in 2:261: a seed produces seven ears, each with a hundred grains, and Allah multiplies for whom He wills. Multiplication may appear as blessing in the giver’s wealth, forgiveness, protection, guidance, benefit to descendants, or reward in the Hereafter. The servant does not dictate the form of repayment; he trusts Allah’s wisdom.

The promise is not a guarantee that every donation produces immediate worldly profit. A person may give and remain poor, but his charity can still be accepted and multiplied beyond worldly measurement. The verse protects generosity from the market’s narrow calculation.

### Allah Gives and Withholds

“Allah withholds and gives abundantly”—*yaqbiḍu wa-yabsuṭu*—reminds people that provision is in His hand. Wealth may expand or contract as a test. The person who gives does not lose control of his future because Allah can replace what he spends. The person who withholds should not assume that hoarding guarantees safety.

This does not deny human economic responsibility. A person budgets, supports dependants, repays debts, and invests lawfully. The verse teaches that planning should not become an excuse for miserliness or the belief that wealth is independent of Allah.

### Spending in Allah’s Cause

The immediate context includes preparation for a just struggle and support for the community. Spending may therefore include equipment, food, medical care, refuge, and assistance to families affected by conflict. It also includes the broad charitable purposes named throughout the Qur’an. The cause must be lawful; donations to terrorism, aggression, corruption, or the abuse of civilians are not a *qarḍ ḥasan*.

Institutions receiving funds must be transparent. A charity cannot invoke Allah’s promise to hide fraud, excessive salaries, or political manipulation. Trustees are responsible for directing money to its stated purpose.

### Charity and the Purification of the Heart

Wealth can become a rival to Allah when a person loves it more than truth and refuses every claim upon it. Spending breaks the illusion of absolute ownership. The giver says through action that money is a trust, not a master. This is why charity is called purification in 9:103.

The recipient also has dignity. A gift should not be accompanied by reminders, insults, public photography without consent, or conditions that create dependence. The Qur’an warns that injury can cancel the reward of charity (2:264). The best giver seeks Allah’s acceptance, not the recipient’s humiliation or the audience’s applause.

### Return to Allah

“To Him you will be returned” gives the metaphor its final seriousness. The wealth left behind will not accompany a person, but its use will. Every coin spent, withheld, earned, or taken unjustly returns to the account before Allah. The wise investor sends a portion ahead through charity and righteous support.

The return also relativizes temporary scarcity. A person may feel that generosity makes him vulnerable, but Allah controls the final balance. The believer gives within ability, asks Allah for blessing, and avoids both waste and fear-driven hoarding.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:245 and 2:261–274 on the good loan, multiplication, charity, and the ethics of giving.
- Qur’an 57:11 and 64:17 on lending Allah a good loan and Allah’s multiplication of reward.
- Qur’an 9:103 on charity as purification and 59:9 on preferring others.
- Qur’an 2:264–267 on sincerity, lawful wealth, and not harming recipients.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that charity does not decrease wealth and the hadith on the seven categories of people shaded by Allah’s mercy, including hidden charity.
- For *qarḍ ḥasan*, *yaqbiḍ*, *yabsuṭ*, multiplication, and return to Allah, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:245.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:246

> Have you not considered the chiefs of the Children of Israel after Moses? They said to a prophet of theirs, “Appoint for us a king so that we may fight in Allah’s cause.” He said, “Would you perhaps refrain from fighting if it were prescribed for you?” They said, “Why should we not fight in Allah’s cause when we have been driven from our homes and separated from our children?” But when fighting was prescribed for them, they turned away, except for a few. Allah knows the wrongdoers.

**Commentary:**

### A Community Seeking Leadership

This story follows the account of a people who feared death and the command to spend and strive. The chiefs of Banū Isrāʾīl asked a prophet to appoint a king so they could fight in Allah’s cause. Their request sounded courageous, but the prophet tested the sincerity behind their words: would they still fight when the command brought real danger, sacrifice, and discipline?

The question is not cynicism. It is prophetic knowledge of human weakness. People often ask for a grand opportunity while imagining that it will cost little. A person may demand change, justice, or victory in the abstract, then retreat when truth requires money, time, risk, or the loss of status.

### Displacement and the Desire for Justice

They cited their expulsion from their homes and separation from their children as reasons to fight. Their grief was real, and defending the oppressed can be a righteous duty. The Qur’an does not shame those who want to return home or protect families. It asks whether their stated motive will remain firm when the path becomes difficult.

The story also shows that fighting is not an individual adventure. The community seeks a king and accepts organized leadership. A just defence requires coordination, discipline, resources, and an authority capable of making decisions and protecting civilians. Private factions that act without accountability create the very disorder the community is trying to end.

### The Failure of Words without Commitment

When fighting was prescribed, most turned away. Their problem was not lack of information; the command had become clear. Their speech had outrun their courage. This is a recurring Qur’anic lesson: claims are tested by action. A person may speak about sacrifice, but Allah knows who remains faithful when comfort is threatened.

The verse does not imply that every person who refuses combat is a wrongdoer. Legitimate exemptions, inability, illness, family obligations, and the absence of lawful authority matter. The criticism concerns those who demanded the command and then abandoned it out of hypocrisy, cowardice, or attachment to worldly safety.

### Leadership and Obedience

The request for a king shows that leadership is necessary for collective action, but it also raises the question of whom the community should follow. A leader must possess the knowledge, character, ability, and trustworthiness required by the task. Popularity, wealth, lineage, and loud promises are not enough.

The community’s obedience is also bounded. It obeys a leader in what is right and refuses a command that violates Allah’s limits. The prophet in the story does not flatter the people; he exposes the gap between their words and their likely conduct. Good leadership tells the truth before asking for sacrifice.

### The Few Who Remained

Only a few remained steadfast. The Qur’an often shows that numbers do not determine truth. A small group with sincerity can withstand a larger group whose commitment is shallow. This does not glorify isolation or contempt for the majority. It encourages believers not to abandon a righteous duty merely because support is limited.

The few must still act wisely. Sincerity without planning is not enough. They need consultation, preparation, lawful authority, and care for those who depend upon them. Allah’s help is sought through both faith and responsible means.

### Allah Knows the Wrongdoers

The closing statement returns judgement to Allah. He knows which people turned away because of legitimate inability and which chose betrayal after demanding the command. Human observers see only part of the situation. This warning also prevents leaders from labelling every reluctant person a hypocrite without understanding his circumstances.

Allah’s knowledge covers the heart, the promise, the fear, the capacity, and the deed. The believer asks Allah for honesty before making a public commitment and for courage after the commitment has been made.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:243–246 on fear, life, spending, leadership, and the test of commitment.
- Qur’an 4:75 and 8:72 on defending the oppressed and supporting those driven from their homes.
- Qur’an 4:59 and 5:55–57 on obedience, leadership, and returning disputes to Allah and His Messenger.
- Qur’an 61:2–3 on saying what one does not do and the seriousness of broken commitment.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on leadership, obedience in what is right, and the danger of making claims without fulfilling them.
- For the historical setting after Mūsā, the chiefs’ request, the testing of intention, and the few who remained, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:246.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:247

> Their prophet said to them, “Allah has appointed Saul as your king.” They said, “How can he have kingship over us when we are more entitled to kingship than he is, and he has not been given abundant wealth?” He said, “Allah has chosen him over you and has increased him greatly in knowledge and physical strength. Allah grants His authority to whom He wills. Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.”

**Commentary:**

### Leadership Is a Trust from Allah

The prophet announces that Allah appointed Ṭālūt, known in the biblical tradition as Saul, as king. The people objected because he did not belong to the lineage they expected and did not possess abundant wealth. Their objection exposes a common assumption: that leadership belongs to the richest family, the most prestigious tribe, or the person who claims the highest status.

The verse does not provide a simple political formula for every historical society. It teaches that authority is a trust and that the qualifications for it must be judged by justice, knowledge, ability, and suitability—not merely by wealth or inherited prestige. In this story, the prophet’s declaration establishes Ṭālūt’s appointment, and the people are tested in whether they will accept a qualified leader rather than their preferred candidate.

### Knowledge and Physical Capacity

Allah increased Ṭālūt in knowledge and physical strength—*basṭatan fī al-ʿilm wa-l-jism*. Knowledge includes strategic judgement, understanding the mission, leadership wisdom, and the ability to distinguish truth from deception. Physical strength includes health, endurance, courage, and the ability to carry the responsibilities of command.

The verse is not praising appearance for its own sake. A beautiful or wealthy person is not automatically qualified. Nor does physical disability disqualify a person from leadership when the relevant role does not require particular physical abilities. The criterion is capacity for the actual trust, combined with knowledge and moral integrity.

### Wealth Is Not the Main Qualification

The people asked how Ṭālūt could rule when he had not been given abundant wealth. The response corrects the belief that money proves wisdom or entitlement. Wealth can support administration, but it can also corrupt judgement, attract flatterers, and make a leader dependent upon the interests of the rich.

A leader should be provided lawful resources to fulfil the office, but personal abundance is not the same as public competence. The poor should not be excluded from responsibility merely because they lack inherited wealth, and the wealthy should not assume that their property gives them a right to rule.

### Allah’s Choice and Human Consultation

“Allah has chosen him over you” refers to the particular divine appointment in this narrative. It does not give every ruler permission to claim that his power is directly chosen by Allah and therefore beyond criticism. Human leaders remain accountable, and the Qur’an commands justice, consultation, and fulfilment of trusts (4:58; 42:38).

A community must examine qualifications and hold its leaders accountable. The story teaches the people to overcome selfish criteria, not to surrender all discernment. In other contexts, Muslims select leaders through consultation and lawful public processes, while every authority is bound by Allah’s moral limits.

### The Test of Obedience

The people had asked for a king to organize the defence of their homes, but they resisted the prophet’s choice because of status and wealth. The test reveals that they wanted victory on their own terms. True obedience accepts a qualified leader even when he is not socially impressive, provided the appointment is lawful and the leader acts within justice.

The following verse gives a sign confirming Ṭālūt’s kingship, and the later verses test whether the people will obey his command at the river. Leadership is therefore established through evidence and then tested through practice.

### Allah’s Vastness and Knowledge

Allah is *Wāsiʿ*, All-Encompassing, and *ʿAlīm*, All-Knowing. His gifts are not limited by the narrow criteria people use. He knows which person can bear a trust, what a community needs, and what hidden consequences follow a choice. His vastness also means that leadership is not a scarce honour that must be monopolized by one family.

A believer should ask Allah to appoint people of knowledge, strength, justice, and mercy, while avoiding envy when responsibility is given to someone else. Leadership is a burden before it is an honour.

### The Character of a Leader

Knowledge and strength require character. Without honesty, knowledge becomes manipulation; without mercy, strength becomes oppression. The Prophet ﷺ taught that authority is a trust and that a person who seeks it out of ambition may be left to himself. A leader should be chosen for service, not for the ability to collect praise.

Citizens and followers also have duties: obeying lawful commands, offering sincere counsel, refusing corruption, and not treating the leader as infallible. The story’s lesson concerns both the qualities of leadership and the heart’s response to it.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:248

> Their prophet said to them, “The sign of his kingship is that the Ark will come to you, carrying tranquillity from your Lord and relics left behind by the family of Moses and the family of Aaron, carried by the angels. Surely in that is a sign for you, if you are believers.”

**Commentary:**

### A Sign for a Doubting Community

The people had objected to Ṭālūt’s appointment because he lacked wealth and expected lineage. Their prophet then gave a sign: the *tābūt*, the Ark or chest associated with the Children of Israel, would return to them. The sign confirmed that the appointment was from Allah rather than a personal claim by Ṭālūt.

The Qur’an does not require believers to accept every leader who claims divine support. In this specific story, a prophet announced a sign from Allah, and the sign was part of the revealed test. Later communities must judge leadership through sound evidence, justice, consultation, and lawful process. No modern political object can simply be identified with the Ark without proof.

### The Ark and the Sacred Relics

The Ark carried relics from the family of Mūsā and Hārūn. Classical exegetes transmitted reports about the contents, including remnants of tablets, the staff of Mūsā, and other sacred items, but the Qur’an does not specify every object. The believer affirms what Allah stated and does not turn uncertain historical reports into creed.

The relics reminded Banū Isrāʾīl of revelation, prophetic guidance, and Allah’s past favour. Their value was not magical power independent of Allah. Sacred objects can serve as reminders when Allah gives them a role, but worship and ultimate reliance belong to Allah alone.

The Companions loved and preserved things connected with the Prophet ﷺ, and authentic hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim record their respect for his hair, water, and clothing. Such reports concern the exceptional blessing of the Prophet’s tangible relics and cannot be used to justify superstition, trafficking in alleged relics, or attributing independent power to objects.

### Sakīnah from the Lord

The Ark carried *sakīnah*, tranquillity and settled reassurance from Allah. *Sakīnah* is not merely a pleasant emotion. It is a divine gift that steadies the heart when fear, disagreement, and uncertainty are intense. The community needed more than a political symbol; it needed inner certainty that the defence ahead was under Allah’s care.

Allah sends tranquillity to believers in other situations, especially when hearts are tested (48:4, 18). It may appear as patience, clarity, unity, and courage rather than the removal of every danger. Ṭālūt’s army would still face a river and a formidable enemy, but sakīnah would help the sincere obey.

### Angels and the Unseen

The Ark was carried by angels. This is part of the unseen that Allah disclosed. The angels act by Allah’s command and do not possess independent power. Their presence turns the return of the Ark into a sign from the Lord, not a political recovery achieved only through human skill.

Muslims affirm the existence and work of angels without imagining their nature beyond revelation. The lesson is that Allah can support a community through means it does not expect. Human beings should use ordinary means while remaining open to the unseen wisdom of Allah.

### Belief Responds to Signs

The verse ends, “if you are believers.” A sign benefits the person who is willing to believe and obey. The same event can be dismissed by a person whose heart is committed to pride. The Israelites had asked for evidence, but the real test was whether they would submit after evidence arrived.

Believers should not demand miracles as a condition for every command. Allah has given the Qur’an, the prophetic model, creation, conscience, and historical signs. When a clear proof is established, the servant acts instead of continually moving the standard of evidence.

### Leadership, Memory, and Identity

The Ark connects present leadership with prophetic history. A community can receive a new leader without abandoning its past; it remembers the revelation and relics that shaped it. But memory must produce obedience, not nostalgia. The people needed to follow Ṭālūt in the present, not merely admire the history of Mūsā and Hārūn.

Muslims likewise honour the Prophet ﷺ and the early generations by following their faith and character rather than turning names, objects, or historical sites into substitutes for obedience.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:249

> When Saul set out with the army, he said, “Allah will test you with a river. Whoever drinks from it is not of me, and whoever does not taste it is of me—except one who takes only a handful. But they drank from it, except for a few. When he and those who believed with him crossed it, they said, “We have no strength today against Goliath and his soldiers.” Those who were certain that they would meet Allah said, “How often a small group has overcome a large group by Allah’s permission. Allah is with the patient.”

**Commentary:**

### The River as a Test

Ṭālūt’s army was tested by a river before facing Jālūt, known in the biblical tradition as Goliath. The command was simple: do not drink from the river except a permitted handful. The test was not about the water’s nutrition alone. It measured discipline, trust, and the ability to obey a clear instruction when thirst and fatigue made disobedience attractive.

A community preparing for a difficult responsibility needs more than numbers. It needs people who can restrain immediate appetite for a higher purpose. The river revealed that many who had promised to fight were not ready to govern their desires.

### “Not of Me”

“Whoever drinks from it is not of me” means that the disobedient person is not counted among those who follow Ṭālūt in this test and mission. It does not automatically mean that every drinker became an unbeliever or left Islam. Qur’anic and prophetic language can describe a person as not belonging to a group in obedience, excellence, or method without declaring him outside the faith.

The exception of the handful shows that the command was not irrational deprivation. A small amount was allowed to meet need. The test required restraint, not self-harm. This is a general principle: obedience is measured within the limits Allah gives, and lawful concessions should not be treated as weakness.

### The Majority Fails the Test

Most of the army drank, leaving only a few who obeyed. Numbers can create confidence, but they cannot replace sincerity. A large group may be vulnerable to panic, peer pressure, and immediate desire, while a small group may possess discipline and certainty.

The few who remained should not become arrogant. Their success was by Allah’s permission, and they still had to face fear. The story praises faithful perseverance, not contempt for people who struggled. In a community, those who are stronger should teach and support the weak rather than celebrate their failure.

### Fear after Crossing

Even after obeying and crossing the river, the believers said they lacked strength against Jālūt and his army. Obedience does not remove natural fear. A person may do the right thing and still feel inadequate before a large challenge. Faith does not require pretending to be invulnerable.

Their honest admission allows the people of certainty to answer with perspective. They do not deny the size of the enemy; they place it beneath Allah’s power. A small group can overcome a large group by Allah’s permission, but this is not a promise that every small force wins or a licence to ignore strategy. It is a call to trust while taking responsible means.

### Meeting Allah

Those certain that they will meet Allah possess *yaqīn*. Belief in the final meeting changes the scale of fear. The enemy may threaten the body, but Allah controls the final destiny. The person who remembers resurrection is less likely to betray truth for temporary safety, though he remains responsible for preserving life and avoiding reckless risk.

Certainty also produces accountability. Victory does not make a person righteous if he fought for pride, and defeat does not make a sincere person a failure. Allah judges intention, conduct, and patience.

### Allah Is with the Patient

The statement “Allah is with the patient” echoes 2:153. Divine companionship is support, guidance, and reward. It does not mean the patient never thirsts, fears, or loses companions. It means that endurance under obedience is seen and strengthened.

*Ṣabr* here includes restraint before the battle, courage after fear, and continuing to obey when the crowd has withdrawn. It also includes not insulting the majority or allowing victory to produce cruelty.

### Discipline and Leadership

Ṭālūt’s leadership required a test that exposed capacity. A commander must know the difference between a real need and uncontrolled indulgence. The people also needed to trust a lawful command rather than debate every instruction while the enemy approached.

In modern life, disciplined communities use training, clear procedures, nutrition, rest, and consultation. The river story is not a command to create arbitrary hardships for followers. Leaders who impose needless deprivation or test people for vanity are not imitating Ṭālūt’s wisdom.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:250

> When they faced Goliath and his soldiers, they said, “Our Lord, pour upon us patience, make our feet firm, and help us against the disbelieving people.”

**Commentary:**

### The Supplication of a Small Army

After the river test reduced the army and fear arose at the sight of Jālūt’s force, the believers turned to Allah. Their prayer contains three requests: an abundance of patience, firmness of feet, and help against the hostile force. They do not claim that their own courage is sufficient. They ask for the inner and outer means of steadfastness.

“Pour upon us patience”—*afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran*—is an image of patience filling and covering them. They know that they need more than a brief burst of courage. Fear, fatigue, grief, and uncertainty can return repeatedly. The servant asks Allah to make patience abundant enough for the full trial.

### Firm Feet

“Make our feet firm” refers literally to standing in battle and metaphorically to remaining firm in faith, discipline, and moral conduct. A person may have physical courage but lose his principles under pressure. The believers ask for both kinds of stability.

Firmness does not mean refusing to retreat when retreat is strategically necessary or remaining in a position that guarantees pointless destruction. It means not abandoning the lawful objective through panic, betrayal, or uncontrolled flight. Sound strategy can include movement, regrouping, negotiation, or withdrawal when these serve protection and justice.

### Help Belongs to Allah

“Help us against the disbelieving people” does not turn the enemy into a dehumanized category. The prayer concerns the hostile army that is actively confronting them. The Qur’an commands justice even toward opponents and forbids transgression. Victory is requested so that aggression may end and the community may worship safely, not so that hatred can be satisfied.

The believers understand that causes matter but are not independent. Training, numbers, leadership, weapons, and planning are means; Allah gives them effect. Reliance upon Him does not excuse neglecting preparation, and preparation does not make Allah unnecessary.

### Prayer before Action

The order of the scene is instructive. The believers do not pray as a substitute for action; they pray before and during responsible action. Supplication aligns the heart with Allah, purifies intention, and protects against arrogance. A person who asks Allah for help should also ask whether his cause, means, and conduct are just.

The Prophet ﷺ made supplication in battle, consulted his Companions, arranged the ranks, and took practical precautions. His example rejects both magical thinking and materialist self-reliance.

### Patience as a Collective Quality

The prayer is in the plural. A community under pressure needs shared patience. Individuals support one another by speaking truthfully, sharing resources, caring for the wounded, and avoiding rumours that spread panic. Leaders should not demand patience while hiding information or wasting lives through vanity.

The strongest person may need support, and the apparently weak person may give the word that steadies the group. The Qur’an praises believers who encourage one another to patience and mercy (90:17).

### A Supplication for Other Struggles

The prayer applies beyond armed conflict. A student facing examinations, a family enduring illness, a community resisting corruption, and a person leaving addiction can ask Allah for patience, firm feet, and help against the forces that pull him away from obedience. The “enemy” may include fear, desire, injustice, or Satan’s whispering, though the believer must not turn ordinary people into symbolic enemies without reason.

The supplication also teaches the correct order of priorities: seek patience before asking for victory. Without patience, success can be corrupted by pride; with patience, even a difficult result can become a means of reward.

- Qur’an 90:17 and 103:1–3 on encouraging one another to patience and truth.

- For *ṣabr*, *thabāt al-aqdām*, and the relationship between supplication and responsible action, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:250.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:251

> So they defeated them by Allah’s permission, and David killed Goliath. Allah gave David kingship and wisdom and taught him whatever He willed. If Allah did not repel some people by means of others, the earth would surely be corrupted. But Allah is full of bounty to all worlds.

**Commentary:**

### Victory by Allah’s Permission

The small believing group defeated the larger force “by Allah’s permission.” This phrase does not deny human effort. The believers obeyed, endured, planned, and fought under Ṭālūt’s leadership. It means that causes do not operate independently of Allah, and success is not proof that the victorious are entitled to pride or that every victorious cause was just.

The same principle protects believers after defeat. A sincere community may lose worldly battles without losing Allah’s favour. The measure is obedience, justice, and patience, not numbers or immediate results. Victory can be a test of gratitude and defeat a test of perseverance.

### David and Goliath

David—*Dāwūd*—killed Jālūt, the champion of the opposing army. The Qur’an mentions the decisive event without glorifying physical strength alone. David was part of a community under lawful leadership and a just cause. His victory illustrates how Allah can bring relief through a person whose apparent resources are small.

The Qur’an later describes David as a prophet, worshipper, judge, and leader. His story therefore continues beyond battlefield success. A person who defeats an enemy but cannot control his own ego has not reached the wisdom Allah gave David.

### Kingship and Wisdom

Allah gave David *mulk*, authority or kingship, and *ḥikmah*, wisdom. Authority without wisdom becomes oppression, while wisdom without the ability to protect people may be unable to restrain aggression. David’s example joins competence with moral judgement. He is later commanded to judge between people with truth and not follow desire (38:26).

This is an important leadership principle. A ruler, judge, or community leader needs knowledge of revelation, understanding of circumstances, courage, consultation, and self-restraint. The possession of power is not a sign of personal holiness. It becomes a trust measured by justice.

### Taught What Allah Willed

Allah taught David what He willed, including judgement, revelation, and skills useful for public welfare. The wording preserves humility: knowledge is granted according to Allah’s will, and no human being possesses all knowledge. A leader should continue learning and consult people with expertise rather than assuming that authority has made him infallible.

The Prophet ﷺ praised useful skills and lawful work. The Qur’an later describes David making armour, and authentic hadith say that he ate from the work of his own hands. A righteous leader can combine worship, craftsmanship, administration, and service.

### Repelling Corruption

“If Allah did not repel some people by means of others, the earth would be corrupted.” This is a general statement about Allah placing checks upon tyranny. He may protect the vulnerable through a just ruler, a courageous witness, a community organization, a court, or people who resist oppression. Without any restraint, powerful groups could consume the weak without consequence.

The verse does not make all conflict beneficial. Human beings must seek peaceful solutions when possible, defend the innocent, and avoid transgression. The “repelling” can include law, mediation, public accountability, and nonviolent resistance as well as lawful defence. The principle is the preservation of justice, not a celebration of violence.

### Power Must Serve Mercy

Allah’s bounty is “to all worlds”—*al-ʿālamīn*. His gifts include life, guidance, opportunities for reform, and the means by which oppression is checked. A victorious community should recognize that its power is a gift and use it to establish justice, protect minorities, feed the poor, and end revenge.

David’s later story contains warnings about judgement and desire. Even a prophet with authority must remain humble before Allah. Political success does not suspend accountability.

### The Meaning of True Victory

The real victory is not merely killing the enemy champion. It is preserving faith, protecting people, and developing a just society afterward. A community that wins a battle and then loots, enslaves, or oppresses has converted victory into corruption. The believer asks Allah for victory over the ego as well as protection from external aggression.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that true strength lies in controlling anger. This inner victory is essential for anyone given power over others.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:252

> These are the revelations of Allah We recite to you in truth, and surely you are among the messengers.

**Commentary:**

### History as Revelation

After recounting the story of the Children of Israel, Ṭālūt, David, and Jālūt, Allah declares that these are His revelations recited in truth. The account is not folklore assembled for entertainment or a political myth invented by the Prophet ﷺ. It is revelation that corrects, selects, and interprets history for moral guidance.

The Qur’an often tells earlier stories without providing every chronological detail. Its purpose is not to satisfy every historical curiosity but to show Allah’s power, the consequences of fear and arrogance, and the qualities required of a community. The omission of unnecessary detail preserves the lesson from being buried under trivia.

### “In Truth”

The phrase *bi-l-ḥaqq* means that the revelation is true in its source and faithful in its message. Allah knows what happened in the past, and He reveals what humanity needs to know. The story teaches that leadership is a trust, numbers do not guarantee victory, patience is essential, and Allah can restrain corruption through people who obey Him.

Truth also means that the stories are presented without tribal propaganda. Banū Isrāʾīl are neither praised as a race nor condemned as a race. The Qur’an acknowledges their prophets and righteous individuals while exposing disobedience. A community’s honour depends on faith and conduct, not ancestry.

### Evidence of Prophethood

The closing statement—“you are among the messengers”—affirms Muhammad ﷺ as a true messenger. He was not present at the ancient events, and the people of Makkah had no ordinary means of reconstructing their details. The accurate theological and moral presentation of the stories forms part of the Qur’an’s evidence.

The Prophet ﷺ did not claim independent access to the unseen. He repeatedly said that he followed what was revealed to him. His role was to recite Allah’s words, explain them, and embody their guidance. Muslims therefore honour the messenger without worshipping him.

### Recitation and Transformation

The verb “We recite” points to the Qur’an as a living recitation—*tilāwah*—not only a written archive. Hearing the story should lead to reflection and action. The believer asks whether he is like the people who promise courage but retreat, like those who obey and remain patient, or like David, who receives authority and wisdom.

Recitation without reflection can become a familiar sound. The purpose of *tilāwah* is to bring the past into the present: fear still tests communities, wealth still distorts leadership, and patience still produces strength.

### Continuity of the Messengers

The verse places Muhammad ﷺ within the chain of messengers. He confirms the prophetic call to worship Allah and completes its guidance. Muslims believe in Mūsā, Dāwūd, and all the prophets, while rejecting the idea that later communities own them exclusively. The prophets were brothers in the same foundation of tawḥīd, even though their laws and historical circumstances differed.

This continuity gives Muslims a respectful way to engage earlier scripture: they honour its original revelation, accept what the Qur’an confirms, and do not treat every later claim as authoritative without the Qur’anic criterion.

### Guidance, Not Mere Information

The story ends at the point where the next section will discuss messengers and their differing degrees. This arrangement invites readers to see history as a preparation for revelation. Knowledge of past victory is valuable only when it teaches humility, courage, justice, and reliance upon Allah.

The Prophet ﷺ was a messenger for all humanity, and his message remains a warning against repeating the old patterns: inherited pride, blind obedience, corruption, and abandoning truth when it becomes costly.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:253

> These messengers—We favoured some of them over others. Among them were those to whom Allah spoke, and some He raised in rank. We gave Jesus son of Mary clear signs and supported him with the Holy Spirit. If Allah had willed, those after them would not have fought one another after clear proofs had come to them. But they differed: some believed and some disbelieved. Had Allah willed, they would not have fought one another, but Allah does what He wills.

**Commentary:**

### All Messengers Are Honoured

The verse says that Allah favoured some messengers over others while Muslims are required to believe in all of them. Preference in rank does not permit insulting or rejecting any prophet. Allah gave each messenger a distinct mission, sign, community, and degree of honour, and He alone knows the full wisdom of those distinctions.

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ taught that no Muslim should demean one prophet to elevate another. He described the prophets as brothers in one faith, and he said that he is the closest of people to Jesus son of Mary in this world and the Hereafter, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Honour is therefore joined to continuity, not rivalry.

### Allah Spoke to Some

“Among them were those to whom Allah spoke” refers especially to Mūsā, who is called *Kalīm Allāh*, the one to whom Allah spoke. The manner of divine speech is affirmed as befits Allah’s majesty, without imagining a human voice, organ, or physical limitation. The verse is a sign of the different honours Allah granted His messengers.

The phrase does not mean that other prophets did not receive revelation. All received revelation through the means Allah chose. It means that Allah gave some a distinctive mode or degree of communication. Believers affirm the report and leave the modality to Allah.

### Degrees of Rank

Some messengers were raised in degrees—*rafaʿa baʿḍahum darajāt*. The highest rank belongs to those Allah identified as especially favoured, including the messengers of firm resolve, and the final messenger Muhammad ﷺ holds the comprehensive mission to humanity. The exact ranking is known fully to Allah, and human beings should not turn it into a competition that leads to disrespect.

Rank before Allah is accompanied by responsibility. A higher degree means a greater burden of conveyance, patience, and leadership. The believer should not seek honour merely for status; he asks for the ability to fulfil the trust given to him.

### Jesus and the Holy Spirit

Allah gave ʿĪsā clear signs, including his birth without a father, speaking in infancy, healing by Allah’s permission, and other miracles described in 3:49 and 5:110. These signs prove his prophethood; they do not make him divine. The repeated phrase “by Allah’s permission” protects tawḥīd and places the miracle within the power of the Creator.

He was supported by the *Rūḥ al-Qudus*, commonly understood as the angel Jibrīl, the Holy Spirit. The Qur’an also describes revelation as a spirit that gives life to hearts. The verse’s immediate meaning concerns divine support for Jesus in his mission, while the broader lesson is that messengers are strengthened by means beyond ordinary human resources.

### Disagreement after Proofs

After clear proofs came, later communities still fought and differed. The problem was not that revelation had failed to clarify everything; human beings brought desire, envy, power, and group loyalty into their response. Some believed and some disbelieved. Clear evidence does not force a person who is determined to protect his interest.

The verse distinguishes the existence of disagreement from the moral cause of disagreement. Honest scholarly difference is not the same as rejecting a clear proof out of hostility. The Qur’an elsewhere praises those who differ while seeking the truth and condemns those whose division is driven by transgression.

### Divine Will and Human Choice

“Had Allah willed, they would not have fought one another” affirms Allah’s power. He could have compelled all people into one outward pattern, but He allowed a world in which moral choice, obedience, rebellion, and testing occur. His allowing an action does not mean that He loves the action or excuses the person who commits it.

The final phrase, “Allah does what He wills,” does not cancel responsibility. The Qur’an repeatedly commands people to believe, choose justice, and avoid oppression. Divine decree encompasses human choices, while human beings are accountable for what they knowingly choose and do.

### Unity without Erasing Truth

The verse does not teach that believers should unite by pretending every belief is identical. The messengers share tawḥīd, and Muslims affirm the distinct revelations and laws Allah gave. Unity means returning to truth and justice, not suppressing clear differences or turning them into hatred.

A Muslim can honour Jesus, Moses, and their followers while remaining firm that worship belongs to Allah alone and that Muhammad ﷺ is the final messenger. Conviction and respect coexist.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:254

> O believers, spend from what We have provided for you before a Day comes in which there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession. The disbelievers are the wrongdoers.

**Commentary:**

### Spend before the Final Day

The believers are told to spend from Allah’s provision before the Day of Resurrection arrives. Wealth is a trust and an opportunity whose usefulness ends at death, while the reward of how it was used remains. The command includes zakāh, support for dependants, voluntary charity, relief, and every lawful expenditure that fulfils Allah’s rights and the rights of people.

“From what We have provided” corrects the illusion of absolute ownership. A person earns through work, but his life, ability, resources, opportunities, and the natural world that sustains him are all gifts from Allah. Spending becomes gratitude when it is lawful, sincere, and directed toward benefit.

### No Bargaining

On that Day there will be no *bayʿ*, no trade or bargaining by which a person can purchase escape. A person cannot offer wealth after death to erase a life of refusal. The Qur’an repeatedly warns that ransom will not be accepted once the final judgement has arrived (3:91; 57:15).

This does not mean that all worldly transactions are bad. Lawful trade is permitted, and honest business can be worship. The warning concerns treating the Hereafter as a market that can be negotiated after the opportunity for deeds has ended. The wise person makes the investment now.

### No Friendship as a Substitute for Faith

There will be no *khullah*, intimate friendship or alliance that can save a person independently of Allah. Family, political connections, popularity, and proximity to a respected leader cannot replace faith and righteous action. This does not deny the emotional value of friendship in the world or the benefit of righteous companionship. It denies the belief that a created relationship can override Allah’s judgement.

The Prophet ﷺ warned his closest relatives to save themselves through faith and deeds, showing that lineage cannot substitute for obedience. A believer loves family and friends by encouraging them toward good, not by promising a rescue he does not control.

### Intercession Belongs to Allah

The verse does not deny every form of intercession. Other Qur’anic passages establish that intercession occurs only by Allah’s permission and for those with whom He is pleased (20:109; 34:23). No prophet, angel, saint, or ancestor intercedes independently or can be invoked as a power alongside Allah.

The correct response is to seek Allah’s mercy directly, follow His Messenger ﷺ, and hope in the intercession He grants. This protects tawḥīd from both denying revealed intercession and turning created beings into objects of worship.

### The Righteous Use Wealth before It Is Gone

The command to spend is urgent because wealth can disappear through death, illness, economic change, or sudden disaster. More importantly, the chance to earn reward ends when the soul departs. The Prophet ﷺ taught that a person’s wealth is truly what he has sent ahead through charity, while what he leaves behind belongs to heirs.

This urgency must be balanced with responsibility. The believer supports family, pays debts, and avoids waste. He does not give in a way that makes dependants beg, but he does not let fear turn provision into an idol.

### Disbelief and Wrongdoing

“The disbelievers are the wrongdoers” identifies the theological injustice of rejecting Allah’s ownership and the practical injustice of withholding rights while relying on wealth and connections. The statement concerns those who persist in disbelief, not a licence to treat all non-Muslims unjustly. Muslims must still act with kindness and justice toward peaceful people of other faiths (60:8).

A believer should also fear forms of wrongdoing within his own life. One may affirm Islam but wrong people through hoarding, fraud, unpaid wages, or refusal to support dependants. The verse’s moral warning reaches anyone who treats Allah’s provision as a private entitlement without accountability.

### Charity and the Heart

Spending purifies attachment. The person who gives does not become poorer in the ultimate account. He learns reliance, compassion, and gratitude. Charity should be protected from showing off, reminders, and injury to the recipient (2:264).

The Prophet ﷺ described hidden charity as one of the deeds that receives special protection on the Day of Resurrection. Quiet giving is not the only valid form, but privacy often protects sincerity.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:255

> Allah—there is no deity except Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all. Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is before them and what is behind them, and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His Kursī extends over the heavens and the earth, and preserving them does not tire Him. He is the Most High, the Most Great.

**Commentary:**

### The Supreme Verse

This āyah is known as *Āyat al-Kursī*, the Verse of the Footstool. It gathers the foundations of tawḥīd, Allah’s perfect life, sustaining authority, knowledge, ownership, permission, power, and transcendence. The verse moves from the declaration of divine oneness to the negation of every weakness and then to the affirmation of Allah’s complete dominion.

The Prophet ﷺ told Ubayy ibn Kaʿb that it is the greatest verse in the Book of Allah, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Its greatness is not a magical property detached from understanding. It is great because it gives the heart a complete vision of who Allah is and why no created rival deserves worship.

### Allah Is al-Ḥayy and al-Qayyūm

Allah is *al-Ḥayy*, the Ever-Living. His life is perfect, beginningless and everlasting, and unlike created life it is not dependent upon food, rest, time, or bodily processes. He is *al-Qayyūm*, the One who sustains Himself and sustains everything else. Every creature depends upon Him at every moment, while He depends upon no one.

This pair of names gives the believer security. Human protectors tire, forget, die, or become unable to help. Allah never loses awareness or power. The servant can turn to Him in the night, in fear, poverty, illness, and confusion, knowing that the Sustainer is always living and never absent.

### No Drowsiness and No Sleep

“Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep.” Human beings require rest because their bodies and minds are limited. Allah is not overcome by weakness, inattention, or fatigue. He does not need sleep in order to recover, and no moment of creation falls outside His care.

The statement is also a spiritual comfort. When every human helper is unavailable, Allah remains aware. He hears the hidden prayer, sees the injustice that no court noticed, and preserves the universe without strain. The believer sleeps after taking lawful means because he trusts the Guardian who never sleeps.

### Complete Ownership

“To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.” The phrase encompasses people, angels, animals, matter, wealth, power, and every visible and unseen realm. Human beings possess property only by delegated permission. This is why wealth must be earned lawfully, spent responsibly, and returned through charity and inheritance.

Ownership also means that no created ruler has absolute authority. Governments, families, employers, and scholars exercise limited trusts. They cannot command disobedience to Allah or claim ownership of people’s souls. Tawḥīd liberates the servant from treating any human power as ultimate.

### Intercession by Permission

“Who can intercede with Him except by His permission?” Intercession—*shafāʿah*—belongs to Allah. He may honour prophets, angels, and righteous servants by allowing them to intercede, but no created being can force Allah, negotiate as an equal, or independently remove punishment. This guards the believer from both denying revealed intercession and worshipping intermediaries.

The verse’s question is rhetorical: no one can intercede without permission. Believers seek Allah directly through prayer, repentance, and obedience. They also hope for the Prophet’s intercession because Allah grants it, without invoking the Prophet ﷺ as an independent divine power.

### Allah’s Encompassing Knowledge

Allah knows what is before and behind them—what is present, future, past, visible, hidden, and connected to their circumstances. Created beings know only what Allah teaches them. They cannot encompass His knowledge except what He wills. The verse establishes Allah’s omniscience without requiring people to imagine how His knowledge relates to time.

This is a source of accountability and comfort. The hidden intention behind a charity, the pain of a widow, and the secret injustice of a powerful person are all known. A person cannot hide a deed from Allah, and a sincere act cannot be lost because humans failed to notice it.

### The Kursī and Allah’s Transcendence

Allah says His *Kursī* extends over the heavens and the earth. The Kursī is an unseen created reality associated with Allah’s dominion. Classical reports and exegetes discuss it as a footstool distinct from the *ʿArsh*, the Throne. The Qur’an does not require us to imagine its form or measure it through human comparison.

Affirming the verse means affirming what Allah said without likening Him to creation. The vastness of the Kursī points to the greatness of the created heavens and earth and, by implication, the immeasurable greatness of their Creator. Allah is not contained by the Kursī or by any created space.

### Preservation without Fatigue

“Preserving them does not tire Him.” The universe is maintained without effort, weakness, or recovery. Human administrators become exhausted by a single household or institution; Allah sustains all worlds simultaneously. This refutes any idea that creation places a burden upon Him or that He needs assistants to complete His power.

The statement also gives believers confidence in divine care. The laws of nature, the order of life, and the final resurrection are not difficult for Allah. What appears impossible to humans is within His effortless power.

### The Most High and Great

*Al-ʿAliyy* is the Most High, exalted above creation and every imperfection. *Al-ʿAẓīm* is the Most Great, possessing perfect majesty. These names complete the verse by directing worship upward in meaning: the heart honours Allah above every rival, fear, desire, and authority.

Reciting Āyat al-Kursī should therefore produce humility and reliance. It is not a charm recited while a person continues to worship wealth, fame, or superstition. Its protection is realized through faith, remembrance, and obedience.

- Qur’an 2:255 on Allah’s oneness, life, sustaining power, ownership, knowledge, Kursī, and transcendence.
- Qur’an 20:109, 34:23, and 53:26 on intercession by Allah’s permission.
- Qur’an 6:59, 11:6, and 35:41 on encompassing knowledge and sustaining creation.
- Qur’an 42:11 and 112:1–4 on Allah’s uniqueness and the impossibility of comparing Him to creation.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the report of Ubayy ibn Kaʿb identifying Āyat al-Kursī as the greatest verse; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, the report of reciting it before sleep, understood as a practice affirmed by the Prophet ﷺ.
- For *al-Ḥayy*, *al-Qayyūm*, *Kursī*, *ʿArsh*, *shafāʿah*, and affirming divine attributes without asking how, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:255.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:256

> There is no compulsion in religion. The right course has become distinct from the wrong. So whoever rejects false deities and believes in Allah has grasped the firmest handhold, which will never break. Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### Faith Cannot Be Forced

“There is no compulsion in religion”—*lā ikrāha fī al-dīn*—is a foundational Qur’anic principle. Faith is recognition, conviction, and willing submission of the heart. A person may be forced to repeat words or perform an outward action, but coercion cannot create sincere īmān. Therefore Muslims invite through truth, wisdom, character, and clear evidence rather than through forced conversion.

The verse does not mean that religion has no truth claims or that all beliefs are equally valid. The next phrase says that the right course has become distinct from error. Islam declares guidance and misguidance to be real, while refusing to make coercion the method by which people are made to accept guidance.

### Clarity and Freedom

The Arabic *rushd* means right direction, maturity, and sound judgement, while *ghayy* means error and deviation. Revelation clarifies the difference, and people are responsible for how they respond. Freedom here is moral freedom under Allah’s knowledge, not freedom from consequences. A person may choose disbelief, but he cannot demand that Allah treat every choice as equally true in the Hereafter.

The Prophet ﷺ conveyed the message, answered questions, made treaties, and lived among communities of different faiths. He did not force the People of the Book to convert. The Qur’an commands Muslims to debate in the best manner (29:46) and to protect one who seeks safety (9:6).

### Rejecting the Ṭāghūt

The believer must reject *ṭāghūt*, every false object or authority that is obeyed or worshipped in opposition to Allah. It includes idols, Satan, tyrannical rulers, corrupt ideologies, and any created power that claims the right to define ultimate truth independently of Allah. Rejecting ṭāghūt does not require attacking people. It means refusing worship, unjust obedience, and false claims of divinity.

A person can respect a lawful government, cooperate with neighbours, and honour parents without giving them obedience in sin. The firm believer retains conscience and refuses to make any human being an absolute authority.

### The Firmest Handhold

“Whoever rejects false deities and believes in Allah has grasped the firmest handhold”—*al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā*. The image suggests a secure rope or handle that does not break. The condition has two parts: negation and affirmation. One leaves false worship and turns to Allah. Merely rejecting one idol without accepting truth leaves the heart empty; merely claiming belief while serving rivals leaves the testimony incomplete.

The handhold is firm because Allah is firm, not because human faith never fluctuates. Believers may experience weakness, grief, or questions, but they return to the Qur’an, Sunnah, prayer, and repentance. Allah strengthens those who seek Him.

### Religion and Public Conduct

No compulsion in religion does not forbid a Muslim community from having moral and legal norms. Every society regulates conduct to protect life, property, family, and public safety. The principle forbids forcing a person’s heart or converting religion into a tool of humiliation. Non-Muslims living under a lawful covenant retain their rights, and Muslims may not abuse them because they do not share the faith.

Likewise, a family cannot force a daughter or son to pretend to believe. Parents may teach, advise, and set reasonable household rules, but they cannot manufacture conviction through violence. The Prophet ﷺ said that consent matters in marriage, and the same moral seriousness applies to religious commitment.

### Invitation without Coercion

The Muslim’s duty is *balāgh*, clear conveyance. He explains tawḥīd, answers sincere questions, and embodies mercy. Guidance itself belongs to Allah (28:56). Coercive preaching often produces hypocrisy, resentment, or outward conformity without the heart’s submission.

This principle is also relevant to intra-Muslim disagreement. A person may advise about prayer, creed, or ethics, but he should not force public declarations or accuse people’s hearts without evidence. Accountability and social protection can exist without pretending that compulsion creates faith.

### Allah Hears and Knows

Allah hears the invitation, the threat, the forced statement, and the private confession of faith. He knows who genuinely believes, who speaks under coercion, who manipulates the principle to avoid accountability, and who is prevented from expressing truth. This is why final judgement cannot be delegated to ordinary people.

The verse gives confidence to the believer and protection to the vulnerable. Truth is clear, but human beings must respond with justice and wisdom.

- Qur’an 10:99, 18:29, and 29:46 on faith, choice, clear conveyance, and respectful debate.
- Qur’an 60:8–9 and 9:6 on justice toward peaceful non-Muslims and protection for those seeking safety.
- Qur’an 28:56 on guidance belonging to Allah and the limits of human control.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the Prophet’s covenants and treatment of non-Muslim communities, and the hadith affirming consent and the rejection of coercion.
- For *lā ikrāha*, *rushd*, *ghayy*, *ṭāghūt*, and *al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:256.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:257

> Allah is the Guardian of those who believe. He brings them out of darknesses into the light. As for those who disbelieve, their guardians are the false deities; they bring them out of the light into darknesses. They are the companions of the Fire, where they will remain forever.

**Commentary:**

### Allah as the Guardian of Believers

Allah is *walī*, Guardian, Protector, and intimate supporter of those who believe. His guardianship includes guidance, care, protection from spiritual confusion, forgiveness, and help in obeying Him. It does not mean that believers will experience no physical pain or worldly loss. The prophets and righteous people were tested, but Allah guarded their faith and gave their trials meaning.

The believer’s first allegiance is to Allah. Family, friends, teachers, governments, and institutions can provide support, but none is an independent guardian. The heart trusts created means while relying ultimately upon Allah. This frees the servant from despair when a human helper fails and from worshipful dependence upon people.

### From Darknesses to Light

The plural “darknesses”—*ẓulumāt*—suggests many forms of confusion and harm: shirk, ignorance, hypocrisy, injustice, sinful desire, fear, and despair. The light—*nūr*—is singular because truth and guidance have one source. Allah brings believers through revelation, the Messenger’s example, sound understanding, repentance, and righteous companionship.

The movement is often gradual. A new believer may leave major sin but still need healing from old habits. A person may know a ruling but need time to develop trust and discipline. Allah’s guardianship includes opening the next step and giving strength to continue. The servant responds by learning and obeying rather than demanding instant perfection.

### False Guardians

Those who disbelieve take *ṭawāghīt*, false guardians and authorities. These can be idols, Satan, tyrannical leaders, ideologies, desires, or social systems that claim ultimate obedience. They promise security, identity, wealth, or power, but their protection ends in deeper darkness. Created powers cannot grant forgiveness, control the unseen, or save a person from Allah’s judgement.

The verse does not mean that every non-Muslim relationship is harmful or that Muslims may treat non-Muslims unjustly. A person may receive lawful worldly assistance from people of any faith and remain obligated to show justice and kindness. The issue is ultimate spiritual guardianship and allegiance, not ordinary cooperation.

### Light and Freedom

Allah’s light clarifies the purpose of life and frees people from slavery to created things. A person who knows that Allah alone controls benefit and harm is less vulnerable to political intimidation, commercial manipulation, and social pressure. He can respect authority without worshipping it and seek wealth without becoming its servant.

The light also reveals injustice. Guidance does not remain private comfort; it requires truthful testimony, fair contracts, care for the weak, and resistance to oppression. A person cannot claim to have left darkness while benefiting from corruption and refusing to see its victims.

### The Companions of the Fire

The final warning describes the end of those who die upon disbelief and remain aligned with false guardians. It is a warning to the living, not a licence to declare the final fate of named individuals. Allah knows what guidance reached each person and what he did with it.

The believer fears that his own attachments may become rivals. He asks Allah to keep him firm and does not assume that a religious identity alone guarantees safety. Guardianship is connected to faith, obedience, and sincere return.

### Allah’s Special Guardianship

A sacred hadith says that whoever shows enmity to one of Allah’s friends, Allah declares war against him, and that the servant draws near through obligations and voluntary deeds until Allah loves him, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. The *awliyāʾ* of Allah are not defined by claims, costumes, or miracles. They are people of faith and taqwā (10:62–63).

This protects the community from false spiritual authorities. A person who claims special guardianship while demanding worship, money, or unquestioning obedience is leading people toward darkness, not light.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:258

> Have you not considered the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had given him kingship? Abraham said, “My Lord is the One who gives life and causes death.” He said, “I give life and cause death.” Abraham said, “Allah brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west.” Thus the disbeliever was confounded. Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.

**Commentary:**

### Power Can Produce Arrogance

The verse presents a ruler who argued with Ibrāhīm about Allah because Allah had given him kingship. The Qur’an does not name the ruler; classical tafsīr commonly identifies him as Nimrod, but the moral lesson does not depend upon the name. Authority gave him the illusion that he could define reality and challenge the Creator.

Power can make a person forget that it is borrowed. A ruler, employer, scholar, or wealthy individual may begin to think that because people obey him, the universe must obey him too. The verse exposes this confusion: human authority is limited, while Allah gives life, causes death, and controls every created power.

### Giving Life and Causing Death

Ibrāhīm begins with a clear proof: “My Lord is the One who gives life and causes death.” The ruler replies that he can give life and death. Classical exegetes explain that he may have released one condemned person and killed another, pretending that this was equivalent to creation and death. His answer was a rhetorical trick, not a genuine response to Ibrāhīm’s argument.

A ruler can decide whether a person lives under a legal sentence, but he cannot create a living being from nothing, control the soul, or reverse death by his own power. Human medicine can preserve life and postpone death through means, but it does not make the physician the Creator. The distinction between cause and ultimate source is central to tawḥīd.

### The Decisive Cosmic Sign

Ibrāhīm then gives a proof the ruler cannot redefine: “Allah brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west.” The challenge moves from a manipulated social example to a universal order beyond the king’s control. The sun’s regular movement is a sign of a Creator whose authority cannot be imitated by political theatre.

The ruler is confounded—*fa-buhita alladhī kafara*. He has no coherent answer. Silence after a clear proof is not the same as humble acceptance; a person may be intellectually defeated yet remain morally stubborn. The Qur’an records the collapse of the argument, while Allah knows whether the heart later repented.

### Debate and Its Limits

Ibrāhīm’s exchange demonstrates that reasoned debate can be useful when it clarifies truth. He begins with a principle, exposes the false analogy, and presents a proof the opponent cannot manipulate. He does not insult the ruler’s body, family, or ethnicity. His confidence comes from evidence and trust in Allah.

The Qur’an also warns against fruitless argument. A debate is beneficial when participants seek truth, understand the evidence, and accept correction. It becomes harmful when the aim is humiliation, performance, or protecting power. Muslims should not imitate the ruler’s arrogance while criticizing it.

### Allah Does Not Guide Wrongdoers

The closing statement says that Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people—*al-qawm al-ẓālimīn*. This does not mean that Allah prevents every wrongdoer from ever receiving information or repentance. It means that the person who persists in injustice and arrogance is deprived of the special guidance that leads to humble acceptance. His wrongdoing becomes a barrier he chooses and reinforces.

The Qur’an calls all people to guidance, and repentance remains open while life remains. A ruler can turn from oppression, a scholar from pride, and a wealthy person from exploitation. But guidance requires abandoning the posture that treats power as proof of truth.

### The Political Lesson

Authority should be measured by justice and humility. A government that provides security does not own its citizens’ souls. A leader cannot declare himself the source of life, law, or prosperity. He is a servant who will answer for blood, property, and speech.

Ibrāhīm’s example also encourages truthful counsel to power. The adviser uses evidence, avoids flattery, and does not assume that a ruler’s confidence makes his argument true. Where direct confrontation creates danger, wisdom may require a different method, but truth must not be sold for safety or favour.

### Creation and Contingency

The sun’s rising from the east reminds people that the world follows an ordered pattern. Regularity does not make creation divine; it makes the Creator’s wisdom visible. The ruler can neither command the sun nor guarantee tomorrow’s sunrise. Every created power is contingent and dependent.

The believer uses this awareness in daily life. He respects worldly causes but does not fear them as independent gods. He seeks lawful help while keeping the heart attached to Allah.

- Qur’an 16:78 and 30:20–25 on life, creation, and signs in the natural order.
- Qur’an 16:125 and 29:46 on debating with wisdom and in the best manner.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on Ibrāhīm’s truthfulness and the prophetic method of correcting false claims without sacrificing tawḥīd.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:259

> Or consider the one who passed by a town whose roofs had collapsed over its walls. He said, “How will Allah give life to this town after its death?” So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years and then raised him. He asked, “How long have you remained?” He replied, “I remained a day or part of a day.” Allah said, “Rather, you remained a hundred years. Look at your food and drink; they have not spoiled. And look at your donkey. We will make you a sign for the people. Look at the bones—how We raise them and then clothe them with flesh.” When it became clear to him, he said, “I know that Allah is capable of everything.”

**Commentary:**

### A Ruined Town and a Question

The verse describes a person who passed a devastated town and wondered how Allah could restore it after its death. The Qur’an does not name the town or the person. Classical exegetes transmitted an identification with ʿUzayr and a particular Israelite town, but the revelation itself leaves the identity open. The lasting lesson is Allah’s power to restore life and the movement from uncertainty to certainty.

The question need not have been arrogant denial. It can be a request to understand how resurrection occurs. Ibrāhīm similarly asked Allah to show him how He gives life to the dead (2:260), and Allah did not condemn him for wanting his heart reassured. The moral difference lies between sincere inquiry and stubborn rejection after truth is clear.

### Death for a Hundred Years

Allah caused the person to die for a hundred years and then raised him. The event makes time itself part of the sign. A century passes, yet the person’s experience is like a day or part of a day. Human awareness of time is limited and subjective, while Allah controls life, death, memory, and perception.

The miracle is not presented as a scientific model of every resurrection. It is a particular sign Allah gave to one person. It demonstrates that the One who created life can suspend and restore it, and that the long interval between death and resurrection is no obstacle to divine power.

### Food and Drink Preserved

The food and drink remained unchanged while the person and his donkey underwent death and restoration. The contrast shows that Allah can preserve one thing, transform another, and reverse a third according to His will. Creation is not an independent system that limits its Creator.

The verse does not invite superstition about preserving food or treating a miracle as an ordinary technique. It calls the reader to recognize divine power and to trust revelation about matters beyond ordinary experience. Scientific investigation can study food decay, but it cannot make this unique sign a routine human process.

### The Donkey and the Bones

The donkey’s remains became a visible sign of bodily resurrection. Allah showed the bones being raised and clothed with flesh. The image directly answers the objection that bones, once scattered and decayed, cannot become living bodies again. If Allah created the body initially, He can reassemble it after death.

The Qur’an answers the same objection in Sūrah Yāsīn: “Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?” Say, “He will give them life who produced them the first time” (36:78–79). The ability to originate life is evidence of the ability to restore it.

### “A Sign for the People”

The person’s experience is made a sign—*āyah*—for humanity. A miracle benefits people when it leads to knowledge and gratitude, not when it becomes a story detached from its Lord. The event teaches that apparent finality is not final for Allah: ruins can be rebuilt, bones can be clothed, and a person can be returned from death.

The verse also challenges civilizational despair. A destroyed town may be physically gone for a period, but communities can be rebuilt through justice, knowledge, work, and Allah’s permission. Hope must not become passivity; people restore what they can while trusting Allah with what exceeds their control.

### The Confession of Knowledge

“When it became clear to him, he said, ‘I know that Allah is capable of everything.’” His knowledge is not only intellectual. It is certainty formed through a sign and directed toward worship. He does not claim that he now understands every mystery. He recognizes the central truth: Allah’s power is unlimited and His resurrection promise is credible.

A believer can similarly say, “I do not know how,” without saying, “It cannot be.” Human inability to imagine a process does not limit Allah. Humility allows the mind to investigate while accepting the limits of created knowledge.

### Resurrection and Moral Responsibility

The story is connected to the Day when every person is raised. Resurrection is not merely a demonstration of power; it is the foundation of accountability. If people return to Allah, then hidden injustice, forgotten charity, and unpunished crimes all have a final reckoning.

This should affect present conduct. The person who believes he will be raised does not treat property, time, or another person’s life as disposable. He prepares for the return through faith, repentance, and good works.

### Evidence and references

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:260

> And when Abraham said, “My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead,” He said, “Do you not believe?” He replied, “Yes, but so that my heart may be reassured.” He said, “Then take four birds, make them accustomed to you, then place a portion of them on each hill, and call them; they will come to you swiftly. Know that Allah is Exalted in Might and Wise.”

**Commentary:**

### Ibrāhīm’s Request for Reassurance

Ibrāhīm asks Allah to show him how He gives life to the dead. Allah asks, “Do you not believe?” Ibrāhīm immediately affirms his faith: “Yes, but so that my heart may be reassured.” His request is not disbelief or denial. It is a desire for *ṭumaʾnīnah*, a deeper settled certainty in the heart.

This distinction is important. A believer may ask how a matter occurs without doubting that Allah can do it. Faith has degrees of certainty, and the heart can seek greater tranquillity through reflection and signs. The Prophet ﷺ taught that sincere questions are not blameworthy when they seek understanding rather than escape from obedience.

### Belief and the Need for Reassurance

Ibrāhīm’s answer shows that intellectual conviction and emotional reassurance can coexist. A person may know that Allah is powerful yet feel shaken by grief, death, or an unanswered question. Asking Allah for clarity can strengthen the heart. What is blameworthy is refusing to accept truth until every unseen reality becomes physically visible.

The Qur’an presents Ibrāhīm as a model of both certainty and inquiry. He questioned his people, observed the signs of the heavens, and rejected false gods. His questions were steps toward tawḥīd, not excuses to remain inactive.

### The Four Birds

Allah commands Ibrāhīm to take four birds, become familiar with them, place portions of them on different hills, and call them. The Qur’an’s details establish that the creatures were separated and that their return occurred by Allah’s command. Classical exegetes discuss the precise manner in which Ibrāhīm handled the birds and the meaning of the instruction to become accustomed to them. The lesson does not depend upon reconstructing every physical detail.

The event is a miracle, not a recipe for human resurrection. It demonstrates that distance, separation, decay, and scattered remains do not prevent Allah from restoring life. Modern science may examine biological processes, but no experiment can reproduce the divine sign described here.

### “They Will Come to You Swiftly”

The birds come swiftly when called. The one who created and scattered them knows each part and can bring them together. The speed of their return displays command over matter, identity, and life. The miracle answers the recurring human objection that a body cannot be restored after its components are dispersed.

It also gives a personal sign to Ibrāhīm. He does not merely hear an abstract argument; he witnesses an event that settles his heart. Allah knows the particular form of reassurance each servant needs, though ordinary believers do not demand miracles on their own terms.

### Allah’s Might and Wisdom

The final names are *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Ḥakīm*. Allah’s might makes resurrection possible, and His wisdom determines how signs are given and what they teach. Power without wisdom would be an incoherent concept, but Allah’s acts are both possible and purposeful.

The sign is not given to satisfy entertainment or curiosity. It teaches resurrection, strengthens a prophet for his mission, and refutes the idea that death is beyond Allah’s control. A miracle should deepen worship rather than become a spectacle.

### Lessons for the Troubled Heart

The verse gives permission to seek reassurance in a respectful way. A grieving person may ask Allah to strengthen his heart, study evidence, speak with a knowledgeable teacher, and observe signs of mercy and creation. He should not be ashamed of the need for comfort. At the same time, he should distinguish a request for reassurance from a demand that Allah obey a private test.

The heart settles through Qur’an, prayer, remembrance, truthful companionship, and reflection. The believer says, “I know Allah is capable,” even while asking for greater *yaqīn*.

### Resurrection and the End of the Passage

The preceding verses have described Allah’s power over life and death, the resurrection of a person after a hundred years, and Ibrāhīm’s own request for reassurance. Together they establish that resurrection is certain and that faith rests upon Allah’s power, not on human imagination. The chapter will move from these signs into further guidance about charity, contracts, and social life, showing that belief in resurrection must shape conduct.

A person who expects to be raised treats every life as sacred, every trust as accountable, and every charitable act as an investment with Allah. Certainty about the Hereafter is not withdrawal from the world; it is a reason to act justly within it.
