# Surah An-Nisa' (The Women, 4) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 1–10

Chapter 3 closed with the people of understanding praying, God answering, and a fourfold command toward *falāḥ*. Chapter 4 opens on a different register: not the aftermath of Uḥud, but the architecture of a community that must protect its weakest. The surah is named *An-Nisā'* — The Women — and its first ten verses already show why. Human origin from one soul, honour of the womb-ties, orphans' wealth, conditional marriage, dowry, guardianship of the incapable, women's share in inheritance, kindness at the division, conscience shaped by fear for one's own children, and fire in the belly for those who eat the orphan's right. Creed and law arrive together. The One who watches is the One who legislates care.

What follows is the opening movement. O humanity: fear your Lord who created you from a single soul and from it its mate, and from both spread many men and women; fear God in Whose name you appeal to one another, and honour the wombs; God is ever Watchful (1). Give orphans their wealth; do not swap bad for good; do not consume their property into yours — a great sin (2). If you fear you will not be just to orphan women, marry other women — two, three, or four; if you fear you will not be just, then one, or those your right hands possess; that is nearer to not oppressing (3). Give women their dowries as a gracious gift; if they remit willingly, take it wholesome (4). Do not give the foolish your wealth which God made your support; feed and clothe them from it; speak kindly (5). Test orphans until marriageable age; if you perceive sound judgment, hand over their wealth; do not consume it wastefully and in haste; the rich guardian abstains, the poor takes a fair amount; witnesses at hand-over; God is sufficient Reckoner (6). Men have a share and women have a share of what parents and near kin leave — little or much — an obligatory portion (7). If relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the division, give them something and speak kindly (8). Let those who would leave weak offspring behind fear (for orphans) as they would for their own; fear God and speak straight (9). Those who consume orphans' wealth unjustly consume fire in their bellies and will roast in a blaze (10).

Five cruxes govern the reading. The first is 4:1 — *nafs wāḥidah*, the mate from it, *arḥām*, and double *taqwā* under *Raqīb*. The second is 4:3 — the orphan-women occasion (ʿĀ'ishah's report in Bukhārī), the ceiling of four, justice as condition, and *mā malakat aymānukum* held in its historical frame without modern whitewash or anachronistic denial. The third is 4:4 — *ṣaduqāt* as *niḥlah*, gift not price. The fourth is 4:5–6 — *sufahā'*, *rushd*, and the ethics of guardianship. The fifth is 4:7–10 — women's obligatory share against jāhiliyya erasure, presence-gifts at division, conscience by analogy to one's own children, and the fire-belly image.

This batch should feel like a door opening into a household and a ledger. God is watching. The orphan is not unsupervised. The woman is not erasable from the will.

## Verse 1

**"O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you."**

In simple terms: O people, be mindful of your Lord who created you from one soul, and from that soul created its spouse, and from the two of them spread many men and women. And be mindful of God — the One in whose name you ask one another for rights — and honour the ties of the womb. God is always watching over you.

**The surah's threshold — taqwā, origin, and the womb.**

**"O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord."** *Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ittaqū rabbakum.*

Not only "O believers." *Al-nās* — people, humanity. The address is wider than the Madinan *ummah* alone, because the claim that follows is about the human race as such. *Ittaqū rabbakum* — have *taqwā* of your Lord: guard yourselves toward Him, be wary of His displeasure, keep the boundary. The Lord-name (*rabb*) already hints at care, upbringing, and claim — the One who raised you is the One you must not wrong by wronging those He tied to you.

**"Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate."** *Alladhī khalaqakum min nafsin wāḥidatin wa-khalaqa minhā zawjahā.*

*Nafs wāḥidah* — a single soul / self / person. The classical mainstream reads this as Adam, peace be upon him, the first human from whom the race begins. *Wa-khalaqa minhā zawjahā* — and He created from it its mate. The mate is Ḥawwā' (Eve) in the received reading. How "from it" was done is not narrated here in mechanics. Many classical commentators transmit the rib-tradition known also from earlier scripture and from prophetic reports about women and the rib — a tradition that must be held as early memory and pastoral colour, not as a laboratory diagram the verse demands. What the verse itself requires is clearer and weightier: humanity is not two unrelated species; woman is not a later afterthought from elsewhere; the pair shares origin. From that shared origin, moral claim follows.

**"And through both He spread countless men and women."** *Wa-baththa minhumā rijālan kathīran wa-nisā'an.*

*Baththa* — He scattered, spread, sowed. From the two, many men and many women. The verse names both genders in the spreading. Diversity of face, tongue, and place is not a threat to the kinship claim; it is the field in which kinship must still be honoured. You do not get to treat the distant branch as non-human. You came from the same pair.

**"And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and honour family ties."** *Wa-ttaqū Allāha alladhī tasā'alūna bihi wa-al-arḥām.*

A second *taqwā*, now with the name *Allāh*. *Alladhī tasā'alūna bihi* — the One by whom / in whose name you ask one another. In Arabian speech and still in Muslim habit, people adjure each other by God when claiming rights: "I ask you by God…" The verse turns that habit into theology. If His name is how you press a claim, then His *taqwā* must govern the claim. *Wa-al-arḥām* — and the wombs / kinship ties. The half-bracketed "honour" marks the translation's supply of a verb; the Arabic pairs *arḥām* with the *taqwā* structure so that the wombs are objects of the same reverent care. *Raḥim* is womb; *raḥmah* is mercy; the linguistic family is not accidental. To cut the womb-tie is to cut against the mercy-pattern God built into birth.

**"Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you."** *Inna Allāha kāna ʿalaykum raqībā.*

*Raqīb* — Watcher, Observer, the One who monitors. Not a distant auditor who checks yearly. Ever over you. The orphan's guardian, the husband at the contract, the heir at the division, the tongue that severs a relative — all operate under a Gaze that does not blink. This closing word is the atmosphere of the whole opening batch and of much of the surah.

**Why this verse begins the surah of women and rights.** Before shares, dowries, and punishments, God places origin and watchfulness. You cannot legislate care for women and orphans on top of a metaphysics that makes them disposable. Shared soul, shared spread, named wombs, named Watcher. Law will follow. Ontology came first.

**The rib reports — grade of certainty.** Prophetic narrations likening woman to a rib (created with a curve; force breaks, gentleness lets you live with the curve) are widely transmitted and used pastorally against harsh "straightening." They are not a verse-mandated embryology of every daughter. Hold the ethics the reports serve — gentleness, not brittle reform of the other — and do not build cosmology the Qur'an left undrawn. The Qur'an's load-bearing claim here is *minhā* and *minhumā*: from one, a mate; from both, the race.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** This week, name one kinship you have been treating as optional — a difficult sibling, an aging aunt, a cousin you only message for favours. Re-enter it under *arḥām* and under *Raqīb*. And when you ask someone "for God's sake," check whether you are willing to be the kind of person God would recognise in the asking.

**Tasā'alūna bihi — the social use of God's name.** The verse notices a speech habit and baptises it into ethics. People already knew how to weaponise the sacred name in bargains and blood-money talks. God says: if My name is in your mouth when you claim, My *taqwā* must be in your hand when you deal. Hypocrisy is adjuring by God while cutting the womb that God named in the same breath.

**Rijālan kathīran wa-nisā'an — both named in the spread.** Some social orders narrate the human story as male lines only, with women as unmarked vessels. This verse refuses that narration at origin-level. Men and women are both the *bathth* — the scattered fruit of the first pair. Rights-talk later in the surah stands on this anthropology.

**Connection forward.** Every orphan rule, marriage rule, and inheritance fraction in what follows is an application of "you are from one soul under a Watcher." When the text gets technical, return here.

**A note on "O humanity" in a Madinan surah.** Madinan legislation often addresses *alladhīna āmanū*. Opening with *al-nās* widens the moral horizon: these cares are not club rules for insiders only. Anyone who shares human origin is inside the claim of *arḥām* and the warning of *Raqīb*, even before they accept the full creed. Believers carry the law; the origin-argument still speaks past the fence.

## Verse 2

**"Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin."**

In simple terms: give orphans their property, and do not swap your bad goods for their good ones, and do not eat their wealth by folding it into yours. That is a great sin.

**The orphan's property — three forbidden moves and one duty.**

**"Give orphans their wealth."** *Wa-ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum.*

*Ātū* — give, bring, hand over. *Al-yatāmā* — the orphans (children whose father has died, in the dominant legal usage). *Amwālahum* — their properties, their wealth. The command is both present care and future release. While they are minor, "give" includes spending on them from what is theirs in a way that preserves the stock; when they mature, "give" means actual transfer of control. Some early reports link the verse to a guardian who refused to release property at majority; the ethic does not depend on locking one name. Society, not only the private guardian, is addressed in the plural.

**"And do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables."** *Wa-lā tatabaddalū al-khabītha bi-al-ṭayyib.*

*Lā tatabaddalū* — do not exchange / substitute. *Al-khabīth* — the bad, foul, defective. *Bi-al-ṭayyib* — for the good, wholesome, excellent. Classical pictures: swap your lean animal for the orphan's fat one and call it even; swap poor goods for their fine goods. The form is fraud wearing the mask of trade. The verse forbids the mask.

**"Nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own."** *Wa-lā ta'kulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum.*

*Lā ta'kulū* — do not eat / consume. *Ilā amwālikum* — into / toward your wealth. Mixing accounts until the orphan's portion disappears into the household total — then living from the blur — is named as eating. Even a religious person can slide here when pots and purses are shared without ledger. The Qur'an refuses the slide.

**"For this would indeed be a great sin."** *Innahu kāna ḥūban kabīran.*

*Ḥūb* — sin, crime, guilt (a weighty word). *Kabīr* — great. Not a technical misdemeanour. Later the Prophet ﷺ will list consuming the orphan's property among the destructive major sins in the famous seven. Verse 2 already sets the temperature.

**Connection to verse 1.** Kinship *taqwā* becomes concrete. The orphan is often a relative. Honouring *arḥām* includes not eating the fatherless child's stock.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** If you manage money for a minor, a special-needs adult, or an estate, run three checks: Is the inventory clean? Have I ever "borrowed" without recording? Is there a date and a witness plan for release? *Ḥūb kabīr* is not only a seventh-century farm problem. It is every blurred ledger where the weak cannot audit you.

**Al-khabīth bi-al-ṭayyib — more than livestock.** The farm examples in early tafsir (fat sheep for lean) teach a pattern that outlives the farm. Substituting your failing business risk onto an orphan's capital, parking your debt against their inheritance, "borrowing" their gold and returning plated metal — same *tabaddul*. The verse is about asymmetric exchange dressed as parity.

**Eating as a Qur'anic verb for unjust use.** *Akl* in this register is not only chewing. It is consumption, absorption, benefit. Rent taken, crop sold, land quietly retitled — all can be *akl*. That is why restoration, when wrong is discovered, must be full, not theatrical.

**Ḥūb kabīr and the seven destructive sins.** When the Prophet ﷺ listed consuming the orphan's wealth among the *sabaʿ mūbiqāt*, he was not adding a new heat the Qur'an lacked. He was confirming verse 2's temperature in the community's moral memory. Treat the convergence as reinforcement, not as a second scripture.

**Now bring this into ordinary life — the three-column audit.** Column one: what is theirs. Column two: what is mine. Column three: what is shared with a written rule. If you cannot fill the columns, you are already near *ḥūb*. Fix the columns before you fix your self-image.

**Plural address — community duty.** *Ātū* is plural. The orphan is not only the private problem of one uncle. Neighbours, judges, and the *ummah*'s public conscience are in the grammar. When a guardian fails, the plural must still have a path to act.

## Verse 3

**"If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice."**

In simple terms: if you fear you will not be fair to orphan girls (under your care), then marry women who seem good to you — two, three, or four. But if you fear you will not be just, then only one, or those your right hands possess. That is more likely to keep you from unfairness.

**Orphan women, the ceiling of four, and justice as the gate.**

**"If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their due rights if you were to marry them."** *Wa-in khiftum allā tuqsiṭū fī al-yatāmā.*

*Khiftum* — you fear. *Allā tuqsiṭū* — that you will not do *qisṭ* (fair measure, equity). *Fī al-yatāmā* — regarding the orphans. The half-brackets "orphan women" / "due rights" / "if you were to marry them" unpack a crux the Arabic leaves dense. The strongest early explanation is ʿĀ'ishah's, recorded in Bukhārī: this is about the orphan girl in the guardian's household, whose wealth and beauty may tempt him to marry her without a proper dower — taking advantage because she has no father to negotiate. If he fears he cannot be just, he must not marry her on those terms; other women are available. A related note in 4:127 returns to orphan women desired or not desired in marriage. Hold ʿĀ'ishah's reading as primary climate. Other classical angles exist (fear of injustice to orphans' wealth if one multiplies marriages; a general link between orphan-fear and marriage-discipline). They need not cancel the Bukhārī report; the verse's spine is fear of injustice as a brake on marriage choices.

**"Then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four."** *Fa-inkiḥū mā ṭāba lakum mina al-nisā'i mathnā wa-thulātha wa-rubāʿ.*

*Inkiḥū* — marry. *Mā ṭāba lakum* — what is good / pleasing to you. *Mathnā wa-thulātha wa-rubāʿ* — two, three, and four. The classical juristic reading across the schools: a maximum of four wives at one time for a free man, not an unlimited harem as in some pre-Islamic practice. The permission is real; it is not a command to marry four. The verse's own next clause conditions it.

**"But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then content yourselves with one."** *Fa-in khiftum allā taʿdilū fa-wāḥidatan.*

*Allā taʿdilū* — that you will not do *ʿadl* (justice, balance). Between co-wives: time, maintenance, fair presence — the measurable justice jurists discuss. (Emotional equality of the heart is later nuanced at 4:129 — you will not be able to hold hearts equal; do not incline wholly and leave another suspended.) Here the brake is clear: fear of not being just → one. The half-bracketed "content yourselves with" marks translation fill; the Arabic's *fa-wāḥidatan* is "then one."

**"Or those bondwomen in your possession."** *Aw mā malakat aymānukum.*

*Mā malakat aymānukum* — what your right hands possess: captives/bondspeople in the historical institution of the time. The translation's "bondwomen" is a fair dynamic choice. **How to speak of this without lying either way.** The Qur'an regulates an existing war-captive and bondage structure in a world where it was ambient; it does not invent it as an ideal to revive. It places this option inside the justice-logic of the verse (a different rights-profile than a free wife in classical fiqh). Modern abolition of chattel slavery is not a betrayal of this verse; it is a historical closing of an institution the moral arc of the revelation already constrained and redirected. Do not pretend the phrase meant "employee." Do not pretend the verse is a timeless recruitment order for concubinage. Grade the institution as historical; keep the justice-principle as enduring.

**"This way you are less likely to commit injustice."** *Dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū.*

*Adnā* — nearer, more likely. *Allā taʿūlū* — that you not incline unfairly / oppress / bear a burden that tilts (root explanations vary slightly: *ʿawl* as incline to injustice, or as family burden). Nearer to not wronging. The telos of the whole verse is not male entitlement maximisation. It is reduction of *ẓulm* — toward orphan girls, toward multiple wives, toward the household.

**What this verse is not.** It is not a slogan that every man should marry four. It is not a blank cheque that cancels 4:129's honesty about the heart. It is not a denial that justice includes dower, maintenance, and time. Read it as a justice-gated permission with a hard preference for one when justice is in doubt.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** If you are not in a polygynous question, the verse still trains you: fear of injustice should change your plans before the damage. If you manage power over someone with less leverage — employee, younger relative, dependent — refuse the marriage-to-the-orphan pattern: do not convert custody into advantage. And if polygyny is live in your context, treat *khiftum allā taʿdilū* as a real door, not a decorative plaque.

**Why orphan women specifically.** The guardian already holds her house, her wealth, her name's vulnerability. Marriage without just dower converts protection into capture. ʿĀ'ishah's explanation is devastating because it is domestic, not abstract: the girl who shares his property, whose beauty and money tempt a cheap contract. God interrupts that temptation with open market language: other women are lawful; you are not cornered into exploiting this one.

**Mathnā wa-thulātha wa-rubāʿ — form and ceiling.** The distributive numbers are read as up to four, not as "two plus three plus four = nine" (a strained reading rejected by the juristic mainstream). Pre-Islamic excess is being capped. Permission sits inside a cap; the cap sits inside justice; justice sits inside fear of God.

**ʿAdl between co-wives.** Classical discussion: nights, housing, maintenance — justiciable. Love of the heart — not fully justiciable (4:129). A man who can pay four dowers but will starve three hearts of fairness is the man *khiftum allā taʿdilū* describes. The verse wants that man to hear "one."

**Bondage clause without theatre.** In the seventh-century world, captives existed after war; the Qur'an repeatedly pushed manumission, kind treatment, and marriage pathways (see also 4:25, 24:33). Citing *mā malakat aymānukum* today as if it were a lifestyle brand is a failure of reading. Citing it as proof the Qur'an is only a slave-code is also a failure. The honest middle: historical regulation; enduring insistence on justice and on not using the weak as a justice-escape hatch that actually increases wrong.

**Dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū — telos statement.** End of verse is purpose clause. Whatever else people load onto 4:3, God states why the structure exists: nearer to not committing *ʿawl*/injustice. Keep that sentence visible when debates heat up.

## Verse 4

**"Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience."**

In simple terms: give women their dowries as a free gift. But if they willingly give back part of it to you, then take it and enjoy it cleanly.

**Dowry as gift — niḥlah — not a purchase price.**

**"Give women you wed their due dowries graciously."** *Wa-ātū al-nisā'a ṣaduqātihinna niḥlah.*

*Ātū al-nisā'a* — give the women. *Ṣaduqātihinna* — their *ṣaduqāt* (dower, bridal gift; related to *ṣidq*, truthfulness — a true gift that confirms the contract). *Niḥlah* — as a gift, gladly, as something due without haggling it into humiliation. ʿĀ'ishah and others gloss *niḥlah* as obligatory; Ibn Jurayj as specified and due. The half-bracketed "you wed" clarifies the recipients. This is not optional tip. It is her right, paid to her — not to her father as sale price in the jāhiliyya sense the verse pushes against.

**"But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience."** *Fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shay'in minhu nafsan fa-kulūhu hanī'an marī'an.*

*Ṭibna… nafsan* — they are pleased in soul / willingly. *ʿAn shay'in minhu* — regarding something of it. *Fa-kulūhu* — then eat / take it. *Hanī'an marī'an* — wholesome and agreeable; with appetite and without spiritual indigestion. Consent must be real — not pressure, not sulking, not "waive or else." If the gift-back is free, the taking is clean. If the "gift-back" is coerced, it is still her *ṣadāq* under another name.

**Connection to verse 3.** The orphan-girl abuse was partly dower-abuse. Verse 4 universalises: every wife, her *ṣadāq*, as *niḥlah*.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** In cultures that shame the dower, inflate it into spectacle, or divert it to the family, return to *niḥlah*: a real gift to her, sized with honesty, paid without theatre that bankrupts, and never clawed back by emotional blackmail. If a wife freely contributes from what is hers, receive it with thanks — and do not build a habit of expecting the waive.

**Ṣadāq and the economics of dignity.** A dower can be modest and still *niḥlah* if it is real and hers. A dower can be enormous and still abusive if it is a public auction of family pride she never controls. The verse cares about direction and ownership: *ātū al-nisā'a* — to the women — *ṣaduqātihinna*.

**Willing remit — the psychology.** *Ṭibna… nafsan* is soul-level willingness. In pastoral life, pressure wears many outfits: tears, silence, "if you loved me," family intermediaries. None of that is *ṭīb nafs*. Teachers who rush men to "take what she offers" without auditing pressure fail this verse.

**Hanī'an marī'an — two words of clean enjoyment.** The Qur'an is not ashamed of lawful enjoyment. It is ashamed of enjoyment built on a woman's unpaid right. Clean conscience is part of the gift's completion when the waive is true.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Write the dower clearly. Pay it. Record it. If years later she freely helps the household from her property, thank her without rewriting history as "she never had a dower." Memory of *niḥlah* protects marriages from quiet resentments.

**Link to replacing a wife (4:20–21).** Later the surah will forbid clawing back a stack of gold after intimacy and a firm pledge. Verse 4 plants the root: what you gave as *niḥlah* is not a deposit you casually retrieve. The ethics of exit begin in the ethics of entry.

## Verse 5

**"Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly."**

In simple terms: do not hand your wealth — which God made a means of standing for you — over to those who cannot manage it wisely; but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them with known kindness.

**Sufahā' — capacity, care, and dignity of speech.**

**"Do not entrust the incapable among your dependants with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you."** *Wa-lā tu'tū al-sufahā'a amwālakum allatī jaʿala Allāhu lakum qiyāman.*

*Al-sufahā'* — the foolish, spendthrift, those lacking *rushd* (sound management). The term can include minors, and adults whose waste or impairment makes full control harmful. Classical fiqh develops interdiction (*ḥajr*) carefully; this verse is the moral root: do not put the household's standing-capital under hands that will wreck it. *Amwālakum* — your wealth (the family's / the guardians' managed wealth in view). *Qiyāman* — a standing, support, means by which you stand. Wealth is not only private pleasure; God named it as social and familial *qiyām*. The half-bracketed "among your dependants" is interpretive help for the scope.

**"But feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly."** *Wa-urzuqūhum fīhā wa-iksūhum wa-qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfan.*

*Urzuqūhum fīhā* — provide for them from it. *Iksūhum* — clothe them. *Qawlan maʿrūfan* — known good speech, decent, recognised as kind. Restriction of financial control is not a licence for humiliation. The incapable person still eats, still wears, still hears respect. Guardianship without dignity is another *ẓulm*.

**Who is included.** Orphans under care; also, in wider classical reading, women or men in the household without full financial competence — always a sensitive application that must not become a blanket patriarchy-text erasing competent women's agency (the surah itself will hand women inheritance and dower as owners). Grade applications: the verse protects *qiyām*; it does not declare half of humanity *safīh* by default.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** If you hold power of attorney, manage a parent's account, or block a teen's card, keep the triad: protect the stock, fund real needs, speak *maʿrūf*. No sneering. No "you'll thank me" cruelty. And if you yourself are reckless with money, hear the verse from the other side: do not demand control you will burn.

**Qiyāman — wealth as standing.** God could have said "your property" and stopped. He added *qiyām*: that by which you stand. Pensions, tools, rent-bearing assets, the grocery float — the means of not collapsing. Handing *qiyām* to *safīh* management is not kindness; it is how families fall and then eat the orphan next door in desperation. Protecting *qiyām* is a social duty.

**Speech as part of the ruling.** Many financial rules in this surah end with a speech command (*maʿrūf*, *sadīd*). God knows that guardianship sins begin in the mouth: contempt, jokes about the dependent, orders barked without explanation. *Qawl maʿrūf* is not etiquette class; it is part of *taqwā*.

**Boundary against misuse.** In some cultures this verse is waved to keep educated women from their own property. That misuse collides with 4:4, 4:7, 4:32, and the whole grain of An-Nisā'. *Safah* is a competence category, not a gender essence. Apply it with evidence of waste or harm, not with stereotype.

**Feed and clothe — minimums of care.** Even under *ḥajr*, the dependent is not a ghost in the house. Food, clothing, and kind speech are named so that "protection of wealth" never becomes starvation with a pious face.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 6

**"Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner."**

In simple terms: test the orphans until they reach marriageable age. If you see they are sound in judgment, hand their property back. Do not waste it and rush to spend it before they grow. If the guardian is rich, let him abstain from taking a fee; if poor, let him take a fair amount. When you hand it over, call witnesses. God is enough as Reckoner.

**Rushd, release, fee, and witnesses — the guardianship protocol.**

**"Test the competence of the orphans until they reach a marriageable age."** *Wa-ibtalū al-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥ.*

*Ibtalū* — test, try, examine. Not a single quiz — a period of watching how they handle small trusts, spending, and judgment. *Balaghū al-nikāḥ* — they reach marriage / marriageable age: physical maturity as the classical anchor, with legal adulthood discussed in the schools around puberty signs or age thresholds. The half-bracketed "the competence of" names what the test seeks.

**"Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them."** *Fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum.*

*Ānastum* — you perceive, sense, find familiar signs of. *Rushdan* — maturity of judgment, rectitude in managing. Both conditions: age-maturity *and* *rushd*. Handing a fortune to a mature-bodied fool is not what the verse orders. Withholding from a *rashīd* orphan past due is the sin of verse 2 continued. *Idfaʿū* — push / pay over to them. Full release.

**"And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up to demand it."** *Wa-lā ta'kulūhā isrāfan wa-bidāran an yakbarū.*

Two adverbs of abuse: *isrāf* — wasteful excess; *bidār* — rushing, hurrying (to spend before they can claim). The fear behind haste: "spend it while I still control it." God names that intention in the grammar of speed.

**"If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision."** *Wa-man kāna ghaniyyan fa-l-yastaʿfif wa-man kāna faqīran fa-l-ya'kul bi-al-maʿrūf.*

*Yastaʿfif* — let him abstain, refrain (from taking a wage out of the orphan's wealth). The rich guardian serves without siphoning. *Fa-l-ya'kul bi-al-maʿrūf* — let him eat according to what is recognised as fair. The poor guardian may take a just fee for real work — not a lifestyle transfer. *Maʿrūf* again: known, defensible, not shameless.

**"When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a vigilant Reckoner."** *Fa-idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa-ashhidū ʿalayhim wa-kafā bi-Allāhi ḥasībā.*

Witnesses at hand-over — protection for both sides against later denial. *Ḥasīb* — Reckoner, enough as accountant. Human witnesses matter; God's reckoning matters more. The half-bracketed "vigilant" colours *ḥasīb*.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** If you are a trustee: schedule the test, document the release, invite witnesses, and decide your fee by *ghinā*/*faqr* honestly. If you were once the orphan: you are allowed to ask for the inventory. *Rushd* includes the courage to receive what is yours and the piety not to accuse without cause.

**Two gates: bulūgh and rushd.** Age alone can still leave a person reckless; *rushd* alone in a child is not yet legal release in the classical frame. The verse sequences them: reach marriageable age, then if you perceive *rushd*, hand over. Guardians who refuse to "perceive" *rushd* because they love control fail the *ānastum* test. Guardians who hand over at the first beard without testing fail the *ibtalū* test.

**Isrāf and bidār as twin thieves.** Waste is loud. Haste is quiet and often worse — accelerated consumption with a smile, "while prices are good," before the orphan can hire a lawyer. God named speed as a moral category.

**The rich guardian's abstinence as spiritual nobility.** *Yastaʿfif* is more than a fee policy. It is a posture: I do not need the orphan's purse to prove my service. In a culture of extracting "costs" from every trust, abstinence is witness.

**Witnesses and Ḥasīb.** Human witnesses reduce dispute; *Ḥasīb* means the clean guardian still does not perform only for the room. God reckons the soft theft no witness saw.

**Now bring this into ordinary life — a release day.** Inventory. Test notes. Fee justification if any. Witnesses. Transfer. Prayer. That is verse 6 as a checklist. Use it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 7

**"For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares."**

In simple terms: men get a share of what parents and near relatives leave, and women get a share of what parents and near relatives leave — whether little or much — a share made obligatory.

**Women inherit — the end of erasure.**

**"For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave."** *Li-l-rijāli naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūna wa-li-l-nisā'i naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūn.*

The sentence is deliberately parallel. *Naṣīb* — share, portion — for men; *naṣīb* for women. Same sources: parents and near kin. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Qatādah: in jāhiliyya practice, adult males inherited; women and children were often excluded. This verse breaks that custom at the root. Later verses (11, 12, 176) detail fractions. Verse 7 establishes the principle before the arithmetic.

**"Whether it is little or much. These are obligatory shares."** *Mimmā qalla minhu aw kathura naṣīban mafrūḍan.*

Little or much — no threshold beneath which women may be skipped. A small cloth among ten heirs still has her portion in the law's eyes (even if, practically, heirs later settle by buy-out with consent). *Naṣīban mafrūḍan* — an allotted, obligatory share. *Farḍ* from God, not a favour from male relatives. The half-bracketed "These are" stitches the grammar.

**What this verse does not yet do.** It does not state the 2:1 male-to-female ratio of 4:11 — that comes later and has its own logic (maintenance duties, etc.) which must be explained there, not smuggled here. Here the load-bearing claim is inclusion and obligation.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** In families that still quietly cut sisters out, or pressure them to "gift back" everything under shame, verse 7 is enough to stop the room: she has a *naṣīb mafrūḍ*. Details of fraction come next; the right to be in the room does not wait.

**Repetition as emphasis.** The verse could have said "men and women have shares." Instead it restates the full clause for each. Rhetoric of justice: she is not an addendum in a footnote. Her sentence matches his.

**Little or much — against the "too small to bother" excuse.** Estates are often messy and small. The temptation is to cash out roughly and silence sisters with a gift. *Mimmā qalla aw kathura* blocks the excuse. Obligation scales down; it does not switch off.

**Mafrūḍ — God-allotted.** Family custom is not a higher court. A father who writes a will to erase daughters collides with what God *faraḍa*. (Bequest rules and the one-third limit belong in later discussion; the principle of her share does not wait.)

**Bridge to 4:11.** Readers eager to fight about ratios should first submit to inclusion. A community that will not give her *any* share has not earned a sophisticated debate about half versus full.

**Aqrabūn — near kin beyond parents.** The verse already looks past the nuclear pair to the wider kin network that 4:11–12 and 4:176 will map. Women's shares are not only daughter-shares; they are sister, mother, wife shares in the fuller system. Verse 7 opens the door without yet listing rooms.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 8

**"If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly."**

In simple terms: if relatives, orphans, or needy people are present when the inheritance is divided, give them something from it and speak to them kindly.

**Presence at the division — mercy around the farā'iḍ.**

**"If non-inheriting relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a small provision from it and speak to them kindly."** *Wa-idhā ḥaḍara al-qismata ulū al-qurbā wa-al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīnu fa-arzuqūhum minhu wa-qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfan.*

*Ḥaḍara al-qismata* — are present at the division. *Ulū al-qurbā* — those of kinship (who may not be sharers in this particular estate). *Al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīn* — orphans and the poor. The half-bracketed "non-inheriting" and "small" are interpretive helps: the verse orders a provision *from it*, not a second full share that overturns the *farā'iḍ*. Ibn ʿAbbās and others treat this as a kindness at the scene of division — when eyes watch wealth move and hearts hunger. Some jurists discussed whether the command remains recommended after the fixed shares were detailed; the moral force does not evaporate. Speak *qawlan maʿrūfan* — do not toss a coin with humiliation.

**Connection.** Verse 7 fixed rights. Verse 8 softens the room around rights. Law without mercy at the edges becomes a spectacle of winners and bystanders.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** At a will-reading or estate meeting, notice who sits without a share. A gift, a kind sentence, a refusal to perform wealth in their faces — that is 4:8. Not a substitute for the legal shares. A human rim around them.

**Ḥaḍara — presence triggers duty.** The verse does not require hunting down every poor person in the city. It addresses the scene: they are here, watching the *qismah*. Eyes create a claim of kindness. Ignoring them while gold is portioned is a hardness the verse refuses.

**Not a sixth sharer class.** Careful teachers distinguish fixed heirs from this presence-gift so that *farā'iḍ* remain stable. The gift is from the heirs' generosity or from a recommended slice before or beside division — practiced variously. Do not invent a new mandatory fraction and call it consensus. Do keep the merciful instinct alive.

**Qawl maʿrūf when you give little.** A small provision with dignity beats a large toss with contempt. The verse pairs *arzuqūhum* and *qūlū* — substance and speech.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** If you administer an estate, put a line in the plan: "presence gifts and hospitality for attendees without shares." Budget kindness. It is Qur'anic.

**Orphans at the division — double visibility.** An orphan may already be an heir; the verse still names orphans among those present, because orphanhood is a vulnerability even when a share exists, and because some orphans present may not share in *this* estate. Kindness does not wait for the chart to finish.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 9

**"Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably."**

In simple terms: let those who fear leaving weak children behind them fear for orphans in the same way. So let them be mindful of God and say straight, just words.

**Conscience by analogy — your children, their children.**

**"Let the guardians be as concerned for the orphans as they would if they were to die and leave their own helpless children behind."** *Wa-l-yakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim.*

The grammar is a moral thought-experiment. Those who, if they left weak offspring behind, would fear for them — let them fear (now) regarding orphans. The half-brackets fill the elided object: fear *for the orphans* as you would fear *for your own*. Empathy is not a mood; it is an order to import your strongest parental fear into someone else's child.

**"So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably."** *Fa-l-yattaqū Allāha wa-l-yaqūlū qawlan sadīdan.*

*Taqwā* again — the surah's opening word-family. *Qawlan sadīdan* — straight speech, just, to the point, not crooked. About the will, about the orphan's reputation, about the division: speak level. Crooked speech is how orphan-wealth is often lost before the hand even takes it — rumours, false debts, whispered "understandings."

**A second classical lean.** Some read this also toward those present at a death-illness who might pressure a will that harms heirs — fear God as you would fear for your own weak children, and speak *sadīd* about bequests. Both leans serve the same spine: do not use words to injure those who cannot answer.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Before you decide a matter for a child not yours, run the verse's test: if these were my children and I were gone, what would I want the guardian to do? Then do that. And audit your speech in estate and family meetings for *sadīd* versus spin.

**Law tarakū — the counterfactual as ethics.** The Qur'an often teaches by imagined future: if you were the dead leaving weak children… Import that fear now. This is denser than "be nice." It hijacks the strongest natural fear for moral use.

**Ḍiʿāfan — weak, fragile offspring.** Not only infants — any children left without power. Orphans match that profile by definition. The guardian who bullies an orphan has forgotten his own children's possible future under someone else's hand.

**Qawl sadīd — straight speech as orphan-protection.** Crooked speech includes: false testimony about the father's debts, smearing the orphan's mother to cut her care, "advising" a will that guts the weak. Straight speech is a form of property protection.

**Connection to verse 1.** *Arḥām* and *Raqīb* become practical: fear God, speak straight, treat their weak as you want your weak treated.

**Now bring this into ordinary life — the night test.** Once this week, before sleep, picture your children in a stranger's guardianship. What would you beg that stranger to do with money, speech, and dignity? Write three lines. Those lines are your policy toward any orphan or weak dependant you touch.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 10

**"Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!"**

In simple terms: those who eat the wealth of orphans unjustly are only eating fire into their stomachs, and they will burn in a blazing fire.

**Fire in the belly — the image that ends the opening.**

**"Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans' wealth in fact consume nothing but fire into their bellies."** *Inna alladhīna ya'kulūna amwāla al-yatāmā ẓulman innamā ya'kulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran.*

*Ya'kulūna* — they eat. *Ẓulman* — wrongfully, as injustice. *Innamā* — only / nothing but. *Fī buṭūnihim nāran* — fire in their bellies. The half-bracketed "in fact" marks the translation's emphasis. What felt like food, property, comfort is reclassified as fire already in the gut — a present-tense spiritual truth that becomes eschatological flame. The Qur'an does not say "it will become fire later" only; it says they are eating fire.

**"And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!"** *Wa-sa-yaṣlawna saʿīran.*

*Sa-yaṣlawna* — they will roast / enter the burn of. *Saʿīr* — a blaze, a fiercely burning fire. Future tense completes the image. Major-sin lists in the Sunnah place consuming the orphan's property beside *riba*, murder, and fleeing battle — weight that matches this verse's temperature.

**ẓulman — the key adverb.** Not every handling of orphan wealth is this crime. The poor guardian's *maʿrūf* fee (verse 6), legitimate spending on the orphan, careful investment — not *ẓulm*. The verse targets wrongful consumption. The fear it creates should produce precision, not paralysis that abandons orphans for fear of touching anything.

**Connection to 1–9.** Watcher (1); great sin (2); justice-gated marriage (3); dower (4); capacity (5); release protocol (6); women's share (7); kindness at division (8); parental analogy (9); fire (10). The opening movement ends in the gut and the blaze so that no one finishes these ten verses thinking orphan-wealth is a soft topic.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** If you have ever "borrowed" from a trust, delayed a release to "use the float," or rationalised a swap, stop and restore. Restoration in this world is cheaper than fire in the next. If you are clean, keep the image anyway — as a guardrail, not as a thrill of fear.

**Innamā ya'kulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran — metaphor with teeth.** Is it "only fire" already, or "as if fire," or "leading to fire"? Classical reading allows the eschatological reality as the base: what they eat becomes fire in the gut on the Last Day; the wording makes the future so certain it is spoken as present diet. Either way, the pastoral hit is the same: stop chewing that property.

**Ẓulman keeps the righteous guardian sane.** Legitimate spending, documented fees for the poor guardian, protecting assets — not this verse's target. The verse is a sword against *ẓulm*, not against care. People who refuse to become guardians out of terror need teaching: fear *ẓulm*, not responsibility.

**Saʿīr and major-sin gravity.** Pair this with verse 2's *ḥūb kabīr*. The opening batch brackets orphan-wealth with great sin and blazing fire. Everything middle — marriage, dower, *rushd* — is partly there to reduce the paths into that fire.

**Final turn for the batch.** If verses 1–9 built a house of care, verse 10 installs the smoke alarm. Ignore it and the house burns from the kitchen where the orphan's bread was stolen.

**Now bring this into ordinary life — restore if needed.** If audit shows wrongful benefit, calculate, return, and seek God's pardon. Do not hide behind "it was complicated." Fire is more complicated.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses open An-Nisā' by binding theology to the household ledger. One human origin; womb-ties under a Watcher; orphans' property fenced; marriage gated by justice; dower as gift; capacity respected without humiliation; *rushd* before release; women named as heirs; mercy at the division; conscience trained by fear for one's own children; fire for wrongful eating.

**1: ontology and watchfulness.** *Nafs wāḥidah*, mate from it, spread of men and women, double *taqwā*, *arḥām*, *Raqīb*.

**2–6: orphan property and care.** Give; no bad-for-good swap; no mixing-to-eat; *ḥūb kabīr*. Justice to orphan women shapes marriage (3). Dower as *niḥlah* (4). *Sufahā'* and *qiyām* (5). Test, *rushd*, release, fee rules, witnesses, *Ḥasīb* (6).

**7–10: inheritance door and orphan climax.** Parallel *naṣīb* for men and women, little or much, *mafrūḍ* (7). Presence-gifts and kind speech (8). Fear-as-for-your-own and *qawl sadīd* (9). Fire in bellies and *saʿīr* (10).

**Threads opened for the surah.**

- ***Taqwā* + *Raqīb/Ḥasīb*** — watchfulness as the climate of law.
- ***Arḥām / kinship*** — from womb-honour into orphan and heir.
- ***Yatāmā*** — property, marriage, presence at division, fire.
- ***Qisṭ / ʿadl / ẓulm*** — justice vocabulary that will govern marriage and beyond.
- ***Nisā' as rights-bearers*** — dower owners, heirs, not erasable wards only.
- ***Maʿrūf / sadīd speech*** — kindness and straightness as legal ethics, not decoration.
- ***Mā malakat aymānukum*** — historical frame noted; justice-principle carried.

**Where the supplied translation has chosen, it has been flagged.** Half-brackets ("honour," "when they reach maturity," "due," "if you were to marry them," "content yourselves with," "bondwomen," "you wed," "among your dependants," "the competence of," "to demand it," "vigilant," "These are," "non-inheriting," "small," "for the orphans," "die and," "their own," "in fact") are interpretive aids. "Family ties" for *arḥām* is fair but softer than "wombs." "Incapable" for *sufahā'* is dynamic. "Sound judgment" for *rushd* is fair. "Bondwomen" for *mā malakat aymānukum* is a choice within the historical sense. None is distortion; each is a choice the reader may weigh.

**The verses to leave this opening with are 1, 7, and 10.** You are from one soul under a Watcher — so honour the wombs. Women have an obligatory share — so end erasure. Those who eat the orphan's wealth eat fire — so keep the ledger clean. An-Nisā' has begun.

Five places in this batch required particular care.

**The first is 4:1.** *Nafs wāḥidah* as Adam in the mainstream; mate from it without forcing lab-detail; *arḥām* as wombs/kinship; rib reports as colour; *Raqīb* as climate.

**The second is 4:3.** ʿĀ'ishah's orphan-girl reading as primary climate; four as ceiling not command; justice as condition; *mā malakat aymānukum* historically framed; telos as nearer to not wronging.

**The third is 4:4–6.** *Niḥlah* as obligatory gift; willing remit only; *sufahā'* without erasing competent women; *rushd* plus maturity; rich abstains, poor takes *maʿrūf*; witnesses.

**The fourth is 4:7–9.** Women's obligatory share against erasure; presence-mercy at division; conscience by analogy to one's own weak offspring; *qawl sadīd*.

**The fifth is 4:10.** Fire-in-bellies as present reclassification and future blaze; *ẓulman* as the key; major-sin weight without condemning legitimate care.

**On the translation.** Choices noted above. Openings preserved byte-for-byte from `verses-4.json`. The translation is not wrong; it has chosen, and the reader is entitled to know what was chosen.

Two boundaries observed throughout. No reading of 4:3 has been allowed to become either libertine maximisation of wives or a dishonest erasure of the historical *milk al-yamīn* phrase. And no reading of *sufahā'* or guardianship has been allowed to become a blanket warrant to humiliate women or dependents who have *rushd*. An-Nisā' begins by protecting the weak under a Watcher. The next batch will enter the arithmetic of shares.
