## Verse 41

Verse 41 says: *Zachariah said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” He said, “Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the answer to Zachariah's request for a sign: his sign will be that he cannot speak to people for three days except by gestures — and in those three days he is to remember his Lord often and glorify Him morning and evening.

"My Lord! Grant me a sign" — Zachariah's request follows the good news of Yahya in 3:39–40. Ibn Kathir records the sequence: when Zakariyya heard the good news, he started contemplating having children at his age, asked "How can I have a son?", and the answer "Thus Allah does what He wills" was given. Then he asked for a sign. Al-Jalalayn's treatment of the parallel passages notes the pattern of the prophets' requests: they ask for a sign because the good news is about an event that both exceeds the natural order and is deeply personal. The request is not a doubt of the news; it is a settled believer's request for the confirmation that will anchor his heart and be a living reminder of the divine power at work in his own household.

"Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures" — the sign is the temporary loss of ordinary speech: *alla tukallima al-nas thalathata ayyam illa ramzan* — you will not speak to people for three days except by gesture, by sign. The sign is parallel to what the Qur'an says of Maryam at the birth of 'Isa (19:26: "say: I have vowed a fast to the Most Merciful, so I will not speak to anyone today"), and it functions as a test of the heart: the one who receives the good news is asked to hold the news in silence, and the silence is itself the proof. The classical exegetes note that the silence was not a prohibition of the remembrance of Allah but of speech with people: the man was cut off from the human audience so that he would be turned to the divine one.

"Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening" — the command that fills the silence: *udhkur Rabbaka kathiran wa sabbih bi al-'ashiyyi wa al-ibkar* — remember your Lord much, and glorify Him in the evening and the morning. The instruction pairs the remembrance that is inward and constant with the glorification that is timed and public — the *tasbih* of the morning and evening, the *adhyar* and *adkhar* that the Qur'an commands in several places (e.g., 33:41–42, 20:130, 50:39–40). Ibn Kathir notes the sign's design: the cessation of speech becomes the occasion of the increase of the divine remembrance; the one who cannot speak to people speaks to his Lord. Al-Jalalayn's reading of the passage (on 3:38–41 as a unit) emphasizes that the silence was the answer to the prophet's request for the sign, and the remembrance was the content of the silence.

There is a deep point in the order of the verse. The sign is not a miracle performed on the world; it is a modification of the prophet himself. Zachariah asked for a sign; Allah gave him a condition — the man's own tongue is suspended, and the suspension is the proof that the One who gave him the good news controls even the organs of speech. The believer who requests certainty often looks for a sign in the world; the Qur'an's answer is that the clearest sign is a change in the self — the tongue that stops speaking to people and turns to Allah. And the verse's final instruction is the believer's daily discipline: the remembrance of the Lord often, and the glorification at the two ends of the day. The prophet who is cut off from speech is the model of the believer who is not cut off from remembrance.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 42

Verse 42 says: *And ˹remember˺ when the angels said, “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world.*

In simple terms, the verse records the address of the angels to Mary: Allah has chosen you, purified you, and selected you above all the women of the worlds.

The verse is the first of the announcements to Maryam that begin the surah's account of the birth of 'Isa. Ibn Kathir frames the section as "The Virtue of Maryam Over the Women of Her Time": "Allah states that the angels spoke to Maryam by His command and told her that He chose her because of her service to Him, because of her modesty, honor, innocence, and conviction. Allah also chose her because of her virtue over the women of the world."

"when the angels said" — the address is by the angels, and Al-Jalalayn identifies the speaker: "namely Gabriel." The plural is the Qur'an's standard usage for the angelic address that conveys the divine word — the same form used in 3:39 for the call of Zachariah and 3:45 for the good news to Maryam. The address to Maryam places her in the company of the recipients of the divine message: a woman being directly spoken to by the angels is a woman in the line of the prophets, not in their shadow.

"Allah has selected you" — the verb is *istafa* — the same verb used of the chosen families in 3:33: *istafa Adam wa Nuh* — Allah chose Adam and Noah. The selection of Maryam is the selection of the house of 'Imran: the word that was used for the lineages is now used for the daughter of the lineage. Al-Jalalayn: "God has preferred you, He has elected you."

"purified you" — *tahharaki* — cleansed, purified. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn explain the purification in the context: "made you pure of the touch of men" — the purity that will be the condition of the birth of a child without a father; and more broadly the purity of faith, conduct, and character. The purification is the divine preparation: before the good news of 'Isa, Maryam was made clean — the vessel was prepared for the word.

"and chosen you over all women of the world" — the second *istafa* of the verse, now with the object *'ala nisa' al-'alamin* — above the women of the worlds. Al-Jalalayn's gloss: "the inhabitants of your time" — the preference is relative to her time and the worlds of the narration, as the hadiths specify. Ibn Kathir collects the two famous hadiths of the Prophet: "The best woman in her time was Maryam, daughter of 'Imran, and the best woman of the Prophet's time is Khadijah, daughter of Khuwaylid" (at-Tirmidhi; the Two Sahihs); and "Many men achieved perfection, but among women, only Maryam the daughter of 'Imran and Asiyah, the wife of Fir'awn, achieved perfection" (Ibn Jarir from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari; the Six with the exception of Abu Dawud; al-Bukhari's wording adds the superiority of 'A'ishah to other women like the superiority of tharid to other food).

For the reader, the verse is decisive against every reading of the Qur'an that treats women as secondary to the message. The verse's structure is the same as the verse of the chosen lineages (3:33): the same word *istafa*, the same elevation, the same divine selection. The chosen woman is addressed by the angels, purified by Allah, selected above the women of the worlds — and the surah's whole account of the birth of 'Isa flows from her. The verse also contains the theology of preparation: the one who is to be the vessel of a divine miracle is first made pure; the selection, the purification, and the elevation belong together as the divine preparation of the one who will carry the Word.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 43

Verse 43 says: *O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives Mary's instruction after her selection: be devoutly obedient to your Lord, prostrate and bow with those who bow.

The verse is the command that follows the selection of 3:42. Ibn Kathir explains the sequence: "Allah states that the angels commanded Maryam to increase acts of worship, humbleness, submission, prostration, bowing, and so forth, so that she would acquire what Allah had decreed for her, as a test for her. Yet, this test also earned her a higher grade in this life and the Hereafter, for Allah demonstrated His might by creating a son inside her without male intervention." The selection is not the end of the story; it is the beginning of the worship — and the worship is the response to the selection.

"Be devout to your Lord" — *iqnuti li Rabbiki* — be obedient, be submissive, be devout: the *qunut*, the standing obedience. Ibn Kathir: "As for Qunut (Aqnuti in the Ayah), it means to submit with humbleness," and he cites 2:116: "all surrender with obedience (Qanitun) to Him." The command is not a ritual formula but the posture of the whole person: the obedience of the one who has been selected.

"prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down" — *usjudi wa irka'i ma'a al-rak'iyin* — prostrate and bow with the bowing ones. The verse names the two postures of the prayer — the *sujud* and the *ruku'* — and its phrasing places Maryam in a congregation: "along with those who bow down." The instruction is both individual ("prostrate yourself") and communal ("with those who bow"): the worship of the one selected is the worship of the community, and the *salat* of Maryam is the *salat* of the believers: the same prostration, the same bowing, the same qiblah.

There are two points of lasting significance in the verse. First, the Qur'an makes the prayer of Maryam a model: the woman chosen above the women of the worlds is commanded to *sujud* and *ruku'* — the same prayer the Muslim performs daily — and the verse is one of the foundational proof-texts for the presence of prayer, prostration, and bowing in the religion of the earlier prophets (the *salat* of the people of the Book, including Maryam and 'Isa, is attested in several verses — cf. 19:30-31, where 'Isa says "He has enjoined prayer and zakat upon me as long as I live"). Second, the verse answers the Christian tradition's tendency to separate Maryam from the ordinary worship: the Qur'an places her in the congregation of the bowers. Her greatness is not the fabrication of a new religion but the perfection of the old worship: obedience, prostration, and the congregation.

For the reader, the verse is the model of the response to the divine selection: if the angels say "Allah has chosen you," the commanded response is not pride but prostration. The believer who receives a gift, a station, or a testimony of the divine favor is being shown the pattern: the greater the selection, the deeper the bow. The *qunut*, the *sujud*, the *ruku'* — the worship that is individual and communal — is the form that the acceptance of the divine favor takes; and the verse's "along with those who bow down" removes the worship from the private tower of the saint and places it in the shared congregation of the believers.

There is also a point of detail in the way the command is addressed. The verse does not command the general worship of the Lord but the *qunut* of the Lord, the *sujud* of the Lord, and the *ruku'* with the bowers — the three are the components of the *salat* as the Qur'an knows it, and the verse is one of the places where the Qur'an testifies that the prayer of the earlier communities had the same form as the prayer of the Muslims. The *qunut* is the standing obedience, the *sujud* and the *ruku'* are the postures of the prayer, and the *ma'a al-rak'iyin* — "along with those who bow" — is the congregation. Maryam is commanded to do all three: the personal obedience, the personal prostration, and the communal bowing. The classical picture of Maryam — the woman secluded in the mihrab, supplied with out-of-season fruit, addressed by the angels — is the picture of the woman of prayer. Her seclusion was not the seclusion of the hermits of later traditions; it was the seclusion of the one who prostrated, bowed, and stood in the congregation of the worship.

The verse also has a quiet polemical edge in the surah's context. The Christians of Najran had come to argue about the Messiah and about the place of his mother in their religion. The Qur'an's answer in 3:43 is not an argument but the picture: this is what Maryam was commanded to do — be devout to your Lord, prostrate, and bow with the bowers. The mother of the Messiah is not the object of the worship; she is the model of the worship. The one who is chosen above the women of the worlds is the one who is commanded to prostrate and to bow with the bowers — and the same command that is given in this verse is the command that the Prophet and his community fulfill in the *salat*: the same qunut, the same sujud, the same ruku', the same congregation of the believers.

The verse therefore works as the surah's demonstration of the continuity of the religion: the religion of Maryam and the Messiah is the religion of the prostration and the bowing, the same religion of the worship of the One — and the one who is chosen is the one who bows. The believers are not asked to build a new theology of Maryam; they are asked to see her in the posture that the Qur'an gives: the devout one, the prostrating one, the one bowing with the bowers — the model of the worship of the Lord for every generation of the believers, and the proof that the religion of the earlier prophets and the religion of the later are one: the worship of the One, in the same postures, in the same congregation.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 44

Verse 44 says: *This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺.*

In simple terms, the verse points to the proof of the revelation: this is news of the unseen sent to Muhammad; he was not present when they cast their pens to decide who would take charge of Mary, nor when they disputed about it.

"This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺" — *min anba' al-ghayb* — from the reports, the tidings of the unseen. Ibn Kathir: "This is a part of the news of the Ghayb which We reveal — and narrate to you, O Muhammad." The verse's claim is the argument for the prophethood itself: Muhammad, an unlettered man in Makkah and Madinah, in a land far from Jerusalem, could have no source for the story of the guardianship dispute of Maryam except the One who witnessed it. The *ghayb* — the unseen — is the domain of the revelation: the news of the unseen is the sign that the news-bearer is from the One who sees the unseen.

"You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary's guardian" — *idh yulquna aqlamahum* — when they threw their pens, their quills. The image is the divination of the priests of the Temple: the casting of the writing instruments upon the water, the lot falling to determine the guardian. Al-Jalalayn's account (on 3:37) records the full narrative: the mother of Maryam took her to the priests, the keepers of the Holy House of Jerusalem, and said, "This here before you is the dedication I offered." They competed for guardianship of her, because she was the daughter of their religious leader. Zachariah said, "I am most worthy of her, for her maternal aunt lives with me." The others said, "Not until we have cast lots." All twenty-nine of them departed to the River Jordan, where they cast their quills, agreeing that the one whose quill remained fast and floated to the surface of the water would be most worthy to be guardian over her. Zachariah's quill remained fast and surfaced. He took charge of her. Ibn Kathir also records the version of Ibn Jarir from 'Ikrimah: the mother took the infant to the rabbis from the offspring of Aaron, responsible for the care of Bayt al-Maqdis; they were the ones who cast lots. The verse's point is not the mechanism but the unseen-ness: whatever the details narrated by the traditions, no one in Madinah was there.

"nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺" — *idh yakhtasimun* — when they disputed, contended. Two events are cited: the casting and the disputing. Ibn Kathir: "You were not present, O Muhammad, when this occurred, so you cannot narrate what happened to the people as an eyewitness. Rather, Allah disclosed these facts to you as if you were a witness, when they conducted a lottery to choose the custodian of Maryam, seeking the reward of this good deed." The narrations of the casting and the dispute are the first of the surah's *ghayb* proofs. The second is to come in 3:44's parallel at 3:52–54 — the plot against 'Isa; and the same argument recurs throughout the surah — the Qur'an's reports of the unseen are the sign of the unseen's revealer.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's method in miniature: the story is told, and the story's impossibility for the teller is noted. Muhammad could not have witnessed it; the People of the Book could have, but they were not the ones narrating it; the only narrator is the One who was there. The verse is also a lesson in the discipline of the prophetic narration: the Prophet is not narrating an eyewitness account or a travelers' tale; he is delivering the news of the unseen, and the authority of the account is the authority of the One who saw. The believer who reads the surah is being taught not merely the story of Maryam but the epistemology of the revelation: the unseen is reported by the Revealer; the report is the sign; and the report's truth is the proof of the messenger.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 45

Verse 45 says: *˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the second good news to Mary: Allah gives her glad tidings of a Word from Him whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary — honored in this world and the Hereafter and among those nearest to Allah.

"the angels proclaimed" — the same angelic address of 3:42, now bringing the good news that is the whole surah's pivot. Ibn Kathir frames the section: "Delivering the Good News to Maryam of 'Isa's Birth": "This Ayah contains the glad tidings the angels brought to Maryam that she would give birth to a mighty son who will have a great future."

"Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him" — *bi kalimatin minhu* — of a Word from Him. The phrase is the Qur'an's own name for the Messiah: a Word from Allah. Ibn Kathir: "a son who will come into existence with a word from Allah, 'Be,' and he was. This is the meaning of Allah's statement (about Yahya) — 'Believing in the Word from Allah' (3:39) — according to the majority of the scholars." The Word from Him is 'Isa himself — the created one who came into being by the word "Be" — and the phrase is the Qur'an's answer to the Christian doctrines of the incarnation and of the Logos: Jesus is a Word from Allah in the sense that he was brought into being by Allah's command, not a word of Allah in the sense of a part of the divine essence. Al-Jalalayn: "a boy whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary. He addresses her, attributing him to her, in order to point out that she will give birth to him without a father — for the custom is to attribute the child to its father."

"his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary" — *al-Masih 'Isa ibn Maryam* — the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary. Ibn Kathir explains the name al-Masih: "because when he touched (Mash) those afflicted with an illness, they would be healed by Allah's leave." The name is both title and description: he is the anointed one, the one sent, the one through whom healing would come by Allah's permission. And he is called "son of Mary" — the genealogy that is itself the sign and the argument: "relates 'Isa to his mother, because he did not have a father" (Ibn Kathir). The surah's insistence on "son of Mary" is deliberate: the one the Christians would call son of God is called son of Mary by the Qur'an — and the sonship of the mother is the refutation of the sonship of the Lord, for the one born of a mother is not the Lord of the mother.

"honoured in this world and the Hereafter" — *wajihan fi al-dunya wa al-akhirah* — held in honor, of high standing, in this world and the Hereafter. Ibn Kathir: "he will be a leader and honored by Allah in this life, because of the Law that Allah will reveal to him, sending down the Scripture to him, along with the other bounties that Allah will grant him with. 'Isa will be honored in the Hereafter and will intercede with Allah, by His leave, on behalf of some people, just as is the case with his brethren the mighty Messengers of Allah." Al-Jalalayn: "honoured in this world through prophethood, and in the Hereafter through his intercession and the high stations." The honor is real and it is granted: the Messiah is honored in both realms — but the honor is the honor of the servant, granted by the One who honors him, not the property of the one honored.

"and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺" — *min al-muqarrabin* — among those brought near, drawn close. The phrase is the Qur'an's highest rank of the believers (cf. 56:88, 56:11 — the *muqarrabun* of the worthy). Jesus is among the nearest: the servant at the highest station of service, brought near by his obedience and his prophethood.

For the reader, the verse is the fulcrum of the surah's whole argument with the Christians. The Qur'an acknowledges everything the Christians claim about the Messiah's greatness — the Word from Him, the honor in this world and the Hereafter, the rank among the nearest — and then the next verses will show the boundary of the claim: the Word from Him is a created one; the honor is the grant; the nearness is the rank of the servant. The Christians of Najran came disputing the divinity of 'Isa; the Qur'an's answer is not the denial of his greatness but the completion of it: he is the highest of the created, the Word brought into being by the command "Be," son of Mary, honored, brought near — and precisely because he is a Word from Him, a creation of His command, he is not the Lord who speaks the Word.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 46

Verse 46 says: *And he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy and adulthood and will be one of the righteous.”*

In simple terms, the verse continues the description of the Messiah: he will speak to people in the cradle and in adulthood, and he will be among the righteous.

The verse is the second half of the angelic good news of 3:45. Ibn Kathir's section on the verse is titled "Isa Spoke When He Was Still in the Cradle." The verse makes two claims: the speech in infancy and the speech in adulthood, and both are miracles of a kind — the first is the miracle of the cradle, the second is the ordinary course of prophethood.

"he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy" — *yukallimu al-nas fi al-mahd* — he will speak to people in the cradle. Ibn Kathir: "calling to the worship of Allah Alone without partners, while still in the cradle, as a miracle from Allah." The speech of the infant is a divine sign: the one who cannot yet speak is made to speak, and the first speech is the declaration of the worship of Allah alone — the same declaration the whole surah is preaching. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes place the cradle speech at the moment of the accusation against Maryam: when she returned with the child and the people accused her, the infant 'Isa spoke in defense of his mother and in declaration of his prophethood (the fuller narrative of 19:27-33). Ibn Kathir records the hadiths of the Prophet: "No infant spoke in the cradle except 'Isa and the companion of Jurayj" (Muhammad bin Ishaq from Abu Hurayrah), and "No infant spoke in the cradle except three: 'Isa, the boy during the time of Jurayj, and another boy" (Ibn Abi Hatim). The cradle speech is unique among the ordinary signs: it demonstrates the power of Allah over the earliest stage of the human life, and it vindicates the honor of the mother.

"and adulthood" — *wa kahlan* — and in manhood, in full adulthood. Al-Jalalayn and Ibn Kathir treat the second the same way: the speech in manhood is "by Allah's revelation to him" (Ibn Kathir) — the prophecy of the adult, the message of the messenger. The verse's pairing is deliberate: the same mouth that spoke in the cradle speaks in manhood; the miracle of the beginning and the mission of the maturity are the same prophecy. The word *kahl* — full adulthood, the prime of life — also has a christological force in the Qur'an: it is the age of the prophet's mission and the age to which the narratives of 3:49-51, 5:110, 61:6 belong.

"and will be one of the righteous" — *min al-salihin* — among the righteous. Ibn Kathir: "in his statements and actions, for he will possess pure knowledge and righteous works." The phrase is the same formula used for Zachariah's request of a son "from among the righteous" (3:46's parallel in the good news of Yahya, 3:39) and for the description of the good offspring (3:38: *dhurriyyah tayyibah*). The Messiah is the completion of the household's prayer: the good offspring asked for by the family is the righteous one whose speech begins in the cradle.

For the reader, the verse completes the portrait of the Messiah that the surah is painting: the Word from Him (3:45), the honor in both worlds (3:45), the nearness (3:45), the speech in the cradle (3:46), the speech in manhood (3:46), and the righteousness (3:46). Every element is high — and every element is the description of a servant. The cradle speech and the adult prophecy are the same voice, and the voice is the voice of the one who says, in the same passage (3:51), "Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him." The verse's portrait is the Qur'an's answer to the Christian inflation of the Messiah's station: the higher the description, the clearer the boundary — the one who is described is described as the servant who was born, spoke, and was righteous, not as the Lord who is eternal.

The verse also contains the Qur'an's deepest picture of the unity of the prophetic mission: the man speaks in the cradle to declare the worship of Allah alone, and the man speaks in adulthood to declare the same worship. There is no infancy-of-the-religion and maturity-of-the-religion; the message of the cradle is the message of the manhood. The first word of the Messiah and the last word of the Messiah are the same word: the Oneness of Allah, the worship of Him alone, the straight path.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 47

Verse 47 says: *Mary wondered, “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” An angel replied, “So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!*

In simple terms, the verse records Mary's question and the divine answer: how can I have a child when no man has touched me? The answer: so will it be — Allah creates what He wills; when He decrees a matter, He only says "Be," and it is.

"My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?" — Mary's question is the question of the whole surah's theology, and it is the question of the human reason confronted with the divine command. Ibn Kathir expands her meaning: "Mary said, 'How can I have a son while I did not marry, nor intend to marry, nor am I an indecent woman, may Allah forbid?'" The question is not a doubt of the message; it is the faithful servant's statement of the natural impossibilities: no marriage, no intention of marriage, no illicit contact — and yet the child is announced. The same structure appeared in Zachariah's question of 3:40, and the two questions are the surah's mirror: the old man and the barren wife; the virgin and no man — both are the "how" of the natural order facing the decree of the Lord.

"An angel replied, "So will it be."" — *kadhalik* — thus it will be, so it is. The answer to Mary is the exact answer given to Zachariah: the same formula. The classical exegetes note the symmetry: the one who answered "How can I have a son?" with "Thus Allah does what He wills" (3:40) is the same One who answers "How can I have a child?" with "Thus it is, Allah creates what He wills." The parallel is the surah's argumentative structure: if the old man is granted a child by the decree, the virgin is granted a child by the same decree — and the second is the greater of the two, because the second is the greater suspension of the natural order.

"Allah creates what He wills" — *Allah yakhluqu ma yasha'* — Allah creates what He wills. Ibn Kathir: "He is Mighty in power and nothing escapes His ability." The sentence is the doctrine in one line: creation is the effect of the will, not of the means; and the will is not constrained by the patterns it has established. Al-Jalalayn's reading: "the One with power to bring about something in other than its natural time is able to bring about a child in old age and with those of his family line all deceased" — and by the same power a child without a father.

"When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, 'Be!' And it is!" — *idha qada amran fa innama yaqulu lahu kun fa yakun* — when He decrees a matter, He only says to it, "Be," and it is. The phrase is the Qur'an's most concise expression of the divine creative power: *kun fa yakun* — Be! and it is. The command is not the work of tools, processes, or materials; the decree and the existence are one. For Mary, the child comes into being by the same command by which the heavens and the earth came into being: the command "Be" is sufficient. The Quran's usage of *kun fa-yakun* in the context of 'Isa (and Adam, 3:59) is deliberate: the birth of the Messiah and the creation of Adam are the two explicit cases where the ordinary means of human generation are suspended, and the command "Be" stands as the surah's argument: to create without a father is no harder for Allah than to create without a father and a mother, and to create without any means is no harder for Allah than to create with means.

For the reader, the verse resolves the birth of 'Isa without reducing it. The miracle is real: the virgin birth is the suspension of the natural order and the sign of the divine power. And the doctrine is intact: the virgin birth is not the evidence of the divinity of the child but the demonstration of the power of the Creator. The one who asks the "how" is being answered by the "He says Be, and it is" — the answer to Zachariah and the answer to Mary and the answer to the Christian's argument about 'Isa and to the modern reader's question about miracles: the means are not the boundary of the decree.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 48

Verse 48 says: *“And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel,*

In simple terms, the verse continues the good news: Allah will teach the child writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel.

The verse is the third element of the angelic announcement to Mary (the Word from Him, 3:45; the speech in the cradle and manhood, 3:46; the answer to the "how," 3:47; and now the teaching, 3:48). Ibn Kathir frames it as part of the good news: "the good news brought to Maryam about 'Isa was even better because Allah would teach him the Book and Al-Hikmah." The teaching is the equipment of the messenger: the one who will carry the message to the Children of Israel is taught the message's instruments.

"writing" — *al-kitab* — the Book, the writing, the Scripture. Ibn Kathir: "It appears that the 'Book' the Ayah mentions" — and the verse's ambiguity is resolved by the context: the *kitab* here is the writing of the Book, the literacy of the revelation, and the verses that follow make it precise. The "Book" is one of the two things the Qur'an says Allah will teach him: *al-kitab wa al-hikmah* — the Book and the wisdom — a formula used in the Qur'an for the divine teaching of the prophets (2:129, 2:151, 62:2: "He teaches them the Book and the Wisdom"). For 'Isa, the Book is the Scripture given to him — the Injil — and the Book that preceded him — the Torah — both named in the next phrase.

"and wisdom" — *al-hikmah* — the wisdom, the judgment, the insight of the prophetic understanding: the application of the Book to the life, the discernment of the truth, the ability to judge and to instruct.

"the Torah and the Gospel" — *al-Tawrah wa al-Injil* — the Torah and the Gospel. Ibn Kathir and the classical readings take the phrase as the two books of the revelation: the Torah that was revealed before him (which he confirms at 3:50) and the Gospel (the Injil) that is revealed to him. The verse's ordering is significant: the one who is coming as a messenger to the Children of Israel is taught the scripture of the people he is sent to — the Torah — and the scripture he will bring — the Gospel — so that his teaching would be continuous: the confirmers of the previous and the bearers of the new.

There is also a christological point in the verse's order, which the classical exegetes treat: the Messiah is taught the Torah and the Gospel. He is not the source of them but the receiver of them; the one who is taught is not the teacher of the teaching's source. The "Word from Him" (3:45) is a created one who is taught the Book, the wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel. The teaching is the divine preparation of the servant for the mission: the child who will speak in the cradle is the adult who will confirm the Torah and bring the Gospel.

For the reader, the verse completes the portrait of the Messiah's authority: the one whose name is high, whose speech begins in the cradle, whose birth is by the command "Be" — is the one who is taught. The teacher is Allah; the taught is 'Isa; the materials are the Book, the wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel. The verse is also the model of the believer's learning: the instruments of the message are learned before the message is carried; the one who speaks the Word is first taught the Word. The surah's own process — the revelation to Muhammad of the unseen reports (3:44), the teaching of the Book and the wisdom (in the Prophet's own case at 62:2) — is the same process: the messenger is the taught one, and the teaching is from the Lord.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 49

Verse 49 says: *and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life— by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe.*

In simple terms, the verse names the mission and the miracles of the Messiah: he is a messenger to the Children of Israel with signs from his Lord — the bird from clay that becomes a bird by Allah's permission, the healing of the blind and the leper, the raising of the dead by Allah's permission, and the knowledge of what people eat and store in their houses — a sign for those who believe.

"a messenger to the Children of Israel" — the verse states the mission's audience: *rasulan ila Bani Isra'il* — a messenger to the Children of Israel. Al-Jalalayn: "He will make him a messenger to the Children of Israel during his tender years or after puberty." The mission is to the people of the Torah: the one who was taught the Torah (3:48) is sent to the people of the Torah; the confirmation of 3:50 is the mission's announcement. Ibn Kathir frames the section "The Description of 'Isa and the Miracles He Performed": "Allah states that the good news brought to Maryam about 'Isa was even better because Allah would teach him the Book and Al-Hikmah."

"I have come to you with a sign from your Lord" — the miracles are introduced as *ayah* — a sign — from the Lord: the proof of the messenger's truthfulness. The sign is not the point of the mission; the sign is the credential of the mission. Al-Jalalayn: "a sign, an indication of my truthfulness from your Lord."

"I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah's Will" — the first miracle is the creation of the bird from clay: *akhluqu lakum min al-tin ka hay'at al-tayr* — I create for you from clay something like the form of a bird; *fa anfukhu fihi* — then I breathe into it; *fa yakun tayran* — and it becomes a bird — *bi idhn Allah* — by Allah's permission, by Allah's leave. Every act of the miracle is qualified: the creation is by His permission, the breath is the human breath, and the becoming is the divine command. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn note the reading "a bat" — the classical tradition says he created a bat, being the most perfectly-created of birds — and the point of the tradition: "they would watch it flying, but when it went out of sight it would fall dead — so that the work of a creature (Jesus) may be distinguished from the work of the Creator (God), and that he might know that perfection belongs to God alone" (Al-Jalalayn). The miracle is the demonstration of the power of Allah through the hands of the servant: the power is Allah's; the servant is the instrument; and the miracle's own structure betrays the boundary — the creature's creation is by permission, and the creature's life is by Allah's will.

"I will heal the blind and the leper" — the second miracle pair: *al-akmah* — the blind, the one blind from birth — and *al-abras* — the leper. Al-Jalalayn: "these two are singled out for mention because with both afflictions the person is completely helpless." The healing of the incurable diseases is the sign that the healer is from the Lord: the physicians of the age — "he was sent in an age characterized by medicinal science" — could not cure them; the messenger of Allah could, by Allah's leave. The tradition (recorded by Al-Jalalayn) adds that he cured through supplication fifty thousand in one day on the condition that each person would become a believer.

"and raise the dead to life— by Allah's Will" — the third miracle: the dead are raised — *uqihi al-mawta* — I give life to the dead — *bi idhn Allah*. The raising of the dead is the sign of the resurrection, which the whole surah has been preaching: the One who raises the dead in the Hereafter is the One who raises the dead through the hands of the Messiah in this life. The classical narratives (Al-Jalalayn) add the specific cases — his friend 'Azar, the son of an old woman, the daughter of a tithe-collector — each raised, living, producing offspring; and Shem, son of Noah, who died again immediately. The traditions preserve the detail; the Qur'an preserves the structure: by Allah's permission, the dead are raised, and the raising is the sign.

"And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses" — the fourth sign is the knowledge of the unseen in the ordinary: *unabbi'ukum bima ta'kuluna wa ma taddakhiruna* — I tell you what you eat and what you store. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn: the prophet would tell people what they had eaten and what they would eat, what they had stored and what they would store — knowledge of the unseen at the level of the household and the pantry. The sign is placed last and it is the smallest; the classical commentary notes the pattern: the miracles move from the nature of the clay and the breath, to the incurable diseases, to the dead, to the ordinary domestic unseen — the whole range of the human life: creation, illness, death, and daily provision. The miracles are the evidentiary ladder and each one is "by Allah's permission."

"Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe" — the verse closes with the condition: *in kunta mu'minin* — if you are believers, if you truly believe. The sign is the proof; the belief is the response; and the sign is enough. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes: "This should be enough divine evidence to bring you to acknowledge the truth of my mission if indeed your hearts have been impressed with the image of religious and spiritual virtues."

For the reader, the verse is the surah's catalogue of the Messiah's signs: the bird, the blind, the leper, the dead, the unseen of the houses — every one of the five is introduced by the messenger and performed by the permission of the Lord. The verse answers the Christian claim of the Messiah's divinity with the Messiah's own words: "I have come with a sign from your Lord"; "by Allah's Will"; "by Allah's Will" — the creed of the servant is written into the miracle. And the verse answers the modern reader's question about the miracles of the prophets: they are not the suspension of the divine law but the direct demonstration of the divine power, and they are the credential of the messenger, not the property of the messenger. The signs were given not to prove that 'Isa was divine but to prove that he was sent — and the sign's own clause "by Allah's permission" is the boundary that the whole passage maintains.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 50

Verse 50 says: *And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me.*

In simple terms, the verse states the mission's relation to the previous scripture: Jesus confirms the Torah that came before him and makes lawful some of what was forbidden to the Children of Israel; he comes with a sign from the Lord; so fear Allah and obey him.

"And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me" — *musaddiqan lima bayna yadayya min al-Tawrah* — confirming what was before me of the Torah. The same confirming relationship that the Qur'an has to the books before it (3:3) is the relationship of the Gospel to the Torah: the confirmation is not the replacement of the previous by the later but the attestation of the previous by the later. Ibn Kathir notes at 3:49 the confirmation is the mission's announcement: the one who was taught the Torah (3:48) confirms the Torah — the confirmers of the previous message and the messenger of the new are continuous. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings place the confirmation in the context of the Messiah's message: he testifies that the Torah is the word of Allah, that the covenant of the prophets continues, and that the previous scripture had announced him.

"and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you" — *li uhillu lakum ba'd alladhi hurrima 'alaykum* — to make lawful for you some of what had been forbidden to you. The verse's phrase is one of the distinctive features of the religion of 'Isa in the Qur'an: the Messiah's law relaxed some of the strictures of the previous dispensation. Ibn Kathir and the classical notes see it as the relief of the burden of the Torah's law for the Children of Israel: the Gospels themselves record the Messiah's loosening of the strict sabbath and food laws, and the Qur'an states the principle — the new messenger is the relief of the old law's burden. The verse's "some" is precise: not the whole law, not the Tawhid or the fundamentals, but the particular strictures that were imposed as a test on the previous people; the same pattern appears in 4:160-161, where the Qur'an says the strictures were a punishment for the wrong-doing of the Children of Israel, and 2:185, where the Qur'an itself says the easier is intended.

"I have come to you with a sign from your Lord" — the mission's credential is repeated from 3:49: *bi ayatin min Rabbikum* — with a sign from your Lord. The signs of 3:49 and the credential of 3:50 are the same thing: the messenger proves his mission by the miracle, and the miracle is from the Lord. The pattern of the Qur'an's messengers: the sign — the proof — the mission; and the response required — belief and obedience.

"so be mindful of Allah and obey me" — *fattaqu Allah wa ati'un* — so fear Allah, be mindful of Allah, and obey me. The two commands are the content of the response: the taqwa of the One who sent the sign and the obedience of the one who bears it. Al-Jalalayn and the classical reading: the obedience of the messenger is the form of the worship of the Lord: the fear of Allah and the obedience to the messenger are one response — as the surah states at 3:31–32 (follow me, obey Allah and the Messenger). The Messiah's own command is the same command as the final Messenger's: the one who fears Allah obeys the messenger whom Allah sent.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's clearest statement of the continuity between the religion of 'Isa and the religion of Muhammad: the same confirmation of the previous Scripture, the same invitation, the same command — fear Allah and obey the messenger. The verse also shows the principle of the law's adaptation: the morality is permanent, the law is erasable, the burden can be lifted. And the verse's closing command is the surah's call to the Christians of Najran in the era of the revelation: the Messiah himself said fear Allah and obey me; the Qur'an is saying: the one you cannot obey now is the one whose command you must still obey — and the command of the Messiah is the command of the final messenger, the same worship of the One, the same obedience of the sent one.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 51

Verse 51 says: *Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord. So worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.’”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the creed of the Messiah: Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him alone — this is the straight path.

The verse is the conclusion of 'Isa's address to the Children of Israel that began at 3:49. Ibn Kathir's framing of the section: the Messiah's message is the message of the prophets — the Oneness of the Lord and the worship of Him alone. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings treat the verse as the declaration at the center of the surah's argument: the one who is called the Word from Him, the one whose name is the Messiah, the one who speaks in the cradle and in manhood — his own creed is the Oneness.

"Allah is my Lord and your Lord" — *inna Allah Rabbi wa Rabbukum* — Allah is my Lord and your Lord. The verse's pronoun structure is central: the Lord is mine and yours — the one who is the Word from Him, the one honored in this world and the Hereafter, the one among the nearest, has a Lord; the one who has a Lord is the servant of the Lord. The phrase is the answer to the claim of the divinity of 'Isa: the Messiah himself declares the Lordship of Allah and his own servantship. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn hold the same line: the Messiah's own testimony — "Allah is my Lord and your Lord" — is the testimony of the one who is not the Lord but the one who says the Lord is both his and ours.

"So worship Him ˹alone˺" — *fa u'buduhu* — so worship Him. The command follows the identity: since Allah is my Lord and your Lord, worship Him — not me, not the angels, not the intermediaries. The worship here is the worship with the exclusive object: the worship of Him alone, the Tawhid that the Qur'an places at the center of the religion of every prophet. The verse is the Messiah's own command on the subject that the surah is disputing with the Christians: the worship belongs to the Lord; and the Lord is mine and yours.

"This is the Straight Path" — *hadha siratun mustaqim* — this is a straight path, the straight path. The phrase is the Qur'an's name for the religion of the Tawhid: the path of the worship of the One is the straight one; the worship of others with Him is the crooked. The Messiah's creed is the creed of the Prophet and the creed of the surah: the straight path is the path that runs from the declaration "Allah is my Lord and your Lord" to the worship of Him alone.

For the reader, the verse is the heart of the surah's answer to Christianity's central question. The Christians of Najran came arguing the divinity of the Messiah; the Qur'an's answer is not a counter-argument but the Messiah's own words: "Allah is my Lord and your Lord. So worship Him alone. This is the Straight Path." The one about whom the claim is made is the one who makes the counter-claim. The verse is also the highest expression of the unity of the prophets: the creed of 'Isa is the creed of Ibrahim and Musa and Muhammad — the one Lord, the one worship, the one straight path. And for the reader, the verse is the personal standard: the straight path is not a mere set of rituals but the confession of the singularity of the Lord in the worship — and the confession is made in the mouth of the highest of the created before the Lord who is mine and yours.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 52

Verse 52 says: *When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people, he asked, “Who will stand up with me for Allah?” The disciples replied, “We will stand up for Allah. We believe in Allah, so bear witness that we have submitted.”*

In simple terms, the verse records the moment of decision: when Jesus sensed the persistence of disbelief among his people, he asked who would be his helpers for Allah; the disciples answered — we are the helpers of Allah; we believe in Allah; bear witness that we have submitted to Him.

"When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people" — *fa lamma ahassa 'Isa minhum al-kufr* — when 'Isa sensed from them the disbelief, the denial. Ibn Kathir: "'Isa felt that they were adamant in disbelief and continuing in misguidance." The sensing is the experience of the messenger of the closing of the people's hearts: the miracles had been performed (3:49), the creed had been declared (3:51), and the response was the hardening. Al-Jalalayn: "he became aware of their disbelief, and they plotted to kill him."

"he asked, "Who will stand up with me for Allah?"" — *man ansari ila Allah* — who are my helpers to Allah, who will be my helpers in the cause of Allah. The question is the messenger's call for the supporters: the same call that the final Messenger would make in the era of the Hijrah. Ibn Kathir notes the parallel: "The Prophet said during the Hajj season, before the Hijrah: 'Who will give me asylum so that I can convey the Speech of my Lord, for the Quraysh have prevented me from conveying the Speech of my Lord?' — until he found the Ansar. The Ansar helped the Prophet and gave him refuge. He later migrated to them, they comforted the Prophet and protected him from all his enemies. This is similar to what happened with 'Isa: for some of the Children of Israel believed in him, gave him their aid and support, and followed the light that was sent with him." Mujahid's reading: "Who would follow me to Allah?" — the helpers are those who follow the messenger to the Lord; the most likely sense per Ibn Kathir is the help of conveying the message: "Who would help me convey the Message of Allah?"

"The disciples replied" — *al-hawariyyun* — the disciples, the helpers. Ibn Kathir: "Hawari in Arabic means 'support.'" Al-Jalalayn: "They were Jesus's intimates and the first to believe in him. They were twelve men of pure white complexion; but some say that they were called hawariyyun because they were bleachers of clothes." The Two Sahihs record the Prophet's hadith at the battle of al-Ahzab: "Every Prophet has a Hawari, and Az-Zubayr is my Hawari" — the discipleship of the helper is the station of the supporter of the prophet. The disciples are the first believers of the religion of 'Isa: the twelve who answered the call at the moment the people denied.

"We will stand up for Allah" — *nahnu ansar Allah* — we are the helpers of Allah. The response takes the call and redirects it: the assistance is not the assistance of 'Isa in a personal cause but the assistance of Allah — the support of the religion of Allah. The believers' profession: we are the helpers of the Lord.

"We believe in Allah" — *amanna bi Allah* — we have believed in Allah. The confession precedes the action: the helpers are the believers; the support of the cause is the form of the belief.

"so bear witness that we have submitted" — *wa ishhad bi anna muslimun* — and bear witness that we are Muslims, that we have submitted. The word *muslimun* is used of the disciples at the very moment the surah is addressing the Christians: the first followers of the Messiah are the ones who submitted, the ones who are called *muslimun* — the ones whose religion is the Islam of 3:19, the submission to the One. Al-Jalalayn: "We accept the truth of God; witness, O Jesus, that we have submitted." The verse is one of the Qur'an's most pointed uses of the word: the faithful of the Messiah are Muslims; and the Christians of Najran who are disputing the Messiah are being shown that the Messiah's own disciples bore witness to the submission.

For the reader, the verse gives the picture of the prophetic community at the moment of the people's denial: the messenger asks for the helpers, and the believers answer. The structure of the answer — the support, the belief, the witness — is the structure of the faith: the declaration of belief, the commitment of the support, the witness of the submission. The verse is also the model for every later community: the believers are those who stand with the prophet, who follow him to Allah, and who bear witness to their submission. And the disciples' own testimony — "we have submitted" — is the verse's quiet answer to the surah's central dispute: the true followers of 'Isa were not the ones who argued for his divinity but the ones who bore witness to their submission; the submission is the religion of every faithful of the Messiah, and the Islam of 3:19 is the name of the disciples' own testimony.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 53

Verse 53 says: *˹They prayed to Allah,˺ “Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations and follow the messenger, so count us among those who bear witness.”*

In simple terms, the verse records the prayer of the disciples: Our Lord, we believe in what You have sent down and we follow the messenger, so write us among the witnesses.

"Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations" — *rabbana amanna bima anzalta* — Our Lord, we believe in what You have sent down. Ibn Kathir: the disciples' prayer continues the confession of 3:52: the belief in what was revealed — the revelation that came before ('Isa confirming the Torah) and the revelation that came with him (the Gospel). The prayer's structure is the faith's structure: the address to the Lord, the belief in His revelation, and the following of His messenger.

"and follow the messenger" — *wa ittaba'na al-rasul* — and we follow the messenger. The following of 'Isa is the form of the belief of the disciples — the same following that the surah has commanded at 3:31 (if you love Allah, follow me) and that the disciples have just confessed at 3:52 (we have submitted). The prayer states the two elements of the faith: the belief in the revelation and the following of the messenger — the attestation of the Book and the submission to the messenger. The same pair recurs in the Qur'an's description of the early Muslims (2:285: "We believe in Allah and what has been sent down to us and what was sent down to Ibrahim... and we do not differentiate between any of His messengers, and we submit to Him").

"so count us among those who bear witness" — *fa uktubna ma'a al-shahidin* — so write us down among the witnesses. Ibn Kathir records the reading of Ibn 'Abbas: "meaning among the Ummah of Muhammad" — the disciples pray to be written among the witnesses; and the tradition's interpretation of the witnesses is the community of the final Messenger. The verse's phrase is the disciples' prayer for the community of the witnesses: the ones who bear witness to the truth of the message and the mission. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes: the witnesses are those who testify to the Oneness and the message; the disciples' prayer is the request to be written in the register of the witnesses. The same phrase appears in the Qur'an of Muhammad's own community (2:143: "so that you may be witnesses over the people").

For the reader, the verse completes the picture of the disciples: the confession of 3:52 (we are the helpers; we believe; we have submitted) becomes the prayer of 3:53 (we believe in what You sent down; we follow the messenger; write us among the witnesses). The progression is the faith's progression: the declaration, the belief, the following, and the prayer for the register. The verse also contains the answer to the surah's dispute with the Christians over the followers of 'Isa: the true helpers and witnesses of the Messiah are those who believe in the revelation and follow the messenger — not those who dispute the messenger's nature, and not those who turn the disciples of the Messiah into the founders of a worship of the Messiah. The same prayer is the prayer of every believer of the community of the witnesses: the believers of the revelation, the followers of the messenger, and the witnesses of the truth.

There is also a structural point in the prayer that the classical exegetes preserve. The prayer of the disciples has three clauses, and the three clauses are the three movements of the believers' response: the belief of the heart ("we believe in Your revelations"), the following of the practice ("and we follow the messenger"), and the request of the standing ("so count us among those who bear witness"). The order is the order of the faith: the belief comes before the following, and the following comes before the witness. There is no witness without the belief, and no following of the messenger without the belief in the revelation. The disciples did not first bear witness and then believe; they first believed, then followed, then asked to be written among the witnesses.

The word *shahidin* — the witnesses — also carries the meaning of the martyrs of the tradition: the *shuhada'* are the ones who testify by their faith and their lives, and in the later vocabulary of the religion the witness is the one who bears testimony even at the cost of the life. The disciples pray to be written among the witnesses, and the prayer is answered in the history of the disciples: the ones who answered the call of the Messiah at the moment of the people's denial became the witnesses of the faith in the age of the persecution. The Qur'an's own account of the witnesses (in the story of the believers of the city, 36:13-14; and in the prayer of the believers at 3:146-148) treats the witnesses as the believers who stand beside the prophets and the helpers.

For the believers of the community of Muhammad, the prayer of the disciples is the prayer of every believer of the community of the witnesses: "Our Lord, we believe in what You have sent down, and we follow the messenger, so count us among the witnesses." The verse is also the bridge between the community of the Messiah and the community of the final Messenger: the disciples' prayer and the believers' prayer are the same prayer, because the religion is the same religion — the belief in the revelation, the following of the messenger, and the prayer for the register of the witnesses. The Christians of Najran who disputed the nature of the Messiah are being shown the prayer of the Messiah's own disciples: the belief, the following, and the witness — not the dispute, and not the worship of the one they followed, but the following of the one who said "Allah is my Lord and your Lord" (3:51).

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 54

Verse 54 says: *And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners.*

In simple terms, the verse states the conflict: the disbelievers plotted against Jesus, and Allah also planned — and Allah is the best of planners.

"And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺" — *wa makaru* — they plotted, they schemed. Ibn Kathir's section title: "The Jews Plot to Kill 'Isa" — "Allah states that the Children of Israel tried to kill 'Isa by conspiring to defame him and crucify him. They complained about him to the king who was a disbeliever. They claimed that 'Isa was a man who misguided people, discouraged them from obeying the king, caused division, and separated between man and his own son. They also said other lies about 'Isa, which they will carry on their necks, including accusing him of being an illegitimate son. The king became furious and sent his men to capture 'Isa to torture and crucify him." The plot was the completed crime of the political and religious powers of the era: the accusation, the defamation, the mobilization of the state, the decree of execution. The disbelievers made a plan.

"but Allah also planned" — *wa makara Allah* — and Allah plotted, planned. The divine plan is the answer to the human plan: the counter-scheme that the human scheme cannot see. Ibn Kathir's narrative: "When they surrounded 'Isa's home and he thought that they would surely capture him, Allah saved him from them, raising him up from the house to heaven. Allah put the image of 'Isa on a man who was in the house; when the unjust people went in the house while it was still dark, they thought that he was 'Isa. They captured that man, humiliated and crucified him. They also placed thorns on his head. However, Allah deceived these people. He saved and raised His Prophet from them." The divine plan is the plan that used the plotters' own instrument — the appearance, the arrest, the execution — and turned it against them: the one they thought they had captured was not the one they captured; the crucifixion of the substitute became the humiliation of the plotters; and the Messiah was raised to the Lord. Al-Jalalayn cites the same narrative of the substitution (the man in the house — in the tradition, the one who was made to resemble 'Isa) and concludes: "Allah deceived these people."

"and Allah is the best of planners" — *wa Allah khayr al-makirin* — and Allah is the best of those who plan. The title of the best of the planners is the theology of the verse: the human plan is real and its instruments are real — the accusation, the king, the arrest, the cross — and the divine plan is the deeper one: the plan that uses the human plan for the divine outcome. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings hold the same: the plotters plotted what they thought was the defeat of the messenger; Allah planned the triumph of the messenger through the defeat of the plot.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's central doctrine of the divine governance of the plot. The believers of every era face the plotters: the accusation, the defamation, the mobilization of power, the decree of destruction; the Qur'an's answer is the verse: they planned, and Allah planned, and Allah is the best of planners. The doctrine has two sides. The comfort: the plot is not the last word; the plan of the plotters is enclosed within the plan of the Lord. And the warning: the plotters' plan becomes the instrument of their own humiliation — the same plan that destroyed the appearance of 'Isa becomes the sign of his elevation and the testimony against the plotters. The believers' response — as the disciples did at 3:52-53 — is not the counterplot but the faith and the following: the belief, the support, and the witness; the plan belongs to Allah, and the best of the planners is the One whose plan the plotters cannot see.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 55

Verse 55 says: *˹Remember˺ when Allah said, “O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself. I will deliver you from those who disbelieve, and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes.*

In simple terms, the verse announces the divine response to the plot: Allah said to Jesus — I will take you and raise you to Myself; I will purify you from the disbelievers; I will place your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment; then to Me is your return, and I will judge between you in your disputes.

"when Allah said, "O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself"" — *inni mutawaffika wa rafi'uka ilayya* — I will take you (in the sense of the taking of the soul at death) and raise you up to Myself. Ibn Kathir treats the *tawaffi* at length: "while you are asleep" — the taking of the soul; the same word used in 6:60 ("It is He who takes your souls by night") and 39:42 ("It is Allah who takes away the souls at the time of their death, and those that die not during their sleep"); the Prophet's supplication upon waking: "All the thanks are due to Allah who brought us back to life after He had caused us to die (sleep), and the Return is to Him." Ibn Abi Hatim recorded that Al-Hasan said the *tawaffi* of 'Isa is in reference to sleep: "Allah raised 'Isa while he was asleep." Al-Jalalayn: "I am gathering you, seizing you, and raising you to Me away from the world without death." The verse is the Qur'an's answer to the plot: the one they planned to kill was not killed by them; he was taken and raised by Allah — and the taking is the divine act, not the act of the plotters. The fuller denial of the crucifixion appears at 4:157: "they killed him not, nor crucified him, but it was made to appear to them."

"I will deliver you from those who disbelieve" — *wa mutahhiruka min alladhina kafaru* — and purify you, cleanse you, deliver you from those who disbelieve. Ibn Kathir: "by raising you to heaven." Al-Jalalayn: "I am cleansing you of... removing you far away from those who disbelieved." The purification of the Messiah from the disbelievers is the end of their power over him: the plot of 3:54 ends with the prophet's removal from their reach.

"and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment" — *wa ja'il alladhina ittaba'uka fawqa alladhina kafaru ila yawm al-qiyamah* — and I will make those who followed you above those who disbelieved until the Day of Resurrection. Al-Jalalayn: "those Christians and Muslims who believed in your prophethood above those who disbelieved in you (namely the Jews), becoming above them through definitive argument and the sword until the Day of Resurrection." Ibn Kathir's section "Altering the Religion of 'Isa" continues the analysis: the elevation of the followers of 'Isa above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment is the divine arrangement, and the followers of the Messiah — the ones who believed in his prophethood and submitted — are the ones who are elevated, not the ones who argued his divinity. The verse's "until the Day of Judgment" sets the treaty: the elevation has a term; the final settlement is the return to Allah.

"Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes" — *thumma ilayya marji'ukum fa ahkumu baynakum fima kuntum fihi takhtalifun* — then to Me is your return, and I will judge between you in what you used to differ. The verse's closing is the settlement clause: the disputes of the Christians and the Jews, of the followers and the deniers, of the communities of the Messiah — the argument about his divinity, his crucifixion, his sonship, his mission — all of it is deferred to the return; and the Judge of the return is Allah. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn: the final judgment of the disputes belongs to Allah; the verse is the answer to every claim and counter-claim of the history of the Messiah. The same doctrine appears at 3:58 ("We recite this to you as one of the signs and a wise reminder") and at the end of the surah's section on the Messiah (3:56-60).

For the reader, the verse is the surah's declaration of the outcome of the Messiah's history: the plot fails; the prophet is taken and raised; the followers are elevated until the Day; and the disputes are deferred to the Judge of the return. The verse's significance for the dispute with the Christians is twofold. First, the crucifixion: the verse denies the plot's success — the one they planned to kill was taken and raised by Allah, and the fuller denial at 4:157 is the surah's answer to the Christian doctrine of the atonement. Second, the elevation of the followers: the promise is not to a sect but to the followers of the prophethood — the ones who believe in the revelation and follow the messenger (3:53): the Muslim believers of 'Isa as much as the disciples. The verse is also the believer's doctrine of the deferred settlement: the disputes of the religion are not settled by the argument of the strongest or the power of the state; they are settled by the Judge of the return, and the return is to Him.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 56

Verse 56 says: *“As for those who disbelieve, I will subject them to a severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the verdict on the disbelievers in the Messiah's story: they will be punished severely in this life and in the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers.

The verse is the first of the two verdicts that follow the divine declaration of 3:55 (the disbelievers and the believers). Ibn Kathir's framing: the verse is the completion of the promise to 'Isa — "As to those who disbelieve, I will punish them with severe torment in this world and in the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers."

"those who disbelieve" — in the context of the verse, the disbelievers are those who rejected the Messiah: the Jews who plotted against him (3:54), those who accused his mother, those who denied his prophethood. Al-Jalalayn's reading: "those who disbelieved in you (namely the Jews)." The disbelief is the disbelief in the messenger of Allah — the same disbelief the surah has been treating throughout: the denial of the signs (3:21), the killing of the prophets (3:21), the refusal to submit to the Book (3:23), the claim of the "few days" (3:24), and the plot against the Messiah (3:54).

"severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter" — *'adhaban shadidan fi al-dunya wa al-akhirah* — a severe torment in this world and in the Hereafter. The double punishment is the Qur'an's standard verdict on the persistent deniers: the world's punishment — the humiliation, the defeat, the consequences of the rejection in history (as at 3:12: "Soon you will be overpowered"; as the Jews' punishment at 3:181; as the defeat of Bani Qaynuqa' in the Prophet's era) — and the Hereafter's punishment — the severe torment of the account. The verse's severity is the severity of the persistent rejection of the clear signs: the Messiah's miracles (3:49) were the clearest of the signs, and the plot was the response; the verdict is the double punishment.

"and they will have no helpers" — *wa ma lahum min nasirin* — and they will have no helpers. The phrase repeats the verdict of 3:22 (the helpers of the killers of the prophets was "no helpers") and answers the question of the plot's support: the plotters had the king, the state, the mob, the power of the era — and the helpers are nothing. The only helper that matters is the Lord, and the Lord is the One they plotted against; the helpers of the disbelievers are the helpers they cannot keep — the same helpers that the surah already declared the world's wealth and children (3:10) cannot provide.

For the reader, the verse is the doctrine of the outcome of the rejection of the Messiah: the rejection is not a private opinion; it is the rejection of the clear sign of the Lord, and its outcome is the double punishment and the absence of the helpers. The verse also answers the apparent victory of the plotters: they won the moment — the arrest, the execution of the substitute, the silencing of the messenger — and they lost the outcome: the severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter, and the helpers that were none. The disbelievers' power in the short term is the reverse of their standing in the long term; the verse's order is the order of the judgment: the disbelief first, the punishment second, the no-helpers third. And the believer who faces the plotters of his own era reads the verse as the guarantee: the plot may have the moment; the verdict belongs to the Judge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 57

Verse 57 says: *And as for those who believe and do good, they will be rewarded in full. And Allah does not like the wrongdoers.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the second verdict of the Messiah's story: those who believe and do good will be given their full reward — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers.

"And as for those who believe and do good" — *alladhina amanu wa 'amilu al-salihat* — those who believe and do righteous deeds. The formula is the Qur'an's standard pairing: the belief and the deeds — the inward faith and the outward work — and the reward belongs to the pair, never to the faith alone nor the deeds alone. In the context of the Messiah's story, the believers and the doers of good are the followers of the Messiah who believed in his prophethood and followed him (3:52-53), the disciples, and the faithful of every generation who accept the message of the Messiah's genuine teaching — the worship of the One and the straight path.

"they will be rewarded in full" — *fa yu waffihim ujurahм* — He will give them their rewards in full. Ibn Kathir: "Allah will pay them their reward in full." The full reward is the reward of the complete account: the one who believes and does good is paid in full — the same fullness of the account that 3:25 declared for the gathering (every soul paid in full) and that 3:185 declares at the end of the surah ("you will be given your full rewards on the Day of Judgment"). The reward of the believers is not the reward of the worldly equivalent; it is the full reward of the Lord.

"And Allah does not like the wrongdoers" — *wa Allah la yuhibb al-zalimin* — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers. The negative of the love of the believers: the verse's closing clause is the boundary — the believers and the doers of good are the ones Allah loves; the wrongdoers are the ones He does not. The *zalimin* — the wrongdoers, the unjust — are the ones who wrong themselves and others: the plotters of 3:54, the deniers of the signs, the killers of the prophets; and the *zulm* — the wrong — is the wrong that the whole surah has been exposing: the rejection of the truth, the murder of the just, the fabrication of the religion, the claim of the "few days," the plot against the messenger. The verse's pairing is the Qur'an's justice: the full reward for the believers, the not-liking for the wrongdoers.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's answer to the question of the ultimate standing of the two parties of the Messiah's history: the believers and the doers of good are paid in full; the wrongdoers are not liked by the Lord. The verse also contains the Qur'an's doctrine of the reward-justice: the reward is the full reward, and the not-liking is the not-liking of the wrong. The believer's comfort: the reward of the believers is not the partial reward of the world but the full reward of the Lord. The wrongdoer's warning: the "not liking" is the standing of the one who is wrong in the sight of the Lord. And the verse's placement at the end of the Messiah's story is the surah's answer to the Christians' claim of the specialness of the believers of the Messiah: the specialness is the belief and the deeds — the faith of the Messiah's teaching and the works of his way — and the verdict is the same verdict that the whole surah has been spelling: the full reward for the believers and the doers of good, the not-liking for the wrongdoers.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 58

Verse 58 says: *We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺ as one of the signs and ˹as˺ a wise reminder.*

In simple terms, the verse steps back from the story and points to its purpose: We recite this to you, O Prophet, as one of the signs and as a wise reminder — the account is a sign of the revelation and a reminder of the wisdom.

"We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺" — *dhalika natluhu 'alayka* — this We recite to you. The verse's "this" gathers the whole passage — the story of Zachariah, Mary, and the Messiah, the miracles, the plot, the verdicts — and the verb *natlu* — We recite, We rehearse — is the Qur'an's verb for the recitation of the revelation: the account is the revealed recitation, the *qur'an* of the story. The verse is the surah's self-reference: the story has been told, and the telling is the recitation of Allah.

"as one of the signs" — *min al-ayat* — from the signs, from the verses, from the signs. The word *ayat* is the word for the verses of the Qur'an and the signs of Allah: the story of the Messiah is one of the signs — the sign of the power of Allah (the virgin birth, the miracles, the raising), the sign of the truthfulness of the messenger (the unseen reports of 3:44), and the sign of the revelation itself (the recitation of the unseen by the unlettered Prophet). The story is the sign: the account of the Messiah is the evidence of the One who recounts it.

"and ˹as˺ a wise reminder" — *wa al-dhikr al-hakim* — and the wise reminder, the reminder of the wisdom. The *dhikr* — the reminder — is the Qur'an's own name for the revelation (15:9: "We have sent down the Reminder, and We will surely preserve it"; 38:1: "the Qur'an, the Reminder"), and the *hakim* — the wise — is the reminder's quality: the reminder that is not merely the report of the past but the wisdom of the present. Ibn Kathir and the classical readings treat the verse as the transition from the narrative to the argument: the story is recited as a sign and a reminder — the sign of the truth and the reminder of the wisdom — and the next verse (3:59) draws the argument: the example of 'Isa is like the example of Adam.

There is also a perspective in the verse's double description. The story is a *sign* — the evidence of the divine power and the prophecy of the unseen — and a *reminder* — the wisdom for the present dispute. The sign answers the question of the *kufr* (disbelief): the reports of the unseen are the proof of the revelation. The reminder answers the question of the *huda* (guidance): the story is the instruction of the present dispute with the Christians of Najran. The same event is both: the sign of the truth and the reminder of the way.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's framing of the whole passage: the story of the Messiah is not a biographical digression but a sign and a reminder. The sign: the power of Allah demonstrated in the creation of Adam, the birth of 'Isa, and the recitation of the unseen. The reminder: the wisdom for the present — the same dispute of the Christians, the same doctrine of the Tawhid, the same test of the followers. The believer reading the surah is reading not the history of the ancient but the sign and the reminder of the present: the sign of the revelation and the reminder of the way.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 59

Verse 59 says: *Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, “Be!” And he was!*

In simple terms, the verse delivers the argument: the case of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like the case of Adam — He created him from dust, then said "Be," and he was. The same command that created Adam without father and mother created Jesus without a father.

The verse is the surah's decisive argument against the claim of the divinity of 'Isa. Ibn Kathir's section: "The Similarities Between the Creation of Adam and the Creation of 'Isa" — "Allah said: Verily, the likeness of 'Isa before Allah — regarding Allah's ability, since He created him without a father — is the likeness of Adam, for Allah created Adam without a father or a mother. Rather: He created him from dust, then said to him 'Be!' and he was. Therefore, He Who created Adam without a father or a mother is able to create 'Isa as well, without a father."

"the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam" — *mathal 'Isa 'inda Allah ka mathal Adam* — the likeness of 'Isa before Allah is like the likeness of Adam. The comparison is exact: the two unique births — Adam without any parents, 'Isa without a father — are the two cases the Qur'an uses to demonstrate the divine power. Al-Jalalayn: "This is a comparison of one remarkable thing with another more remarkable, so that it convinces the disputer and establishes itself in one's mind more effectively." The comparison's direction is important: the more remarkable case (Adam — no father, no mother, created from dust) is used to explain the less remarkable ( 'Isa — a mother, no father). If the Christian's argument is "he was born without a father, therefore he is divine," then Adam — born without father or mother — is even more divine by the same argument; and the absurdity of the claim about Adam establishes the absurdity of the same claim about 'Isa. Ibn Kathir states it plainly: "If the claim is made that 'Isa is Allah's son because he was created without a father, then the same claim befits Adam even more. However, since such a claim regarding Adam is obviously false, then making the same claim about 'Isa is even more false."

"He created him from dust, then said to him, "Be!" And he was!" — *khalaqahu min turabin thumma qala lahu kun fa yakun* — He created him from dust, then said to him, "Be," and he was. The two cases converge on the command *kun* — Be: the same command that created Adam from dust is the command that created 'Isa in the womb; the command is the divine method of creation, and the command is the same for the two. Ibn Kathir completes the argument with the threefold demonstration of the divine ability: "by mentioning these facts, Allah emphasizes His ability, by creating Adam without a male or female; Hawa' from a male without a female; and 'Isa from a mother without a father — compared to His creating the rest of creation from male and female. This is why Allah said in Surah Maryam: 'And We made him a sign for mankind' (19:21)." The three original cases of creation — the first man, the first woman, the Messiah — are the three deviations from the ordinary pattern, and the three are the demonstrations of the power of the Creator, not the evidence of divinity of the created.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's argument in one image. The Christian's claim: the miracle of the birth proves the divinity. The Qur'an's answer: the miracle proves the power of the Creator, and the Creator's power is demonstrated even more clearly in Adam — who was created from dust without any parents. The command "Be" is the thing that both cases share, and the command belongs to the One who says it, not to the one who comes into being by it. The verse also carries the doctrine of the *kun fa yakun* forward to the whole theology: the same command that created Adam and 'Isa is the command that creates the universe, that raises the dead, that gathers the Day; the "Be" is the signature of the Creator, and the created is the one who comes into being by the signature. The believer reads the verse and the doctrine is complete: the Messiah is the highest of the created — the Word from Him, the honored, the nearest — and the created is created by the command of the Creator, and the Creator is the One who says "Be."

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 60

Verse 60 says: *This is the truth from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt.*

In simple terms, the verse seals the argument: this is the truth from your Lord — so do not be among those who doubt.

"This is the truth from your Lord" — *al-haqq min Rabbika* — the truth from your Lord. The verse's declaration is the doctrine's seal: the account of the Messiah — the Word from Him, the created by the command, the likeness of Adam, the servant of the Lord — is the truth, and it is from the Lord. Ibn Kathir: "This is the only true story about 'Isa, and what is beyond truth save falsehood?" The phrase answers the whole dispute: the arguments of the Christians and the Jews over the Messiah are the dispute; the truth is the account of the Qur'an; and the truth is from the Lord — not from the traditions of the people, not from the arguments of the scholars, but from the One who sent the Messiah.

"so do not be one of those who doubt" — *fa la takun min al-mumtarin* — so do not be among those who doubt, those who are in doubt, those who doubt the truth. The verse's address is to the Prophet — and through him to the reader: the truth has been declared; the doubt is the only remaining option; and the doubt is the option the verse forbids. Ibn Kathir's connection: "Allah next commands His Messenger to call those who defy the truth, regarding 'Isa, to the Mubahalah (the curse)" — the verse that follows (3:61) is the challenge to the disputers: after the knowledge has come, the dispute is settled by the invocation of the curse of Allah upon the liars. The verse's "do not be of those who doubt" is the surah's warning not to the deniers but to the believers: the truth has been stated, and the believer stands on the truth, not in the doubt.

There is a great subtlety in the verse's structure: the command "do not be one of those who doubt" comes after the truth's declaration and before the mubahalah's challenge. The order is the order of the surah's method: the narrative (3:35-58), the argument (3:59), the declaration of the truth (3:60), and the challenge (3:61). The truth has been established by the narrative and the argument; the doubt is the failure to accept the establishment; the challenge is the call to the disputers to invoke the curse if they persist. For the believers, the verse is the declaration of the certainty: the story of the Messiah is the truth from the Lord, and the believer's standing is the standing of the certainty — not the doubt of the disputers.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's answer to the question of the Christian's argument: the truth has been stated, and the truth is from the Lord. The verse also corrects a common misreading of the Muslims' engagement with the People of the Book: the engagement was not the doubt of the truth but the declaration of it — the truth of the Messiah's servanthood, the truth of the divine unity, the truth of the command "Be," and the challenge to the disputers. And the verse's personal address — "do not be one of those who doubt" — is the address to every reader who encounters the story of the Messiah: the truth has been stated; the doubt is a choice; and the certainty is the standing of the believer of the truth from the Lord.
