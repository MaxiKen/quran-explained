# Sūrah 3 — Āl ʿImrān (The Family of ʿImrān)

*200 verses; Medinan — al-Qurṭubī reports consensus on its classification.* **[Qurṭubī]** **[Ibn Kathīr]**

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* ·
**[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr
al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** Its opening moves from the lettered utterance (3:1) to an explicit confession of divine oneness (3:2), then establishes the Qurʾān’s relation to earlier revelation (3:3–4). **[Maʿārif]** reads 3:1–5 as an opening exposition of *tawḥīd* and the sūrah as an extension of al-Baqarah’s closing plea for help against disbelief. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]** connect the opening eighty-odd verses with the debate over Jesus involving the Christians of Najrān; **[Ibn Kathīr]** identifies the first eighty-three verses with their delegation’s arrival in Madīnah in 9 AH. These reports give the transmitted setting for the opening, while the sources differ in how they describe its span.

**Its merit in the Sunnah.** In a report in Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ calls al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān the *al-Zahrawān* (“the two radiant ones”): they will come on the Day of Resurrection like two clouds or canopies, pleading for their companions **[Qurṭubī]**. Qurṭubī also transmits a report from ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd calling Āl ʿImrān a treasure for the poor person who rises with it at night; its chain includes Jābir al-Juʿfī, whom the editor describes as very weak *(weak)* **[Qurṭubī]**. A report attributed to ʿUthmān promises that reciting the sūrah’s closing verses at night earns the reward of a whole night’s vigil; Qurṭubī notes that this route contains Ibn Lahīʿah *(weak)* **[Qurṭubī]**. He also transmits a Friday-recitation report from Makḥūl promising the reciter the angels’ prayers until evening *(weak)*, and a story attributed to Abū l-Salīl about a fugitive who recited Āl ʿImrān in a snake-filled valley and emerged unharmed *(weak)* **[Qurṭubī]**.

---

## 3:1

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.*

**Meaning.** These are three separated letter-names; the commentators here do not offer a definitive decoding. **[Jalālayn]** says God knows best what He means by them; **[Saʿdī]** likewise counsels restraint without revealed evidence. **[Maʿārif]** places them among the *mutashābihāt*, whose meaning it describes as a secret between God and His Messenger. **[Ṭabarī]** refers back to his treatment at 2:1 rather than repeating it; **[Ibn Kathīr]** does the same. **[Qurṭubī]** says the views surveyed at the opening of al-Baqarah remain possible here, since the following declaration of God’s oneness forms a complete sentence.

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** links the opening of the sūrah with arguments made by Christians from Najrān about Jesus. The account he transmits through Ibn Isḥāq describes their delegation debating the Prophet in Madīnah. **[Qurṭubī]** gives its traditional details: sixty riders, fourteen notables, and three leading figures—al-ʿĀqib ʿAbd al-Masīḥ, al-Sayyid al-Ayham, and the bishop Abū Ḥārithah. He associates the opening eighty-odd verses with this dispute; **[Ibn Kathīr]** gives the number as eighty-three. The letters themselves do not name Jesus, but they begin the passage whose next verse states the point of divine oneness at issue.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** discusses how the final *mīm* is joined to the following *Allāh* in continuous recitation: grammarians differ over the vowel and the elision of the connecting hamzah, treating it as a matter of articulation rather than a change in the letters’ meaning.

**Reflection.** The verse permits faithful recitation without a forced explanation. Its restraint is not indifference: the next verse moves from what is left unexplained to a clear declaration of God’s oneness. **[Jalālayn] [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**


## 3:2

*God—there is no deity but He, the Living, the Self-Subsisting.*

**Meaning.** The sentence confines divinity and worship to God. **[Ṭabarī]** argues that everything besides Him is His creation and possession, so worship belongs to the Creator, not to a created rival. **[Maʿārif]** calls this a categorical statement of *tawḥīd* and reads the paired Names as a rational proof: a dependent being cannot claim ultimate honour and power; only the One who is eternally alive and sustains Himself and all things merits worship. **[Jalālayn]** glosses the Names as “the Living, the Eternal.” **[Ṭabarī]** explains *al-Ḥayy* as the One whose life never ends and prefers *al-Qayyūm* to mean the One who governs, preserves, provides for, and directs every thing, following Mujāhid and al-Rabīʿ. **[Saʿdī]** extends this sustaining to religious, worldly, and created affairs.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** recalls a report identifying God’s Greatest Name in the declaration here together with the verse of the Throne (2:255). **[Qurṭubī]** transmits a report from Asmāʾ bint Yazīd that locates the Greatest Name in 2:163 and this verse. The reports thus preserve more than one identification.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** records *al-Qayyām* as a reading attributed to ʿUmar and *al-Qayyim* in the codex attributed to ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd; **[Ṭabarī]** notes that these forms are close in sense but prefers the mass-transmitted reading *al-Qayyūm*.

**Rulings.** In a legal aside, **[Qurṭubī]** reports that ʿUmar began Āl ʿImrān in the ʿIshāʾ prayer, reciting its first hundred verses in one *rakʿah* and the rest in the next. Mālik is reported to regard dividing a sūrah between two *rakʿahs* as unobjectionable though not the usual practice; Qurṭubī endorses its permissibility, citing the Prophet’s division of al-Aʿrāf across the two *rakʿahs* of Maghrib *(digression)*.

**Cross-references.** The fuller discussion of *al-Ḥayy* and *al-Qayyūm* at 2:255 is noted by **[Ibn Kathīr]** and **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The One on whom all things depend has no need of them; the Names make dependence, rather than power or status, the ground of worship. **[Maʿārif] [Saʿdī]**

## 3:3

*He has sent down upon you the Book in truth, confirming what came before it; and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel.*

**Meaning.** The “Book” is the Qurʾān. **[Ṭabarī]** reads “in truth” as truthful revelation that resolves disputes about the earlier scriptures and the claims made by the Christians of Najrān. The Qurʾān confirms the books and messengers that preceded it; since they share one Revealer, their essential truth is coherent, whereas a book from another source would contain much contradiction. **[Ibn Kathīr]** adds that the earlier scriptures attest to the Qurʾān, while the Qurʾān confirms what was true in them, including glad tidings of Muḥammad’s prophethood. **[Maʿārif]** sees the verse as a reported proof that the prophets before Muḥammad taught the same *tawḥīd*, not a new doctrine introduced by the Qurʾān; **[Saʿdī]** describes the Qurʾān as completing and sealing that message. **[Ibn Kathīr]** identifies the Torah as the revelation to Moses and the Gospel as that given to Jesus; **[Maʿārif]** stresses that the terms refer to those original revelations, not by themselves to the present Old and New Testaments.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** and **[Jalālayn]** distinguish *nazzala* for the Qurʾān’s gradual, repeated revelation from *anzala* for the Torah and Gospel, which they describe as sent down at once. **[Study Quran]** records both this distinction and the view that the two verbs can be interchangeable. It also notes that “what is before it” is usually read as earlier revelation but can idiomatically mean what is present before it. Its discussion distinguishes the original revelations from later interpretations and cautions against treating the verse as an endorsement of every subsequent reading of scripture **[Study Quran]**.

**Cross-references.** 2:41; 2:89; 5:46–47; 6:92; 7:157 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The verse presents continuity without erasing the Qurʾān’s own claim: revelation has one source, while the Qurʾān confirms and clarifies what came before. **[Ṭabarī] [Maʿārif]**

## 3:4

*…aforetime, as guidance for mankind. And He sent down the Criterion. Truly those who disbelieve in God’s signs shall have a severe punishment; and God is Mighty, Possessor of Retribution.*

**Meaning.** The Torah and Gospel were guidance before the Qurʾān. **[Ibn Kathīr]** specifies that they guided their communities in their own times; **[Saʿdī]** describes the Qurʾān as the final guidance that distinguishes truth from falsehood and the path of felicity from the path of ruin. The meaning of *al-Furqān* (“the Criterion”) is disputed. **[Jalālayn]** takes it to encompass the revealed scriptures, which distinguish truth from falsehood. **[Qurṭubī]** and **[Ibn Kathīr]** identify it with the Qurʾān. **[Ṭabarī]** reports that reading too, but prefers “the decisive separation between truth and falsehood” in disputes—especially the dispute about Jesus—because the Qurʾān has already been named “the Book” in the preceding verse. **[Study Quran]** likewise notes several interpretations: the Qurʾān itself, discernment of truth and falsehood (including in the question of Jesus), the Psalms, or a quality shared by the revealed scriptures.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads disbelief in God’s signs as rejecting His proofs of divine unity and of Jesus’ created status after the evidence has been made clear. The warning of severe punishment is followed by the assurance that God is *ʿAzīz*, unconquerable in His rule, and *dhū intiqām*, able to requite those who knowingly reject His signs; **[Jalālayn]** notes that none can prevent Him from carrying out His promise or threat.

**Cross-references.** For *al-Furqān*: 2:53, 2:185; 8:29; 21:48; 25:1 **[Study Quran]**. For “Mighty, Possessor of Retribution”: 5:95; 14:47; 39:37 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** Guidance is a mercy, but clarity also makes refusal consequential: the verse asks its hearers to attend to evidence rather than resist it. **[Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī]**

## 3:5

*Nothing is hidden from God on earth or in heaven.*

**Meaning.** **[Jalālayn]** takes this to include every existent thing, universals and particulars; “heaven and earth” name the whole domain known to human perception. **[Qurṭubī]** expands the claim across what has been, what is, what will be, and what will never be, and makes it an argument against treating Jesus as divine when things are hidden from him. **[Ṭabarī]** applies it directly to the Najrān debate: God knows the disputants’ intentions and what they were attempting to establish about Jesus. **[Ibn Kathīr]** emphasizes that nothing in either heaven or earth escapes God’s perfect knowledge. **[Saʿdī]** and **[Maʿārif]** place this knowledge alongside divine power as evidence that worship belongs to God alone.

**Cross-references.** 14:38; 27:75 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** No hidden argument or private intention is outside divine knowledge. The next verse brings that all-encompassing knowledge close to the individual by speaking of formation in the womb. **[Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī]**

---

## 3:6

*He it is Who forms you in the wombs however He wills. There is no deity but He, the Mighty, the Wise.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** says God fashions each person as He wills—male or female, with differing complexions—and that Jesus, like every other human being, was formed in his mother’s womb. **[Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr]** draw the argument against making Jesus divine: the one who is formed is a creature, not the independent Creator. **[Jalālayn]** notes variation in sex and appearance; **[Saʿdī]** adds fully or incompletely formed bodies and the stages of creation. **[Maʿārif]** reads the knowledge and power displayed in 3:5–6 as proofs that only God deserves worship. **[Study Quran]** likewise notes that the verse is often read as an implicit argument against divinizing Jesus, since he too was once in the womb.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** cites a report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from Thawbān: a Jewish questioner asks about the child, and the Prophet ﷺ describes the man’s fluid as white and the woman’s as yellow; when they meet, whichever predominates is associated, by God’s permission, with a male or female child. **[Ṭabarī]** also transmits an expanded report through al-Suddī, attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd, and other Companions: after three forty-day stages, an angel mixes earth into the embryo, forms it, and writes its sex, felicity or misery, provision, lifespan, and afflictions *(weak)*. The editor notes that al-Ṭabarī had earlier said he did not know this chain to be sound, and distinguishes this wording and chain from related reports in Bukhārī and Muslim.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads “the Mighty, the Wise” as a declaration that no rival shares God’s rule and no one can prevent His judgement. **[Qurṭubī]** also turns the verse against those who treat nature as an independent creator *(digression)*.

**Cross-references.** 22:5 **[Study Quran]**; 23:12–14 **[Qurṭubī]**; 39:6 **[Ibn Kathīr]**.

**Reflection.** The verse places every person’s form and origin within God’s knowledge and wisdom; bodily difference is not self-creation. **[Saʿdī]** Qurṭubī preserves an anecdote about Ibrāhīm b. Adham, who said he was occupied by four uncertainties: which group he belonged to at the primordial covenant, what was decreed for him in the womb, whether he would die in faith, and with which group he would stand at the final judgement *(digression)* **[Qurṭubī]**.

## 3:7

*He it is Who sent down to you the Book. In it are verses clear and decisive—they are its foundation—and others ambiguous. Those whose hearts contain deviation follow what is ambiguous, seeking discord and its interpretation. None knows its interpretation except God. Those firmly rooted in knowledge say, “We believe in it; all is from our Lord.” None take heed except people of understanding.*

**Meaning.** The *muḥkamāt* are the clear, decisive verses; *umm al-kitāb* makes them the Book’s core and the point of return when another passage admits more than one reading. **[Ṭabarī]** calls them the basis of religion and its rulings; **[Qurṭubī]** likewise says an ambiguous passage must be referred to what is clear. **[Ibn Kathīr]** includes among the clear verses those setting out lawful and forbidden acts, obligations, limits, and rulings; he lists abrogated verses, parables, oaths, and matters to be believed but not acted upon among the ambiguous. **[Jalālayn]** gives the opening letters of some sūrahs as examples of the ambiguous, while **[Maʿārif]** treats expressions such as “a spirit from Him” and “His Word” about Jesus as passages whose intended sense is not self-evident. Commentators do not give one exhaustive list: they variously classify clear and ambiguous passages by whether a text has one meaning or several, whether it is acted upon or abrogated, or whether its meaning is accessible to human knowledge. **[Study Quran]** adds distinctions such as universal moral commands versus variable details, and passages known to scholars versus what only God knows; these are overlapping interpretive schemes, not one agreed catalogue.

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] [Maʿārif]** connect the verse with the Christians of Najrān, who cited Jesus being called God’s “Word” and “Spirit” as proof of his divinity; the reply is to read such expressions alongside clear statements that Jesus is God’s servant and created, such as 3:59 and 43:59. **[Study Quran]** also records this argument from 4:171. **[Maʿārif]** specifically cautions against extracting a doctrine of divinity from these expressions while ignoring the Qurʾān’s explicit account of Jesus as a created servant. **[Ṭabarī]** transmits another occasion: Jewish questioners tried to calculate the duration of Muḥammad’s community from the separate letters at the openings of sūrahs. He takes the warning more broadly, applying it to anyone who bends a possible reading away from the clear evidence in order to mislead.

**Language.** *Muḥkam* suggests firmness or determination; *mutashābih* means resembling or echoing other things and can describe a passage open to several readings, not simply an unintelligible one. **[Study Quran]** notes that the Qurʾān can be called wholly *muḥkam* in 11:1 and wholly *mutashābih* in 39:23, in different senses: perfected and internally harmonious. It also explains *umm al-kitāb* as the Book’s archetype or principal source, a phrase also used for the Preserved Tablet (13:39; 43:4). *Taʾwīl* can mean explanation, but also the final reality or outcome to which a matter returns; this difference shapes how the verse is punctuated. In later scholarly usage it could be distinguished from *tafsīr* as deeper interpretation, though **[Study Quran]** notes that the Qurʾān and many commentators use the terms nearly synonymously. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]**

**Belief.** **[Saʿdī]** says the sound method is to return what seems unclear to the decisive passages: all of the Qurʾān comes from God, is true, and does not contradict itself. The heart’s purpose matters: **[Ṭabarī]** understands *fitnah* here as the pursuit of doubt and confusion, not merely disbelief; people with a prior inclination use a text’s possible senses to support what they already wish to claim. **[Maʿārif]** counsels affirming the passages whose intended meaning is beyond one’s knowledge rather than forcing a personal interpretation. Its preferred position identifies the firmly rooted with the *ahl al-sunna wa-l-jamāʿa*, who give weight to interpretations transmitted from the Companions and early generations and leave the rest to God. The firmly rooted are marked by both knowledge and humility, not by claiming mastery of every hidden reality.

**Interpretive dispute.** The Arabic permits a pause after “except God,” making “Those firmly rooted in knowledge say…” a new sentence; this is the majority view reported by **[Qurṭubī]**, and the reading preferred by **[Ṭabarī] [Jalālayn] [Maʿārif]**. Ṭabarī understands the unknowable *taʾwīl* here as the hidden time of the Hour and the end of the community’s appointed term: the firmly rooted affirm that God alone knows it. He argues that the Qurʾān was sent as guidance, so what people need for faith and practice is explained in revelation or by the Prophet; the exact time of an event they do not need to know is what God has reserved. Other scholars join “those firmly rooted” to God as knowers of the interpretation; reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid say that learned people know its explanation, and **[Ibn Kathīr]** cites the Prophet’s prayer that Ibn ʿAbbās be taught *taʾwīl* in this sense. **[Qurṭubī]** reports the pause-after-God view as the majority position, but ultimately distinguishes realities reserved for God—such as the Hour—from ambiguous wording that learned interpreters can resolve through Arabic and the clear verses. **[Saʿdī]** similarly says the pause is right if *taʾwīl* means ultimate outcome, but joining the rooted scholars is preferable if it means explanation. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]** preserve both readings and distinguish explanation from knowledge of ultimate reality.

**Readings.** **[Ṭabarī]** cites Companion reading reports attributed to Ubayy and ʿAbd Allāh that make the pause after “except God” explicit and begin a separate clause, “the firmly rooted in knowledge say…”; he uses them in support of his preferred parsing.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif]** cite ʿĀʾishah’s report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim: after reciting this verse, the Prophet ﷺ said that when people are seen following its ambiguous passages, they are the ones God has named, so believers should beware them. **[Ibn Kathīr]** also transmits a saying attributed to Nāfiʿ b. Yazīd: those firmly rooted are humble before God, seek His pleasure, and neither boast over those above them nor belittle those below them. **[Ṭabarī]** records a report that describes the firmly rooted as truthful in speech, upright in heart, and chaste; however, its editor cites the transmitter’s severe criticism and Aḥmad’s judgment that his reports were fabricated *(weak)*.

**Rulings.** In a digressive legal survey, **[Qurṭubī]** separates those who weaponize equivocal passages to discredit revelation, those who insist on corporeal readings of divine attributes, qualified interpreters who offer linguistically possible readings without claiming certainty, and questioners whose probing is not malicious *(digression)*. He records severe juristic judgments—including takfīr and capital punishment—against the first categories, disagreement over permissible interpretation, and disciplinary admonition for the last. He cites ʿUmar’s punishment of Ṣabīgh b. ʿAsal for persistently probing difficult passages; Ṣabīgh later repented, though accounts differ over the details *(digression)*.

**Stories & occasions.** **[Qurṭubī]** also relates that Abū Umāmah, on seeing the heads of Khārijite rebels, recited this passage and applied its warning to those who pursued ambiguous texts; he wept for them as people who had once belonged to the Muslim community *(digression)*.

**Cross-references.** 3:59; 4:171; 43:59 **[Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]**. For the Qurʾān as *muḥkam* and *mutashābih* in different senses: 11:1; 39:23 **[Study Quran]**. For *umm al-kitāb*: 13:39; 43:4 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** calls returning the ambiguous to the clear a mark of sound understanding; **[Ibn Kathīr]** notes that both kinds of verse come from one Lord and testify to one another. **[Study Quran]** warns that interpreters can call a passage “clear” when it suits them and “ambiguous” when it does not *(digression)*. The verse therefore asks not only what a reader can make a text say, but whether the reading is disciplined by the Book’s clearest teachings and by intellectual humility.

## 3:8

*Our Lord, do not let our hearts swerve after You have guided us; grant us mercy from Your Presence. You are indeed the Bestower.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads this as the continued prayer of the firmly rooted in knowledge: they ask God not to turn their hearts away from the truth after guiding them, especially not to let them imitate those who exploit ambiguous verses to create confusion. The mercy they ask for is enabling and steadfastness in the faith they have professed. **[Jalālayn]** likewise glosses the plea as protection from inclining away from truth through an interpretation unsuited to them. **[Qurṭubī]** records several explanations: they may be asking not to be burdened with trials or duties beyond their strength, and more generally not to be left to a deviation that would bring divine punishment. **[Maʿārif]** emphasizes that their prayer seeks lasting salvation, not worldly advantage.

**Context.** The supplicants are the “firmly rooted in knowledge” of 3:7, whose response to revelation is both faith and humility. Their prayer shows that knowledge does not make anyone self-sufficient or immune to losing the way. **[Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif]** transmit Umm Salamah’s report that the Prophet ﷺ often prayed, “O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion.” When she asked why he prayed so, he replied that every human heart is between two of God’s fingers: He makes it firm or turns it aside as He wills. The report then applies this verse as a prayer for steadfastness; al-Tirmidhī grades the report *ḥasan*, and al-Ṭabarī’s editor calls its chain sound **[Ṭabarī]**. **[Qurṭubī]** also reports that Abū Bakr recited this verse in the third rakʿah of Maghrib during the wars against apostasy, as a supplication for the community.

**Rulings.** From Abū Bakr’s practice, **[Qurṭubī]** notes that a form of *qunūt* may be made in Maghrib and, in a grave crisis, in any prayer; the scholars he cites differ over the regular practice.

**Belief.** The prayer acknowledges that hearts are not beyond God’s power to guide or turn. **[Maʿārif]** stresses that believers therefore ask Him for firmness rather than presuming their present faith is secure. **[Saʿdī]** also links deviation to a person’s prior choice to turn from truth and choose falsehood; when God then leaves that person to the course he chose, it is recompense, not injustice. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]** use the verse in a theological digression against the Muʿtazilite claim that God does not cause hearts to deviate *(digression)*.

**Language.** *Tuzigh* means to incline or swerve from the right course. **[Qurṭubī]** explains “from Your Presence” (*min ladunka*) as a plea for mercy granted by God’s grace, not a reward owed for the supplicants’ own deeds. **[Study Quran]** notes that *ladun* can mean “at” or “in the presence of,” and that this wording later contributed to the term *al-ʿilm al-ladunī*, knowledge granted by God. Qurṭubī rejects the use of the verse to claim that book learning is a veil and that esoteric knowledge makes study unnecessary *(digression)*.

**Readings.** **[Qurṭubī]** records Abū Wāqid al-Jarrāḥ’s reading with the verb assigned to the hearts—“let our hearts not swerve”—rather than the standard wording, “do not cause our hearts to swerve”; he says both preserve the prayer for protection from deviation.

**Cross-references.** 61:5; 9:127; 6:110 **[Saʿdī]**. For “from Your Presence”: 18:10; 18:65 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The prayer follows confession of belief with a request to remain faithful: conviction is not treated as a possession that needs no care. It asks for both steadfastness and mercy, recognizing that guidance is a gift to seek continually. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

## 3:9

*Our Lord, surely You will gather mankind on a Day of which there is no doubt. Surely God never breaks the promise.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads the statement as both confession and supplication: the firmly rooted affirm the gathering for judgement, while implicitly asking God to forgive them on that Day and keep them steadfast in faith and good deeds until death. Their appeal rests on His promise to admit those who believe, follow His Messenger, and obey His revelation. **[Ibn Kathīr]** explains that God will assemble creation, judge their disputes, and reward or punish them for what they did. **[Qurṭubī]** identifies the verse as an affirmation of resurrection; he cites al-Zajjāj’s view that this is knowledge of the promised return accessible to the firmly rooted, while those who follow ambiguous passages deny it. **[Jalālayn]** notes that the prayer shows the believers’ concern for the Hereafter and the reward of remaining on the path of guidance. **[Maʿārif]** likewise frames the preceding prayer for steadfastness as a request for salvation in the next life.

**Belief.** The gathering is certain, and God’s promise is not subject to failure. **[Saʿdī]** says belief in resurrection and requital is foundational to sound hearts: it motivates readiness for that Day and desire for good, while restraining evil.

**Language.** *Mīʿād* denotes an appointed meeting or promised tryst. **[Qurṭubī]** derives it from the root of “promise”; **[Study Quran]** notes its use for God’s promises and threats concerning the Hereafter. **[Jalālayn]** observes a shift from addressing God as “You” to speaking of “God” in the final clause, which may be read as a divine affirmation. **[Study Quran]** relates *mīʿād* to *mawʿid* and to *waʿd* and *waʿīd*—promise and threat—and notes that these terms can refer both to what is promised and to its fulfillment.

**Cross-references.** On the appointed tryst: 13:31; 34:30; 39:20 **[Study Quran]**. For related use of “tryst”: 11:17; 20:58 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The verse turns the scholars’ prayer from asking for inner steadiness to remembering the day on which every person will stand before God. Certainty about that meeting makes present choices matter. **[Saʿdī] [Ṭabarī]**
