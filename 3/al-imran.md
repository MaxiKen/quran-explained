# Sūrah 3 — Āl ʿImrān (The Family of ʿImrān)

*200 verses; Madinan — revealed in Madīnah; the opening section (down to the verse of *mubāhala*, 3:61) was sent down in connection with the Christian deputation from Najrān in the ninth year of the Hijrah, though a few verses — including 3:31 in a celebrated reading — came earlier [Ibn Kathīr], [Ṭabarī]. Its names: **Āl ʿImrān** ("the family of ʿImrān") — where ʿImrān is the father of Maryam and an ancestor of Jesus (3:35–36); and **Ṭayyibah**, "the goodly", in the Torah of the variant [Qurṭubī], [Maʿārif]. It has 200 verses, 3,503 words and 14,525 letters per the Kufan reckoning, 2,003 verses according to some others [Qurṭubī]. The Prophet taught that it and al-Baqarah together would come on the Day of Resurrection "like two clouds, or two black canopies, between which there is a light, or like two flocks of birds lined up in rows, pleading on behalf of their people" (Muslim, al-Nawwās b. Samʿān) [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. "Whoever recites the last verses of Āl ʿImrān in a night is written as having stood the night in prayer" (Dārimī, via Makḥūl and ʿUthmān b. ʿAffān; its chain passes through Ibn Lahīʿa, whose narrations are weak) [Qurṭubī]. ʿAbdallāh b. Masʿūd: "Āl ʿImrān is the treasure of the poor man — he stands with it in the last part of the night" (Dārimī) [Qurṭubī]. The two are called *al-Zahrāwayn*, "the two shining ones", from the light of their meanings to the reciter here, or the full light that will accompany their people on the Day of Resurrection, or — Abū Dāwūd's reading — because both contain God's Greatest Name: 2:255 and 3:1–2 [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. A tradition told by Abū l-Salīl: in a valley haunted by snakes, a man who had incurred blood took refuge there; the two monks on the ridge read Sūrat Ṭayyibah over him through the night, and he emerged unharmed [Qurṭubī]. The sūrah is dense with *tawḥīd* — the opening verses establish the *isnād* of the Book and the falseness of divinity shared with another; the story of ʿImrān's family at its centre, the *mubāhala* and the *āyāt al-taḥrīr* at its shoulders, and the deliverances that punctuate its closing counsel to the believers [Maʿārif], [Ṭabarī].

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* · **[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** Āl ʿImrān was revealed in Madīnah and addresses, in order, the foundations of God's unity against the Christian delegation's challenge, the history of the family of ʿImrān and the making of Maryam, the call to hold fast to God, the *mubāhala* with the Najrānites and the verses that vindicate the Prophet's family, the rules for the community in battle and worship, the fates of those who refused belief from ʿĀd to Banū Qurayẓa, the *āyat al-istighfār* and the great verses on trust (*tawakkul*, 3:159), and the counsel that concludes the Book's longest Madinan exposition [Maʿārif], [Ṭabarī]. **[Maʿārif]** The sūrah reads as the natural sequel to al-Baqarah: the Fātiḥah asked for guidance to the straight path; al-Baqarah answered that prayer by sending down a guidance and answered with the corpus of law; and Āl ʿImrān now treats the Muslims — on whom the prayer was answered — how to face the disbelievers against whom they had asked for victory, verbally and physically. The verse "Help us, then, against disbelieving people" (2:286) was the hinge, and the *tawḥīd* passages of Āl ʿImrān (the doctrine of Oneness that is the basic point of difference between Islam and other religions) are read as the worked-out reply.

## 3:1 — الم

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.*

**Meaning.** The letters that open sūrahs of the Qurʾān are called *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* — "isolated letters", each pronounced separately, without the vowel that the letters normally bear when in combination. **[Saʿdī]** They are among the letters whose meaning is known to God alone. **[Jalālayn]** "God knows best what He means by these letters." **[Ibn Kathīr]** The safest course, and the position of Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān, ʿAlī and Ibn Masʿūd, is that they belong to the secrets God has not communicated; that is also why no explanation is reported from the Prophet. **[Qurṭubī]** They have been read, read aloud, recited and taught from the first generation without anyone ever asking what they mean — and the meaning of the letters of the Qurʾān should therefore be left where God has left it. **[Maʿārif]** Three positions have been held: that they are abbreviations naming the sūrahs at whose head they stand; that they are symbols of the Divine Names; and — the position preferred by the Companions, the Tābiʿīn and the later authoritative scholars — that they are among the secrets of God, whose meaning He alone knows, or which He entrusted privately to the Prophet without its being communicated. **[Study Quran]** The verse-by-verse key not given: 3:1 is empty in the Study Quran (see 2:255 for the fullest discussion of *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm*).

**[Qurṭubī]** The sūrah's opening is *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* followed without pause by *Allāh*: "الم الله", the *alif* of the *basmalah* still serving as the *alif* of *waṣl*. The leading reading keeps the *alif* of *waṣl* and reads *alif lām mīm Allāhu* — the *mīm* taking *fatḥa* to avoid two consecutive *kasras* (Sībawayh); Abū l-ʿAlāʾ al-Kisāʾī: when the spelled letters meet *alif waṣl*, the *alif* is elided and the letter is given the *fatḥa* of the elided *alif* — *alif lām mīm, Allāhu*. Al-Farrāʾ: the underlying form is "alif lām mīm, Allāhu", the *hamza*'s vowel cast forward onto the *mīm*. The reading *alif lām mīm Allāhu* with a *kasra* on the *mīm*, to ease two silents, is recorded from al-Akhfash b. Saqqīt; the *kasra* is heavy, and al-Zajjāj and al-Naḥḥās pronounced it an error. The classical reading of the community keeps the *fatḥa* on the *mīm*. The reading "الم الله" with a cut *alif* of *waṣl*, as if one were pausing at "الم" as one pauses between numbers (one, two, three) without actually stopping, is recorded from al-Ḥasan, ʿAmr b. ʿUbayd, ʿĀṣim al-Jaḥdarī and Abū Jaʿfar al-Ruʾāsī.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** "Read al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān, for taking them up is a blessing and leaving them is regret; the sorcerers (*al-baṭala*) cannot [prevail against] them" (Muslim, Abū Umāmah) — Muʿāwiya b. Sallām glossed *al-baṭala* as "sorcerers" [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. Abū Umāmah: the two sūrahs will come on the Day of Resurrection like two clouds or two black canopies between which there is light, or like two flocks of birds lined up in rows, pleading on behalf of their people (Aḥmad, Muslim) [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]; al-Nawwās b. Samʿān (Muslim): "the Qurʾān will be brought on the Day of Resurrection with those who used to act upon it — Sūrat al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān preceding it — and the Messenger of God ﷺ struck three parables for them which I forgot after — that they are like two clouds, or two black canopies between which there is a light, or like two flocks of birds lined up pleading on behalf of their people" [Qurṭubī]. Asmāʾ bint Yazīd (Abū Dāwūd, Ibn Mājah): God's Greatest Name lies in these two verses — "and your God is One God, there is no god but He, the Compassionate, the Merciful" (2:163), and the verse of Āl ʿImrān, "God, there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsisting" [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. The two are called *al-Zahrāwayn*, "the two shining ones" [Qurṭubī].

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb once prayed the ʿIshāʾ and opened with Āl ʿImrān, recited "الم الله لا إله إلا هو الحي القيوم" (in the reading *al-qayyām*), then read one hundred verses in the first *rakʿa* and the remaining hundred in the second (al-Kisāʾī's chain) [Qurṭubī]. Our scholars noted that one is not meant to recite a single sūrah across two *rakʿas*; if one does, it suffices; there is nothing wrong with it (Mālik) — the correct position is that it is permitted, since the Prophet read al-Aʿrāf in the *Maghrib*, splitting it across two *rakʿas* (Nasāʾī, Abū Muḥammad ʿAbd al-Ḥaqq) [Qurṭubī]. The reading *al-qayyām* is reported from ʿUmar; in the codex of ʿAbdallāh b. Masʿūd the word appears as *al-qayyīm* [Qurṭubī].

**Readings.** *Alif lām mīm Allāhu* with *fatḥa* on the *mīm*: the reading of the community; *alif lām mīm Allāhu* with *kasra* on the *mīm*: al-Akhfash, disallowed; *alif lām mīm Allāhu* with the *alif* of *waṣl* cut: al-Ḥasan, ʿAmr b. ʿUbayd, ʿĀṣim al-Jaḥdarī, Abū Jaʿfar al-Ruʾāsī; *al-qayyūm* and *al-qayyām* (ʿUmar); *al-qayyīm* (Ibn Masʿūd) [Qurṭubī].

**Stories & occasions.** **[Qurṭubī]** **[Ibn Kathīr]** **[Ṭabarī]** The sūrah opens with *alif lām mīm* and the verse that follows because a Christian deputation from Najrān came to the Prophet ﷺ in Madīnah in the ninth year of the Hijrah, sixty riders, of whom fourteen were of their chiefs, in whom three bore the deciding authority: **al-ʿĀqib**, the leader of the group and their counsellor, whose name was ʿAbd al-Masīḥ; **al-Sayyid**, their refuge and provider in adversity, whose name was al-Ayham; and **Abū Ḥāritha b. ʿAlqama** of Bakr b. Wāʾil, their bishop, scholar and head of their seminary (*madras*), who had laboured at their books and been honoured by the Christian kings of Rome with bishopric, patronage, churches and generous provision, on account of his learning and effort in their religion. They entered the Prophet's mosque after the ʿAṣr prayer wearing striped Yemeni cloaks (*ḥabarāt*) over robes and mantles; the Companions said they had never seen a deputation to match them in comeliness or bearing. Their prayer time came and they rose to pray facing east in the Prophet's mosque, and the Prophet said: "Let them be" [Ṭabarī], [Qurṭubī], [Ibn Kathīr]. They debated with him about Jesus for several days, alleging that he was God, that he was the son of God, that he was the third of three — the three positions of Christianity in summary. They argued that he was God because he raised the dead, healed the sick, told of unseen things, and shaped birds of clay and breathed life into them, all by God's permission (a sign for him, āyatan lahu min al-ʿālamīn). They argued that he was the son of God because he had no known father and spoke in the cradle, a thing no one among the children of Adam had done before. They argued that he was the third of three because God said "We did, We commanded, We created, We decreed" — and if He were One, He would say "I did, I commanded, I created", but in fact He is He and Jesus and Mary. The Qurʾān's first words were revealed against these claims: God opening the sūrah by clearing His own self of what they had said, asserting His unity in creation and command, with no associate [Ṭabarī]. They eventually refused the Prophet's call to *mubāhala*, asked for payment of the *jizya*, and returned to their land [Ṭabarī], [Qurṭubī].

**Reflection.** God opens the sūrah — and the sequence of Madinan revelations in which the new community would face the doctrinal challenge of the Christians — with letters whose meaning He has kept for Himself. The silence is the lesson: even the opening word is a marker of God's sovereignty, and the believer's first act before the verse is humility, the recognition that some things are not his to know [Saʿdī], [Maʿārif]. **[Maʿārif]** The sūrah opens with a verse whose sound alone carries the doctrine — *Allāh*, *lā ilāha illā huwa*, *al-ḥayy*, *al-qayyūm* — so that the *tawḥīd* on which the rest of the sūrah turns is recited before it is unpacked. From the very first letters the listener is taught what the Najrānites denied.
