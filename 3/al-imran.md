# Sūrah 3 — Āl ʿImrān (The Family of ʿImrān)

*200 verses; Medinan — al-Qurṭubī reports consensus on its classification.* **[Qurṭubī]** **[Ibn Kathīr]**

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* ·
**[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr
al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** Its opening moves from the lettered utterance (3:1) to an explicit confession of divine oneness (3:2), then establishes the Qurʾān’s relation to earlier revelation (3:3–4). **[Maʿārif]** reads 3:1–5 as an opening exposition of *tawḥīd* and the sūrah as an extension of al-Baqarah’s closing plea for help against disbelief. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī]** connect the opening eighty-odd verses with the debate over Jesus involving the Christians of Najrān; **[Ibn Kathīr]** identifies the first eighty-three verses with their delegation’s arrival in Madīnah in 9 AH. These reports give the transmitted setting for the opening, while the sources differ in how they describe its span.

**Its merit in the Sunnah.** In a report in Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ calls al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān the *al-Zahrawān* (“the two radiant ones”): they will come on the Day of Resurrection like two clouds or canopies, pleading for their companions **[Qurṭubī]**. Qurṭubī also transmits a report from ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd calling Āl ʿImrān a treasure for the poor person who rises with it at night; its chain includes Jābir al-Juʿfī, whom the editor describes as very weak *(weak)* **[Qurṭubī]**. A report attributed to ʿUthmān promises that reciting the sūrah’s closing verses at night earns the reward of a whole night’s vigil; Qurṭubī notes that this route contains Ibn Lahīʿah *(weak)* **[Qurṭubī]**. He also transmits a Friday-recitation report from Makḥūl promising the reciter the angels’ prayers until evening *(weak)*, and a story attributed to Abū l-Salīl about a fugitive who recited Āl ʿImrān in a snake-filled valley and emerged unharmed *(weak)* **[Qurṭubī]**.

## 3:1

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.*

**Meaning.** These are three separated letter-names; the commentators here do not offer a definitive decoding. **[Jalālayn]** says God knows best what He means by them; **[Saʿdī]** likewise counsels restraint without revealed evidence. **[Maʿārif]** places them among the *mutashābihāt*, whose meaning it describes as a secret between God and His Messenger. **[Ṭabarī]** refers back to his treatment at 2:1 rather than repeating it; **[Ibn Kathīr]** does the same. **[Qurṭubī]** says the views surveyed at the opening of al-Baqarah remain possible here, since the following declaration of God’s oneness forms a complete sentence.

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** links the opening of the sūrah with arguments made by Christians from Najrān about Jesus. The account he transmits through Ibn Isḥāq describes their delegation debating the Prophet in Madīnah. **[Qurṭubī]** gives its traditional details: sixty riders, fourteen notables, and three leading figures—al-ʿĀqib ʿAbd al-Masīḥ, al-Sayyid al-Ayham, and the bishop Abū Ḥārithah. He associates the opening eighty-odd verses with this dispute; **[Ibn Kathīr]** gives the number as eighty-three. The letters themselves do not name Jesus, but they begin the passage whose next verse states the point of divine oneness at issue.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** discusses how the final *mīm* is joined to the following *Allāh* in continuous recitation: grammarians differ over the vowel and the elision of the connecting hamzah, treating it as a matter of articulation rather than a change in the letters’ meaning.

**Reflection.** The verse permits faithful recitation without a forced explanation. Its restraint is not indifference: the next verse moves from what is left unexplained to a clear declaration of God’s oneness. **[Jalālayn] [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**


## 3:2

*God—there is no deity but He, the Living, the Self-Subsisting.*

**Meaning.** The sentence confines divinity and worship to God. **[Ṭabarī]** argues that everything besides Him is His creation and possession, so worship belongs to the Creator, not to a created rival. **[Maʿārif]** calls this a categorical statement of *tawḥīd* and reads the paired Names as a rational proof: a dependent being cannot claim ultimate honour and power; only the One who is eternally alive and sustains Himself and all things merits worship. **[Jalālayn]** glosses the Names as “the Living, the Eternal.” **[Ṭabarī]** explains *al-Ḥayy* as the One whose life never ends and prefers *al-Qayyūm* to mean the One who governs, preserves, provides for, and directs every thing, following Mujāhid and al-Rabīʿ. **[Saʿdī]** extends this sustaining to religious, worldly, and created affairs.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** recalls a report identifying God’s Greatest Name in the declaration here together with the verse of the Throne (2:255). **[Qurṭubī]** transmits a report from Asmāʾ bint Yazīd that locates the Greatest Name in 2:163 and this verse. The reports thus preserve more than one identification.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** records *al-Qayyām* as a reading attributed to ʿUmar and *al-Qayyim* in the codex attributed to ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd; **[Ṭabarī]** notes that these forms are close in sense but prefers the mass-transmitted reading *al-Qayyūm*.

**Rulings.** In a legal aside, **[Qurṭubī]** reports that ʿUmar began Āl ʿImrān in the ʿIshāʾ prayer, reciting its first hundred verses in one *rakʿah* and the rest in the next. Mālik is reported to regard dividing a sūrah between two *rakʿahs* as unobjectionable though not the usual practice; Qurṭubī endorses its permissibility, citing the Prophet’s division of al-Aʿrāf across the two *rakʿahs* of Maghrib *(digression)*.

**Cross-references.** The fuller discussion of *al-Ḥayy* and *al-Qayyūm* at 2:255 is noted by **[Ibn Kathīr]** and **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The One on whom all things depend has no need of them; the Names make dependence, rather than power or status, the ground of worship. **[Maʿārif] [Saʿdī]**

## 3:3

*He has sent down upon you the Book in truth, confirming what came before it; and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel.*

**Meaning.** The “Book” is the Qurʾān. **[Ṭabarī]** reads “in truth” as truthful revelation that resolves disputes about the earlier scriptures and the claims made by the Christians of Najrān. The Qurʾān confirms the books and messengers that preceded it; since they share one Revealer, their essential truth is coherent, whereas a book from another source would contain much contradiction. **[Ibn Kathīr]** adds that the earlier scriptures attest to the Qurʾān, while the Qurʾān confirms what was true in them, including glad tidings of Muḥammad’s prophethood. **[Maʿārif]** sees the verse as a reported proof that the prophets before Muḥammad taught the same *tawḥīd*, not a new doctrine introduced by the Qurʾān; **[Saʿdī]** describes the Qurʾān as completing and sealing that message. **[Ibn Kathīr]** identifies the Torah as the revelation to Moses and the Gospel as that given to Jesus; **[Maʿārif]** stresses that the terms refer to those original revelations, not by themselves to the present Old and New Testaments.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** and **[Jalālayn]** distinguish *nazzala* for the Qurʾān’s gradual, repeated revelation from *anzala* for the Torah and Gospel, which they describe as sent down at once. **[Study Quran]** records both this distinction and the view that the two verbs can be interchangeable. It also notes that “what is before it” is usually read as earlier revelation but can idiomatically mean what is present before it. Its discussion distinguishes the original revelations from later interpretations and cautions against treating the verse as an endorsement of every subsequent reading of scripture **[Study Quran]**.

**Cross-references.** 2:41; 2:89; 5:46–47; 6:92; 7:157 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The verse presents continuity without erasing the Qurʾān’s own claim: revelation has one source, while the Qurʾān confirms and clarifies what came before. **[Ṭabarī] [Maʿārif]**

## 3:4

*…aforetime, as guidance for mankind. And He sent down the Criterion. Truly those who disbelieve in God’s signs shall have a severe punishment; and God is Mighty, Possessor of Retribution.*

**Meaning.** The Torah and Gospel were guidance before the Qurʾān. **[Ibn Kathīr]** specifies that they guided their communities in their own times; **[Saʿdī]** describes the Qurʾān as the final guidance that distinguishes truth from falsehood and the path of felicity from the path of ruin. The meaning of *al-Furqān* (“the Criterion”) is disputed. **[Jalālayn]** takes it to encompass the revealed scriptures, which distinguish truth from falsehood. **[Qurṭubī]** and **[Ibn Kathīr]** identify it with the Qurʾān. **[Ṭabarī]** reports that reading too, but prefers “the decisive separation between truth and falsehood” in disputes—especially the dispute about Jesus—because the Qurʾān has already been named “the Book” in the preceding verse. **[Study Quran]** likewise notes several interpretations: the Qurʾān itself, discernment of truth and falsehood (including in the question of Jesus), the Psalms, or a quality shared by the revealed scriptures.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads disbelief in God’s signs as rejecting His proofs of divine unity and of Jesus’ created status after the evidence has been made clear. The warning of severe punishment is followed by the assurance that God is *ʿAzīz*, unconquerable in His rule, and *dhū intiqām*, able to requite those who knowingly reject His signs; **[Jalālayn]** notes that none can prevent Him from carrying out His promise or threat.

**Cross-references.** For *al-Furqān*: 2:53, 2:185; 8:29; 21:48; 25:1 **[Study Quran]**. For “Mighty, Possessor of Retribution”: 5:95; 14:47; 39:37 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** Guidance is a mercy, but clarity also makes refusal consequential: the verse asks its hearers to attend to evidence rather than resist it. **[Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī]**
