# Sūrah 3 — Āl ʿImrān (The Family of ʿImrān)

*200 verses; Madinan — al-Qurṭubī records consensus that the sūrah is Madinan, and Ibn Kathīr notes that its first eighty-three verses concern the Christian delegation from Najrān that reached Madīnah in the ninth year after the Hijrah **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. It is one of "the two luminous ones", al-Zahrāwān, paired with al-Baqarah in the well-known ḥadīth **[Qurṭubī]**.*

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* ·
**[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr
al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** Āl ʿImrān is named for the household of ʿImrān, the line that produced Mary and Jesus, and its opening eighty-odd verses are directed at the Christian disputants from Najrān who argued with the Prophet about Jesus **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. After the opening declaration of God's oneness, life and self-subsistence (vv. 1–6), it states the Qurʾān's own distinction between decisive and ambiguous verses and warns against those who pursue the latter (v. 7) **[Study Quran]**, **[Ṭabarī]**. It then moves through the stories of Zachariah, Mary and Jesus (vv. 33–63), the call to the People of the Book to a common word (v. 64), a long polemic against their claims and conduct, the command to consult them and the battle of Uḥud with its aftermath, closing with the call to perseverance and the promise to those who remember God **[Study Quran]**, **[Maʿārif]**. Its place in the whole is, in Maʿārif's reading, an expansion of the petition at the end of al-Baqarah — "help us against the disbelieving people" — since it sets out how the community is to deal with its opponents, by argument and, where required, by struggle **[Maʿārif]**. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Naqqāsh reported that its name in the Torah is *Ṭayyiba*, and the reports on its merit are gathered at its opening below.

---

## 3:1 — الم

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.*

**Meaning.** The sūrah opens, like al-Baqarah, with three isolated letters. **[Ṭabarī]** He begins by referring back to his treatment of *alif-lām-mīm* and of the Name *Allāh* at the opening of Sūrah 2, judging what he said there sufficient so that it need not be repeated, and he turns instead to the declaration that follows. **[Maʿārif]** The letters belong to the *mutashābihāt* — expressions of hidden meaning whose reality is a secret between God and His Messenger, the discussion of which the sūrah itself supplies a little later, at v. 7 — and the words that immediately follow them, *lā ilāha illā Huwa*, state the doctrine of *tawḥīd* outright: there is absolutely nothing worthy of worship besides God. **[Jalālayn]** "God knows best what He means by these letters." **[Saʿdī]** They are among the letters whose meaning none knows but God; he heads his section on the sūrah with the note that it is Madinan. **[Maʿārif]** The first five verses as a whole expound *tawḥīd*, the single point on which Islam differs from other religions and by which a believer is distinguished from a disbeliever: whoever affirms God's oneness together with all His prophets is a Muslim, and whoever does not is not. **[Study Quran]** Its introduction places the sūrah in the Madinan period, notes that it is named for the "house" of ʿImrān mentioned at v. 33, and observes that it carries the Qurʾān's most direct discussion of how scripture is to be interpreted (v. 7) — so the letters at its head are read by the commentators in a sūrah that will itself ask what is decisive in revelation and what is ambiguous, and who may interpret it. **[Qurṭubī]** He points out that because this sūrah contains the self-standing sentence "God, there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsistent", every view recorded on the opening letters in al-Baqarah is applicable here too.

**Context.** The opening of the sūrah is tied by the earliest sources to the Christian delegation of Najrān. **[Ṭabarī]** Through Muḥammad b. Isḥāq, from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr: sixty riders came to the Prophet in Madīnah — fourteen of them notables, and among the fourteen three men to whom authority returned: al-ʿĀqib, the commander of the people, their man of judgement and counsel, named ʿAbd al-Masīḥ; al-Sayyid, their mainstay and the master of their assembly, named al-Ayham; and Abū Ḥāritha b. ʿAlqama of Bakr b. Wāʾil, their bishop, scholar, prayer-leader and man of their school, whom the Christian Roman kings had honoured, funded and built churches for. **[Qurṭubī]** They entered upon him after the ʿAṣr prayer wearing robes and cloaks of striped Yemeni cloth, and his Companions said they had never seen a delegation like them for bearing and dignity; when their time of prayer came they stood and prayed in the Prophet's mosque facing east, and he said, "Leave them." **[Ṭabarī]** They argued with him about Jesus, claiming he was God, that he was the son of God, and that he was the third of three — arguing for the first from his raising the dead, healing the sick, telling of hidden things and making a bird from clay by God's leave; for the second from his having no known father and speaking in the cradle; and for the third from God's saying "We did", "We commanded", "We created", "We decreed". **[Qurṭubī]** Some eighty-odd verses from the beginning of this sūrah were revealed about them and their claims. **[Ṭabarī]** When they refused to accept the truth after the proof was set out, the Prophet called them to the *mubāhala*; they declined, asked that the *jizyah* be accepted from them, and he accepted it and they returned to their land. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He dates the delegation to the ninth year after the Hijrah and says the first eighty-three verses of the sūrah relate to it. **[Ṭabarī]** He adds that although the argument was aimed at them, everyone who shares their description — taking a lord, god or object of worship other than God — is included in the proof God set out.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** Muslim narrated from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān al-Kilābī that the Prophet said the Qurʾān and its people will be brought on the Day of Resurrection, preceded by Sūrah al-Baqarah and Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, and he struck three similitudes for them — as two clouds, or two black shades with light between them, or two flocks of birds with wings spread — pleading on behalf of their companions; and Muslim also narrated from Abū Umāmah al-Bāhilī, "Recite the Qurʾān, for it comes as an intercessor for its people on the Day of Resurrection; recite the two luminous ones, al-Baqarah and Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, for they will come like two clouds or two shades or two flocks of birds pleading for their companions." **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Dārimī narrated from ʿAbdallāh b. Masʿūd, "What an excellent treasure for the poor man is Sūrah Āl ʿImrān: he stands with it at the end of the night" *(its chain runs through Jābir al-Juʿfī, whom Qurṭubī's editor records as weak and given to confusion)*; a report from Makhūl — *mursal* — says that whoever recites it on Friday, the angels bless him until evening; and a report from ʿUthmān b. ʿAffān says that whoever recites the end of Sūrah Āl ʿImrān in a night is credited with a night of standing *(its chain contains Ibn Lahīʿah, whose transmissions are disputed)*. **[Qurṭubī]** A story is told of a man who had shed blood and took refuge in the valley of Majanna, a valley in which no one walked without being struck by a snake; two monks on its rim said he was surely lost, then saw him open Sūrah Āl ʿImrān — one saying, "He is reciting Sūrah Ṭayyiba, perhaps he will be saved" — and he came out unharmed *(a pious anecdote, not a basis for rulings)*. **[Qurṭubī]** Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah narrated from Asmāʾ bint Yazīd that the Greatest Name of God is in two verses: "Your God is one God, there is no god but He, the Compassionate, the Merciful" and "God, there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsistent" — the latter being the opening of this sūrah. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He says the same, that the Greatest Name is contained in these two verses, referring to his discussion of Āyat al-Kursī.

**Readings.** **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Ḥasan, ʿAmr b. ʿUbayd, ʿĀṣim b. Abī al-Najūd and Abū Jaʿfar al-Ruʾāsī recited "Alif Lām Mīm. Allāhu" cutting the connective *alif*, as though stopping on the letters, in the way one stops on the names of numbers while intending to continue; al-Akhfash permitted *kasrah* on the *mīm* to avoid two quiescent letters meeting, but al-Zajjāj called that an error and said the Arabs do not say it because it is heavy. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Naḥḥās reports that the common reading is the one the early grammarians discussed: Sībawayhi held that the *mīm* was opened with *fatḥah* to avoid two quiescents, choosing *fatḥah* so as not to combine a *kasrah* with a following *yāʾ* and preceding *kasrah*; al-Kisāʾī held that when a connective *alif* meets the spelled-out letters it is dropped and the letter is moved with the vowel of that *alif*; and al-Farrāʾ held that the original is as al-Ruʾāsī recited it, and the *hamzah*'s vowel was then thrown onto the *mīm*. **[Ṭabarī]** The reading that is not permissible to depart from, in his view, is the one transmitted by the reciters of the Muslim cities by widespread transmission and fixed in their codices.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** On the title *al-Zahrāwān* given to al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān, three views are recorded: that the two are the shining ones, from *zahr*, either because of the guidance their reader receives from their lights — that is, their meanings — or because of the complete light that accrues from reciting them on the Day of Resurrection; and that they are so named because they share the Greatest Name of God. **[Qurṭubī]** The likeness of the two sūrahs to two clouds means, in his explanation, that their reader is in the shade of their reward, just as it is said that a man is in the shade of his charity; their being "black" is from their density, which shades those beneath from the sun's heat. **[Maʿārif]** The sūrah's opening thus links back to the close of the one before it: the prayer for guidance in al-Fātiḥah was answered by the sending down of the Qurʾān in al-Baqarah, and al-Baqarah's closing petition for help against the disbelievers is answered here by instruction in how that confrontation is to be conducted.

**Reflection.** The sūrah that will argue with the People of the Book opens not with a proof but with letters whose meaning God kept to Himself — a reminder that revelation comes with both plain guidance and matters entrusted to God alone, and that faith begins by accepting both **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Saʿdī]**. It opens, too, by denying that divinity belongs to any but the One who is living and self-subsistent, which is the ground on which every later argument of the sūrah about Jesus rests **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Maʿārif]**. To read these letters with *al-Baqarah* behind them and the story of Najrān ahead is to be told at the outset that the debate about Jesus is settled not by marvels but by who alone is worthy of worship **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Study Quran]**.

## 3:2 — ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ ٱلْقَيُّومُ

*God — there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsistent.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "There is no god but He" is information from God to His servants that divinity belongs to Him alone to the exclusion of all gods and rivals, and that worship is neither valid nor permissible for any other, because He is alone in lordship and unique in divinity; everything besides Him is His possession and His creation, with no partner in His authority. He makes this an argument against them: since that is so, it is not permissible for them to worship another or to associate anyone with Him in His authority, when every object of worship besides Him is owned and every revered thing besides Him is created, and it belongs to the owned to single out his owner with obedience. **[Ṭabarī]** He reports that God began this sūrah with this denial and this self-description as an argument against a party of Christians who came from Najrān and disputed with the Prophet about Jesus and deviated concerning God. **[Jalālayn]** "God! There is no god except Him, the Living, the Eternal." **[Maʿārif]** The two names that follow supply a rational proof of oneness: worship is to present oneself before another in utter submission and humility, and that requires the one worshipped to stand at the highest point of honour and power and to be most perfect from every angle; whatever cannot sustain its own being but depends on another for its very existence can claim no honour or power in its own right. It follows that idols carved in stone, water, trees, even angels and messengers — none of them is worthy of worship; the only being worthy of worship is the One who has always been alive and present and always will be, self-subsisting, and that is God alone.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** On *al-Ḥayy*: one group said it describes Him by remaining and negates the death that overtakes every living thing besides Him — Muḥammad b. Isḥāq, from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr, glossed it "the One who does not die, while Jesus died and was crucified in the claim of those scholars of theirs who argued with the Prophet", and al-Rabīʿ said "living, He does not die". **[Ṭabarī]** Others said it means that the governing of everything He wills is easy for Him and nothing He intends is withheld from Him, unlike the gods and rivals that have no governance; others, that it is the life that has always been and always will be His attribute. **[Ṭabarī]** His own conclusion: God describes Himself by the permanent life that has no end or interruption and denies of Himself the perishing and cutting-off of life that comes to every living creature at its term; He tells His servants that He it is who deserves their worship and divinity, the Living who neither dies nor perishes, whereas every one they have taken as a lord besides Him dies and every one they have claimed as a god besides Him perishes — and one who perishes, passes away and comes to nothing cannot be a god deserving worship instead of the God who neither perishes nor dies. **[Saʿdī]** *Al-Ḥayy* is the One of complete life; *al-Qayyūm* is the One who subsists by Himself and upholds the affairs of His creation, having established their religious affairs, their worldly affairs and the decrees governing them. **[Ṭabarī]** On *al-Qayyūm*, Mujāhid said "the One standing over everything"; al-Rabīʿ said "the One who watches over everything, preserving it, keeping it and providing for it"; another view reads it as standing in His place — negating change and movement from place to place — and Ibn Isḥāq's gloss, "standing in His place of authority over His creation, not moving, whereas Jesus in their claim moved from the place he was in and went elsewhere", belongs to this reading. **[Ṭabarī]** He prefers the first: it is a description of God as the One who undertakes every affair — its provision, its protection, its governance and its disposal, from the Arabs' saying "so-and-so stands by the affairs of this town", meaning the one who manages them. **[Ṭabarī]** Grammatically, *al-Qayyūm* is of the pattern *fayʿūl* from "God stands by the affairs of His creation", originally *qaywūm*, the first *wāw* being assimilated to the preceding quiescent *yāʾ*; *al-Qayyām* is *fayʿāl* and *al-Qayyim* is *fayʿil*, and all three are chosen for the intensification of praise they carry over the simple *qāʾim* — which is why ʿUmar is reported to have favoured *al-Qayyām*, that being the pattern prevalent in the speech of the people of the Ḥijāz for such roots. **[Qurṭubī]** He reports the reading of ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb, *al-Qayyām*, and that in the codex of ʿAbdallāh b. Masʿūd it stands as *al-Qayyim*. **[Jalālayn]** "The Living, the Eternal." **[Study Quran]** It refers the reader to its fuller discussion of these two Names at 2:255.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** Through Asmāʾ bint Yazīd, the Prophet said the Greatest Name of God is in this verse and in the verse of al-Baqarah that speaks of the one God — a report he also cites through Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He mentions the ḥadīth he gave at Āyat al-Kursī, that God's Greatest Name is contained in these two verses — the verse of the Throne and this opening of Āl ʿImrān — and refers the detailed explanation of the Names to that place. **[Ṭabarī]** He transmits the disputation attributed to al-Rabīʿ: the Christians came and argued with the Prophet about Jesus, saying "Who is his father?", and he asked them, in a series of points they conceded one by one, whether a son does not resemble his father, whether their Lord is living and never dies while Jesus is subject to perishing, whether their Lord is the maintainer of everything, guarding, preserving and providing it while Jesus owns nothing of that, whether nothing in earth or heaven is hidden from God while Jesus knows nothing of it except what he was taught, whether God formed Jesus in the womb as He willed, whether their Lord does not eat, drink or relieve Himself while Jesus was carried and born and suckled and ate and drank; and when, having conceded all this, they still refused, God revealed this opening of the sūrah.

**Belief.** The verse states both halves of the creed in a single line: that worship is due to none but God, and that the One to whom it is due is characterised by life without end and by self-subsistence that upholds everything other than Him **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Saʿdī]**. **[Qurṭubī]** al-ʿAzīz is the One who cannot be overcome and al-Ḥakīm the possessor of wisdom — the attributes with which the sūrah closes this opening passage at v. 6. **[Maʿārif]** Later in the sūrah the same pair of attributes, knowledge without limit and power without limit, is used to show that no being whose knowledge and power are defective can be given the station of worship. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He treats the verse as the affirmation that none has the right to be worshipped but the Ever-Living, the One who sustains and protects all that exists.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** The wording coincides with the opening of Āyat al-Kursī (2:255), where he has already explained these Names. **[Study Quran]** It sends the reader to its commentary on 2:255 for the same two Names. **[Ṭabarī]** He refers back to his earlier discussions of *al-Ḥayy* and *al-Qayyūm* in the commentary on that verse. **[Saʿdī]** The opening pair is immediately followed, in his reading, by the sending down of the Book, so that the One who is living and self-subsistent is the One who sent it.

**Reflection.** The argument of the verse is not that God is greater than other claimants but that the very category of divinity belongs to the One whose life has no term and whose upholding is needed by everything else **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Maʿārif]**. Everything people have worshipped besides Him is itself held in being from moment to moment, which is why the Qurʾān keeps returning to these two Names whenever the question of worship arises **[Saʿdī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. To hold them together is also to hold reverence and trust together: the Living who never dies is the one to be feared, and the Self-Subsistent who maintains every creature is the one in whom reliance is placed **[Saʿdī]**, **[Study Quran]**.

## 3:3 — نَزَّلَ عَلَيْكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ بِٱلْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًۭا لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ وَأَنزَلَ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةَ وَٱلْإِنجِيلَ

*He has sent down the Book to you with the truth, confirming what was before it; and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The address is to Muḥammad: your Lord, and the Lord of Jesus, and the Lord of everything, is the Lord who has sent down to you the Book — meaning the Qurʾān — "with the truth", that is, with the truthfulness in what the people of the Torah and the Gospel differed about and in what those who dispute with you from among the Christians of Najrān and the rest of the idolaters have opposed you in; "confirming what was before it" means that the Qurʾān confirms the books God sent down before it to His prophets and messengers and verifies what His messengers brought from Him, since the One who sent down all of it is one, so there can be no discrepancy in it. **[Ṭabarī]** He cites Mujāhid's gloss, "confirming what was before it of book or messenger", Qatāda's, "the books that passed before it", Ibn Isḥāq's, "with the truth, that is, in what they differed", and al-Rabīʿ's equivalent wording. **[Jalālayn]** "He has revealed to you, O Muḥammad, the Book, the Qurʾān, enveloped by the truth, with veracity in what it announces, confirming what was before it of Books; and He revealed the Torah and the Gospel." **[Ibn Kathīr]** "With truth" means there is no doubt or suspicion that it is from God; and He revealed the Qurʾān with His knowledge, the angels testify to that and God suffices as witness; "confirming what came before it" means the previously revealed books — those books testify to the truth of the Qurʾān, and the Qurʾān testifies to the truth of what they contain, including the good tidings of Muḥammad's prophethood and of the revelation of the glorious Qurʾān. **[Ṭabarī]** "And He sent down the Torah" — to Moses — "and the Gospel" — to Jesus — "aforetime", that is, before this Book which He has sent down to you; and "as a guidance to mankind" is a clarification from God to people of what they differed about: His oneness, the believing of His messengers, and His description of you, O Muḥammad, as His prophet and messenger, together with the other laws of His religion. **[Ṭabarī]** Qatāda said of the two books: they are two books God sent down containing clarification from Him, a protection for whoever takes them, believes them and acts by them. **[Saʿdī]** "Confirming what was before it" of the books means it attests what they attested and agrees with them and declares true the messengers who brought them; and God sent down the Torah and the Gospel before this Book as guidance for people, then completed the message and sealed it with Muḥammad and his mighty Book, by which He guided creation out of misguidance and rescued them from ignorance, and by which He separated truth from falsehood, felicity from wretchedness, and the straight path from the roads of Hell.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** The Qurʾān came down in instalments, bit after bit, which is why *nazzala* is used, whereas the Torah and the Gospel came down in a single act, which is why *anzala* is used; "with the truth" means with truthfulness, and it was also said, with the prevailing proof. **[Qurṭubī]** Grammatically, the *bāʾ* of *bi-l-ḥaqq* is a circumstantial qualifier referring to the Book, attached to an elided word — understood as "coming with the truth" — and not to the verb, since the verb has already taken two objects; and *muṣaddiqan* is a confirming rather than a transitional circumstance, since it is impossible that the Book should not confirm — this being the position of the majority, though some allow transition, in the sense that it confirms itself and confirms others. **[Qurṭubī]** On *al-Tawrāh*: the majority derive it from *w-r-y*, light and radiance, from the striking of the fire-stick, on the pattern *tawriyah* or *tawʿilah*, with the *yāʾ* changed to *alif*; al-Khalīl made it *fawʿalah* from *wawriyah*, the first *wāw* changed to *tāʾ* as in *tawlaj*; and it was also derived from *tawriyah*, allusion and concealment, although the majority prefer the first because God called the Torah *furqān* and *ḍiyāʾ*, light. **[Qurṭubī]** On *al-Injīl*: it is *ifʿīl* from *najl*, origin — so it is the origin of sciences and wisdoms — or from *najaltu al-shayʾ*, I extracted it, because sciences and wisdoms are extracted by it; other derivations given are from the widening of the eye (*najal*), and from *tanājul*, contention, because people contend over it. **[Qurṭubī]** Some said both words are Syriac, and al-Thaʿlabī reported that in Syriac it is *inkliyūn*; al-Jawharī said the Gospel is the book of Jesus and is treated as both masculine and feminine — feminine for the page, masculine for the book — and it was said that the Qurʾān too may be called *injīl*, as in the report of Moses' private converse. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Ḥasan recited *al-Anjīl* with *fatḥah* on the *hamzah*, the rest with *kasrah*, as two dialects. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He sent down the Torah to Moses son of ʿImrān and the Gospel to Jesus son of Mary, aforetime, that is before the Qurʾān was revealed, as guidance to mankind in their time. **[Study Quran]** It notes that *Tawrāt* for Muslims usually means the five books of Moses, much less commonly the whole Tanakh, and *Injīl* the revelation given to Jesus, while real questions remain about how these relate to the present text of the Bible; and it observes that there was most likely no Arabic translation of the Bible in the seventh century, such a translation appearing only a century or more later.

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** This verse carries the "reported proof" of God's oneness as distinct from the "rational proof" of the verse before it: the *tawḥīd* the Qurʾān sets out is not something peculiar to it or to the Prophet of Islam, for God sent scriptures such as the Torah and the Gospel and His prophets in earlier times, and all of them professed it and all of them brought it as their message; when the Qurʾān came it simply confirmed their truth and brought no new claim that could make it hard to understand or accept. **[Maʿārif]** Its footnote insists that whenever the Qurʾān speaks of "the Torah and the Gospel" it means the original books revealed to Moses and Jesus, and that these must not be confused with the present Old and New Testaments. **[Study Quran]** It sets out the range of positions this generates: if both the texts and the interpretations of the People of the Book are sound, their failure lies in moral conduct alone, which seems unlikely since the Qurʾān criticises some of their doctrines; if the texts are sound but the interpretations wrong, that supports charges of distorting meaning rather than text; and one may also hold that the Gospel given to Jesus is conveyed by the existing Gospel without being identical with it, which agrees with the Christian view that the text is guided and sanctioned by God though not dictated. **[Study Quran]** It closes with the ḥadīth, "Do you not see these Jews and Christians? They read the Torah and the Gospel and do not act in accord with them." **[Ibn Kathīr]** The verse establishes that the revelation to Muḥammad and the revelations before it are one message from one source.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** It reads the first words of v. 4 as continuous with v. 3, and notes that some find in the two verbs — *nazzala* for the Qurʾān, *anzala* for the Torah and Gospel — a distinction between gradual and single-act revelation, while others, including al-Ṭabarī, hold that the two verbs are used interchangeably in the Qurʾān. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He links the threat that follows to those who deny and reject the signs of God despite the clarity of what came to them. **[Ṭabarī]** He notes that since the One who sent all of them down is one, there can be no contradiction among them — an argument he uses elsewhere from 4:82.

**Reflection.** The verse teaches the believer to read the earlier scriptures neither as null nor as untouched: the Qurʾān confirms the revelation behind them while judging the claims made in their name **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Maʿārif]**. That is a demanding stance, since it requires distinguishing the message God sent from the uses to which communities have put it — the very distinction the sūrah will press in its long address to the People of the Book **[Study Quran]**. And it sets the terms of Muslim confidence: the truth of this Book is not a party claim but the attestation of a single message running from Moses and Jesus to Muḥammad **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Saʿdī]**.

## 3:4 — مِن قَبْلُ هُدًۭى لِّلنَّاسِ وَأَنزَلَ ٱلْفُرْقَانَ ۗ إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ بِـَٔايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌۭ شَدِيدٌۭ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ عَزِيزٌۭ ذُو ٱنتِقَامٍ

*aforetime, as guidance to mankind. And He sent down the Criterion. Those who disbelieve in the signs of God — for them is a severe punishment; and God is Mighty, Possessor of Vengeance.*

**Meaning.** **[Jalālayn]** "Before" means before revealing the Qurʾān, "as guidance" being a circumstantial qualifier meaning *guiding from error*, "to people" meaning those who followed those two Books; and he notes that God uses *anzala* for the two earlier books because they were revealed in a single act and *nazzala* for the Qurʾān, which came in stages. **[Ṭabarī]** "And He sent down the Criterion" means He sent down the separation between truth and falsehood in what the parties and the adherents of the different religions disputed about concerning Jesus and other matters. **[Ṭabarī]** He explains that *al-Furqān* is of the pattern *fuʿlān* from "God separated truth and falsehood", that is, He judged between them by giving victory to truth over falsehood, either by the conclusive proof or by conquest and overwhelming force. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The Criterion is the distinction between misguidance, falsehood and deviation on the one hand and guidance, truth and piety on the other, because of what it contains of indications, signs, plain evidences and clear proofs, and because of its explanations and clarifications. **[Ṭabarī]** Among the commentators, some directed the meaning to the separation between truth and falsehood in the matter of Jesus — this is the report of Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr, "the separation between truth and falsehood in what the parties differed about of the matter of Jesus and others" — and others to the separation between truth and falsehood in legal rulings and the laws of Islam: Qatāda said the Criterion is the Qurʾān, which God sent down to Muḥammad and by which He separated truth from falsehood, making lawful its lawful and forbidden its forbidden, laying down its laws, setting its limits and imposing its obligations, with its commands to obey and its prohibitions of disobedience; and al-Rabīʿ said the same in brief, that the Criterion is the Qurʾān separating truth from falsehood. **[Ṭabarī]** He prefers the first: since God had already informed us of the sending down of the Qurʾān in "He has sent down to you the Book with the truth confirming what was before it", and that Book is the Qurʾān and nothing else, there is no point in repeating it, so here the Criterion is the judgement God gave between His prophet and those who disputed with him about Jesus and other matters, by the conclusive proof that cuts off their excuse and that of their like among the disbelievers.

**Rulings.** **[Jalālayn]** He notes that the Criterion is mentioned after the three scriptures so as to embrace every revealed scripture besides them, meaning the scriptures that distinguish truth from falsehood. **[Qurṭubī]** He states simply that the Criterion is the Qurʾān, as established earlier, and reports Ibn Fūrak's view that "a guidance to mankind" is to be qualified by "the God-fearing" as at 2:2, so that the general is returned to the particular. **[Study Quran]** It records the range: *al-Furqān* is usually taken here to be the Qurʾān itself, though the word has a wider sense, as shown by its connection with Moses and Aaron at 2:53 and 21:48; some understand it as "discernment", God's indication of what is true and false concerning Jesus, since otherwise the Qurʾān would be denoted twice in one verse by both "the Book" and "the Criterion"; others say it is the Psalms, and still others that it is a second description of the three scriptures just mentioned as guidance, meaning that all of them contain what separates truth from falsehood, permitted from forbidden.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** "Those who disbelieve in the signs of God" are the ones who denied God's marks, proofs and arguments for His oneness and divinity and for Jesus being His servant, and who took the Messiah as a god and lord or claimed him as a son of God: for them is a severe punishment from God on the Day of Resurrection. **[Ṭabarī]** The verse also indicates, he argues, that the Criterion just mentioned is the decisive proof belonging to the people of truth against the people of falsehood, because He follows it immediately with the threat against those who denied that very separation which He sent down to distinguish the one who is in the right from the one who is in the wrong. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Those who disbelieve are those who denied, refused and unjustly rejected the signs; for them is severe torment on the Day of Resurrection. **[Ibn Kathīr]** "God is Mighty" means His grandeur is invincible and His sovereignty unbounded, so nothing can prevent Him from effecting His promise and His threat; "Possessor of Retribution" means He takes retribution from those who reject His signs and defy His honoured messengers and great prophets. **[Jalālayn]** "For them awaits a terrible chastisement; God is Mighty, victorious in His affair so that nothing can prevent Him from effecting His promise and His threat; Lord of Retribution with a severe punishment for those that disobeyed Him." **[Saʿdī]** The passage closes with the contrast already drawn in his reading of v. 3: those who believe in the Book and are guided by it obtain abundant good and reward immediate and deferred, while those who disbelieve in the signs God has set out in His Book and upon the tongue of His messenger have a severe punishment, and God is Mighty, Possessor of vengeance against whoever disobeys Him. **[Maʿārif]** The final verses of this opening group strengthen the doctrine of oneness on the ground that the attributes of all-encompassing knowledge and unlimited power are strong proofs that He alone is to be worshipped; anyone whose knowledge is imperfect and whose power is limited cannot be entrusted with so exalted a station. **[Study Quran]** It notes that the description "Mighty, Possessor of Vengeance" recurs at 5:95, 14:47 and 39:37, and that the threat here falls on those who reject the signs with which God has separated truth from falsehood.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** It directs the reader to 2:53, 2:185, 8:29, 21:48 and 25:1 for the Criterion, and to 5:46–47 for the way Jesus and his message are said to confirm the Torah. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He ties the punishment to the Day of Resurrection and to the rejection of messengers, and reads the whole clause as a threat that matches the warning given at the close of the Najrān passage. **[Ṭabarī]** He grounds the argument on the principle stated at 4:82, that a revelation from other than God would contain much contradiction, so a single source cannot conflict with itself. **[Jalālayn]** He recalls 2:25 and 2:70 as instances of the same verb "to be alike" used of things resembling one another in appearance while differing in reality.

**Reflection.** Guidance and judgement belong together: the same God who sends books as guidance sends the criterion that separates true from false, and the two cannot be pulled apart **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. Holding them together protects against two opposite errors — treating revelation as mere inspiration without judgement, or treating judgement as a weapon without guidance **[Study Quran]**. The warning that closes the verse is not an addition to the argument but its consequence: once the criterion has come, persisting in what it has exposed is no longer ignorance **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Saʿdī]**.

## 3:5 — إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يَخْفَىٰ عَلَيْهِ شَىْءٌۭ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِى ٱلسَّمَآءِ

*Nothing is hidden from God, on earth or in heaven.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God means that nothing that is in the earth and nothing that is in the heaven is hidden from Him, and the rhetorical point is: how, when I know all things, could what these Christians of Najrān who argue with you about Jesus son of Mary conceal — in what they say about him — be hidden from Me? **[Ṭabarī]** He cites Muḥammad b. Isḥāq, from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr: "God knew what they wanted and what they plotted and what they likened to God in their saying about Jesus, making him a lord and a god, while they had knowledge of other than that, in delusion about God and disbelief in Him." **[Jalālayn]** "Nothing — no existent thing whatever — is hidden in heaven and earth from God, on account of His knowledge of universals and particulars"; and he adds that heaven and earth are specified because sense perception does not go beyond them. **[Ibn Kathīr]** God states that He has perfect knowledge of the heavens and the earth and that nothing in them is hidden from His watch; the two verses that follow then declare that He is the shaper of what is in the wombs, so that He is the Creator and therefore the only one worthy of worship, without partners, His being the perfect might, wisdom and judgement. **[Qurṭubī]** This is a report of His knowledge of things in detail, and the like of it is frequent in the Qurʾān: He knows what has been, what will be, and what will not be and how it would be if it were — so how, he asks, could Jesus be a god or the son of a god when things are hidden from him? **[Saʿdī]** It belongs to the completeness of His self-subsistence that His knowledge encompasses creation: nothing is hidden from Him in the earth or in the heaven, not even what is in the bellies of the pregnant.

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** Together with the verse after it, this verse fortifies the doctrine of oneness on the ground that the attributes of all-encompassing knowledge and unlimited power are strong proofs that He alone possesses eternal, all-embracing knowledge and that His power controls everything, and that He alone rightly deserves to be worshipped; anyone whose knowledge is imperfect and whose power is limited cannot be entrusted with that exalted station. **[Maʿārif]** The argument is aimed directly at the claim made for Jesus: a being from whom things are hidden cannot be the One to whom nothing is hidden. **[Qurṭubī]** He makes the same point by way of the divine attribute of knowing the non-existent and how it would be, which no creature shares. **[Ṭabarī]** The point is pressed as a rebuke of concealment: the disputants conceive designs and imagine them hidden, while the One they claim to be arguing about is Himself within the knowledge of God. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He reads the pair of verses as establishing that God is the Creator of all that is in the wombs and therefore the sole object of worship, and notes that the argument bears on the claim of the Christians, that Jesus is a created servant formed in the womb like the rest of mankind. **[Saʿdī]** Since His knowledge reaches what is hidden in the wombs, His care for His servants runs from the beginning of their creation to the end of their affairs with no associate, from which it follows necessarily that none but He deserves worship. **[Study Quran]** It notes the parallel texts at 14:38 and 27:75 for the statement that nothing on earth or in heaven is hidden from God.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ṭabarī]** He records the Companion report on the formation of the embryo, transmitted by al-Suddī from Abū Mālik and Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn ʿAbbās, and from Murra al-Hamdānī from Ibn Masʿūd, and from some of the Companions: when the drop falls into the womb it travels through the body for forty days, then is a clot for forty days, then a lump for forty days; and when it is to be created, God sends an angel to form it, who brings dust between his two fingers, mixes it into the lump and kneads it with it, then forms it as he is commanded, saying: male or female? wretched or happy? and what is his provision, his life-span, his trace, his afflictions? — and God answers and the angel writes; and when that body dies it is buried where that dust was taken from *(Ṭabarī's editor notes that a ḥadīth on the creation of the human being in the mother's belly, in other wordings and through other chains, is in Muslim and in Bukhārī's chapters on the beginning of creation and on menstruation)*. **[Ṭabarī]** He also records Qatāda's saying on the same words: our Lord is able, by God, to form His servants in the wombs as He wills, male or female, black or red, complete in creation or incomplete.

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** The verse is read by him against the Najrān dispute throughout: the delegation's arguments were exposed by a knowledge that misses nothing, and the sūrah answers their claims about Jesus by attributing to God alone what they attributed to him. **[Qurṭubī]** He states the same connection in his note that the verse refutes the Christians of Najrān by an attribute no creature shares. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He places the verse immediately before the declaration that God forms you in the wombs, so that knowledge and creative power are presented together as the two divine prerogatives. **[Maʿārif]** The reported proof of the previous verse is thus completed: the God whom the earlier scriptures proclaimed is the God whose knowledge lacks nothing, and the claim advanced for Jesus fails on both counts.

**Language.** **[Jalālayn]** He explains the specification of "earth" and "heaven" as being because perception does not reach beyond them, so the denial of concealment is made where the hearer's own knowledge stops. **[Saʿdī]** He reads "nothing" at its widest — not some class of things but any thing at all, including what is hidden in wombs. **[Qurṭubī]** He takes the negation as covering the detailed knowledge of everything, in contrast to a knowledge of universals only. **[Ṭabarī]** He stresses that the address is phrased as a question of rebuke — "how could it be hidden from Me?" — which turns a statement of doctrine into an argument against a specific claim.

**Reflection.** The verse is a warning before it is a comfort: nothing plotting, nothing concealed motive, nothing privately plotted escapes the One who is being argued about **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. It also sets the ground for trust, since the God who lacks no knowledge of His servants' condition is the one whose decree over them cannot be unjust **[Saʿdī]**, **[Maʿārif]**. And it quietly relocates the whole dispute of the sūrah: the question is not whether Jesus did marvellous things but who alone knows all things and forms all things **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Study Quran]**.

## 3:6 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُصَوِّرُكُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْحَامِ كَيْفَ يَشَآءُ ۚ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*He it is who forms you in the wombs as He wills. There is no god but He, the Mighty, the Wise.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God means that He is the One who forms you, making you forms and shapes in your mothers' wombs as He wills and loves — making this one male and that one female, this one black and that one red — thereby making known to His servants that everyone whom women's wombs have contained was shaped and created by Him as He willed, and that Jesus son of Mary is among those whom He formed in his mother's womb and created there as He willed; and that if he were a god he would not be among those whom his mother's womb contained, because wombs are not wrapped around the creator of what is in them — they are wrapped only around the created. **[Ṭabarī]** He cites Muḥammad b. Isḥāq, from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr: "Jesus was among those formed in the wombs — they neither deny nor dispute that — just as others of the children of Adam were formed, so how could he be a god when he was in that station?"; and al-Rabīʿ said the same in brief, that God formed Jesus in the womb as He willed. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The verse means that He creates you in the wombs as He wills, whether male or female, handsome or otherwise, happy or miserable; and He is the Creator and therefore the only deity worthy of worship, without partners, His being the perfect might, wisdom and decision. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He presses the point against the Christians: Jesus is a created servant just as God created the rest of mankind, for God created him in his mother's womb and shaped him as He willed, and his creation passed from stage to stage as God said at 39:6, "He creates you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in three veils of darkness." **[Jalālayn]** "He it is Who forms you in the wombs as He will — as males or females, white, black or otherwise. There is no god except Him, the Mighty in His Kingdom, the Wise in His actions." **[Saʿdī]** Among the perfections of His self-subsistence is that He forms you in the wombs how He wills — male or female, complete or deficient in creation — passing through the stages of His creating and His wondrous wisdom; and since such is His way with His servants, and such His immense care for their condition from the moment He originates them to the end of their affairs, with no partner in any of it, it follows necessarily that none deserves worship but He. **[Qurṭubī]** He adds that the verse also refutes the "naturalists", who make nature an independent agent, a point he has already dealt with in his commentary on the verse of divine oneness.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** *Taṣwīr* is derived from *ṣāra ilā kadhā*, "it inclined to such-and-such", so a form is what inclines to a likeness and a shape; and *raḥim*, womb, is from *raḥmah*, mercy, since it is a means by which mercy is shown. **[Qurṭubī]** "As He wills" covers beauty and ugliness, blackness and whiteness, tallness and shortness, soundness and defect, and beyond that wretchedness and felicity. **[Ṭabarī]** "There is no god but He" is, in his reading, God's declaring Himself free of any peer or like in His lordship and of the permissibility of divinity for another, and a belying of those who said what they said about Jesus — the delegation of Najrān who came to the Prophet and everyone else who held their view — and of all who claim an object of worship alongside God or acknowledge a lord other than Him. **[Ṭabarī]** *Al-ʿAzīz* is the One from whose vengeance no one who is targeted by it can be saved and from whom no refuge or fortress delivers, because of the might before which every creature is humbled and every existent bows; *al-Ḥakīm* is the Wise in His governance, in leaving His creatures without excuse and in following up His proofs against them, so that he who perishes perishes by clear evidence and he who lives lives by clear evidence. **[Ṭabarī]** Ibn Isḥāq's gloss is "the Mighty in His vengeance on whoever disbelieves in Him when He wills, the Wise in His excusing of Himself and His proof to His servants", and al-Rabīʿ's is "mighty in His vengeance, wise in His command". **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-ʿAzīz* is the One who cannot be overcome, and *al-Ḥakīm* is the possessor of wisdom or the One who makes firm — the latter, he says, being more specific to the mention of forming. **[Jalālayn]** He pairs the two Names with "Mighty in His Kingdom" and "Wise in His actions".

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** In the *musnad* of Ibn Sanjar he cites the report that God creates the embryo's bones and cartilages from the man's semen and its fat and flesh from the woman's, taking it as the clearest proof that the child is from the fluid of both — a reading he confirms from 49:13, "We created you from a male and a female". **[Qurṭubī]** In *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, in the ḥadīth of Thawbān, a Jew came to the Prophet and asked about the child; he said: "The man's fluid is white and the woman's fluid is yellow; when they meet, if the man's fluid prevails over the woman's the child is male by God's leave, and if the woman's prevails over the man's it is female by God's leave" — with the rest of the ḥadīth. **[Ṭabarī]** He records the report of Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and some of the Companions on the forty-day stages and the angel sent with dust, set out at 3:5 above, and Qatāda's comment that the Lord is able to form His servants as He wills. **[Qurṭubī]** He relates the saying attributed to Ibrāhīm b. Adham, that he was kept from transmitting ḥadīth by four preoccupations, the second of which was the moment he was formed in the womb and the angel set over the wombs asked, "Lord, is he wretched or happy?" — and he did not know what the answer had been *(a devotional anecdote illustrating the verse's reach, not a legal source)*.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** The verse closes the opening argument of the sūrah: the One who forms in the wombs is the One who deserves worship, and the one who was formed in a womb does not. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He states it as the proof that Jesus is a created servant, formed and changing stage by stage, and asks how one who was carried, nourished and formed could be divine as the Christians claim. **[Maʿārif]** Verse 6 draws attention to the fact that God shapes all men and women in their mothers' wombs according to His supreme wisdom, He who has created billions of human beings with clearly distinguishable features establishing the identity of each individual as distinct from every other; He is therefore the only One who should be worshipped. **[Saʿdī]** The two Names at the end confirm it: the Mighty who subdued creation by His strength and is exalted above being described by any defect or blamed attribute, and the Wise in His creating and in His law. **[Study Quran]** Many see this verse as an implicit argument against divinising Christ, since Jesus himself was once within the womb, and it refers to 22:5 for the fuller description of development in the womb.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites 39:6, "He creates you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in three veils of darkness", to show that formation is a process undergone by the created. **[Study Quran]** It refers to 22:5 for the stages of the embryo. **[Qurṭubī]** He refers to his commentary on Sūrah al-Ḥajj and Sūrah al-Muʾminūn for the elaboration of formation, and to the ḥadīth of Ibn Masʿūd, and to Sūrah al-Shūrā for the discussion of the fluids. **[Ṭabarī]** He relates the verse back to the Najrān delegation's own concessions, reported at 3:1–3:2, that Jesus was carried, born, suckled, ate and drank. **[Saʿdī]** He connects the forming in the wombs with the knowledge of what is hidden stated at 3:5 — even what is in the bellies of the pregnant is not hidden from Him.

**Reflection.** The argument from the womb is the most intimate of the sūrah's arguments: everyone, including the one about whom the dispute began, began as something formed, stage by stage, by another **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. It puts worship on its proper footing — not admiration for wonders but acknowledgement of the One who brought every worshipper into being **[Maʿārif]**, **[Saʿdī]**. And it joins power to wisdom: the same God who forms as He wills is the Mighty who cannot be resisted and the Wise whose forming is never arbitrary **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Jalālayn]**.

## 3:7 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلَ عَلَيْكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ مِنْهُ ءَايَٰتٌۭ مُّحْكَمَٰتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَٰبِهَٰتٌۭ ۖ فَأَمَّا ٱلَّذِينَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌۭ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَٰبَهَ مِنْهُ ٱبْتِغَآءَ ٱلْفِتْنَةِ وَٱبْتِغَآءَ تَأْوِيلِهِۦ ۗ وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُۥٓ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ ۗ وَٱلرَّٰسِخُونَ فِى ٱلْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ ءَامَنَّا بِهِۦ كُلٌّۭ مِّنْ عِندِ رَبِّنَا ۗ وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّآ أُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ

*He it is who has sent down the Book to you; in it are verses that are decisive — they are the mother of the Book — and others that are ambiguous. As for those in whose hearts is swerving, they follow what is ambiguous in it, seeking discord and seeking its interpretation; and none knows its interpretation but God. And those firmly rooted in knowledge say, "We believe in it; all is from our Lord" — and none remembers save those who possess intellect.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The One from whom nothing in earth or heaven is hidden is the One who has sent down the Qurʾān to you, O Muḥammad. The *muḥkamāt* are those verses that have been made firm by clarification and detail, whose proofs and indications have been established for what they were made to indicate — the lawful and unlawful, promise and threat, reward and punishment, command and prohibition, report and parable, exhortation and lesson, and the like; they are called "the mother of the Book" because they are the great body of the Book and the place to which its people resort when needed, just as the Arabs call the banner that gathers an army its "mother" and the one who manages the weightiest affair of a town its "mother". **[Ṭabarī]** The *mutashābihāt* are those that resemble one another in recitation while differing in meaning, as at 2:25 where the provision of Paradise is "alike" in appearance yet differs in taste, and 2:70 where the cow "was alike to them" in description while its kinds differed. **[Jalālayn]** "He it is Who revealed to you the Book wherein are verses that are clear, lucid in proof, forming the Mother Book, the original basis for rulings, and others allegorical whose meanings are not known, such as the opening verses of some sūras"; and he notes that God calls the whole Qurʾān "clear" at 11:1 — meaning it contains no imperfections — and "consimilar" at 39:23 — meaning its parts resemble each other in beauty and veracity. **[Ibn Kathīr]** In the Qurʾān there are verses that are *muḥkamāt*, entirely clear and plain, these being the foundations of the Book, plain for everyone; and there are verses that are *mutashābihāt*, not entirely clear to many or to some people; whoever refers the *mutashābih* back to the *muḥkam* has been rightly guided, and whoever does the opposite has gone astray — which is why God called the decisive ones "the foundations of the Book", the basis to which reference is made for clarification. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites Muḥammad b. Isḥāq b. Yasār's gloss of the decisive verses as "containing proof of the Lord, immunity for the servants and a refutation of opponents and of falsehood; they cannot be changed or altered from what they were meant for", and of the ambiguous ones as capable — though not permitted — of being altered, being a test from God to His servants just as He tested them with the lawful and the unlawful, so they must not be twisted to a false meaning. **[Saʿdī]** The Book contains the decisive, whose meanings are clear and evident and not confusable with others, and the ambiguous, which admit more than one sense without any one of the two possibilities being settled by themselves until they are joined to the decisive; those in whose hearts is sickness, swerving and deviation follow the ambiguous because of their bad intent, inferring from it their false theses and spurious opinions, seeking discord and distorting His Book and interpreting it to their own tastes and schools so as to go astray and lead astray. **[Study Quran]** Each part of the Qurʾān conventionally called a "verse" in English means originally "a sign", and here Qurʾānic signs are described as either *muḥkam* or *mutashābih*; the verb *ḥakama* means lexically to curb, repel, command or judge, hence "determine", and to be *mutashābih* is for things to resemble one another, as at 2:25, 2:70, 2:118, 6:99, 6:144 and 13:16. **[Maʿārif]** It states the background against which the verse was revealed: a group of Christians came to the Prophet and spoke with him about religion; he refuted the doctrine of the Trinity in detail and proved God's oneness from His eternal existence, His perfect power, His all-encompassing knowledge and His unique creativity, which belongs to One who is indivisible — premises they had to concede, so that once *tawḥīd* was established the belief in the Trinity was shown to be untenable. They then raised doubts about expressions in which Jesus is called *Rūḥ Allāh*, the Spirit of God, or *Kalimat Allāh*, the Word of God, claiming these gave him a share in divinity. Through this verse, Maʿārif says, God put an end to those doubts: such expressions are of the class of the *mutashābihāt*, where the outward meaning is not what is intended; they are a secret between God and His Messenger whose reality cannot be disclosed to others, for whom it is not even fitting to go about determining their possible meanings. One must believe in them, holding that whatever God has chosen to mean by them is the truth, and it is not permissible to dig deeper or pursue further research in that direction.

**Rulings.** **[Ṭabarī]** He records the disagreement over what the decisive and the ambiguous are. Some said the decisive are those by which one works — the abrogating verses or those that establish rulings — and the ambiguous those whose working has been left, the abrogated: this is reported from Ibn ʿAbbās by several chains, specifying the three verses at 6:151–153 and those at 17:23–39 as examples of the decisive, and from Ibn Masʿūd and others, and from Qatāda, al-Rabīʿ and al-Ḍaḥḥāk. **[Ṭabarī]** Others said the decisive are those in which God has made firm His clarification of the lawful and unlawful, and the ambiguous those whose meanings resemble one another though their wordings differ — the view of Mujāhid, who instanced 2:26, 6:125 and 47:17. **[Ṭabarī]** Others said the decisive are those that admit only one interpretation and the ambiguous those that admit several — the gloss of Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr: in the decisive is the proof of the Lord, the protection of the servants and the repelling of opponents and falsehood, with no diverting or distorting them from what they were set for; the ambiguous have diverting, distorting and interpretation, and God tests His servants by them as He tested them with the lawful and unlawful, and they are not to be turned to falsehood. **[Qurṭubī]** He reports the further views: that the decisive are verses that stand by themselves and need no reference elsewhere, such as "there is none comparable to Him" and "I am indeed most forgiving to whoever repents", while the ambiguous are like "God forgives all sins", which must be referred back to the others; that the decisive is the Fātiḥah, without which the prayer is not valid; that it is Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ, since it contains nothing but oneness; and that the whole Qurʾān is decisive or, alternatively, entirely ambiguous, which he rejects as missing the sense of this verse. **[Qurṭubī]** Ibn Khwayz Mindād held that the legally operative ambiguity is the kind in which scholars differ over which of two verses abrogates the other — instancing the disagreement over the waiting period of a pregnant widow, where ʿUmar, Zayd b. Thābit and Ibn Masʿūd held that the shorter sūrah abrogated four months and ten days while ʿAlī and Ibn ʿAbbās held it did not — and over whether the bequest to an heir was abrogated, and over conflicts between two texts, two reports or two analogies. **[Qurṭubī]** He adds that a verse read in two ways, or a word that is general or summary and needs explanation, is not of this ambiguous type, since the obligation is the measure the word covers, and the two readings are like two verses by both of which one must act. **[Ṭabarī]** On "those in whose hearts is swerving": it means inclination away from the truth and deviation from it; Mujāhid glossed it as doubt, Ibn Isḥāq as "inclination away from guidance". **[Ṭabarī]** On "seeking discord", some said it means seeking idolatry — al-Suddī and al-Rabīʿ — and others seeking ambiguities and confusion — Mujāhid, "the ambiguities by which they were destroyed", and Ibn Isḥāq, "confusion"; he prefers the second, since those about whom the verse was revealed were already idolaters, so it makes no sense to say they sought idolatry. **[Study Quran]** It notes that *fitnah* can also mean strife, that the interpreters take it here as referring to *shirk* or to the doubts created in hearts, and that one can read the rebuke as directed at the deviancy in hearts that interpret the Qurʾān for false reasons. **[Maʿārif]** It draws from the mention of the two classes a general principle which, if grasped, uproots many doubts and disputes: the *muḥkamāt* are those verses whose meaning is open and clear to anyone who knows the rules of Arabic grammar reasonably well, while the *mutashābihāt* are those whose meanings and explanations are not clear to such a person; God called the first "the mother of the Book" because they are the root and essence of all teaching, free of doubt and ambiguity, and therefore the correct method with the second kind is to harmonise them with the first. The rule that follows is that any interpretation of the *mutashābihāt* that contradicts the *muḥkamāt* is to be rejected outright, and only an interpretation consistent with the verses of established meaning may be accepted — as, for example, when the Qurʾān states plainly at 43:59, "he is nothing but a servant upon whom We bestowed Our blessing", and at 3:59, "the likeness of Jesus with God is as the likeness of Adam, whom He created from dust", both of which show Jesus to be a chosen servant created by God and therefore refute the Christian claim of godhead and sonship.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** Imām Aḥmad recorded from ʿĀʾishah that the Prophet recited this verse up to "men of understanding" and said: "When you see those who argue about it, they are the ones God meant — so beware of them"; al-Bukhārī recorded the like in his *Tafsīr* of this verse, as did Muslim in the Book of Decree and Abū Dāwūd in the section on the Sunnah. **[Jalālayn]** He reports the same from ʿĀʾishah through the two Shaykhs, and adds the report of al-Ṭabarānī in *al-Kabīr* that Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī heard the Prophet say, "I fear nothing for my community except three faults", one of them being that the Book will be opened before them and the believer will want to interpret it, while none knows its interpretation save God; and those firmly rooted in knowledge say, "We believe in it; all is from our Lord." **[Qurṭubī]** Muslim narrated the ḥadīth of ʿĀʾishah in the same terms: "When you see those who follow what is ambiguous in it, those are the ones God named — beware of them." **[Qurṭubī]** He appends the account of Abū Umāmah, who passed the heads set up at the steps of the mosque of Damascus and was told they were the heads of Khārijites brought from Iraq; he called them the dogs of Hell three times, said they were the worst slain under the shade of the sky and that blessed is the one who kills them and is killed by them, wept out of pity for them because they had been people of Islam and left it, then recited this verse and "be not like those who divided and differed after the clear proofs came to them" (3:105), and said he had heard it from the Prophet not once or twice but many times *(a transmitted report of the verse's application to a specific group, cited by the commentators as an illustration rather than as a ruling against named individuals)*. **[Ṭabarī]** He records the report of Ibn ʿAbbās, when the Khārijites and their demeanour before the Qurʾān were mentioned to him: "They believe in its decisive part and perish at its ambiguous part", and he recited this verse. **[Ṭabarī]** He also records the view, which he prefers, that the verse came down concerning those who disputed with the Prophet by means of the ambiguous parts of the Book — whether about Jesus or about the term of his community — and that the latter is more likely, since "none knows its interpretation but God" points to a matter of appointed terms that they sought to know from the isolated letters, whereas the matter of Jesus had been made clear to the Prophet and his community. **[Qurṭubī]** He relates that a group of Jews, among them Ḥuyayy b. Akhṭab, came to the Prophet and said: we hear that "Alif Lām Mīm" has been revealed to you; if you are truthful, the dominion of your community will last seventy-one years, since *alif* is one, *lām* thirty and *mīm* forty — and "none knows its interpretation but God" was revealed. **[Maʿārif]** It records the same ḥadīth from ʿĀʾishah — "when you see people busy investigating the *mutashābihāt*, stay away from them, for these are the people God spoke of" — citing Bukhārī; and it adds, from Ibn Kathīr citing al-Ṭabarānī, the tradition in which the Prophet named three things he feared for his community: that they grow wealthy and envy one another until there are rifts and bloodshed; that the Book of God be trifled with, so that even the uninitiated claim to understand it perfectly through translations and what was never meant to be understood becomes the very object of their deliberations, although God alone knows its meaning; and that they let their knowledge, once it has grown, go to waste by abandoning the effort to increase it further.

**Readings.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** The scholars of recitation differ about pausing at God's Name in this verse; the pause after "except God" is reported from ʿĀʾishah, ʿUrwah, Abū al-Shaʿthāʾ and Abū Nahīk, so that "those firmly rooted in knowledge" begins a new statement whose predicate is "say: we believe in it". **[Ṭabarī]** He sets out both readings: some hold that God alone knows the interpretation of the ambiguous and that the firmly rooted are introduced by a fresh statement, believing in the decisive and the ambiguous alike while not knowing its interpretation — reported from ʿĀʾishah, Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr, ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, Abū Nahīk al-Asadī, Hishām b. ʿUrwah from his father, and Mālik; and others hold that "those firmly rooted in knowledge" is conjoined to the Name of God, so that they know the interpretation and say "we believe in it" — reported from Mujāhid, who related that Ibn ʿAbbās said, "I am among those who know its interpretation." **[Qurṭubī]** He reports that the majority — Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿĀʾishah, ʿUrwah, ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, and the grammarians al-Kisāʾī, al-Akhfash, al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿUbayd — take it as severed, and that Mujāhid alone conjoined it; al-Khaṭṭābī argued that God made the verses two kinds and reserved knowledge of the ambiguous to Himself, then praised the firmly rooted for saying "we believe in it", and that were the conjunction intended, "all is from our Lord" would have no point. **[Qurṭubī]** He then reports the other side at length, including that the conjunction is related from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Rabīʿ, Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr and al-Qāsim b. Muḥammad, that naming them "firmly rooted" implies they know more than the merely decisive, and that the Prophet's prayer for Ibn ʿAbbās — "O God, give him understanding in the religion and teach him the interpretation" — supports it; and his own resolution is that the ambiguous is of two kinds: what cannot be known at all, such as the spirit and the Hour, which no one attempts, and what admits several senses in the language and can be interpreted soundly and stripped of unsound interpretations, such as "a spirit from Him" said of Jesus — and it is of this second kind that the firmly rooted know a great deal, each according to what is granted him. **[Jalālayn]** He takes the pause at "save God", explaining that the firmly rooted say of the ambiguous part that it is from God and that they do not know its meaning, while all of it, clear and ambiguous alike, is from their Lord. **[Study Quran]** It notes that an alternative pause in the Arabic yields "And none know its interpretation save God. And those firmly rooted in knowledge say…" — reserving all interpretation of the ambiguous to God and characterising the firmly rooted merely by their faith — but adds al-Thaʿlabī's observation that in practice all schools of thought interpret the entire Qurʾān, passing over no aspect of it in silence, though degrees of uncertainty remain.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** He explains why "the mother of the Book" is singular although its predicate is plural: the meaning is that the decisive verses together are the mother of the Book, not that each one is; he compares 23:50, "We made the son of Mary and his mother a sign", not "two signs", because together they are one sign in what they were made to signify, even though each singly contains a sign. **[Ṭabarī]** He rejects the Basran grammarian's attempt to explain it as quotation of another's words, on the ground that God is not here reporting anyone's saying "the mother of the Book". **[Ṭabarī]** *Ukhar* is the plural of *ukhrā*, and he sets out the two grammatical explanations for its being left without nunation; and *mutashābihāt* means resembling one another in recitation while differing in meaning. **[Study Quran]** *Taʾwīl* is etymologically "bringing back to the origin", and in Qurʾānic usage it is the act of taking something unclear, ambiguous, symbolic, allegorical or metaphorical and returning it to its true meaning — used also of dreams in Sūrah 12 and of the Companion of Moses at 18:78 — while in later classical learning *tafsīr* came to mean the transmission of texts and authoritative quotations and *taʾwīl* what a commentator then draws out; but for many commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, whose own work is subtitled *taʾwīl āy al-Qurʾān*, the two are near synonyms, and the separate sense of *taʾwīl* is later than the Qurʾān's usage. **[Ṭabarī]** On "and none knows its interpretation but God": he takes it as the time of the Rising and the expiry of the term granted to Muḥammad and his community and what is to come, known to no human who hopes to reach it by calculation, astrology or divination; and he notes the disagreement over the sense of *al-taʾwīl* sought — Ibn ʿAbbās related it to the Day of Resurrection, al-Suddī to the outcomes of the Qurʾān and when the abrogating verse would come to abrogate the abrogated, and Ibn Isḥāq to their interpreting it according to the error they had embarked on — preferring, with Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Suddī, the reading that they sought knowledge of a time hidden from them, since the plain meaning of "We created" and "We decreed" was understood even by ignorant idolaters. **[Ṭabarī]** On "all is from our Lord": it means all of it, the decisive and the ambiguous alike, is the revelation and inspiration of God to His prophet Muḥammad; Ibn ʿAbbās glossed it "what has been abrogated of it and what has not", Qatāda "they believed its ambiguous and acted by its decisive", and Ibn ʿAbbās in another report "we believe the decisive and follow it as religion, and we believe the ambiguous without following it as religion, and all of it is from God". **[Ṭabarī]** On "none remembers save those who possess intellect": it means none is admonished and restrained from saying of the ambiguous verses what he has no knowledge of, except those of understanding and intelligence; and Ibn Isḥāq's gloss is that none remembers, in a matter like this — that is, returning the interpretation of the ambiguous to the known interpretation of the decisive so that the two agree upon one meaning — except those possessed of intellect. **[Jalālayn]** He notes the assimilation in *yadhdhakkar*, originally *yatadhakkar*. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-rusūkh* is firmness in a thing; he records the report that when the Prophet was asked about those firmly rooted in knowledge he said: "He whose oath is true, whose tongue is truthful and whose heart is upright"; and he answers the objection — why should the Qurʾān contain ambiguity when God charged the Prophet to explain it to the people? — by saying the wisdom in it, God knows best, is that the excellence of the scholars may appear, since were all of it plain the superiority of some over others would not show. **[Study Quran]** The *muḥkam* are "the Mother of the Book" also in the sense that they comprise or entail the Book or are its focus and point of convergence, and the phrase recurs at 13:39 and 43:4, where many identify the mother of the Book with the Preserved Tablet (85:22).

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** He holds that everything God sent down was sent as a clarification for the Prophet and his community and a guidance to the worlds, so it cannot contain what they have no need of, nor what they need while having no way to its interpretation; what they need God has explained by the indication of the Book and the tongue of His messenger, while what they do not need — such as the length of the interval before the Hour — He has kept to Himself, and that is what the Jews sought from the isolated letters and were told they would not attain. **[Ṭabarī]** He rules, accordingly, that everything besides that is decisive, either because it bears a single sense needing no further explanation, or because, though it has several aspects, God's clarification or His messenger's suffices; and knowledge of that, he says, will not depart from the scholars of the community. **[Qurṭubī]** He reports the view he calls the best account of the ambiguous: that it is what no one has any way to know, of what God has kept exclusively to Himself — such as the time of the Hour, the emergence of Gog and Magog, the Dajjāl and Jesus, and the isolated letters at the openings of the sūrahs. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He explains that the *muḥkamāt* are the verses that explain the abrogating rulings, the permitted, the prohibited, the legal penalties, the obligations and what must be believed and acted upon, while the *mutashābihāt* include the abrogated verses, the parables, the oaths, and what is to be believed but not acted upon — and he cites the fourfold division of *tafsīr* reported from Ibn ʿAbbās: a *tafsīr* the Arabs know by their language, a *tafsīr* no one is excused for not knowing, a *tafsīr* the scholars know, and a *tafsīr* only God knows. **[Study Quran]** It surveys the consequences: at one extreme are literalists who see only one discrete true meaning identified with the outward sense even if unknown; at the other are those who dispense with the plain sense and embrace metaphor, often to avoid attributing created qualities to God, which tends to an impoverished rationalised meaning; most interpreters affirm the plain sense while plumbing deeper levels. **[Study Quran]** It adds that Sufis such as Ibn ʿArabī and Rūmī and philosophers such as Mullā Ṣadrā affirm all levels at once, holding that literalists err in reading everything as decisive while rationalists read everything as ambiguous as determined by their own sense of plausibility, and that beyond the correct application of the distinction, only certain spiritual qualifications of the reader open the Book's deeper meaning, since a blind or hardened heart cannot understand the fullness of God's word. **[Ṭabarī]** He states the general principle that the verse, though revealed about those unbelievers, applies to every innovator in God's religion who turns his heart to an innovation, interprets an ambiguous verse to support it, argues with the people of truth by it and turns away from the clear indications of the decisive verses, seeking to confound them — whoever he is, Christian, Jew, Magian, Sabaean, Ḥarūrī, Qadarī or Jahmī — in accordance with the Prophet's words, "When you see those who argue by it, they are the ones God meant; beware of them." **[Maʿārif]** On who the firmly rooted in knowledge are, it reports that Muslim scholars have differed, and gives as the weightier position — abridged from Mazharī — that they are the people of the Sunnah and the main body of the Muslims: those who accept only the interpretation and explanation of the Qurʾān and Sunnah authentically transmitted from the Companions, the righteous generations after them and the consensus of the community; who hold the *muḥkamāt* to be the axis and centre of the Book; and who, humbly confessing the limits of their knowledge and insight, leave to God the real meanings of the *mutashābihāt* that lie beyond their comprehension. They are never proud of their knowledge, however perfect, nor of the strength of their faith; instead they constantly pray to God for steadfastness, grace and mercy. They do not have mischief-prone temperaments that drive them to make the *mutashābihāt* alone the object of their intellectual pursuit, and they take both kinds to be the truth because both have issued from one single source. The meanings of the decisive verses were needed and useful, and God has not kept them hidden but made them very open and clear; the meanings of the ambiguous verses He has for wise reasons chosen not to disclose, so finding them out is simply not necessary for us — believing in them as they are is sufficient.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** It notes that the Qurʾān can be understood as wholly *muḥkam*, from 11:1, and wholly *mutashābih*, from 39:23, while the present verse says it is partly one and partly the other; that both hearts (9:117; 61:5) and eyes (33:10) can swerve; that *fitnah* as strife occurs at 2:217 and 8:39; and that the description of human beings as fuel for the Fire occurs at 2:24, 21:98 and 66:6. **[Jalālayn]** He cites 11:1 and 39:23 for the two descriptions of the whole Book. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites 3:59, "the likeness of Jesus with God is as the likeness of Adam", and 43:59, "he was not more than a servant", as the decisive verses that refute the Christian use of "a spirit from Him" and "His word"; and 4:82 for the principle that a scripture from other than God would contain much contradiction. **[Ṭabarī]** He cites 6:158, on the day when some sign of the Lord comes and faith no longer profits a soul, as an instance of what God has explained to the extent needed while keeping its timing hidden. **[Qurṭubī]** He cites 7:53, "do they await anything but its interpretation? On the day its interpretation comes…", as the proof that *taʾwīl* here is the outcome and not the explanation.

**Reflection.** The verse is the Qurʾān's own instruction about how it is to be read, and its guidance is as much about character as about method: the difference between the two parties lies in the heart before it lies in the text **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Saʿdī]**. The remedy it sets out is to return the ambiguous to the decisive, so that the Book interprets itself rather than being made to serve a prior conclusion **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. And its closing note — that none remembers but those possessed of intellect — makes the whole matter one of remembrance: the right use of scripture is not cleverness but the admonition that keeps a person from saying of God what he does not know **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Study Quran]**. **[Maʿārif]** Those of gentle nature, it adds, do not busy themselves investigating the ambiguous verses; they accept them, believe them to be the true word of God and recognise that He has for a wise reason not disclosed their meaning — which is the safest and most cautious course, while those in whose hearts is crookedness press on regardless to extract meanings that suit their fancies.

## 3:8 — رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا وَهَبْ لَنَا مِن لَّدُنكَ رَحْمَةً ۚ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ

*"Our Lord, do not make our hearts swerve after You have guided us, and grant us from Yourself a mercy; You are the Bestower."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The firmly rooted in knowledge say — having declared their belief in the ambiguous part of the Book and in its decisive part as the revelation of their Lord — "our Lord, do not make our hearts swerve after You have guided us", meaning: do not make us like those whose hearts swerved from the truth and who were turned away from Your path; "do not make our hearts swerve" is do not incline them away from Your guidance after You guided us and granted us success in believing in the decisive and the ambiguous of Your Book; "and grant us from Yourself a mercy" means grant us from You enabling grace and firmness for what we are upon of affirming the decisive and ambiguous of Your Book; "You are the Bestower" means You are the One who gives His servants success and rightness to stand fast by His religion and to declare true His Book and His messengers. **[Ṭabarī]** Ibn Isḥāq's gloss runs: "do not incline our hearts, even if we incline through what we do" — that is, they asked God to make their hearts firm in faith even when their deeds fall into some disobedience, which is why Ṭabarī's editor prefers the reading "by our acts" to "by our bodies". **[Jalālayn]** "Our Lord, do not cause our hearts to deviate — do not cause them to incline away from the truth in their desire to interpret it in ways inappropriate for us, as You caused the hearts of those others to deviate — after You have guided us, after You have shown us the way to it; and give us mercy from You as a strengthening; You are the Bestower." **[Ibn Kathīr]** They say: do not deviate our hearts from the guidance after You allowed them to acquire it, and do not make us like those in whose hearts is wickedness, those who follow the ambiguous; rather, make us remain firmly on Your straight path and true religion; "from Ladunka" means from You; "mercy" means that by which You make our hearts firm and increase our faith and certainty; "You are the Bestower". **[Qurṭubī]** The sentence contains an elision — understood as "they say" — and is a report about the firmly rooted; alternatively the sense is "say, O Muḥammad". **[Saʿdī]** "Do not incline our hearts" means do not turn them away from the truth to falsehood; "and grant us from Yourself a mercy by which our affairs are set right"; and *al-Wahhāb* means the One abounding in bounty and gifts.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** He draws from the verse a refutation of the Qadariyya — the deniers of decree, by whom he means the Muʿtazila — who hold that God's making a heart swerve is injustice. His argument is that were it as they say, those who pray "our Lord, do not make our hearts swerve after You have guided us" would deserve blame rather than the praise God gives them, since on that reading they would be asking God not to wrong them, which is ignorance in the asker, for God does not wrong His servants, as He has told them at 41:46, "your Lord is no wrongdoer of His servants", and there is no sense in asking Him to be as He has already declared Himself to be. The sound conclusion, he says, is that it is justice from God to turn away the heart of whom He wills from obedience, which is why those who ask Him not to turn them away are praised: they have directed their request to its proper object and put their petition in its proper place, in keeping with the many reports of the Prophet's own pleading with his Lord in this very matter despite his station. **[Qurṭubī]** He says the same in brief: the verse is a proof against the Muʿtazila in their claim that God does not lead His servants astray, since if the turning of hearts were not from Him it would not be permissible to pray for the removal of what He cannot do. **[Maʿārif]** The verse shows that guidance and going astray are from God alone: when He intends to guide someone He makes his heart incline to what is good and right, and when He decides to let someone go astray He turns his heart away from the straight path; He is absolute in power and does what He wills, and therefore those who care about remaining firm go to the source and ask Him for steadfastness. **[Saʿdī]** He reconciles the two sides by the texts on earning: God has told us in other verses the causes by which the hearts of the deviant swerve, and those causes are their own acquisition — "when they swerved, God made their hearts swerve" (61:5), and "then they turned away, so God turned their hearts away", and "We turn their hearts and their sight away, as they did not believe in it the first time" (6:110). So if a servant turns away from his Lord and befriends His enemy, sees the truth and turns from it and sees falsehood and chooses it, God entrusts him to what he chose for himself and makes his heart swerve as a punishment for his own swerving; God wronged him not — he wronged himself, and he should blame none but his own soul that commands to evil. **[Study Quran]** It observes that this verse, like many others, raises the question of whether and how God causes or denies faith to a human heart — a question it treats in its essay on the Qurʾān and the schools of Islamic theology.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ṭabarī]** He records from Umm Salamah, with an isnād he judges sound, that the Prophet said, "O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion", then recited "our Lord, do not make our hearts swerve after You have guided us" to the end of the verse. **[Ṭabarī]** In the longer version, from Umm Salamah through Shahr b. Ḥawshab: the Prophet used to say often in his supplication, "O God, Turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion"; she said, "Messenger of God, is the heart really turned over?" and he said, "Yes — God created no human being among the children of Adam but that his heart is between two of the fingers of the Merciful; if He wills He steadies it and if He wills He makes it swerve. So we ask God our Lord not to make our hearts swerve after He has guided us, and we ask Him to grant us from Himself a mercy; He is the Bestower." She asked him to teach her a supplication for herself and he told her to say: "O God, Lord of the prophet Muḥammad, forgive me my sin, remove the rancour of my heart, and grant me refuge from the seditions that lead astray." *(Ṭabarī's editor notes that the ḥadīth is in Aḥmad and Tirmidhī, who graded it ḥasan, and that al-Haythamī declared it weak in two places through Shahr b. Ḥawshab — who is weak though declared trustworthy by some — and ḥasan in a third.)* **[Ṭabarī]** He also records the same supplication through Jābir and through Anas — in Anas's version the family said, "Messenger of God, we have believed in you and declared true what you brought; is there anything to fear for us?" and he said, "Yes; hearts are between two of the fingers of God, He turns them" — and through al-Nawwās b. Samʿān al-Kilābī, who added that the Prophet said "O Turner of hearts, make our hearts firm on Your religion" and that the Balance is in the hand of the Merciful, who raises some peoples and lowers others until the Day of Resurrection. **[Qurṭubī]** In *al-Muwaṭṭaʾ*, Abū ʿAbdallāh al-Ṣunābiḥī said: I came to Madīnah in the caliphate of Abū Bakr and prayed the Maghrib behind him; he recited in the first two rakʿahs the Opening of the Book and a short sūrah of the *mufaṣṣal*, then stood for the third and, drawing so close that his garments almost touched mine, I heard him recite the Opening and this verse, "our Lord, do not make our hearts swerve after You have guided us". **[Qurṭubī]** The scholars said his reciting this verse was a form of *qunūt* and supplication because of what the Muslims faced in the affair of the apostates; and *qunūt* in the Maghrib is permitted by a group of scholars, as it is in every prayer when some great matter befalls the Muslims and alarms them and they fear for themselves. **[Qurṭubī]** Tirmidhī narrated from Shahr b. Ḥawshab that he asked Umm Salamah what the Prophet's most frequent supplication was when he was with her, and she said: "O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion"; when she said how often he said it, he explained that there is no human being whose heart is not between two of the fingers of God — whom He wills He steadies and whom He wills He makes swerve — and Muʿādh then recited this verse; Tirmidhī graded the ḥadīth *ḥasan*. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Ibn Jarīr recorded from Umm Salamah that the Prophet used to supplicate "O You Who changes the hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion", then recited the verse.

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** He raises the objection — how could they fear being transferred to corruption when they had already been guided? — and gives three answers: that they asked not to be tested with what would be too heavy for them to perform, as at 4:66, "and had We prescribed for them, 'slay yourselves' or 'go forth from your dwellings'"; that, as Ibn Kaysān said, they asked not to swerve so that God would not make their hearts swerve, in keeping with 61:5 — meaning, keep us firm upon Your guidance, now that You have guided us, and let us not swerve and so deserve that You make our hearts swerve; and that the verse is disconnected from what precedes it, God having taught His servants this supplication after mentioning the people of swerving, so that they should not be of the blameworthy party just described. **[Qurṭubī]** He draws the ruling on *qunūt* from Abū Bakr's practice, as above — that supplication of this kind in the prayer is legislated when the community is afflicted. **[Qurṭubī]** *(digression)* He warns against the ignorant among the Sufis and the heretics among the Bāṭiniyya, who clutch at this and similar verses to claim that knowledge is only what God grants without acquisition and that looking into books and papers is a veil — a claim he rejects, and which he says he will refute in its place. **[Qurṭubī]** On the meaning of the gift: mercy is an attribute of the Essence, and an attribute cannot itself be "given", so the sense is "grant us a blessing issuing from mercy"; and the request is *from You*, as pure grace, not by any cause from us nor by any deed of ours — which is why the verse contains surrender and casting oneself down.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** *Ladun* has four dialectal forms, the most eloquent being *ladun* with *fatḥah* on the *lām*, *ḍammah* on the *dāl* and quiescent *nūn*; he also discusses the morphology of *wahaba/yahabu*, noting that the root is *yawhibu* with *kasrah* on the *hāʾ*, so that the *wāw* is dropped as it falls between a *yāʾ* and a *kasrah* — and that whoever says the root is *yawhabu* with *fatḥah* is mistaken, since then the *wāw* would not be dropped, as it is not in *yawjalu*. **[Study Quran]** It notes that "a mercy" could also be read simply as "mercy", and that the preposition *ladun* — "at" or "in the presence/possession of" — is rendered "Presence", comparing 18:10 and especially 18:65 on knowledge from Our Presence, the Qurʾānic theme from which the term *al-ʿilm al-ladunī*, "God-given knowledge", arises in later Islamic spirituality. **[Qurṭubī]** He records the variant reading attributed to Abū Wāqid al-Jarrāḥ, "lā tuzagh qulūbanā", assigning the act to the hearts themselves, and says that on either reading the sense is: let there not be from You the creating of swerving in them so that they swerve. **[Ṭabarī]** He glosses *zāgha ʿan al-ḥaqq* — to swerve from the truth — as *zayghan wa-zayaghānan wa-zayghūghah wa-zuyūghan*, and "God made him swerve", *azāghahu*, as His inclining him.

**Cross-references.** **[Qurṭubī]** He cites 4:66 and 61:5 for the two answers to the objection, and 3:105 for the verse Abū Umāmah recited alongside this one. **[Saʿdī]** He cites 61:5, the verse on those who turned away, and 6:110 as the evidence that swerving is a consequence of the servant's own choice. **[Study Quran]** It refers to 18:10 and 18:65 for *ladun*, and to its essay on the Qurʾān and schools of theology for the doctrinal question. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He reads the verse as continuing the speech of the firmly rooted begun at v. 7, so that the two verses form a single prayer. **[Jalālayn]** He makes the contrast explicit: as You caused the hearts of those others to deviate.

**Reflection.** The prayer is put in the mouths of the most learned, not the least — which is the verse's sharpest lesson: knowledge does not make a person safe from swerving, and the scholars of the community are the ones who most often ask to be kept firm **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. It also tells the believer where to take the problem of a wavering heart, not to argument but to the One who turns hearts **[Maʿārif]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. And because it asks for mercy "from You" and not for a wage, it leaves no room for the self-congratulation that so often accompanies learning **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Saʿdī]**.

## 3:9 — رَبَّنَآ إِنَّكَ جَامِعُ ٱلنَّاسِ لِيَوْمٍۢ لَّا رَيْبَ فِيهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُخْلِفُ ٱلْمِيعَادَ

*"Our Lord, You are the Gatherer of mankind for a Day about which there is no doubt; God does not fail the tryst."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The firmly rooted say, together with their declaration "we believe in the ambiguous of the verses of our Lord's Book — all of the decisive and the ambiguous that is in it is from our Lord": "our Lord, You are the Gatherer of mankind for a Day about which there is no doubt; God does not fail the tryst." **[Ṭabarī]** He notes that this is speech in which what is mentioned suffices for what is left unmentioned: the sense is, "our Lord, You will gather mankind on the Day of Resurrection, so forgive us on that day and pardon us, for You do not break Your promise that whoever believes in You, follows Your messenger and acts by what You commanded him in Your Book, You will forgive him on that day." **[Ṭabarī]** The point of their asking, then, is a request to their Lord that He keep them firm upon the good insight they have — faith in God and His messenger and in the revelation that came to them — until He takes them in the best state of their deeds and faith; for if He does that for them, Paradise becomes due to them, since He has promised that whoever does that of His servants He will admit to Paradise. So the verse, though it comes in the form of a report, is in its sense from them a petition, a supplication and an entreaty to their Lord. **[Ibn Kathīr]** They say in their supplication: O our Lord, You will gather Your creation on the Day of Return, judge between them and decide concerning what they disputed; then You will reward or punish each according to the deeds he did in this life. **[Jalālayn]** "Our Lord, You shall gather mankind for a day — that is, on a day of which there is no doubt, no uncertainty — that is the Day of Resurrection, when You will requite them for their deeds as You had promised; verily God will not fail the tryst, His promise of the Upraising." **[Jalālayn]** He notes the shift from second to third person, that these last words may be God's own speech, and says the purpose of the supplication in this form is to show that their concern is with the matter of the Hereafter, and that it is for that reason they ask God for adherence to the path of guidance in order to attain its reward. **[Qurṭubī]** "Gatherer of mankind" means raising them and giving them life after their dispersal, and in this is an acknowledgement of the Resurrection; al-Zajjāj said this is the interpretation which the firmly rooted knew and affirmed, and in which they differed from those who followed what was ambiguous to them in the matter of the Resurrection until they denied it outright. **[Saʿdī]** It is the completion of the speech of the firmly rooted, and it contains acknowledgement of the Resurrection and the recompense, and certain faith, and that God must bring about what He promised — and that entails what it requires and demands of action and preparation for that Day, for faith in the Resurrection and the recompense is the root of the soundness of hearts and of the desire for good and the fear of evil which are the foundation of all goods. **[Maʿārif]** *(Maʿārif treats v. 9 together with v. 8 in a single block, as the continuation of the prayer of the firmly rooted.)* It is the second excellence of theirs that it records: having been granted excellent knowledge, they were not proud of that excellence but chose to believe in what comes from their Lord, and they pray for steadfastness upon the right path — not for any gain of this world but for salvation in the life to come.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** "For a Day in which there is no doubt" means no doubt or uncertainty, which he says he has established with its proofs earlier; "for a Day" means "on a Day", the day on which God gathers His creation to decide between them at the standing of presentation and reckoning. **[Ṭabarī]** *Al-mīʿād* is of the pattern *mifʿāl* from *al-waʿd*, the promise. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-rayb* is doubt, whose senses he has set out in al-Baqarah, and *al-mīʿād* is *mifʿāl* from the promise. **[Study Quran]** The tryst, *mīʿād*, is an appointed meeting or something promised, used often of God's promises and threats regarding the Hereafter (13:31; 34:30; 39:20); it is similar in meaning to *mawʿid*, also rendered tryst (20:58; 11:17), and both are related etymologically to the theologically important *waʿd* and *waʿīd* — promise and threat — which also refer to the thing promised or threatened (4:122; 9:111; 10:48; 36:52), for which it refers to its essay on the Qurʾān and the schools of Islamic theology and philosophy. **[Jalālayn]** He explains "no doubt" as "no uncertainty".

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** The verse is an affirmation of the Resurrection, and al-Zajjāj makes it the test case distinguishing the firmly rooted from those who pursued ambiguity: the former knew the gathering and affirmed it, while the latter followed what seemed ambiguous to them about it until they denied it. **[Ṭabarī]** He reads the sentence, though phrased as information, as in substance a request for forgiveness and pardon on that Day, grounded on God's promise to forgive whoever believes, follows the messenger and acts by the Book. **[Ṭabarī]** He adds that it is in reality a request that God keep them firm until death in the best state of their faith and deeds, since on that condition Paradise is promised to them. **[Saʿdī]** Faith in the gathering and the recompense is what makes the heart sound and produces desire for good and dread of evil — the two motives on which all good rests. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The gathering is for judgement and decision between them concerning what they disputed, followed by reward or punishment according to deeds. **[Jalālayn]** Their concern with the Hereafter is shown by the very form of the prayer: they ask God for adherence to guidance in order to attain its reward. **[Maʿārif]** Guidance and straying being from God alone, those who care about remaining firm go to the source and pray for steadfastness rather than trusting in their own state. **[Study Quran]** The term for the tryst belongs to the vocabulary of promise and threat that runs through the Qurʾān's eschatology.

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** He places the verse within the single speech of the firmly rooted that begins at v. 7 with "we believe in it; all is from our Lord" and runs through the two petitions; the petition for firmness and the petition on the Day of Gathering are one movement of the same prayer. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He likewise treats vv. 8–9 as what "they say" after their declaration of belief. **[Jalālayn]** He ties the purpose of the supplication to the preceding verse: their concern is the Hereafter. **[Saʿdī]** He reads the acknowledgement of the gathering as the completion of the stance the firmly rooted took when they refused to pursue the ambiguous. **[Qurṭubī]** He sets it against the denial of the Resurrection by those who followed what was ambiguous to them. **[Maʿārif]** The prayer for the Hereafter rather than for this world is, in his reading, the mark that distinguishes them from the people of deviance described at v. 7.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** It cites 13:31, 34:30 and 39:20 for *mīʿād*, 20:58 and 11:17 for *mawʿid*, and 4:122, 9:111, 10:48 and 36:52 for *waʿd* and *waʿīd*. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He relates the gathering to the Day of Return and to the judgement of what they disputed. **[Qurṭubī]** He refers back to his treatment of *rayb* in al-Baqarah. **[Ṭabarī]** He refers back to his earlier proofs of the meaning of "no doubt". **[Ṭabarī]** He grounds the promised forgiveness on the condition stated in the Qurʾān's promises to those who believe, follow the messenger and act by the Book. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Zajjāj's contrast is with those who denied the Resurrection by following what was ambiguous to them.

**Reflection.** The verse shows what belief in the Hereafter does: it is not an extra article tacked onto faith but the weight that gives every other choice its seriousness **[Saʿdī]**, **[Jalālayn]**. Those who are most learned are those who most often name the Gathering, because the knowledge that does not end in the meeting with God is the knowledge that puffs up **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Maʿārif]**. And the phrase "God does not fail the tryst" closes the opening of the sūrah on a note of certainty: both the promise and the threat will be kept, so the only question left is which of the two a person has made his own **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Study Quran]**.

## 3:10 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لَن تُغْنِىَ عَنْهُمْ أَمْوَالُهُمْ وَلَآ أَوْلَٰدُهُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًۭٔا ۖ وَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمْ وَقُودُ ٱلنَّارِ

*Those who disbelieve — neither their wealth nor their children will avail them anything against God; and it is they who are the fuel of the Fire.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "Those who disbelieve" are those who denied the truth they had come to know of the prophethood of Muḥammad — among the Jews of the Children of Israel and their hypocrites, and the hypocrites of the Arabs and their unbelievers — those in whose hearts is swerving, who follow the ambiguous part of God's Book seeking discord and seeking its interpretation. "Their wealth and their children will not avail them anything against God" means that their wealth and children will not save them from God's punishment if He brings it down upon them — in this world, for their denial of the truth after it had been made clear to them and for their pursuit of the ambiguous seeking confusion — nor will that wealth and those children ward it off from them in any degree; while in the Hereafter they are "the fuel of the Fire", that is, its firewood. **[Jalālayn]** "As for the disbelievers, neither their riches nor their children will avail — will protect — them against God, that is, against His chastisement; those — they shall be fuel for the Fire, they shall constitute what the Fire will be fuelled by." **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Those who disbelieve" means they disbelieved in God's signs, denied His messengers, defied His books and did not benefit from what He revealed to His prophets; "neither their properties nor their offspring will avail them whatsoever against God" — meaning they will be the wood with which the Fire is kindled and fed. **[Qurṭubī]** The sense is plain: their wealth and their children will not push away from them anything of God's punishment; and Abū ʿUbayda said that "against God" here means "with God". **[Saʿdī]** Having mentioned the Day of Resurrection, God mentions that all who disbelieve in Him and deny His messengers must enter the Fire and burn in it, and that their wealth and children will not avail them anything of God's punishment. **[Maʿārif]** The text now shifts from the verbal confrontation with the disbelievers to the warnings of the coming physical encounter, as at v. 12, "you shall soon be overcome", while the verses before it — vv. 10 and 11 — serve as the introduction. **[Study Quran]** The Qurʾān frequently notes that in the logic of this world riches buy preferential treatment and children provide status, protection and a means of surviving beyond death, and these are mentioned along with other desirable things of this life at v. 14.

**Context.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** He treats v. 11 with v. 10: "Like the behaviour of the people of Pharaoh and those before them — they denied Our signs, so God seized them for their sins; and God is severe in punishment." On *ka-daʾb*, al-Ḍaḥḥāk related from Ibn ʿAbbās that it means "like the behaviour of the people of Pharaoh", which is also the explanation of ʿIkrima, Mujāhid, Abū Mālik and al-Ḍaḥḥāk; other scholars said it means "like the practice, the conduct, the likeness of the people of Pharaoh" — and all these senses are possible, since *daʾb* means practice, behaviour, tradition and habit. The verse indicates that the disbelievers will not benefit from their wealth or offspring; rather they will perish and be punished, the same end met by the people of Pharaoh and the nations before them who rejected the messengers, the signs and the proofs they were sent with; and "God is severe in punishment" means His punishment is severe and painful, none can escape His grasp and nothing escapes His knowledge. **[Saʿdī]** There will befall them in this world, as befell Pharaoh and the rest of the nations that denied God's signs, seizures and punishments: "God seized them for their sins", hastening for them worldly punishments joined to the punishments of the Hereafter; "and God is severe in punishment" — so do not make light of His punishment, lest persisting in unbelief and denial become easy for you. **[Ṭabarī]** He identifies the class addressed as those who denied the truth after recognising it, which he has already described at v. 7 as those who pursue the ambiguous seeking discord.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** Ibn al-Mubārak transmitted from al-ʿAbbās b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib that the Prophet said: "This religion will spread until it crosses the seas and until the seas are crossed on horses in the path of God; then peoples will come who recite the Qurʾān and, when they have recited it, say 'who recites better than us? who knows more than us?'" — then he turned to his companions and said, "Do you see any good in those?" They said no. He said: "Those are from you, those are from this community, and those are the fuel of the Fire." **[Study Quran]** It records the same tradition in brief: the Prophet described a future time when those who recite and teach the Qurʾān, considering no one to be superior to them because of their position, would be an example of "fuel for the Fire". **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites the Qurʾānic corroborations: 40:52, "the Day when their excuses will not profit the wrongdoers; theirs will be the curse and theirs the evil abode"; 9:55, "so let not their wealth nor their children amaze you"; and 3:196–197, "let not the going to and fro of the disbelievers in the land deceive you — a brief enjoyment, then their refuge is Hell, and evil is the resting place"; and 21:98, "you and what you worship besides God are fuel for Hell". **[Ṭabarī]** He records no Prophetic report here beyond the exegetical definition of *waqūd* as firewood, referring back to his treatment of the word in al-Baqarah.

**Readings.** **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Sulamī recited "lan yughniya" with *yāʾ*, because the verb precedes and a separator comes between it and its subject; and al-Ḥasan recited "yughnī" with *yāʾ* and quiescence of the final *yāʾ* for lightening, as the poet said, "sufficient is despair of Asmāʾ as a sufficer" — where the right form would be *kāfiyan*, but he left the *yāʾ* — and al-Farrāʾ cited parallel lines. **[Qurṭubī]** On *waqūd*: read with *fatḥah* it is the noun for firewood; al-Ḥasan, Mujāhid and Ṭalḥa b. Muṣarrif read it "wuqūd" with *ḍammah* on the *wāw*, implying an elided genitive — understood as "the firewood of the burning of the Fire"; in Arabic, when the *wāw* is vowelled with *ḍammah* one may also say *uqūd*, as in *uqqitat*; and *wuqūd* with *ḍammah* is also the verbal noun, as in "the Fire was lit". **[Jalālayn]** He notes the two readings, *waqūd* as fuel and *wuqūd* as the act of burning.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** *Waqūd* is the name for firewood, a point he has made in al-Baqarah. **[Ṭabarī]** He glosses "they are the fuel of the Fire" as "its firewood". **[Study Quran]** Human beings as fuel for the Fire is mentioned also at 2:24, 21:98 and 66:6. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He explains the image as the wood with which the Fire is kindled and fed. **[Ṭabarī]** On "will not avail them": the verb means they will not stand in place of what is needed, so the wealth and children on which reliance is placed in this world fail entirely at the point where they are needed. **[Saʿdī]** He expands "avail" to cover both worlds: not in the Hereafter, and not in this world either, where the disbelieving nations were seized as Pharaoh was seized.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** It gathers the parallels: 3:116, 26:88 and 60:3 for wealth and children not availing; 16:72 and 17:6 for God having bestowed them in the first place; 8:28 and 64:14–15 for their being a trial; 9:55, 9:85 and 68:10–14 for their being a means by which disbelievers come to punishment; 9:69, 18:39–40, 23:55 and 34:35 for worldly advantage not being a sign of favour; 18:46 and 34:37 for righteousness and piety enduring rather than wealth and children; and 57:20, 63:9 and 71:21 for wealth and children distracting from the remembrance of God. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites 40:52, 9:55, 3:196–197 and 21:98, as above. **[Qurṭubī]** He refers back to his discussion of *waqūd* in al-Baqarah. **[Ṭabarī]** He refers back to the same discussion, and to v. 7 for the description of those whose hearts swerve. **[Saʿdī]** He reads the verse with v. 11, "God seized them for their sins", as a single warning. **[Maʿārif]** He looks forward to v. 12, "you shall soon be overcome", as the warning whose introduction these verses are.

**Reflection.** The verse names the two things people most rely upon, and declares them weightless at the one moment that matters — not because wealth and children are evil but because they are gifts, and gifts cannot be pleaded against the giver **[Study Quran]**, **[Saʿdī]**. The image of fuel is deliberately humbling: what a person spent his life gathering becomes the material of his own punishment, since the Fire is fed by the very selves and possessions that were preferred to God **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. And the warning falls heaviest on those who use the Qurʾān itself as a ground for pride, whom the Prophet named as fuel for the Fire in his own community **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Study Quran]**.

## 3:11 — كَدَأْبِ ءَالِ فِرْعَوْنَ وَٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ ۚ كَذَّبُوا۟ بِـَٔايَٰتِنَا فَأَخَذَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ شَدِيدُ ٱلْعِقَابِ

*Like the wont of the house of Pharaoh and those before them: they denied Our signs, so God seized them for their sins; and God is severe in retribution.*

**Meaning.** The verse completes the thought of 3:10. **[Ṭabarī]** "Those who disbelieve — their wealth and their children will not avail them anything against God when Our punishment alights on them, as was the wont (*sunnah*) of the house of Pharaoh and their custom (*ʿādah*)"; and "those before them" are the nations before them who denied Our signs, so We seized them for their sins and destroyed them when they denied — their wealth and children availed them nothing when Our might came upon them, just as those who were hastened into punishment for denying their Lord before the house of Pharaoh: the people of Noah, the people of Hūd, the people of Lot and their like. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He reads the two verses as one statement: the disbelievers will be the fuel of the Fire — the wood with which it is kindled and fed, as in "certainly you and what you worship besides God are fuel for Hell" (21:98) — and what they were granted in this world of wealth and offspring will not save them from His punishment. **[Jalālayn]** "Their way is as the way, that is the habit, of Pharaoh's folk and the communities before them, such as ʿĀd and Thamūd, who denied Our signs; God seized them — He destroyed them — for their sins," this last clause explaining the one before it. **[Saʿdī]** Having mentioned the Day of Resurrection, God now mentions that all who disbelieve in Him and deny His messengers must enter the Fire and roast in it, and that their wealth and children will not avail them anything of God's punishment; and that there will befall them in this world of seizures and punishments what befell Pharaoh and the rest of the nations that denied God's signs — "God seized them for their sins" — so that He hastened for them worldly punishments joined to the punishments of the Hereafter.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** The root of *daʾb* is "you kept at a thing" (*daʾabta fī al-amri*), meaning you persisted and laboured in it; the Arabs then carried the word over to affair, matter and habit, citing the line of Imruʾ al-Qays. He then records the interpretive difference with its chains. **[Ṭabarī]** Al-Rabīʿ b. Anas said "like their wont (*sunnah*)". **[Ṭabarī]** Al-Ḍaḥḥāk — through Juwaybir — said "like the deed (*ʿamal*) of the house of Pharaoh", and Ibn ʿAbbās — through Abū Rawq — said "like their making (*ṣunʿ*)". **[Ṭabarī]** ʿIkrimah and Mujāhid said "like the doing (*fiʿl*) of the house of Pharaoh, like their affair (*shaʾn*)". **[Ṭabarī]** Ibn Zayd said "like their doing, like their denial when they denied the messengers", and recited "like the wont of the people of Noah" (40:31), adding that *al-daʾb* is deed. **[Ṭabarī]** Al-Suddī took it as "like the denial of the house of Pharaoh" — God mentions the disbelievers and their acts of denial as the likeness of the denial of those before them in rejection and falsification. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Al-Ḍaḥḥāk reported that Ibn ʿAbbās said the verse means "like the *behaviour* of the people of Pharaoh", and that this is the explanation of ʿIkrimah, Mujāhid, Abū Mālik and al-Ḍaḥḥāk; other scholars said "practice", "conduct" or "likeness"; all of these are sound, for *daʾb* means practice, behaviour, tradition and habit. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-daʾb* is habit and affair (*al-ʿādah wa-al-shaʾn*); one says "the man kept at his work" when he strove and exerted himself, and "the two that keep on" are night and day. **[Qurṭubī]** Abū Ḥātim records hearing Yaʿqūb mention *ka-daʾbi* with the *hamzah* opened, and being asked as a boy on what footing *ka-daʾbi* is admissible; he answered that he supposed it from *daʾiba yadʾabu daʾaban*, and Yaʿqūb accepted it and marvelled at the soundness of his reasoning at so young an age, though he did not know whether it is actually said. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Naḥḥās rejected that as an outright error: *daʾiba* is never used; the grammarians — among them al-Farrāʾ in his book of verbal nouns — transmit only *daʾaba yadʾabu duʾūban* and *daʾban*, as in Imruʾ al-Qays's "like your wont with Umm al-Ḥuwayrith before her, and her neighbour Umm al-Rabāb at Maʾsal". **[Qurṭubī]** *Daʾb* with the *hamzah* quiesced is admissible, like *shaʿr* and *shaʿar* or *nahr* and *nahar*, because the word contains a guttural letter. **[Study Quran]** *Daʾb*, translated "affair" in the sense of "matter" or "case", can also be taken as "wont" or "way"; and the "seizing" (*akhadha*, literally "taking") is a word that in Qurʾānic usage often means "punished" (4:153; 7:73).

**Readings.** **[Qurṭubī]** The commentators differ on what the prefixed *kāf* attaches to. **[Qurṭubī]** One view is that it stands in the position of a nominative, the sense being "their wont is like the wont of the house of Pharaoh" — the doing of these disbelievers with you is like the doing of Pharaoh's house with Moses. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Farrāʾ held that the meaning is "the Arabs disbelieved as the house of Pharaoh disbelieved"; al-Naḥḥās objected that the *kāf* cannot be attached to *kafarū*, because that verb lies inside the relative clause. **[Qurṭubī]** Others attach it to "so God seized them" — He seized them with a seizing like His seizing of Pharaoh's house; others to "their wealth and their children will not avail them" — they did not avail them just as wealth and children did not avail Pharaoh's house; and this, he notes, answers those who held back from the struggle saying "our wealth and our families keep us busy". **[Qurṭubī]** It may also be governed by a verb understood from the word "fuel", the comparison then lying in the burning itself, which is supported by "and there encompassed the house of Pharaoh an evil punishment: the Fire, to which they are exposed morning and evening; and on the Day the Hour arises, admit the house of Pharaoh to the severest punishment". **[Qurṭubī]** The first view is the weightiest and is preferred by more than one scholar: Ibn ʿArafah glossed it "like the habit of Pharaoh's house — these unbelievers have made a habit of heresy and of harassing the Prophet as Pharaoh's house made a habit of harassing the prophets", and al-Azharī said the same. **[Qurṭubī]** The parallel phrase in Sūrah al-Anfāl, "like the wont of Pharaoh's house", means by contrast that those were requited with killing and capture as Pharaoh's house was requited with drowning and destruction. **[Qurṭubī]** "Our signs" may denote the recited signs, or the signs erected as pointers to His oneness.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** He sets beside it "the Day when their excuses will not profit the wrongdoers; theirs is the curse and theirs is the evil abode" (40:52); "let not their wealth nor their children amaze you; God only intends to punish them with these in the life of this world, and that their souls depart while they are disbelievers" (9:55); and "let not the going to and fro of the disbelievers in the land deceive you — a brief enjoyment, then their refuge is Hell, and evil is that resting place" (3:196–197). **[Ṭabarī]** Ibn Zayd's reading is supported by "like the wont of the people of Noah" (40:31). **[Study Quran]** The illustration is that the worldly wealth of the Egyptians and of others did not help them when God destroyed them (7:133–36).

**Belief.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "God is severe in retribution" means that His punishment is severe and His torment painful: none can escape His grasp, nor does anything escape His knowledge; He does what He wills and prevails over all things, everything is humbled before Him, and there is no deity worthy of worship nor any Lord other than Him. **[Saʿdī]** The point is a warning: do not make light of His punishment, lest persisting in unbelief and denial become easy for you. **[Maʿārif]** In the sequence of the passage, these two verses form the introduction to what follows: the discourse now moves from verbal confrontation with the deniers to the warning of a physical encounter, stated in verse 12 — "you shall soon be overcome".

**Reflection.** The verse answers the objection that prosperity proves God's approval: what Pharaoh's house owned did not delay its punishment by an hour, and the same pattern governs every generation that denies after the proof has reached it **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Jalālayn]**. The word chosen for that pattern — *daʾb*, a settled habit rather than a single act — tells the reader that destruction comes not from one slip but from a way of life persisted in **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. It also places the Quraysh and the Jews of Madīnah inside history rather than outside it: the same law that drowned Pharaoh's house and levelled the towns of ʿĀd and Thamūd is now addressed to them, and the warning is issued before the blow falls so that it may be averted **[Saʿdī]**, **[Maʿārif]**.

## 3:12 — قُل لِّلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ سَتُغْلَبُونَ وَتُحْشَرُونَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ ۚ وَبِئْسَ ٱلْمِهَادُ

*Say to those who disbelieve: "You will be overcome and mustered into Hell — and evil is the resting-place."*

**Meaning.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** God commands the Prophet to proclaim to the disbelievers: "You will be defeated" in this life, "and gathered together" on the Day of Resurrection, "to Hell, and worst indeed is that place of rest." **[Jalālayn]** "Say, O Muhammad, to the disbelievers among the Jews: 'You shall be vanquished' — or read *sa-yughlabūn*, 'they shall be vanquished' — in this world, through being killed or taken captive and made to pay the *jizyah*, which is what actually took place; 'and mustered' — read both ways, *wa-tuḥsharūna* or *wa-yuḥsharūna* — in the Hereafter to Hell, which you shall enter: an evil cradling, an evil resting place." **[Ṭabarī]** He treats the verse as a divine announcement of defeat in this world and of the Fire in the next, and argues that the second-person reading is the one demanded by the flow of the address, since the verse that follows — "there was a sign for you in two hosts that met" — turns back to the same audience. **[Qurṭubī]** "Say to those who disbelieve: you will be overcome" — meaning the Jews — "and mustered into Hell": into it in the Hereafter. **[Maʿārif]** Since a reader might object that not all the disbelievers of the world have in fact been overcome, the doubt is unfounded: the disbelievers meant here are the disbelievers and the Jews of that particular time, among whom the pagans were overcome by being killed or taken prisoner, and the Jews were overcome by killing and imprisonment and also by the *jizyah* and by expulsion. The "overcoming" of disbelievers mentioned in the verse therefore simply does not refer to disbelievers universally.

**Context.** **[Qurṭubī]** Muḥammad b. Isḥāq related that when the Messenger of God inflicted defeat on Quraysh at Badr and came back to Madīnah, he gathered the Jews together and said, "O company of Jews, beware of God lest there befall you what befell Quraysh on the day of Badr, before there befalls you what befell them; you know that I am a prophet sent, you find that in your Book and in God's covenant with you." They replied, "O Muḥammad, do not let it delude you that you killed a few raw men (*aghmār*) with no knowledge of war and took your chance against them. By God, if you fight us you will know that we are the real men." God then sent down "Say to those who disbelieve: you will be overcome", with the *tāʾ*, meaning the Jews. This, **[Qurṭubī]** notes, is the report of ʿIkrimah and Saʿīd b. Jubayr from Ibn ʿAbbās. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Muḥammad b. Isḥāq b. Yasār recorded that ʿĀṣim b. ʿUmar b. Qatādah said that when the Messenger of God gained the victory at Badr and returned to Madīnah, he gathered the Jews in the market-place of Banū Qaynuqāʿ. **[Qurṭubī]** In the report of Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn ʿAbbās, the Jews rejoiced at what befell the Muslims at Uḥud and the verse was then revealed, so that on that reading the meaning is with the *yāʾ* — "they will be overcome", meaning Quraysh — and "they will be mustered" with the *yāʾ* in both verbs; and that is the reading of Nāfiʿ. **[Study Quran]** Some hold that the verse is directed at the idolatrous Makkans; others report that the Prophet admonished some of the Jews of Madīnah, on his victorious return from Badr, that their fate could be like that of the Quraysh, and that they haughtily dismissed the warning, after which the verse was revealed.

**Readings.** **[Study Quran]** Some read the verbs in the third person — "that they shall be vanquished" — which does not change the meaning; the Qurʾān contains instances of such shifts in grammatical person. **[Jalālayn]** Both readings are recorded for both verbs, and he explains the second-person reading as addressed to the Jews of Madīnah and the third-person reading as referring to them. **[Ṭabarī]** He weighs the two readings against the surrounding verses and reasons from the address of 3:13, "there was a sign for you", that the second-person reading is the coherent one; on the third-person reading the sense would be "say to the Jews: the Arab polytheists will be vanquished and mustered into Hell". **[Qurṭubī]** He records both occasions of revelation, one supporting each reading, without declaring either wrong.

**Language.** **[Study Quran]** *Mihād*, rendered "resting place", can mean a bed or even a cradle — lexically a place smoothed out and prepared for rest — yet it is usually used in connection with Hell, although in 78:6 and 43:10 the related term *mahd* is used of the earth. **[Qurṭubī]** On the literal sense of *al-mihād*: it is the apparent sense of the verse that Hell is meant. **[Qurṭubī]** Mujāhid, however, said the meaning is "evil is what they have spread for themselves", so that the sense becomes: evil is the deed of theirs that brought them to Hell.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** He prefaces the passage with the report, through Ibn Isḥāq, that the Prophet gathered the Jews in the market of Banū Qaynuqāʿ after Badr and addressed them there, and he reads the verse as God's own proclamation of the outcome to them through His Prophet. **[Saʿdī]** He treats the verse as news and glad tidings for the believers and a warning to the disbelievers that they must be overcome in this world; and it came about exactly as God said, for they were overcome with an overcoming that had no like and no parallel.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** The verse echoes other verses that warn of defeat in this world together with punishment in the next — 5:33; 5:41; 22:11; 33:57. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He sets the prediction of 3:12 beside the fulfilment narrated in 3:13, and reads "there was a sign for you" as addressed to the very Jews who had spoken as they did in the market of Banū Qaynuqāʿ.

**Belief.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** The "sign" that follows is, in his words, a proof that God will make His religion prevail, award victory to His Messenger, make His word manifest and His religion the highest; the defeat announced here belongs to this life and the mustering to the next, so that the verse joins the two together. **[Saʿdī]** The fulfilment is itself the argument: what happened at Badr is among God's signs pointing to the truthfulness of His Messenger and to the fact that he is upon the truth while his enemies are upon falsehood.

**Reflection.** The warning is spoken to people at the moment of their greatest confidence — the Jews of Madīnah had just watched the Muslims win at Badr and answered with contempt **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. It announces a defeat they cannot see coming and then names the two-stage consequence: being overcome here, being mustered into Hell hereafter **[Jalālayn]**, **[Study Quran]**. And the promise was kept in history, in the fate of the Arab pagans and then of the Jewish clans of Madīnah, which is why the verse is read as a sign rather than a threat **[Saʿdī]**, **[Maʿārif]**.

## 3:13 — قَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ ءَايَةٌۭ فِى فِئَتَيْنِ ٱلْتَقَتَا ۖ فِئَةٌۭ تُقَٰتِلُ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ وَأُخْرَىٰ كَافِرَةٌۭ يَرَوْنَهُم مِّثْلَيْهِمْ رَأْىَ ٱلْعَيْنِ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ يُؤَيِّدُ بِنَصْرِهِۦ مَن يَشَآءُ ۗ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةًۭ لِّأُو۟لِى ٱلْأَبْصَٰرِ

*There was a sign for you in two hosts that met — one host fighting in the way of God and another disbelieving, whom they saw as twice themselves, by the seeing of the eye. God strengthens with His help whom He wills; in that is a lesson for those who have insight.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "Say, O Muḥammad, to those of the Jews who disbelieve and who are in the midst of your town: 'There was for you a sign' — that is, a mark and a proof of the truth of what I say, that you will be overcome — 'and a lesson'." **[Ṭabarī]** Qatādah, through Saʿīd, glossed it "a lesson and a matter for reflection", and al-Rabīʿ the same, adding "and something to ponder". **[Ṭabarī]** "In two hosts" means two parties and two factions, *al-fiʾah* being a band of people; "that met" for war; one of the two hosts being the Messenger of God and those with him who witnessed Badr, the other the idolaters of Quraysh. **[Ṭabarī]** He transmits this identification from Ibn ʿAbbās through Saʿīd b. Jubayr or ʿIkrimah, from ʿIkrimah through Ibn Jurayj, and from Mujāhid through three separate chains. **[Qurṭubī]** There is no disagreement that the two hosts are the day of Badr, the Muslims and the idolaters; what is disputed is who is addressed. **[Qurṭubī]** It may be the believers, or all the disbelievers, or the Jews of Madīnah, and each of these has been held by some; the benefit of addressing the believers being to steady their hearts and to encourage them to advance against twice their number and more, as indeed happened.

**Readings.** **[Ṭabarī]** The reciters differ over *yarawnahum* / *tarawnahum*. **[Ṭabarī]** The reciters of Madīnah read with the *tāʾ*: "there was a sign for you, O Jews, in two hosts that met, one fighting in the way of God and the other disbelieving, you seeing the idolaters as twice the Muslims by the seeing of the eye" — an admonition, meaning: you have a lesson, O Jews, in what you saw of the fewness of the Muslims and the multitude of the idolaters, and of the triumph of the former despite their number over the latter in spite of theirs. **[Ṭabarī]** Most of the reciters of Kūfah and Baṣrah read with the *yāʾ*, meaning that the Muslims who fight in God's way see the disbelieving band as twice the Muslims in magnitude: you have, O company of Jews, a lesson in two hosts that met, one fighting in God's way and one disbelieving, these Muslims with their few numbers seeing those idolaters in their great numbers as twice them. **[Jalālayn]** "They — the disbelievers, numbering almost a thousand — saw them, the Muslims, twice the like of them, that is, more numerous than themselves, as the eye sees, in manifest vision." **[Ibn Kathīr]** He records both: some said the idolaters thought the Muslims twice their number, God making that illusion a factor in the victory Islam had over them; and it has also been said that the Muslims saw twice as many idolaters as they were, yet God gave them victory over them. **[Qurṭubī]** Nāfiʿ reads *tarawnahum* with the *tāʾ* and the rest with the *yāʾ*; Abū ʿAmr objected that the *tāʾ* reading would require "twice *you*" (*mithlaykum*), but al-Naḥḥās replied that the objection does not hold, since it may mean "twice your companions". **[Qurṭubī]** Makkī observed that the *tāʾ* reading follows on from the address in "for you", so the address is best taken as to the Muslims and the pronoun as the idolaters; and although the *tāʾ* would strictly require *mithlaykum*, that is not permitted because it departs from the written text, so the discourse moves from address to third person as in "until when you are in the ships and they sail with them" and "whatever you give of *zakāh*… those are the ones who receive manifold". **[Qurṭubī]** On that reading the sense is: you, O believers, see the idolaters as twice what they were in number, though they were three times you, so God reduced them in your eyes to twice your number in order to strengthen your hearts and bring about the engagement.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** ʿAbdallāh b. Masʿūd said: "When we looked at the forces of the disbelievers we found them twice as many as us; then we looked at them again and we thought they did not have one man more than we had" — and he recited "and when you met, He showed them to you as few in your eyes, and made you appear few in their eyes" (8:44). **[Ṭabarī]** He transmits the same report with its chain through al-Suddī, from Murrah al-Hamdānī, from Ibn Masʿūd, who said "this is the day of Badr", and then distinguishes two "reductions": the first is that God showed the Muslims the idolaters as only twice their number when they were in fact more, and the second is the one Ibn Masʿūd described, that He then showed them to be equal in number. **[Ṭabarī]** A second party held that the Muslims saw them at their actual number, without any reduction in their eyes, but that God simply aided them; and God's saying to the Jews "there was a sign for you in them" was to frighten them with the prospect of the same being brought upon them at Muslim hands. **[Ṭabarī]** He reports this from Ibn ʿAbbās: the verse was sent down concerning the lightening of the burden at Badr; the believers that day were three hundred and thirteen men, the idolaters six hundred and twenty-six, and God aided the believers. **[Ṭabarī]** Al-Ṭabarī rejects that figure, saying it contradicts the reports that have come down in multiplicity about the number of the idolaters at Badr, over which people differed only between "a thousand" and "between nine hundred and a thousand". **[Ṭabarī]** In support of "a thousand", he transmits from ʿAlī the account of the two men found at Badr, one of whom was a client of ʿUqbah b. Abī Muʿayṭ; the Muslims kept asking him how many the people were and he kept answering "they are, by God, many, and fierce in their might", until they brought him to the Messenger of God, who asked him how many camels they slaughtered; he said ten a day, and the Messenger of God said: "The people are a thousand." **[Ṭabarī]** He also transmits from Ibn Masʿūd: "We captured a man of them at Badr and said, 'How many were you?' He said: 'A thousand.'" **[Maʿārif]** The disbelievers had about a thousand men, seven hundred camels and a hundred horses, while the Muslim *mujāhidīn* were a little over three hundred, with seventy camels, two horses, six coats of mail and eight swords. **[Jalālayn]** The Muslims numbered three hundred and thirteen men, most of them on foot, with two horses, six coats of mail and eight swords; the disbelievers numbered almost a thousand.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** When the two camps saw one another, the Muslims thought the idolaters twice their number so that they would trust in God and seek His help, while the idolaters thought the believers twice their number so that they would feel fear, horror, fright and despair; and when the two camps stood in ranks and met in battle, God made each camp appear smaller in the eyes of the other so that they would be encouraged to fight one another, "so that God might accomplish a matter already ordained" (8:42) — meaning that truth and falsehood be distinguished and the word of faith prevail. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He also cites "and God had already made you victorious at Badr when you were a weak little force" (3:123). **[Maʿārif]** The Muslims turned to God more fervently because they trusted His promise: "if there be among you a hundred steadfast, they shall overcome two hundred" (8:66). **[Study Quran]** The commentators sometimes try to reconcile this verse with 8:44, because tradition consistently numbers the Quraysh army at a thousand or nearly so and the Muslims at just over three hundred, and doubling the latter gives less than a thousand; but an army three times one's own might well appear as only double, and the wording here differs from 8:44, which speaks explicitly of God causing each side to see the other as fewer. **[Study Quran]** One explanation is that "twice" could mean "twice again", implying three times in all, which would approximate the traditionally accepted ratio of three to one; others connect it to 8:66; and the actual numbers are not crucial to the message, as the different ratio in 8:65 shows.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** "There was" (*kāna*) is used in the masculine rather than "there was" (*kānat*) because the femininity of *āyah* is not real; alternatively it refers to "a clarification", so the sense goes to the meaning rather than the word, as in the line of Imruʾ al-Qays where a feminine noun is followed by a masculine adjective because the poet had the branch in mind. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Farrāʾ said it is put in the masculine because an attribute intervenes between the noun and the verb. **[Qurṭubī]** On *fiʾah*: the majority read *fiʾatun* in the nominative, meaning "one of the two is a host"; al-Ḥasan and Mujāhid read *fiʾatin* in the genitive as a substitute; Ibn Abī ʿAblah read both in the accusative — Aḥmad b. Yaḥyā said this is admissible as a circumstantial phrase, "they met differing, one believing and one disbelieving", while al-Zajjāj said it is an accusative of specification. **[Qurṭubī]** A band of people is called *fiʾah* because one returns to it (*yufāʾu ilayhā*) in time of hardship; al-Zajjāj derived it from splitting a head with a sword. **[Ṭabarī]** He gives the grammar of *fiʾatun* being raised as a new subject after "in two hosts that met", illustrated by the line of Kuthayyir, "I was like a man with two legs, one leg sound and one leg which time struck and crippled", and by the verse of al-Najāshī on the two Azd; the genitive would also be admissible on resumption, but he will not permit reciting it because the authoritative reciters are agreed against it, and the accusative would be admissible if it meant "differing". **[Study Quran]** "By the seeing of the eye" indicates that this is a seeing with the eye, which is why the verb takes a single object.

**Belief.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "God strengthens with His help whom He wills" — this should be an example for those who have intelligence and sound comprehension; they should reflect on God's wisdom, His decrees and His determining, that He gives victory to His believing servants in this life and on the Day when the witnesses stand forth. **[Saʿdī]** In this there is a lesson for people of insight: were it not that this is the truth which, when it confronts falsehood, crushes it and falsehood vanishes, then by the reckoning of material causes the outcome would have been the reverse. **[Maʿārif]** That a small group with very little to fight with should defeat a far stronger group and win, as was prophesied while the Muslims were still in Makkah, is an unusual event full of lessons for the observant and the discerning.

**Reflection.** The sign is not the battle alone but the arithmetic of it: a hundred to a thousand and yet the victory went to the few, so the outcome is a proof and not merely an event **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Maʿārif]**. The visions each side was given were themselves part of the outcome — the believers were shown a host they could face so that they would rely on God rather than on their numbers, and the idolaters were shown a host before which their hearts would fail **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Study Quran]**. Addressed to the Jews of Madīnah who had dismissed Badr as luck, the verse asks them to read the same arithmetic about themselves **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Jalālayn]**. It closes by naming the faculty required to read it: *insight*, not eyesight — the numbers were visible to everyone in the valley that day, and only some drew the conclusion **[Saʿdī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**.

## 3:14 — زُيِّنَ لِلنَّاسِ حُبُّ ٱلشَّهَوَٰتِ مِنَ ٱلنِّسَآءِ وَٱلْبَنِينَ وَٱلْقَنَٰطِيرِ ٱلْمُقَنطَرَةِ مِنَ ٱلذَّهَبِ وَٱلْفِضَّةِ وَٱلْخَيْلِ ٱلْمُسَوَّمَةِ وَٱلْأَنْعَٰمِ وَٱلْحَرْثِ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ مَتَٰعُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ وَٱللَّهُ عِندَهُۥ حُسْنُ ٱلْمَـَٔابِ

*Made attractive to people is the love of desires — of women and children, of hoarded heaps of gold and silver, of horses of mark, of cattle and tilled land. That is the enjoyment of the life of this world; and with God is the fair return.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God means: the love of what they desire of women, children and the rest of what He enumerated has been made attractive to people. **[Ṭabarī]** The intent of it, in his reading, is to rebuke the Jews who preferred this world and the love of leadership in it over following Muḥammad, after they had known him to be truthful. **[Jalālayn]** "Beautified for mankind is the love of lusts — that which the soul lusts after and calls for — beautified by Satan, or by God as a test — of women, children, stored-up heaps of gold and silver, horses of mark, that is fine horses, cattle, namely camels, cows and sheep, and tillage, the cultivation of land. That which is mentioned is the comfort of the life of this world, enjoyed while it lasts but then perishing; but God — with Him is the more excellent abode, the place of return, which is Paradise, and for this reason one should desire none other." **[Ibn Kathīr]** God mentions the delights He has placed in this life for people — women and children — and He began with women because the trial through them is more tempting. **[Saʿdī]** God informs us in these two verses of the state of people in preferring this world to the Hereafter, and sets out the enormous difference between the two abodes: these things have been adorned for people, so they gaze at them with their eyes, deem them lawful in their hearts, and their souls cling to their pleasures; every group of people inclines to one kind among these kinds, and they have made them their greatest concern and the limit of their knowledge; and yet with all this they are a small enjoyment, cut off in a short span. **[Study Quran]** Although the objects of desire named here are spoken of positively elsewhere in the Qurʾān as things for which one should be grateful in this world, here their seductive side is brought out, echoing "wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world" (18:46). **[Qurṭubī]** The verse, on either reading of the adorner, is an opening exhortation to all people, and contained within it is a rebuke to those of Muḥammad's contemporaries among the Jews and others.

**Context.** **[Maʿārif]** The preceding verses spoke of countering the hostility of the disbelievers with struggle; now the text explains why the disbelievers indulge in hostility against Islam and the Muslims at all. The reason for that, and for every evil deed, is the love of the life of this world: all sorts of people line up against the truth — some driven by greed for wealth or power, some goaded by lust, some defending false ancestral customs — and all of it is to grab a share in the temporal enjoyments of the present life, which these verses describe.

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** The scholars differ over who does the adorning. **[Qurṭubī]** One party said God adorned it, which is the apparent sense of the statement of ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb — and al-Bukhārī narrated it; the revelation says "We have made whatsoever is on the earth an adornment for it", and when ʿUmar said "Now, my Lord, when You have adorned it for us!", there came down "Say: shall I tell you of something better than that?" (3:15). **[Qurṭubī]** Another party said the adorner is Satan, which is the apparent sense of the saying of al-Ḥasan, who asked, "Who adorned it? None disparages it more severely than its Creator." **[Qurṭubī]** God's adorning is by bringing things into existence, preparing them for use and instituting the constitution that inclines towards them; Satan's adorning is by whispering, deception, and by making attractive the taking of them from other than their proper channels. **[Ṭabarī]** He records the saying of al-Ḥasan with its chain through Abū Nuʿaym from Abū al-Ashʿath: "Who adorned it? None is more severe in censuring it than its Creator." **[Ṭabarī]** And he records from Abū Bakr b. Ḥafṣ b. ʿUmar b. Saʿd that ʿUmar said: when "made attractive to people is the love of desires" came down, I said, "Now, my Lord, when You have adorned it for us!" — and then "Say: shall I tell you of something better than that?" was sent down.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** The majority read *zuyyina* as a passive with *ḥubbu* in the nominative; al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Mujāhid read *zayyana* as an active verb with *ḥubban* in the accusative. The *hāʾ* of *al-shahawāti* is vowelled to distinguish the noun from the adjective. **[Qurṭubī]** Following desires is ruinous and obeying them is destruction. **[Study Quran]** *Qinṭār* is rendered "heaps"; some try to give an exact measure for it, but in this context it should simply be taken as denoting a vast amount, hence "hoarded heaps", matching the consonance of the Arabic *qanāṭīr muqanṭarah*. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-qanāṭīr* is the plural of *qinṭār*, as in "and you have given one of them a *qinṭār*"; it is a great bundle of wealth, and it is also said to be the name of the standard by which things are weighed, as the *raṭl* and the *rubʿ* are; the Arabs say "the man has *qanṭara*" when his wealth reaches the point of being weighed by the *qinṭār*. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Zajjāj derived it from tying a thing fast and making it firm — the Arabs say "I *qanṭartu* the thing" when I made it firm, and the bridge (*qanṭarah*) is named for its firmness, as in Ṭarafah's line "like the arch of the Byzantine, whose lord swore it should be encompassed until it is raised with baked brick" — so that a *qinṭār* is, as it were, a tied bundle of wealth. **[Ibn Kathīr]** As for "horses of mark" (*musawwamah*), Ibn ʿAbbās said they are branded, beautiful horses, and this is the explanation of Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Saʿīd b. Jubayr, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAbdallāh b. Abzā, al-Suddī, al-Rabīʿ b. Anas and Abū Sinān and others; Makhūl said it refers to the horse with a white blaze and white feet. **[Study Quran]** "Horses of mark" refers to either branded or beautiful horses. **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Cattle" means camels, cows and sheep, and "tillage" means the land used for farming and growing plants. **[Ibn Kathīr]** "That is the enjoyment of the life of this world" means these are the delights of this life and its short-lived joys, and "but God, with Him is the fair return" means the best destination and reward.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** Muslim narrated from Anas that the Prophet said, "Paradise is hedged about with things disliked, and the Fire is hedged about with desires"; and he adds a reported saying, "the way to Paradise is rough and uphill and the way to the Fire is smooth and level", which carries the same sense. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Bukhārī and Muslim narrated: "I have not left behind me a trial more harmful to men than women." **[Ibn Kathīr]** He records the same ḥadīth in the *Ṣaḥīḥ*, and notes that when one enjoys women for the purpose of having children and preserving his chastity, that is encouraged; there are many ḥadīths encouraging marriage, among them "the best of this community are those of it with the most wives", "this world is a provision, and the best of its provision is a righteous wife", and "women and perfume have been made beloved to me, and the comfort of my eye is in the prayer". **[Ibn Kathīr]** ʿĀʾishah said: nothing was more beloved to the Messenger of God than women, except horses — and in another narration, than horses except women. **[Ibn Kathīr]** On children he cites, "Marry the loving, the fertile, for I will vie in numbers with the other communities through you on the Day of Resurrection". **[Qurṭubī]** He adds that the Prophet said to al-Ashʿath b. Qays, of the son of the daughter of Ḥamzah: "…they are the fruit of hearts and the coolness of the eyes, and yet with all that they are a cause of cowardice, of miserliness and of sorrow" — meaning that children make a father hang back from danger for fear of leaving them orphaned, make him withhold what he should spend, and make him grieve when harm befalls them. **[Ṭabarī]** On the measure of the *qinṭār* he records reports with their chains: from Muʿādh b. Jabal, Ibn ʿUmar, ʿĀṣim and Abū Hurayrah, "a thousand and two hundred *ūqiyah*"; from Ubayy b. Kaʿb, attributed to the Prophet, the same figure *(Ibn Kathīr judged this version unsound and most likely a saying of Ubayy rather than of the Prophet)*; from al-Ḥasan, Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḍaḥḥāk, "a thousand and two hundred dīnār" — and of silver, a thousand and two hundred *mithqāl*; from Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḍaḥḥāk, "twelve thousand dirhams, or a thousand dīnār"; from Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib, Qatādah, Abū Ṣāliḥ and al-Suddī, "eighty thousand" or "a hundred *raṭl* of gold", the *raṭl* being eight thousand *mithqāl*; from Mujāhid and Ibn ʿUmar, "seventy thousand dīnār"; and from others, "the fill of a bull's hide of gold". **[Ibn Kathīr]** Abū Hurayrah said, "The *qinṭār* is twelve thousand *ūqiyah*, each *ūqiyah* better than what is between heaven and earth" — recorded by Ibn Jarīr; and the commentators differ widely over its amount, all of which indicates that it is a large sum, as al-Ḍaḥḥāk and others said. **(digression: the exact measures recorded for the *qinṭār*)** **[Ibn Kathīr]** Imam Aḥmad recorded that Abū Dharr said the Messenger of God said: "There is no Arabian horse but it is granted two supplications at every dawn: 'O God, You have made me subservient to the son of Adam, so make me among the dearest of his wealth and household to him'."

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** Women are mentioned first because souls reach out to them most, for they are the snares of Satan and the trial of men; there are two trials in women and one in children — the two in women being that she may lead a man to cut his ties of kinship, since a woman commands her husband to cut himself off from his mother and sisters, and that a man is tried by gathering wealth from the lawful and the unlawful; and the one in children is the gathering of wealth for their sake. **[Qurṭubī]** Hence the instruction to look for the woman of religion: "Keep to the one with religion" — narrated by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah; and in the *Sunan* of Ibn Mājah, from ʿAbdallāh b. ʿUmar, the Prophet said, "Do not marry women for their beauty, for their beauty may ruin them; and do not marry them for their wealth, for their wealth may make them insolent; but marry them for religion — and a black slave-woman with a pierced nose who has religion is better." **[Qurṭubī]** Ibn Masʿūd narrated that the Messenger of God said, "Do not lodge your women in upper rooms and do not teach them writing" *(weak)*. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The desire for wealth is sometimes the product of arrogance and of wanting to dominate the weak and control the poor, and that conduct is prohibited; and sometimes the wish for more money is in order to spend it on acts of worship, kindness to family and kin and the various acts of righteousness and obedience, and that conduct is praised and encouraged in the religion. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The desire for horses is of three kinds: one who keeps them for use in God's way and uses them in battle when the occasion requires, and this owner is rewarded; one who keeps them for boasting and out of enmity to the people of Islam, and this one earns a burden; and one who keeps them to meet his needs and breed them and who does not forget God's right due on them, and these horses are a shield of sufficiency for their owner, as appears in the ḥadīth on "make ready against them whatever you can of force and of tethered horses" (8:60).

**Cross-references.** **[Maʿārif]** The wisdom behind the adorning of such things is stated elsewhere: "We made whatever is on the earth an adornment for it, so that We may test which of them is best in deed" (18:7). **[Maʿārif]** As for the verses in which such adorning is attributed to Satan — "Satan made their deeds seem fair to them" (8:48; 16:63; 27:24; 29:38) — the reference there is to things that are evil religiously and rationally, or to a false sense of adornment that transgresses the limits God has set; otherwise the adorning of lawful things is not absolutely bad, and may hold some benefit, which is why the act is also attributed to God. **[Study Quran]** The verb *zuyyina* describes a common theme connecting fallen human beings to their ephemeral desires (2:212; 9:37) and can mean, in other contexts, "to adorn" (15:16). **[Study Quran]** For cattle, see 6:138; for the "fair return", referring to the Garden, see 13:29 and Sūrah 38.

**Reflection.** The verse explains rather than merely condemns: God built the attraction into human nature so that the world keeps turning, so that we can even imagine the blessings of the next life, and so that the choice between the two is real **[Maʿārif]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. That is why the same objects are praised elsewhere and are named here only for their seductive side — the danger lies not in owning them but in their becoming the whole horizon **[Study Quran]**, **[Saʿdī]**. The list itself, running from persons to gold to beasts to soil, traces how far the heart can travel from God while thinking it is only providing for a family **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. And the closing clause is the whole remedy in half a line: these are the provision of a life that ends; with God is the fair homecoming **[Jalālayn]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**.

## 3:15 — قُلْ أَؤُنَبِّئُكُم بِخَيْرٍۢ مِّن ذَٰلِكُمْ ۚ لِلَّذِينَ ٱتَّقَوْا۟ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ جَنَّٰتٌۭ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا وَأَزْوَٰجٌۭ مُّطَهَّرَةٌۭ وَرِضْوَانٌۭ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ بَصِيرٌۢ بِٱلْعِبَادِ

*Say: "Shall I tell you of something better than that? For those who are God-fearing, with their Lord are gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein, and spouses made pure, and contentment from God. And God sees the servants."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God means: say, O Muḥammad, to the people for whom the love of desires — of women, children and the rest of what your Lord mentioned — has been made attractive: "Shall I tell you", that is, shall I inform you and make you know, "of something better than that", that is, better and more excellent for you than what has been made attractive to you in this world of the love of women, children and heaped-up measures of gold and silver, and the kinds of wealth that are the provision of this life. **[Ṭabarī]** "Those who are God-fearing" are those who feared God and obeyed Him by discharging His obligations and avoiding His acts of disobedience, and "with their Lord" means with their Lord, their Master. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The verse means: say, O Muḥammad, to the people, "Shall I tell you of what is better than the delights and joys of this life that will soon perish?" God then informed them of what is better: for those who have *taqwā* there are gardens with their Lord beneath which rivers flow, rivers running through it carrying various kinds of drink — honey, milk, wine and water — such as no eye has seen, no ear has heard and no human heart has conceived. **[Jalālayn]** "Say, O Muhammad, to your people: 'Shall I tell you, shall I inform you, of something better than that? — than what has been mentioned of lusts; this interrogative is meant as an affirmative — for those who are fearful of idolatry, with their Lord — *ʿinda rabbihim* is the predicate, the subject being what follows — are gardens underneath which rivers flow, abiding therein, decreed for them: therein is eternal life when they enter it; and spouses purified of menstruation and other impurities; and beatitude — read *riḍwān* or *ruḍwān*, meaning much pleasure — from God; and God is Seer, knower, of His servants, requiting each of them according to his deeds." **[Saʿdī]** Then He informs us that those who fear God and stand in His service have better than these pleasures: they have all kinds of good things and abiding bliss such as no eye has seen, no ear has heard and no human heart has conceived; they have God's good pleasure, which is greater than everything; and they have spouses purified of every blemish and defect, beautiful in character and perfect in nature — for negation entails its opposite, so purifying them of blemishes entails describing them with perfections. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-riḍwān* is a verbal noun from *al-riḍā*: when the people of the Garden enter it, God says to them, "Do you want anything I should increase you in?" and they say, "Our Lord, what is better than this?" and He says, "My pleasure — and I shall never be angry with you after it ever again," narrated by Muslim. **[Ibn Kathīr]** "And God will be pleased with them" means His pleasure descends upon them and He will never be angry with them after it, which is why He says in Sūrah Barāʾah, "and contentment from God is greater" (9:72) — greater than the abiding bliss He has granted them.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** The scholars of Arabic differ over where the interrogation in this sentence ends. **[Ṭabarī]** Some said it ends at "than that", after which the statement of what awaits the God-fearing begins — "for those who fear, with their Lord are gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein" — and that is why *jannātun* is in the nominative. **[Ṭabarī]** Those who say this allow no reading of the word but the nominative, since it is the predicate of a subject and is not a resumption of "better", in which case the genitive would be admissible; and even though it is on their view a predicate of a subject, it still spells out the meaning of the "better" that God commanded His prophet to ask about, and *al-jannātu* is raised by the prepositional phrase "for those who fear, with their Lord". **[Ṭabarī]** Others said something close to this, but allowed both the genitive and the nominative for *jannātin*: the genitive as a resumption of "better", and the nominative by taking "for those who fear" as the predicate of a subject. **[Ṭabarī]** Others said the interrogation in fact ends at "with their Lord", and then "gardens beneath which rivers flow" begins a new sentence, so that the sense is: "Say: shall I tell you of something better than that for those who fear, with their Lord?" — and then as though it were said, "What is it for them?" or "What is that?" — the answer being: it is "gardens beneath which rivers flow". **[Ṭabarī]** His own preference is the first view: the interrogation ends at "better than that", and what follows is a fresh statement about those to whom the gardens belong, "for those who fear, with their Lord are gardens", so that it comes out as a statement of fact spelling out the meaning of the "better" about which he was told to ask, and no suppressed pronoun is then needed. **[Ṭabarī]** "Abiding therein" is in the accusative as a circumstantial phrase. **[Qurṭubī]** The interrogation ends at "than that"; "for those who fear" is a fronted predicate and *jannātun* is raised as the subject; alternatively the limit is "with their Lord", and *jannātun* is then raised by an implied subject — "that is gardens" — and on that reading the genitive *jannātin* is admissible as a substitute for "better", which is not admissible on the first reading. **[Qurṭubī]** Ibn ʿAṭiyyah said that this verse and the one before it correspond to the Prophet's saying, "A woman is married for four things: for her wealth, her lineage, her beauty and her religion — so take the one with religion, may your hands be dusty", narrated by Muslim and others; "so take the one with religion" is the counterpart of this verse, and what precedes it is the counterpart of the previous one. God mentioned these verses as a consolation with regard to this world and a strengthening of the souls of those who leave it. **[Qurṭubī]** In "and God sees the servants" there is both promise and threat.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Study Quran]** The Prophet described an exchange between God and the inhabitants of the Garden: "Are you content?" — "Why should we not be content, when You have given us what no one else among Your creation has been given?" — "Shall I give you what is better than that?" — "Our Lord, what could be better than that?" — "I shall loose My contentment upon you and not be angry with you ever again." **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Purified spouses" means purified of filth, dirt, harm, menstruation, post-natal bleeding and everything else that affects women in this world. **[Maʿārif]** He notes that the blessings named in the Hereafter are only three — gardens, purified spouses and God's approval — as against the six named in the previous verse, and explains the omissions. **[Maʿārif]** Children are not mentioned because a person loves children in this world as helpers and strengtheners and as the means by which his name lives on; in the Hereafter he will need no such help, nor will he "die" so as to need an heir. Whoever has children will have them in the Garden; whoever has none will simply have no desire for them; and should someone wish for them, God would grant them — in a ḥadīth in *Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī* the Prophet said that if an inhabitant of Paradise wishes for a child, its conception, birth and growth will be completed in a single moment. *(digression: the reports on children and on farming in Paradise)* **[Maʿārif]** Gold and silver are not mentioned because they are needed in this world to buy things with; in the Hereafter there will be no buying, selling or payment, and whatever the inhabitant of Paradise needs will be provided at once — though Paradise is not without gold and silver, sound reports mentioning places there with bricks of gold and silver laid in courses, and they were not considered worth mentioning in view of the uniqueness of that life. **[Maʿārif]** Horses are omitted because in the Hereafter there is no journeying to be covered, though sound ḥadīths do establish that fine horses will be presented to the inhabitants of Paradise on Fridays to ride to visit friends and kin; cattle and tillage likewise, since what they provide has already been provided without their mediation — although should someone there wish to try farming for no necessary reason, his wish would be granted, everything needed being assembled at once and ploughing, sowing, ripening and reaping accomplished in a single moment, as appears in some reports in al-Ṭabarānī *(weak)*. **[Maʿārif]** After such a comprehensive declaration, nothing further need be named individually: the blessings mentioned are already there without any need to ask for them.

**Belief.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Spouses purified" and "contentment from God" belong to the promise made to the God-fearing, and the contentment is the greatest of the gifts, greater than the abiding bliss itself; and "God is All-Seer of His servants" means that He gives each provision according to what he deserves. **[Saʿdī]** "And God sees the servants": He eases each of them towards what He created him for. As for the people of felicity, He eases them to work for the abiding abode, and they take from this present life what helps them to worship and obey Him; as for the people of wretchedness and turning away, He assigns them to the works of wretchedness, and they are content with the life of this world, are at ease in it and take it as their settled home. **[Maʿārif]** God's approval is the greatest blessing, after which there is no danger of His displeasure; and since the danger of displeasure does not exist in Paradise, there is also no danger that its blessings will ever be withdrawn or diminished. **[Maʿārif]** He sums the whole subject up in the ḥadīth: "The world is accursed and accursed is what is in it, except what is used to seek the face of God" — and in another narration, "except the remembrance of God and what He loves, and except a scholar and a student" — reported by Ibn Mājah and al-Ṭabarānī from Abū Hurayrah.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** "That" refers to the aforementioned passions, which are a pale shadow of the joys of the Hereafter; for the Garden's rivers and purified spouses, see 2:25 and 4:57. **[Study Quran]** God's contentment is at the pinnacle of the paradisal state, since it is greater even than the Gardens (9:72); it is sought in this world too (5:16; 48:29; 57:27; 59:8), and God and human beings can be reciprocally content with one another, as in "return to your Lord, content, contenting" (89:28), and in 5:119; 58:22; 98:8; and contentment and good pleasure are opposed to God's wrath (3:162; 47:28). **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites 9:72, "and contentment from God is greater".

**Reflection.** The verse answers the previous one in the same register it was asked: not by forbidding what is attractive but by naming something better **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Ṭabarī]**. The comparison is deliberately lopsided — six fading goods against three abiding ones, and even those three crowned by a gift that is not a thing at all but a relationship: that God is pleased and will not be displeased again **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Study Quran]**. The grammar itself is read as carrying the point: the question ends and the good news begins, so that what follows is stated as fact rather than offered as a bargain **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Qurṭubī]**. And the closing name of God as the One who sees the servants turns the whole passage into a matter of who is being watched — the eye of the heart, not the ledger of the world **[Saʿdī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**.

## 3:16 — ٱلَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَآ إِنَّنَآ ءَامَنَّا فَٱغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا وَقِنَا عَذَابَ ٱلنَّارِ

*Those who say: "Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us our sins and shield us from the punishment of the Fire."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The sense is: "Say: shall I tell you of something better than that, for those who fear — those who say, 'Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us our sins and shield us from the punishment of the Fire'." **[Ṭabarī]** He glosses it: those who say "we have affirmed You, Your Prophet and what he brought from You" — "so forgive us our sins", meaning cover our sins for us by Your pardoning them and by Your leaving off punishing us for them; "and shield us from the punishment of the Fire", meaning avert from us Your punishing us with the Fire — that is, do not punish us, our Lord, with the Fire. **[Ṭabarī]** He notes that they single out being shielded from the punishment of the Fire because whoever is moved away from the Fire that Day has triumphed in escaping God's punishment and has won the fair homecoming. **[Ibn Kathīr]** God describes the God-fearing, His pious servants, whom He promised tremendous rewards: those who say "Our Lord, we have indeed believed" in You, Your Book and Your Messenger; "so forgive us our sins" — because of our faith in You and in what You have legislated for us, so forgive us our errors and our shortcomings by Your bounty and mercy; "and save us from the punishment of the Fire." **[Jalālayn]** "Those" is either an adjectival qualification of, or a substitution for, the previous "those" — those who say: "Our Lord, we believe in You and in Your Prophet, so forgive us our sins and guard us from the chastisement of the Fire." **[Saʿdī]** These are the ones firmly rooted in knowledge, the people of knowledge and faith, who take their faith as the means of approaching their Lord for the forgiveness of their sins and for protection from the punishment of the Fire; and this is among the means God loves — that a servant approach his Lord by the very faith and righteous deeds with which God has favoured him, asking that He complete His favour upon him by the full reward and by the averting of the punishment. **[Qurṭubī]** "Our Lord" means "O our Lord"; "we have believed" means we have affirmed; "so forgive us our sins" is a supplication for forgiveness; and "shield us from the punishment of the Fire" has been dealt with already in al-Baqarah. **[Study Quran]** "Those who" refers to the reverent servants of the previous verse; and the verb "shield us" is etymologically related to reverence (*taqwā*), as when one is shielded from the avarice of his own soul (59:9; 64:16), which relates to the sense of *taqwā* as being mindful and on guard when it comes to sin and to obedience to God.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** The phrase "those who say" admits two grammatical positions: the genitive, as a resumption of the first "those" — "for those who fear"; and the nominative, as the beginning of a new sentence, since it stands at the opening of a verse other than the one containing the first "those". **[Ṭabarī]** He compares the nominative to God's saying "God has bought from the believers their selves and their wealth" (9:111), followed at the opening of the next verse by "the repentant, the worshippers" (9:112); and he adds that the genitive would also have been admissible. **[Qurṭubī]** "Those" is a substitute for "for those who fear", and one may also take it as a nominative — "they are those who" — or as an accusative of praise. **[Ṭabarī]** On the verb: the root of *qinā* is "God protected so-and-so from such-and-such", meaning He warded it off from him, "so He protects him"; and when someone asks for it he says "shield me from such-and-such".

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Qurṭubī]** The commentators differ on the meaning of "those who seek forgiveness before the dawn": Anas b. Mālik said they are the ones who ask forgiveness, and Qatādah said they are the ones who pray; al-Qurṭubī adds that there is no contradiction, for they pray and they also seek forgiveness. **[Qurṭubī]** The dawn is singled out because it is the place of acceptance and the time when supplication is answered; and he cites the ḥadīth, narrated by al-Tirmidhī, explaining Jacob's words to his sons "I shall ask my Lord to forgive you" (12:98) — "he deferred that until the dawn" — together with the report that the Prophet asked Gabriel which part of the night is most heard, and he answered, "I do not know, except that the Throne trembles at the dawn." **[Ibn Kathīr]** He likewise reports that when Jacob said to his children "I shall ask my Lord to forgive you", he waited until the latter part of the night to make his supplication, and he records the ḥadīth of the descent — that in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s, the *Musnad* and the *Sunan* collections, through several Companions, the Prophet said that every night, when the last third of it remains, our Lord descends to the lowest heaven saying, "Is there anyone to ask Me, that I may grant him? Is there anyone to call upon Me, that I may answer him? Is there anyone seeking My forgiveness, that I may forgive him?" **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-saḥar* may be read with the *ḥāʾ* open or quiescent; al-Zajjāj said it runs from when the night turns away until the second dawn breaks, and Ibn Zayd said it is the last sixth of the night. **[Qurṭubī]** Seeking forgiveness is something to which encouragement is given: God has praised those who seek forgiveness in this verse and elsewhere, saying "and in the dawns they would seek forgiveness". **[Qurṭubī]** Anas b. Mālik said: we were commanded to seek forgiveness seventy times at the dawn. *(weak)* **[Ibn Kathīr]** The two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s record from ʿĀʾishah that the Messenger of God performed the *witr* in the first part of the night, in the middle of it and in the last part of it, and then later on he would perform it only in the last part. **[Ibn Kathīr]** ʿAbdallāh b. ʿUmar used to pray at night and would ask, "O Nāfiʿ, is it the last part of the night yet?"; and if Nāfiʿ said yes, Ibn ʿUmar would begin supplicating to God and seeking His forgiveness until dawn — reported by Ibn Abī Ḥātim.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** The supplication is made with faith as its ground: "forgive us our sins *because* we have believed in You and in what You have legislated for us" — faith, not merit, is what they bring. **[Saʿdī]** It belongs to the means God loves that a servant should come to his Lord through what God has Himself bestowed on him of faith and righteous works, asking that the favour be completed by the reward and by the averting of the punishment. **[Ibn Kathīr]** What is asked for is forgiveness of errors and shortcomings by God's bounty and mercy, and protection from the Fire itself.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** The verb "shield us" also appears at 2:201, 3:191 and 40:7. **[Qurṭubī]** He refers back to his treatment of the phrase in al-Baqarah. **[Study Quran]** For the night vigil, see 17:79; 25:64; 73:20.

**Reflection.** The God-fearing introduced in the previous verse are not described first by what they own or even by what they do, but by what they say in private — a prayer of four short clauses in which faith is stated, forgiveness is asked, and the Fire is named **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. They come with their faith as their only claim, which is the pattern Saʿdī singles out as a means God loves **[Saʿdī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. And the grammar ties the whole passage together: the same people who are promised gardens are the people who pray to be kept from the Fire, so that hope and fear are held in one sentence **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Study Quran]**.

## 3:17 — ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ وَٱلصَّٰدِقِينَ وَٱلْقَٰنِتِينَ وَٱلْمُنفِقِينَ وَٱلْمُسْتَغْفِرِينَ بِٱلْأَسْحَارِ

*The patient, and the truthful, and the devout, and those who spend, and those who seek forgiveness at the dawns.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "The patient" are those who were patient in hardship and affliction and in the moment of battle; "the truthful" are those who were true to God in what they said by making real their acknowledgement of Him and of His Messenger and of what he brought from Him, through acting on what He commanded and keeping back from what He forbade; and "the devout" are those who obey Him. **[Ṭabarī]** He notes that he has already set out the meaning of each of these words with its supporting evidence and with the reports of those who held a view about them, so there is no need to repeat it here. **[Ṭabarī]** Qatādah, through Saʿīd, said: "the truthful" are a people whose mouths were truthful and whose hearts and tongues were upright, and who were truthful in secret and in public; "the patient" are a people who were patient in obeying God and patient in keeping away from what He has forbidden; and "the devout" are those who obey God. **[Ṭabarī]** "Those who spend" are the ones who pay the *zakāh* on their wealth and disburse it in the ways God has commanded, and who spend their wealth in the directions God has permitted them to spend it in. **[Ibn Kathīr]** "The patient" are those who are patient while performing acts of obedience and abandoning the prohibitions; "the truthful" are those who are true in their proclamation of faith by performing the difficult deeds; "the devout" are those who submit and obey God; "those who spend" spend from their wealth on all the acts of obedience they were commanded, being kind to kith and kin, helping the needy and comforting the destitute; and "those who seek forgiveness at the dawns" — which testifies to the virtue of seeking God's forgiveness in the latter part of the night. **[Jalālayn]** "The patient in obedience and against disobedience — *al-ṣābirīna* is an adjectival qualification of *alladhīna* — truthful in their faith, obedient, compliant before God, expenders of charity, imploring God's pardon by saying 'Lord, forgive us' at daybreak, in the last part of the night, singled out here for mention because it is the time of unawareness and of the joy of sleep." **[Saʿdī]** Then He described them with the most beautiful of qualities: patience, which is holding the soul to what God loves in pursuit of His pleasure — they are patient in obeying God, patient in keeping away from disobeying Him and patient under His painful decrees; truthfulness in words and states, which is the correspondence of the outward with the inward and a true resolve to travel the straight path; devotion, which is constant obedience accompanied by sustained humility and lowliness; spending in the paths of good and upon the poor and those in need; and seeking forgiveness, especially at the dawns, for they would extend the prayer to the time of the dawn and then sit seeking God's forgiveness.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** These words, and the rest like them, are in the genitive as a resumption of "those who say, 'Our Lord, we have believed'", and their being in the genitive shows that "those who say" is itself in the genitive, resuming "for those who fear, with their Lord". **[Qurṭubī]** "The patient" means patient against acts of disobedience and desires, though it is also said to mean patient in acts of obedience; "the truthful" means truthful in deeds and words; "the devout" are the obedient; and "those who spend" do so in the way of God. **[Qurṭubī]** He adds that these meanings have been set out fully in his commentary on al-Baqarah, and that God has explained in this verse the character of the God-fearing who were promised the gardens. **[Study Quran]** "Devoutly obedient" renders *qānit*, which carries the sense of conformity, but also of constancy, standing and silence (see 4:34). **[Study Quran]** "Those who spend" spend in the way of God, which carries the sense of giving for the good and not only of monetary expenditure. **[Study Quran]** "Before dawn" renders *asḥār*, the last part of the night before the morning twilight (see also 51:18).

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ṭabarī]** On the meaning of "those who seek forgiveness at the dawns" the commentators differed. **[Ṭabarī]** Some said they are the ones who pray at the dawns: Qatādah said, through Saʿīd and through ʿĪsā b. Abī Jaʿfar from his father, "they pray at the dawns". **[Ṭabarī]** Others said they are simply those who seek forgiveness; and he transmits the report, through Ḥurayth b. Abī Maṭar from Ibrāhīm b. Ḥāṭib from his father, that he heard a man in the dawn in a corner of the mosque saying, "Lord, You commanded me and I obeyed You — and this is the dawn, so forgive me", and he looked and it was Ibn Masʿūd *(its chain contains narrators graded weak by the hadith critics)*. **[Qurṭubī]** Sufyān al-Thawrī said: I have heard that when the first part of the night comes, a caller cries out, "Let the devout stand", and they stand praying thus until the dawn. **[Study Quran]** The Prophet would rise for prayers before dawn and strongly encouraged others to do so; for the supererogatory night prayer see 17:79, 25:64 and 73:20. **[Study Quran]** In Islamic spirituality the night vigil is profoundly significant: it is when one is alone with none to see, when the world is quiet, and when there is an ease of concentration and inwardness — the inverse of one's usual state during the day, when the egotistical passions are awake but the spiritual heart is often asleep.

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** Seeking forgiveness is something to which encouragement is given, and God has praised those who seek forgiveness in this verse and in others, saying "and in the dawns they would seek forgiveness". **[Ṭabarī]** Spending is specified as the payment of the *zakāh* and as expenditure in the channels God has permitted, which is the framework within which "those who spend" is to be understood.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** For the night vigil see 17:79; 25:64; 73:20; and for *asḥār*, 51:18. **[Qurṭubī]** He cites "and in the dawns they would seek forgiveness" as the parallel term of praise. **[Ṭabarī]** He cross-refers to his own earlier treatments of "the patient", "the truthful" and "the devout" elsewhere in the commentary.

**Reflection.** Five qualities are given, and they are arranged as a whole character rather than a checklist: the endurance to keep to the path, the honesty to mean what one says, the constancy to stay in obedience, the open hand, and then the dawn **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Saʿdī]**. The last of them is placed at the end because it closes the circuit — a day of patience, truth, obedience and giving ends by asking forgiveness for it, at the hour when no one is watching and the Throne, in the report, trembles **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. That the patient, truthful, devout and generous are also the ones who beg pardon tells the reader that no amount of good work makes a servant independent of forgiveness **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Jalālayn]**. It is the quietest item on the list, and the one that names the time at which faith is most itself **[Study Quran]**, **[Saʿdī]**.

## 3:18 — شَهِدَ ٱللَّهُ أَنَّهُۥ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ وَٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْعِلْمِ قَآئِمًۢا بِٱلْقِسْطِ ۚ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*God bears witness that there is no god but He — and the angels, and those possessed of knowledge — upholding justice. There is no god but He, the Mighty, the Wise.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God means: God bears witness that there is no god but He, and the angels bear witness, and those possessed of knowledge; the angels being conjoined to the Name of God, with *annahu* opened because the verb "bore witness" governs it. **[Ibn Kathīr]** God bears witness, and God suffices as a Witness — He is the most truthful and most just of witnesses, and His word is the absolute truth; "that there is no god but He" means that He alone is the Lord and God of all creation, and everyone and everything are His servants, His creation and in need of Him, while God is the Rich, free of need of anyone or anything. **[Jalālayn]** "God bears witness — that is, He has made it clear to His creation through proofs and signs that there is no god, none truly worshipped in existence, except Him; He has borne witness to this and the angels have borne witness to it by affirming it, and those of knowledge among the prophets and the believers through their conviction and in words; upholding, constantly and uniquely maintaining, His creation with justice — *qāʾiman* is in the accusative as a circumstantial qualifier; there is no god except Him, repeated for emphasis; the Mighty in His Kingdom, the Wise in His actions." **[Maʿārif]** The three witnesses are of different orders. God's own testimony is figurative in the sense that His being and attributes and the manifestations of His power and creativity everywhere are open signs of His oneness, and the messengers and books He sent all confirm that truth. The second testimony is that of the angels, who are close to God, carry out His instructions and testify, knowing and seeing, that God alone is worthy of worship. The third is that of the people of knowledge. **[Saʿdī]** This is the greatest of the true testimonies — issuing from the Mighty King, from the angels and from the people of knowledge — concerning the greatest thing testified to: God's oneness and His upholding of justice. That entails testimony to the whole of the revealed law and the whole of the recompense, since the origin and foundation of the law and the religion is the oneness of God and singling Him out in worship.

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** Some of the Baṣran grammarians took "God bore witness" to mean "God decreed", raising "the angels" in the sense "and the angels are witnesses, and those possessed of knowledge". **[Ṭabarī]** "Upholding justice" means that He is the One who undertakes justice among His creation; *al-qisṭ* is justice, from "he is equitable" and "he has been just" when he acts justly; and *qāʾiman* is in the accusative as a circumstantial qualifier. **[Qurṭubī]** "Those possessed of knowledge" has been variously identified: it has been said to mean the prophets; Ibn Kaysān said the Emigrants and the Helpers; Muqātil said the believers among the People of the Book; and al-Suddī and al-Kalbī said all the believers, which is the most apparent because it is general. **[Qurṭubī]** Saʿīd b. Jubayr said that there were three hundred and sixty idols around the Kaʿbah, and when this verse came down they fell prostrate *(Isrāʾīliyyāt)*. **[Maʿārif]** The expression *ulū al-ʿilm* means the blessed prophets and the men who have the knowledge of Islam; and it is also possible that the term refers in an absolute sense to those who, by inquiry on sound intellectual principles or by reflection on the universe, could arrive at knowledge of the unity of God, even if they are not scholars in the technical sense whose knowledge is acquired through a formal discipline from Islam's own sources. **[Study Quran]** "Upholding justice" is thought by most to refer to God, but grammatically it could refer to the angels and the possessors of knowledge as well — an opinion made more plausible by the very similar wording of 5:8, "be steadfast for God, bearing witness to justice", and of 4:135. It can also be read in connection with the opening of the next verse, so that "God, the angels and the believers bear witness that truly the religion…".

**Readings.** **[Ṭabarī]** The reciters of the Muslims read *annahu* with the *alif* opened, since "bore witness" governs the first *anna*, and read the second *inna* with the *alif* broken as the beginning of a new sentence — that is, "truly the religion with God is Islam" (3:19). **[Ṭabarī]** One of the later grammarians, however, read both with the *alif* opened, meaning "God bears witness that there is no god but He, and that the religion with God is Islam", conjoining the second *anna* to the first and eliding the conjunction while intending it. **[Ṭabarī]** He argued from a reading attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, who is said to have read the first *anna* broken and the second opened, and from a reading of Ibn Masʿūd, who is said to have read the first opened and the second broken; and he claimed that his own reading of both as open combines the two. **[Ṭabarī]** Al-Ṭabarī rejects this: the reading departs from the reading of all the reciters of Islam, early and late, on the strength of an interpretation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd whose ascription is not known through any chain, sound or unsound; and it is enough to witness its error that it falls outside the reading of the people of Islam. The correct reading is the first *alif* opened and the second broken, as a fresh beginning. **[Ṭabarī]** A report from al-Suddī is cited that would support the second reading — "God bears witness, He and the angels, and the knowledgeable among people, that the religion with God is Islam" — and al-Ṭabarī shows that even on that interpretation two analyses remain open, one treating the first *anna* as a parenthetical and the witnessing as governing the second. **[Jalālayn]** He records the variant reading *anna* as an inclusive substitution for *annahu* to the end of the verse, giving "God bears witness that the religion with God is Islam".

**Context.** **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Kalbī said that when the Messenger of God appeared in Madīnah, two rabbis of the rabbis of Syria came to him; when they sighted Madīnah one said to the other that the city resembled the description of the city of the prophet who would appear at the end of time, and when they came before him they recognised him by the description and the marks. They said, "You are Muḥammad?" He said, "Yes." They said, "And you are Aḥmad?" He said, "Yes." They said, "We will ask you about a testimony, and if you tell us of it we shall believe in you and affirm you." He said, "Ask me." They said, "Tell us of the greatest testimony in the Book of God," and God sent down to His prophet "God bears witness that there is no god but He, and the angels, and those possessed of knowledge, upholding justice", and the two men submitted and affirmed the Messenger of God. **[Maʿārif]** Imām al-Baghawī reports the same account, with the two Jewish scholars recognising Madīnah from the description in the Torah as the city of the last prophet, then finding in him every attribute the Torah had foretold, and asking him which is the greatest witness in the Book of God, whereupon this verse was revealed as the answer and both of them embraced Islam at once.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Maʿārif]** According to a ḥadīth in the *Musnad* of Aḥmad, when the Prophet recited this verse at ʿArafāt he said immediately afterwards, "And to that, O my Lord, I too am a witness." **[Maʿārif]** A narration from Imām al-Aʿmash says that whoever recites this verse and then says, "And to that, O my Lord, I too am a witness", God will say to the angels on the Last Day: "My servant made a promise, and I am the foremost to keep a promise when I make it, so let My servant be admitted into Paradise." **[Maʿārif]** Another ḥadīth, from Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī, says that whoever recites the Throne Verse (2:255), this verse, and "Say: O God, Master of the Kingdom…" to the end of 3:26–27, God will forgive all his sins, admit him to Paradise and take care of seventy of his needs, the least of them his forgiveness *(reported by al-Daylamī; weak)*. **[Qurṭubī]** He cites the Prophetic saying "The scholars are the heirs of the prophets", and a report he transmits with its chain through al-Barāʾ: "The scholars are the heirs of the prophets; the people of heaven love them and the fish in the sea seek forgiveness for them when they die, until the Day of Resurrection" *(its chain is graded broken and the hadith disputed)*, together with the ḥadīth of Abū al-Dardāʾ narrated by Abū Dāwūd on the same subject. **[Qurṭubī]** Ghālib al-Qaṭṭān relates that he came to Kūfah and lodged near al-Aʿmash, and that on the night he meant to set out for Baṣrah, al-Aʿmash rose for the night vigil and recited this verse, then said, "And I bear witness to what God has borne witness to, and I entrust this testimony to God as a deposit with Him, and the religion with God is Islam," repeating it several times. When Ghālib asked him about it, al-Aʿmash made him wait a full year and then related it, through Abū Wāʾil, from ʿAbdallāh b. Masʿūd, that the Prophet said: "The companion of it will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and God will say, 'My servant made a covenant with Me, and I am the most deserving of those who fulfil: admit My servant into Paradise'" *(its chain is graded problematic)*. **[Ibn Kathīr]** He notes that the great merit of this verse is that God mentions the testimony of the angels and of those who have knowledge after His own, and that this verse emphasises the great virtue of those who have knowledge; and he cites 4:166 as the parallel in which God bears witness to what He has sent down to the Prophet.

**Belief.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Upholding justice" means in all that He does; "there is no god but He" thus emphasises the fact; and "the Mighty, the Wise" is the Mighty One who is not subject to weakness through His might and greatness, the Wise in all His statements, actions, legislation and decrees. **[Saʿdī]** The testimony to oneness and to God's upholding of justice entails testimony to the whole revealed law and to the whole recompense: the legal acts of worship, transactions and their consequences, and the commands and prohibitions, are all justice and equity in which there is no wrong or oppression in any respect, but rather the utmost wisdom and soundness; and the recompense for good and bad deeds is likewise all equity and justice. **[Study Quran]** God is the supreme witness in that He was when there was nothing else with Him — as is said in a well-known ḥadīth, "God was, and there was nothing other than Him" — and He thus has most right to be called a witness; God is spoken of as Witness throughout the Qurʾān, as in "and Thou art Witness over all things" (5:117), and He bears witness along with the angels in 4:166. **[Study Quran]** See also 7:172, where the primordial covenant between human beings and God is framed as humans bearing witness to the Lordship of God.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "But God bears witness to what He has sent down to you" (4:166). **[Study Quran]** 5:117; 4:166; 5:8; 4:135; 7:172. **[Qurṭubī]** "Say: what thing is greatest in testimony? Say: God" — cited by al-Saʿdī as the proof-text for the greatness of this testimony.

**Reflection.** The verse is constructed as a courtroom in which the first witness is God Himself, and the second and third are the inhabitants of the unseen and the inhabitants of the seen **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Jalālayn]**. To be named alongside the angels is, as the commentators keep repeating, the charter of the people of knowledge — and the condition of that honour is that what they testify to is the same thing God testifies to, not their own speculations **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Saʿdī]**. The words "upholding justice" sit between the two declarations of oneness, so that the unity of God is stated twice and joined by equity: the God who is one is the God who wrongs no one **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Study Quran]**. And the report of the two Syrian scholars asking for the greatest testimony in the Book of God, and receiving this verse, shows what the verse was understood to be — the single sentence around which everything else turns **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Maʿārif]**.

## 3:19 — إِنَّ ٱلدِّينَ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْإِسْلَٰمُ ۗ وَمَا ٱخْتَلَفَ ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا جَآءَهُمُ ٱلْعِلْمُ بَغْيًۢا بَيْنَهُمْ ۗ وَمَن يَكْفُرُ بِـَٔايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَرِيعُ ٱلْحِسَابِ

*Truly the religion with God is Islam. And those who were given the Book did not differ except after knowledge had come to them, through insolence among themselves. Whoever disbelieves in the signs of God — God is swift in reckoning.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** *Al-dīn* in this place means obedience and lowliness, as in the poet's line, "…and the people, except us, were *dīnā*", meaning made obedient and abased; and in the line of al-Qaṭāmī, "Nawār used to *tadīnuka* the religions", meaning she used to abase you; and in the line of al-Aʿshā, "he *dāna* Rabāb when they hated *al-dīn*", where *dāna* means he abased and *al-dīn* means obedience. **[Ṭabarī]** *Al-islām* likewise is submission with abasement and humility, the verb being "he entered into *al-silm*", just as one says "the people were stricken with drought" when they entered drought and "they entered spring" when they entered spring — so *aslama* means he entered into submission, which is yielding with humility and leaving off resistance. **[Ṭabarī]** The meaning is therefore: the obedience that is truly obedience with Him is obedience to Him, and the acknowledgement of tongues and hearts of servitude and lowliness to Him, and their yielding to Him in obedience in what He commanded and forbade and their abasing themselves to Him in that — without exalting themselves against Him or swerving from Him and without associating any of His creation with Him in servitude or divinity. **[Ṭabarī]** He records this from Qatādah — "Islam is the testimony that there is no god but God and the acknowledgement of what came from God; it is the religion God prescribed for Himself, with which He sent His messengers and to which He guided His friends; He accepts no other and rewards with nothing else" — and from Abū al-ʿĀliyah through al-Rabīʿ — "Islam is sincerity to God alone, worshipping Him with no partner, establishing the prayer, paying the *zakāh*, and all the other obligations following upon these" — and from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr through Ibn Isḥāq — "what you are upon, O Muḥammad, of declaring the oneness of the Lord and affirming the truthfulness of the messengers". **[Jalālayn]** "The religion with God, pleasing to Him, is submission to the One God — *al-islām*, that is, the divine law with which the messengers were sent, founded upon the affirmation of God's oneness. Those who were given the Scripture, the Jews and the Christians, differed in religion, some affirming oneness and others rejecting it, only after the knowledge of oneness came to them, through transgression on the part of the disbelievers among themselves. And whoever disbelieves in God's signs — God is swift at reckoning, that is, at requiting him." **[Saʿdī]** God informs us that the religion which is God's religion, and apart from which nothing else is accepted, is Islam — which is yielding to God alone, outwardly and inwardly, in what He has legislated through the tongues of His messengers, as He said, "whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will not be accepted from him, and in the Hereafter he will be among the losers". Whoever follows a religion other than the religion of Islam has not in reality submitted to God, because he has not travelled the path which God laid down through His messengers. **[Qurṭubī]** *Al-dīn* in this verse is obedience and the religious community (*al-millah*), and *al-islām* means faith together with the acts of obedience, according to Abū al-ʿĀliyah and the majority of the theologians. **[Qurṭubī]** The original sense of the two terms "faith" and "Islam" is that they differ, on the basis of the ḥadīth of Gabriel; they may also be used as synonyms, each named by the name of the other, as in the ḥadīth of the delegation of ʿAbd al-Qays, where he commanded them to faith in God alone and then asked, "Do you know what faith is?", and they said, "God and His messenger know best", and he said, "The testimony that there is no god but God and that Muḥammad is the Messenger of God, the establishing of the prayer, the paying of the *zakāh*, the fast of Ramaḍān, and that you give a fifth of the spoil"; and likewise his saying, "Faith is seventy-odd branches, the least of them removing a harmful thing from the road and the highest of them saying 'there is no god but God'" — narrated by al-Tirmidhī, with Muslim adding "and modesty is a branch of faith".

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** The word *dīn* has more than one meaning in Arabic, one of them being "the way"; in Qurʾānic usage it stands for the principles and injunctions common to all the prophets, from Adam to Muḥammad, while *sharīʿah*, *minhāj* and the later term *madhhab* cover the subsidiary injunctions that differed from age to age and community to community — as in "He has laid down for you of the religion what He enjoined upon Noah" (42:13). **[Maʿārif]** The real meaning of *islām* is to submit oneself to God and be obedient to His commands; on that meaning, those who believed in the prophets and messengers of their time and were obedient to the commands of God they brought were all entitled to be called Muslims, and their religion was Islam — it was in this sense that Noah said "I have been commanded to be among the Muslims" (10:72), that Abraham described himself and his community as Muslims (2:128), and that the disciples of Jesus said "bear witness that we are Muslims" (3:52). **[Maʿārif]** Sometimes the word is applied specifically to the religion and law brought finally by the last of the prophets, which abrogated all previous ways in religion and which shall hold until the Day of Resurrection — the sense in which the ḥadīth of Gabriel explains Islam. **[Maʿārif]** The word in this verse carries the likelihood of both meanings; on either reading the end result is the same, namely that after the appearance of the Prophet, the only religion deserving the name Islam is the one conforming to the Qurʾān and the Sunnah, and no previously abrogated religion is acceptable or is even properly called Islam at a later stage. **[Ibn Kathīr]** God states that there is no religion accepted with Him from anyone except Islam, which includes obeying all the messengers until Muḥammad, who closed their commission and thereby closed every path to God except through him; so after God sent Muḥammad, whoever meets God following a path other than his, it will not be accepted from him — as He said, "whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will never be accepted from him" (3:85). **[Study Quran]** The exclusivist interpretation is qualified by verses such as 2:62 and 5:69, and this verse has sometimes been understood more inclusively; *islām* is understood here both as the proper name of the religion revealed to Muḥammad and in its more universal sense as willing submission to God, in which sense the Qurʾān describes earlier prophets as *muslimūn* (2:128; 2:132; 3:52; 5:111).

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** In "and those who were given the Book did not differ except after the knowledge came to them, insolently among themselves" there is a transposition, the meaning being "they did not differ, insolently among themselves, except after the knowledge came to them", according to al-Akhfash; and *baghyan* is in the accusative either as the object denoting reason or as a circumstantial phrase referring to "those who". **[Ṭabarī]** *Al-baghy* is insolence: God tells His servants that they came to the falsehood they came to and said the unbelief they said on the basis of knowledge of the wrongness of what they were saying.

**Context.** **[Qurṭubī]** Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr said that those meant by this verse are the Christians, and that it is a rebuke to the Christians of Najrān; al-Rabīʿ b. Anas said those meant are the Jews; and the expression "those who were given the Book" covers both Jews and Christians, meaning that they differed about the prophethood of Muḥammad after the knowledge had come to them, namely the description of him and of his prophethood in their books. **[Qurṭubī]** It has also been said that it means: those who were given the Gospel did not differ about Jesus and did not split into factions concerning him except after the knowledge had come to them that God is one God and that Jesus is God's servant and His messenger. **[Ṭabarī]** He takes the same line: those who were given the Gospel — and that is "the Book" mentioned in this verse — differed about Jesus and invented lies against God in what they said concerning him, their differences multiplying among them and their word splitting until some of them made lawful the blood of others, "except after the knowledge had come to them, insolently among themselves", meaning except after they had known the truth in what they were differing about and had become certain that they were speaking falsehood in the enormous fabrication they uttered. **[Ibn Kathīr]** God states that those who were given the Scripture beforehand divided in the religion after God sent the messengers and revealed the books to them, having thus provided them with the proofs that should have prevented them from doing so; they differed over the truth out of envy, hatred and enmity for one another, and that hatred made some of them defy those whom they hated even when those they hated were in the right.

**Cross-references.** **[Study Quran]** It mentions 3:85 and 2:62, and the passages 3:3–4 and 3:113–15, and notes a near parallel in 98:4. **[Study Quran]** The reckoning is described as taking place both in this world and the next (13:41; 2:202; 3:199), and knowledge itself can be double-edged (2:26). **[Ibn Kathīr]** He cites 3:85 as the verse that seals the point. **[Ṭabarī]** He refers to his earlier discussion of *al-baghy* and of the parallel verse.

**Reflection.** The surah has spent its opening verses arguing with the Christians of Najrān about Jesus, and here it states the principle on which that argument rests: the religion God accepts is submission, and the earlier communities were not lost through ignorance but through insolence after knowledge had reached them **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Ṭabarī]**. That is the sharpest edge of the verse — the difference is located not in the evidence but in the will, in envy and rivalry between people who knew better **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Saʿdī]**. At the same time the word *islām* is left carrying both of its senses, so that Noah, Abraham and the disciples of Jesus are all described as submitting, and the claim being made is about what has been revealed now, not about who was saved then **[Maʿārif]**, **[Study Quran]**. The closing threat is measured to the crime: the God they rejected is swift in reckoning, and the knowledge they were given is exactly what will be reckoned **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Jalālayn]**.

## 3:20 — فَإِنْ حَآجُّوكَ فَقُلْ أَسْلَمْتُ وَجْهِىَ لِلَّهِ وَمَنِ ٱتَّبَعَنِ ۗ وَقُل لِّلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَٱلْأُمِّيِّۦنَ ءَأَسْلَمْتُمْ ۚ فَإِنْ أَسْلَمُوا۟ فَقَدِ ٱهْتَدَوْا۟ ۖ وَّإِن تَوَلَّوْا۟ فَإِنَّمَا عَلَيْكَ ٱلْبَلَٰغُ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ بَصِيرٌۢ بِٱلْعِبَادِ

*So if they argue with you, say: "I have submitted my face to God, and those who follow me." And say to those who were given the Book and to the unlettered: "Have you submitted?" If they submit, they are guided; but if they turn away, upon you is only the delivery. And God sees the servants.*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "So if they argue with you, O Muḥammad — the company of the Christians of Najrān, disputing with you about Jesus in falsehood — then say: I have yielded myself to God alone with my tongue, my heart and all my limbs." **[Ṭabarī]** He explains why the command specifies the *face*: because the face is the noblest organ of the son of Adam in his regard, and in it lies his beauty and his dignity, so that when his face humbles itself before a thing, whatever is beneath it in honour among the organs of his body has humbled itself too. **[Ṭabarī]** "And those who follow me" means: those who follow me have also submitted their faces to God with me, the *man* being conjoined to the *tāʾ* in "I have submitted", as reported from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr through Ibn Isḥāq: "if they argue with you with the falsehood they bring you — their saying 'we created', 'we did', 'we made', 'we commanded' — these are only fallacious semblances in which they know what is true, then say: I have submitted my face to God, and those who follow me." **[Ṭabarī]** "And say to those who were given the Book" — of the Jews and the Christians — "and to the unlettered", who have no book, of the idolaters of the Arabs: "Have you submitted?" — meaning: say to them, have you singled out the oneness of God and made worship and divinity sincere to the Lord of the worlds, to the exclusion of all the rivals and associates you join with Him in your worship and in your acknowledgement of their lordship, while you know that there is no lord other than Him and no god besides Him? "And if they submit" — if they yield to singling out God's oneness and making worship and divinity sincere to Him — "then they are guided", meaning they have found the path of truth and taken the clear road. **[Jalālayn]** "So if the disbelievers dispute with you concerning religion, say to them: 'I have surrendered my countenance to God' — that is, I have submitted to Him — 'I and whoever follows me'; *wajh*, 'countenance', is chosen here because of its noble character, for the other parts of the body will just as soon surrender once the countenance has; and say to those who have been given the Scripture, the Jews and the Christians, and to the uninstructed, the Arab idolaters: 'Have you submitted?' — that is, 'Submit!' If they have submitted, they have been guided from error; but if they turn their backs on Islam, your duty is only to deliver the message; and God sees His servants and so requites them for their deeds — this statement was revealed before the command to fight them had been revealed." **[Ibn Kathīr]** "I have submitted myself to God and those who follow me" means: say, "I have made my worship sincere for God alone, without partner, rival, offspring or companion," and "those who follow me" are those who followed my religion and embraced my creed, as in "say: this is my way; I invite to God with sure knowledge, I and whoever follows me" (12:108). **[Study Quran]** To submit one's face signifies a reorientation of one's being: the face represents what is most intimate, most direct in reflecting the states of the soul, highest in outward appearance and the most beautiful part of the human body.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** "If they argue with you" means if they contend against you with falsified sayings and sophistries; so entrust your affair to what you have been charged with of faith and delivery, and God's help is upon you. **[Qurṭubī]** "My face" (*wajhī*) means my self, as in the ḥadīth "my face has prostrated to the One who created it and formed it"; it is also said to mean one's aim or direction, as when one says "so-and-so went out in the direction of such-and-such" — and al-Qurṭubī prefers the first, since the face is the noblest of a person's organs and the most comprehensive of the senses. **[Qurṭubī]** "And those who follow me": *man* stands in the nominative, conjoined to the *tāʾ* in "I have submitted", so that the sense is "and those who follow me have submitted too"; the conjunction to a nominative pronoun without an emphatic separate pronoun is admissible here because of the separation between them. **[Qurṭubī]** Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr and Yaʿqūb keep the *yāʾ* of *ittabaʿanī* on the original form, while the others drop it in following the codex, in which it is written without a *yāʾ*. **[Qurṭubī]** "And if they turn away, upon you is only the delivery": *al-balāgh* is the verbal noun of "he reached", with the middle radical lightened, meaning that upon you is only to deliver; and it has been said that this was abrogated by the command to fight. **[Qurṭubī]** Ibn ʿAṭiyyah replied that this needs to be dated: on the apparent view that these verses were revealed concerning the delegation of Najrān, the meaning is simply that upon you is to deliver what has been sent down to you, including what it contains of fighting and other matters. **[Study Quran]** "Unlettered" is thought to refer to the idolatrous Arabs, who had no scripture, or to anyone without a divine book; see 62:2, where "the unlettered" is used of those among whom Muḥammad was sent, and 2:78, where the term appears in connection with the People of the Book. **[Ṭabarī]** He discusses the grammar of "if they submit, then they are guided" following an interrogation, showing that this is admissible in Arabic when the utterance, though cast as a question, is meant as a command — as in "will you then desist?" (5:91), or as in the disciples' question to Jesus (5:112), or a man saying to another "are you not going to leave us alone?", meaning "leave us alone". **[Ṭabarī]** He notes that this is why the apodosis is treated like the apodosis of a command in the reading of Ibn Masʿūd, "Say: shall I guide you to a trade that will save you from a painful punishment? Believe!" (61:10–11), where the imperative interprets it, whereas in the reading of the Muslim community the verse is a statement and the apodosis attaches to the question. **[Qurṭubī]** Al-Zajjāj said "have you submitted?" is a threat, which is apt because the sense is "have you submitted or not?"; and the use of the past tense in "they are guided" is an exaggeration affirming that guidance has actually been realised for them.

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** He identifies those who argue as the delegation of Christians from Najrān disputing about Jesus. **[Qurṭubī]** The view that the verse was revealed concerning the Najrān delegation is the basis on which Ibn ʿAṭiyyah explains "upon you is only the delivery" without recourse to abrogation. **[Maʿārif]** The surah began with the confirmation of divine oneness and the refutation of the Trinity; answered here are the argumentations in which the disbelievers and deniers among the People of the Book persist even after the truth of Islam has been proved conclusively.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ṭabarī]** He transmits, through Ibn Isḥāq from Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar b. al-Zubayr, the report that God then gathered the people of the two scriptures together and mentioned what they had introduced and innovated, of the Jews and the Christians, saying "those who disbelieve in the signs of God and slay the prophets without right" up to "say: O God, Master of the Kingdom, You give the kingdom to whom You will". **[Ṭabarī]** He also transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ibn Jurayj, that "the unlettered" are those who do not write.

**Rulings.** **[Ṭabarī]** "If they turn away, upon you is only the delivery" means: you are only a messenger charged with delivery — nothing else is upon you but conveying the message to those of My creatures to whom I have sent you, and discharging the obedience I have laid upon you. **[Qurṭubī]** He records the view that this was abrogated by the verse of fighting, and Ibn ʿAṭiyyah's response that abrogation cannot be asserted without knowing the chronology, and that on the reading that ties the passage to Najrān the meaning is delivery of everything that was sent down, including the command to fight. **[Jalālayn]** The statement was revealed before the command to fight them had been revealed.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** "And God sees the servants" means: God has knowledge of which of His servants will accept what I have sent you with and so obey you by submitting, and which of them will turn away from it, rejecting what I have sent you with, and so disobey you by refusing Islam. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Their reckoning is with God and their return and final destination is to Him; it is He who guides whom He wills and leaves to stray whom He wills, and He has perfect wisdom and the unequivocal proof in all of this — as He says, "He cannot be questioned for what He does, while they will be questioned" (21:23), because of His perfect wisdom and mercy. **[Saʿdī]** When God had made clear that the true religion with Him is Islam, and the People of the Book had confronted the Prophet with disputation and the proof had been established against them and they had stubbornly rejected it, God commanded him at that point to say and declare that he had submitted his face — his outward and his inward — to God, and that those who follow him have likewise joined him in that pure yielding; and to say to all people, of the People of the Book and of the unlettered ones, those who have no book among the Arabs and others, that if they submit they are upon the straight path, guidance and truth, and if they turn away their reckoning is with God, and he has nothing upon him but the delivery, which he has discharged, having established the proof against them.

**Cross-references.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Say: this is my way; I invite to God with sure knowledge, I and whoever follows me" (12:108); "Say: O mankind, I am the Messenger of God to you all" (7:158); "Blessed is He who sent down the Criterion upon His servant that he may be a warner to all the worlds" (25:1); "He cannot be questioned for what He does, while they will be questioned" (21:23). **[Study Quran]** For submitting the face, see 2:21 and 31:22; for seeking the Face of God, 2:272 and 13:22; "everything perishes save His Face" (28:88; cf. 55:26–27); "I have turned my face toward Him who created the heavens and the earth" (6:79); "set thy face toward the religion as a ḥanīf" (10:105). **[Study Quran]** The idea that the Prophet's duty is only to convey is also found in 88:21–22, "thou art but a reminder, thou art not a warder over them", and in 2:272, 13:40 and 29:18; and "sees" renders *baṣīr*, which connotes not only sight but knowledge and penetrating insight.

**Reflection.** The instruction is not to win the argument but to declare a position and then invite: "I have submitted my face to God, and those who follow me" — a statement of where the speaker stands, addressed past the disputants to everyone listening **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Ibn Kathīr]**. The choice of the *face* as the thing submitted is the whole of it: what is noblest and most exposed in a person is what is placed down first, and the rest follows **[Qurṭubī]**, **[Jalālayn]**, **[Study Quran]**. Then the Prophet is told to stop: after the proof is delivered, the refusing is theirs and the accounting is God's, which frees the caller from both despair and coercion **[Ṭabarī]**, **[Saʿdī]**. It is a verse about the limits of argument, written at the end of eighty verses of argument — and it ends by naming the One who sees the servants, which is where the matter is left **[Ibn Kathīr]**, **[Study Quran]**.
