## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:21

> And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment?[187]

**Commentary:**

### Marriage Is a Firm Covenant

The verse asks how a husband could take back the dowry after intimacy and after the wife has taken a firm commitment from him. The marriage covenant, *mīthāq ghalīẓ*, is not a casual arrangement that can be discarded whenever one party feels dissatisfied. It joins emotional intimacy to responsibility, trust, and accountability before Allah.

### Intimacy Creates Responsibility

The verse speaks with dignity about marital intimacy. Sexual relations within marriage are not shameful, but they deepen the obligations between spouses. A husband cannot enjoy a wife’s affection and companionship and then treat her financial rights as disposable when the relationship ends.

The dowry remains her property, and intimacy does not turn it into a refundable payment.

### The Dowry and the Woman’s Right

Verse 4:4 established the dowry as the woman’s right. This verse reinforces that right at the moment when a husband might be tempted to reclaim it. Divorce may be lawful, but lawful separation must still honour the rights created by the marriage.

Pressure, threats, emotional cruelty, or manufactured accusations cannot make an unjust seizure legitimate.

### Divorce with Dignity

The Qur’an permits separation when a marriage cannot continue, while prohibiting revenge and financial exploitation. A former couple may no longer share a home, but they remain bound by duties of fairness, confidentiality, and care for any children.

Ending a marriage should not erase the good that existed or justify exposing private matters to humiliate the other spouse.

### A Covenant before Allah

The “firm commitment” is ultimately a commitment made under Allah’s witness. A person may deceive relatives, conceal assets, or manipulate social expectations, but Allah knows whether the covenant was honoured. This awareness should restrain both spouses from betrayal.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:22

> Do not marry former wives of your fathers—except what was done previously. It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice.

**Commentary:**

### Ending a Pre-Islamic Practice

The verse prohibits a man from marrying a woman who was previously married to his father. In pre-Islamic society, a widow or divorced wife could be treated as part of a deceased man’s inherited household, and his son might claim the right to marry her. Islam ended this practice decisively.

Marriage is not an inheritance right that passes from one male relative to another.

### The Sanctity of Family Boundaries

The prohibition protects the dignity of the father, the son, and the woman. Certain relationships are made permanently unlawful because family roles require clear boundaries, trust, and protection from sexual confusion or coercion.

The son must honour the former wife of his father as a prohibited relative, not view her as an available marital replacement.

### “What Was Done Previously”

The exception refers to relationships that occurred before the prohibition was revealed. It is not a continuing permission after the ruling became known. Qur’anic legislation sometimes acknowledges past conduct while establishing a clear boundary for the future.

The wording also demonstrates Allah’s mercy: people are not held to a new ruling before it reaches them, but once the command is known, obedience is required.

### Shameful and Evil

Allah uses several strong descriptions to expose the moral ugliness of the practice. It violated natural family honour, exploited women, and blurred the limits that protect households. Social custom cannot make an exploitative relationship respectable.

The verse challenges every culture that treats women as possessions passed between men.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:23

> ˹Also˺ forbidden to you for marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother’s daughters, your sister’s daughters, your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters, your mothers-in-law, your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers—but if you have not, then you can marry them —nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together at the same time —except what was done previously. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Permanently Prohibited Relations

This verse lists women who are permanently prohibited for marriage, *maḥramāt*. The categories include close blood relatives: mothers, daughters, sisters, aunts, nieces, and similar relationships. These boundaries protect family structure, prevent exploitation, and preserve relationships of care from being converted into sexual competition.

The list is not a statement that these women are inferior. It establishes relationships in which marriage is permanently inappropriate.

### Foster Relationships

Islam gives recognised breastfeeding relationships a legal effect. A foster-mother and foster-sister can become prohibited for marriage under the conditions established by Islamic law. The Prophet ﷺ said that breastfeeding makes prohibited what lineage makes prohibited.

This protects a child’s nursing family and prevents people from treating foster relationships as irrelevant once childhood has passed. Proper records of breastfeeding are therefore important in communities where the ruling applies.

### Marriage by Affinity

Some prohibitions arise through marriage. A man’s mother-in-law becomes permanently prohibited once the marriage contract is valid. A son’s wife is prohibited to his father, and a father’s wife is prohibited to his son. These rules preserve family trust and prevent the household from becoming a field of rivalry.

A biological son’s wife is included; preserving lineage means that adopted relationships are not falsely treated as biological lineage, though other rules of modesty and guardianship still apply.

### Stepdaughters and Consummation

A stepdaughter under a man’s guardianship is prohibited if he has consummated marriage with her mother. If the marriage to the mother ended before consummation, the verse permits marriage to the daughter because the relevant affinity prohibition was not activated. This technical ruling must never be used to groom, pressure, or exploit a child.

The welfare, maturity, consent, and protection of the person remain essential, and guardianship is a trust rather than ownership.

### Two Sisters Together

A man may not be married to two sisters simultaneously. The prohibition protects sisterly bonds from being placed in a destructive rivalry. Marriage to one sister may become permissible after the first marriage has genuinely ended and the relevant waiting period has passed.

### Mercy in Legislation

The reference to past practice recognizes what occurred before the command was revealed, while the closing names of Allah—All-Forgiving, Most Merciful—show that law is accompanied by mercy. Mercy does not erase the boundary after it is known; it gives people a path to correct their lives.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:24

> Also ˹forbidden are˺ married women—except ˹female˺ captives in your possession.[189] This is Allah’s commandment to you. ˹But˺ lawful to you are all beyond these—as long as you seek them with your wealth in a legal marriage, not in fornication. Give those you have consummated marriage with their due dowries. It is permissible to be mutually gracious regarding the set dowry. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Commentary:**

### Respecting Existing Marriages

The verse begins by protecting marriage: a married woman is not available for another man to claim. A person may not interfere in an existing marriage or treat a woman as an object of acquisition. The exception mentioned belongs to the historical law of captives taken in a formally regulated conflict, not to ordinary marriage, migration, or private ownership.

### The Historical Captive Context

In the societies addressed by the Qur’an, war captivity and slavery already existed. The Qur’an regulated the institution, required lawful authority, protected captives, encouraged emancipation, and placed sexual relations within recognized legal boundaries rather than allowing unrestrained abuse. It did not authorize kidnapping, trafficking, private enslavement, or assault.

In the modern world, slavery is prohibited and no person may claim ownership of another. This verse cannot be used to justify sexual violence or the exploitation of prisoners. Captivity in a conflict does not give an individual permission to abuse anyone.

### Lawful Marriage, Not Fornication

The verse makes the governing rule explicit: lawful relationships require seeking a spouse with wealth through a legal marriage, not through *zinā*. Marriage creates rights, responsibilities, public recognition, dowry, maintenance, and protection of lineage. Desire alone cannot make an unlawful relationship lawful.

### The Dowry Is Due

Once a marriage is consummated, the wife is entitled to her due dowry. The payment is not a reward for sexual access; it is a marital right attached to the covenant. The husband must not delay, reduce, or reclaim it through pressure.

The phrase “mutually gracious” allows spouses to adjust a set dowry voluntarily after the right is known. It does not allow one party to coerce the other.

### Lineage and Social Protection

The rules surrounding marriage protect lineage, children, inheritance, and the emotional stability of families. Lawful marriage makes responsibilities identifiable. Exploiting ambiguity in order to avoid maintenance or conceal paternity contradicts the verse’s purpose.

### Allah’s Knowledge and Wisdom

Allah is All-Knowing of human needs, vulnerabilities, motives, and social consequences. He is All-Wise in setting boundaries that protect people from desire becoming oppression. Believers should approach sensitive rulings with knowledge, caution, and reverence rather than extracting a licence from a historical phrase.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:25

> But if any of you cannot afford to marry a free believing woman, then ˹let him marry˺ a believing bondwoman possessed by one of you. Allah knows best ˹the state of˺ your faith ˹and theirs˺. You are from one another.

> [190] So marry them with the permission of their owners,[191] giving them their dowry in fairness, if they are chaste, neither promiscuous nor having secret affairs. If they commit indecency after marriage, they receive half the punishment of free women.[192] This is for those of you who fear falling into sin. But if you are patient, it is better for you. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Human Dignity Is Not Measured by Status

The verse discusses marriage to believing bondwomen within the historical institution of slavery. It immediately reminds believers that Allah knows the reality of faith and that “you are from one another.” A woman’s enslaved status does not make her less human, less morally accountable, or unworthy of honour.

The verse therefore challenged class arrogance and required that she be treated as a believer and a person with rights.

### A Historical Legal Context

Slavery and captivity existed in the societies addressed by the Qur’an. The Qur’an regulated the institution, restricted routes into it, encouraged emancipation, required humane treatment, and opened legal paths to freedom. This verse is not permission for modern slavery, trafficking, kidnapping, or sexual exploitation.

In a world where slavery is prohibited, no one may claim ownership of another or use this verse to justify a relationship with a captive or vulnerable person.

### Marriage Requires Permission and Dowry

The bondwoman is to be married with the permission of those who legally held authority over her in that historical system, and she must receive a fair dowry. The requirement of marriage rejects the idea that sexual access is available without obligations. The woman has a defined right to dignity, financial recognition, chastity, and protection.

Consent and absence of coercion are essential to the moral purpose of marriage. Ownership could never make abuse righteous.

### Chastity and Public Rights

The verse describes a chaste woman as neither promiscuous nor engaged in secret affairs. This is a standard of conduct for marriage, not a licence to investigate, shame, or punish people through rumour. Accusations require evidence and lawful authority, as the Qur’an makes clear elsewhere.

### The Historical Punishment Clause

The stated distinction in punishment belonged to the legal framework of that historical society and could only be applied through legitimate judicial authority and strict evidence. It never permitted private violence. Contemporary legal systems prohibit slavery and regulate criminal punishment through public law, so no individual may attempt to revive an enslaved legal category or enforce penalties himself.

### Patience Is Better

Allah says that patience is better for those who fear falling into sin. This encourages restraint, chastity, lawful marriage, and work toward freedom rather than treating a vulnerable person as a solution to desire. Self-control protects both people from a relationship shaped by exploitation.

### Mercy and Equality before Allah

The verse closes with forgiveness and mercy. Faith and repentance remain open, while the underlying principle is that social status does not erase human kinship. The person who has power is tested more severely in how he uses it.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:26

> It is Allah’s Will to make things clear to you, guide you to the ˹noble˺ ways of those before you, and turn to you in mercy. For Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Commentary:**

### Divine Clarification

After explaining marriage, inheritance, orphan care, and sexual ethics, Allah states that He intends to make matters clear. Revelation brings moral clarity where custom, desire, and social power can create confusion. The believer does not have to invent justice from personal preference; Allah explains rights and limits.

Clarity includes principles, specific rulings, warnings, and the Prophetic explanation of how those rulings are lived.

### The Ways of Earlier Believers

Allah guides believers to the noble ways of those before them. The Qur’an teaches through the experiences of earlier prophets and communities: their faith, justice, repentance, patience, and the consequences of betrayal. History is not merely entertainment; it is moral instruction.

The phrase does not mean copying every practice of earlier communities. It means following the upright guidance that Allah approved and learning from what revelation records.

### Mercy in Law

Divine law protects people from exploitation and gives them paths back after mistakes. The repeated mention of Allah’s mercy shows that legal boundaries are not signs of divine harshness. They restrain harm, preserve dignity, and make repentance possible.

A believer should study rulings with humility rather than turning them into weapons against others.

### Allah’s Knowledge and Wisdom

Allah is All-Knowing of human nature, family relationships, economic vulnerability, desire, and social consequences. He is All-Wise in placing each ruling where it best protects faith and people.

Human beings may not immediately understand every detail, but they can trust that the law is not arbitrary or based on ignorance of their needs.

### Clarity Requires Learning

If Allah makes matters clear, believers have a duty to learn accurately. Simplistic slogans, cultural assumptions, and isolated quotations cannot replace sound scholarship. The Sunnah, language, context, and the complete Qur’anic legal framework all matter.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:27

> And it is Allah’s Will to turn to you in grace, but those who follow their desires wish to see you deviate entirely ˹from Allah’s Way˺.

**Commentary:**

### Allah Calls toward Repentance

Allah’s will for believers is mercy and return. He makes the lawful clear, warns against harm, and opens the door of *tawbah*. Even when people fail, Allah invites them to come back rather than remain trapped in shame or sin.

Divine mercy is active guidance: it teaches people how to correct their conduct and restore rights.

### The Pressure of Unrestrained Desire

Those who follow their desires want believers to deviate completely. “Desire” here refers to passions that reject Allah’s guidance, including sexual licence, greed, pride, tribalism, and the wish to control others. Desire itself is not always sinful; lawful affection, ambition, and enjoyment can be blessings when governed by revelation.

The danger is making desire the authority that decides right and wrong.

### Complete Deviation

The expression “deviate entirely” indicates a movement away from Allah’s path in belief, morals, and social practice. A person may begin by relaxing one boundary and eventually reject the idea that any boundary should govern him.

The Qur’an warns believers to recognize pressures that present moral surrender as freedom or exploitative custom as tradition.

### Distinguishing Mercy from Manipulation

Not every invitation to change is harmful. Believers should listen to sincere advice and correct misunderstandings. But they should resist pressure from people who benefit when faith becomes weak, family rights disappear, or sexual and financial exploitation is normalized.

Truth is measured by revelation and justice, not by the confidence of the speaker.

### A Prayer for Steadfastness

The believer should ask Allah to keep the heart firm, seek beneficial knowledge, and choose companions who encourage obedience. Mercy is not a reason to become careless; it is a reason to respond quickly when Allah opens a path of return.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:28

> And it is Allah’s Will to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak.

**Commentary:**

### Divine Ease

Allah intends to lighten human burdens. The laws explained in this passage protect people while recognizing that human beings have limits. Divine commands are not designed to crush humanity, but to guide weak and dependent creatures toward what is good.

Ease does not mean that every desire is permitted. It means that Allah’s law contains concessions, realistic responsibilities, and paths of repentance rather than demanding the impossible.

### Human Weakness

Human weakness includes physical limitation, emotional vulnerability, susceptibility to desire, forgetfulness, social pressure, and dependence upon food, rest, companionship, and resources. A sound religious life acknowledges these realities instead of pretending that people are angels.

Recognizing weakness should lead to humility and wise safeguards, not despair or deliberate surrender to sin.

### Concessions and Responsibility

The Qur’an allows lawful alternatives for those unable to marry freely, gives concessions in purification and prayer, and accepts repentance after wrongdoing. These concessions must be used honestly. A person cannot call deliberate injustice a weakness while refusing the means Allah provided for restraint and repair.

### Compassion in Family Law

The surrounding rulings protect orphans, women, spouses, and those with limited wealth. Family law must therefore be applied with mercy. Guardians should not demand what a vulnerable person cannot bear, and spouses should not exploit natural emotional or financial dependence.

### Designing a Life that Supports Obedience

Because people are weak, believers should build practical supports: lawful marriage, good companionship, modesty, financial planning, rest, honest advice, and prompt repentance. Avoiding temptation is often wiser than testing one’s strength unnecessarily.

### Hope after Failure

Human weakness explains why the Qur’an repeatedly opens the door to Allah’s forgiveness. A believer who falls should not excuse the sin, but should rise, repair harm, and return. Allah’s mercy is stronger than the servant’s weakness when the servant sincerely seeks Him.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:29

> O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but rather trade by mutual consent. And do not kill ˹each other or˺ yourselves. Surely Allah is ever Merciful to you.

**Commentary:**

### Wealth Must Be Acquired Lawfully

Allah prohibits consuming one another’s wealth through *bāṭil*: theft, fraud, bribery, gambling, usury, deception, coercion, breach of trust, and other unlawful means. The verb “devour” conveys greed that consumes another person’s property without regard for their dignity or rights.

A transaction can be profitable and still be sinful if it is built on deception or exploitation.

### Mutual Consent in Trade

The Qur’an permits trade when it is based on genuine mutual consent. Buyers and sellers should understand what is being exchanged, what it costs, and what responsibilities follow. Consent obtained through threats, hidden defects, false advertising, manipulation, or extreme exploitation is not the sound consent envisioned here.

Mutual agreement also does not make a prohibited product or contract lawful. Consent operates within Allah’s ethical boundaries.

### Economic Justice

The verse applies to wages, loans, contracts, inheritance, business partnerships, online commerce, and public funds. Honest records, clear terms, fair measurement, and timely payment protect consent. Employers must not exploit workers’ vulnerability, and customers must not deceive sellers or evade rightful payment.

Islamic commercial ethics joins private freedom with public justice.

### Do Not Kill One Another

The prohibition against killing includes murder, unlawful violence, oppression that foreseeably destroys life, and organised aggression. The phrase “or yourselves” also includes suicide and self-destruction. Human life belongs to Allah and is not a disposable possession.

This does not condemn someone suffering from mental illness or excuse society from providing compassionate care. It commands protection, treatment, and support for people in danger.

### Mercy as the Foundation

Allah’s mercy appears in the protection of property and life. Legal limits are not obstacles to human flourishing; they prevent the strong from consuming the weak. A believer shows gratitude for this mercy by becoming safe for other people’s wealth and bodies.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:30

> And whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire. That is easy for Allah.

**Commentary:**

### A Warning after the Prohibition

The previous verse protected wealth and life. This verse warns anyone who violates those protections sinfully and unjustly. The reference includes consuming property through unlawful means, murdering, and deliberately harming others. Social crimes are also sins before Allah, not merely violations of civil order.

### “Sinfully and Unjustly”

The wording highlights deliberate wrongdoing. A genuine accident, mistake, or act under coercion is not judged in the same way as intentional exploitation or violence. Allah knows circumstances that human courts may only partially see.

This distinction should encourage careful justice rather than careless accusations.

### The Fire and Human Rights

The threat of the Fire shows how seriously Islam treats people’s rights. Worship cannot compensate for deliberately stealing, murdering, or abusing others while refusing to repent. A person who wrongs creation must seek the forgiveness of the Creator and repair the harm done to creation.

### Easy for Allah

Nothing is difficult for Allah, including resurrection, judgement, and punishment. “Easy” does not mean insignificant. It reminds the wrongdoer that wealth, influence, escape, and legal manipulation cannot make justice impossible for Allah.

### Repentance and Restoration

The warning should lead to immediate repentance. The wrongdoer must stop the abuse, return property, compensate victims, submit to lawful accountability, and seek forgiveness. If the victim is unavailable, qualified guidance is needed to determine how to restore the right without causing further harm.

### Protecting Life and Property Today

Modern examples include corruption, financial scams, trafficking, domestic violence, murder, reckless endangerment, and institutional abuse. Communities must protect victims and establish fair systems of accountability rather than hiding crime to preserve reputation.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:31

> If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour.[193]

**Commentary:**

### Major and Lesser Sins

The Qur’an distinguishes between major sins, *kabāʾir*, and lesser misdeeds. Major sins include acts for which revelation threatens a specific punishment, curse, or severe warning, such as shirk, murder, adultery, consuming an orphan’s wealth, false accusation, and deliberate financial injustice.

The distinction does not make lesser sins harmless. Small sins can accumulate when a person treats them lightly or refuses to repent.

### Avoidance Is Active

To avoid major sins means more than rarely committing them. It includes staying away from their causes, repenting after a slip, returning people’s rights, and building safeguards against recurrence. A person should not deliberately approach a major sin while imagining that minor forgiveness will cover him.

### Allah’s Absolution

Allah promises to absolve lesser misdeeds for those who sincerely avoid major sins. This is a gift of mercy, not a right that makes Allah indebted to the servant. The believer combines effort with hope and never relies upon deeds alone.

Where a lesser sin involves another person’s right, restoration remains necessary.

### The Place of Honour

The place of honour is Paradise and the dignity Allah grants to the obedient. The verse redirects the believer from the false honour of wealth, status, and social approval to lasting acceptance before Allah.

### The Prophetic Explanation

The Prophet ﷺ mentioned seven destructive sins, including associating partners with Allah, magic, unjust killing, consuming an orphan’s wealth, consuming ribā, fleeing from battle, and falsely accusing chaste believers. The list illustrates severe examples; it does not mean that major sins are limited to exactly seven.

### A Balanced Moral Outlook

The verse prevents two extremes. A person must not trivialise grave sins, and he must not despair over lesser mistakes when he is sincerely striving. Regular repentance, prayer, good deeds, and avoidance of destructive behaviour purify the believer.

- Qur’an 4:29–31 on life, property, major sins, forgiveness, and honour.
- Qur’an 53:32 on avoiding major sins and indecencies while recognizing human weakness.
- Qur’an 42:37 and 25:68–71 on avoiding major sins and repenting after wrongdoing.
- Qur’an 11:114 on good deeds erasing misdeeds.
- Qur’an 39:53–54 on Allah’s mercy and the command to return to Him.
- Qur’an 3:133–136 on repentance, forgiveness, and the qualities of the people of Paradise.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith of the seven destructive sins and reports on prayer and good deeds erasing lesser sins.
- For *kabāʾir*, *sayyiʾāt*, *nukaffir*, and the place of honour, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:31.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:32

> And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Commentary:**

### Envy and Dissatisfaction

Allah warns believers not to crave what He has given some people over others. This is not a prohibition on ambition or on asking for lawful improvement. It is a warning against *ḥasad*: wishing that another person lose a blessing, resenting Allah’s distribution, or allowing comparison to poison the heart.

People differ in wealth, health, family, abilities, opportunities, and responsibilities. These differences are tests, not proof that one person is loved and another is worthless.

### Equal Accountability and Reward

Men are rewarded for their deeds and women for theirs. The verse affirms that Allah does not make a person’s reward depend on belonging to a particular sex. Each person is judged by faith, intention, effort, and action.

A woman’s good deeds are not secondary to a man’s, and a man cannot inherit spiritual merit from his family or social position.

### Ask Allah for Bounty

Instead of staring resentfully at another person’s provision, believers are told to ask Allah for His bounty. Supplication turns envy into hope and comparison into worship. It also teaches a person to seek blessing through lawful effort rather than unlawful appropriation.

Asking Allah’s bounty includes requesting knowledge, health, provision, righteous relationships, and the ability to use blessings well.

### Different Gifts, Shared Dignity

Allah may give people different gifts because their responsibilities and tests differ. Difference does not justify oppression, exclusion, or contempt. Communities must still ensure fair opportunity, fulfil rights, and prevent people from using alleged superiority to abuse others.

### Knowledge of Every Circumstance

Allah has perfect knowledge of all things. He knows what a person can bear, what hidden support he has, what responsibility accompanies a blessing, and what sacrifice lies behind another person’s apparent success. Human beings see only a small portion of the story.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:7, 4:11–12, and 4:32 on inheritance, lawful provision, gender, and reward for deeds.
- Qur’an 16:71 on differences in provision as a test and not a basis for domination.
- Qur’an 49:13 on honour through *taqwā*, not wealth, race, or status.
- Qur’an 3:195 and 33:35 on equal reward for believing men and women.
- Qur’an 113:5 on seeking refuge from the evil of envy.
- Qur’an 59:9 and 64:16 on generosity, self-restraint, and preferring others over the self.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports warning against envy and teaching believers to look at those with less worldly provision while striving in good deeds.
- For *tamannī*, *faḍl*, *kasb*, *ḥasad*, and equal moral accountability, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:32.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:33

> And We have appointed heirs to what has been left by parents and next of kin. As for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share.[194] Surely Allah is a Witness over all things.

**Commentary:**

### Inheritance Follows Real Relationships

Allah appoints heirs for what parents and close relatives leave behind. Inheritance is therefore connected to recognised family relationships and rights, not merely to affection, popularity, or the person who happens to control the estate. The detailed shares in verses 4:7, 4:11, 4:12, and 4:176 form a coherent legal system.

### Covenants and Pledged Relationships

The verse also mentions people with whom binding pledges were made. Classical scholars discussed the early arrangements of alliance, brotherhood, and contractual support and the share due under those agreements. The wider principle remains important: promises and lawful commitments create responsibilities that cannot be discarded when property becomes available.

Modern examples include enforceable contracts, lawful bequests, trusts, and obligations toward dependants.

### A Share Does Not Mean Unrestricted Alteration

Giving a pledged person his or her share must be understood alongside the fixed inheritance rules. A private agreement cannot simply cancel the shares Allah assigned to parents, children, spouses, or other lawful heirs. Where a contractual benefit is permitted, it must be documented and administered without harming the prescribed heirs.

### Honouring Promises

Wealth often tests the sincerity of a promise. People may honour a pledge while relationships are beneficial, then abandon it after death or when money is at stake. Allah commands fidelity because He is the Witness over every agreement, spoken or written.

### Documentation and Transparency

A responsible person should record debts, bequests, guardianship arrangements, and lawful obligations while alive. Clear documents prevent relatives from fighting and protect people who may lack the power to prove an agreement after the deceased has gone.

Witnesses, legal records, and independent advice are means of fulfilling the Qur’anic ethic.

### Allah Is the Witness

Allah witnesses every inheritance dispute, hidden promise, forged document, and act of honesty. Human courts may only see the evidence presented, but Allah knows the original intention and the full history of the pledge.

- Qur’an 4:7, 4:11–12, 4:33, and 4:176 on inheritance, heirs, and pledged relationships.
- Qur’an 2:180–182 on bequests, witnesses, and preventing harm to heirs.
- Qur’an 5:1 and 16:91 on fulfilling covenants and not breaking oaths after confirmation.
- Qur’an 8:75 on blood relatives having greater entitlement in Allah’s Book.
- Qur’an 2:282 on documenting financial obligations and using witnesses.
- Qur’an 4:58 and 23:8 on fulfilling trusts.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on prescribed inheritance shares, fulfilling covenants, and the rights created by lawful commitments.
- For *mawālī*, *ʿuqūd*, *aqdat aymānukum*, *naṣīb*, and Allah’s witnessing of agreements and estates, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:33.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:34

> Men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially. And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with.[195] And if you sense ill-conduct from your women, advise them ˹first˺, ˹if they persist,˺ do not share their beds, ˹but if they still persist,˺ then discipline them ˹gently˺.[196] But if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them. Surely Allah is Most High, All-Great.

**Commentary:**

### Caretaking Is Responsibility

The verse describes men as caretakers, *qawwāmūn*, because they are tasked with financial support and responsibility for the household. This is a duty of service, protection, provision, and accountability—not a declaration that every man is morally superior to every woman or that a husband owns his wife.

A man who claims authority while refusing maintenance, safety, consultation, and kindness has abandoned the responsibility that the verse places upon him.

### Financial Support and Leadership

The verse connects household responsibility with provision. The husband is required to spend according to his means, provide a safe home, and protect the family’s rights. Caretaking should be exercised through mercy, consultation, and justice rather than domination.

Leadership in Islam is answerability before Allah. The more authority a person has, the more seriously he is questioned about how he used it.

### Righteous Women and Trust

Righteous women are described as devoutly obedient and protective in the husband’s absence of what Allah entrusted to them. Their primary obedience is to Allah; marital cooperation is valid only within what is lawful and just. They protect the household’s privacy, property, dignity, and covenant.

This description does not erase a woman’s independent property, intellect, consent, or right to address wrongdoing.

### Responding to Marital Ill-Conduct

The verse refers to *nushūz*, serious ill-conduct or persistent marital rebellion that threatens the covenant—not every disagreement, preference, complaint, or assertion of a wife’s rights. The steps mentioned aim at de-escalation: sincere advice, temporary separation in the bed, and a final disciplinary measure under strict limits.

A husband may not use this verse to punish a wife for speaking, refusing abuse, seeking her lawful rights, or disagreeing with him.

### No Violence or Humiliation

The phrase “discipline them gently” cannot justify domestic violence. The Prophet ﷺ never struck a woman or servant, condemned harming women, and taught that the best believers are best to their families. Classical legal discussions placed strict limits on any disciplinary gesture: no injury, bruising, breaking, humiliation, striking the face, or continued violence. Many contemporary scholars emphasize non-violent or symbolic readings and the prophetic model as decisive guidance.

In any case, private violence, coercive control, and abuse are sinful and unlawful. A person facing danger should seek protection and lawful intervention, not submit to a claim of religious permission.

### If They Change Their Ways

If the conflict ends, the husband is forbidden from acting unjustly or searching for ways to continue punishment. The verse closes the door to revenge. If reconciliation cannot be achieved, verse 4:35 directs the couple toward mediation; separation may be safer and more honest than ongoing harm.

### Mutual Accountability

This verse addresses the husband’s response to a wife’s *nushūz*. The Qur’an also addresses a husband’s indifference or neglect in 4:128 and provides reconciliation and separation mechanisms. Marital ethics are not a one-way system of male privilege.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:19, 4:34–35, and 4:128–130 on kindness, caretaking, marital conflict, reconciliation, and separation.
- Qur’an 2:228 and 30:21 on reciprocal rights, affection, mercy, and tranquillity.
- Qur’an 9:71 on believing men and women as mutual protectors and allies.
- Qur’an 2:231 and 65:6 on not harming women during marriage or separation.
- Qur’an 4:1 and 4:29 on dignity, consent, and protection from abuse.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2328, the report that the Prophet ﷺ did not strike a woman or servant; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports commanding kindness to wives and condemning harm.
- For *qiwāmah*, *qānitāt*, *ḥāfiẓāt*, *nushūz*, *waʿẓ*, *hajr*, and the strict limits and prophetic model of non-violence, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:34.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:35

> If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers. If they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware.

**Commentary:**

### Mediation before Breakdown

When serious conflict threatens to split a marriage, the Qur’an recommends appointing a mediator from each family. These representatives should be trustworthy, fair-minded, discreet, and concerned with justice rather than family pride. Their role is to understand the conflict and seek a safe resolution.

Mediation is not a licence for relatives to pressure a spouse into remaining in an abusive home.

### Two Mediators and Balanced Understanding

A mediator from each side can prevent one family from controlling the narrative. Each should listen carefully, protect confidential information, and identify practical causes of conflict: financial neglect, communication failure, interference by relatives, betrayal, violence, or incompatible expectations.

Professional counsellors, safeguarding officers, and legal advisers may also assist where family mediators lack the necessary expertise.

### Reconciliation Must Be Genuine

The verse says that if the couple desires reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony. Reconciliation requires willingness from both spouses, honest acknowledgement of harm, changed behaviour, and enforceable boundaries. It cannot be reduced to telling a victim to endure repeated abuse for the sake of appearances.

Forgiveness without safety and reform can simply return a person to danger.

### Allah Knows the Hidden Conflict

Allah is All-Knowing and All-Aware of the private history of the marriage. Mediators may be deceived by charming speech or family pressure, but Allah knows the real harm, the sincere effort, and the hidden intention.

This knowledge calls mediators to be impartial and spouses to speak truthfully.

### Separation May Be the Responsible Outcome

If genuine reconciliation is impossible, a just separation may be better than endless conflict. The Qur’an does not treat the continuation of every marriage as more important than safety, dignity, and justice. The aim of mediation is truth and welfare, not preserving a household’s public image at any cost.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:34–35 and 4:128–130 on marital conflict, mediation, settlement, and separation.
- Qur’an 49:9–10 on reconciling disputing parties with justice.
- Qur’an 2:229–232 on divorce, release with kindness, and preventing harm.
- Qur’an 30:21 on affection and mercy as the aims of marriage.
- Qur’an 65:1–7 on lawful separation, waiting periods, housing, and maintenance.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 16:90 on justice even where family loyalties are involved.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on reconciliation, arbitration, kindness between spouses, and preventing harm.
- For *ḥakam*, *shiqāq*, *iṣlāḥ*, *ṭāʿah*, and family mediation governed by justice and safety, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:35.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:36

> Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him. And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, ˹needy˺ travellers, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession.
>
> Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful—

**Commentary:**

### Tawḥīd and Social Ethics

The verse begins with worshipping Allah alone and immediately connects *tawḥīd* to kindness toward people. Worship is not complete when it leaves a person arrogant, cruel, or indifferent to those around him. The rights of creation are part of the practical expression of the rights of the Creator.

### Parents and Relatives

Kindness to parents includes respectful speech, service, patience, and support when they are elderly or vulnerable. It does not require obeying a parent in sin or accepting abuse. Relatives also have rights of contact, assistance, and fair treatment, while family loyalty must remain within justice.

### Orphans and the Poor

Orphans and the poor may lack the power to demand what they need. Kindness therefore includes material support, protection from exploitation, respectful speech, education, and advocacy. A gift that humiliates the recipient is not the excellence Allah calls for.

### Near and Distant Neighbours

The Qur’an includes both the near and distant neighbour. This can encompass a neighbour related by faith or family and one who is not, as well as people who live nearby but come from a different culture or religion. Neighbourly kindness includes safety, privacy, assistance, and not causing nuisance or harm.

A person’s worship is contradicted when his neighbour fears his speech, violence, or property.

### Companions and Travellers

The close companion may include a spouse, friend, colleague, classmate, or travelling companion. The needy traveller, *ibn al-sabīl*, may have resources at home but be stranded away from them. Kindness responds to present need rather than judging a person by assumptions.

### Historical Bondpeople

The verse includes those in one’s possession within the historical institution of bondage. Their inclusion among people owed kindness challenged the treatment of bondpeople as disposable objects. Islamic teachings required humane treatment, feeding and clothing them, and encouraged emancipation. Today, slavery and ownership of persons are unlawful; the principle applies to domestic workers, employees, prisoners, migrants, and anyone under another’s authority.

### Kindness as Worship

Kindness, *iḥsān*, includes small daily acts: a greeting, a fair wage, a meal, a ride, patient listening, protecting someone’s reputation, and intervening against abuse. Allah places these ordinary relationships immediately after tawḥīd because faith must be visible in them.

- Qur’an 4:36–38 on worship, kindness, arrogance, miserliness, and showing off.
- Qur’an 17:23–26 on parents, relatives, the poor, and balanced spending.
- Qur’an 2:177 and 90:12–16 on righteousness, freeing people, feeding the needy, and caring for orphans.
- Qur’an 24:27–29 on neighbourly privacy and permission.
- Qur’an 107:1–7 on neglecting orphans and the poor.
- Qur’an 49:13 and 5:2 on human dignity and cooperation in righteousness.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on kindness to parents, neighbours, travellers, orphans, and bondpeople.
- For *tawḥīd*, *iḥsān*, *jār*, *ibn al-sabīl*, *qarābah*, and kindness to all under one’s responsibility, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:36.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:37

> those who are stingy, promote stinginess among people, and withhold Allah’s bounties. We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Arrogance and Boasting

The verse continues the warning against those who refuse social kindness. Arrogance, *kibr*, makes a person see himself as too important to serve others, while boasting turns blessings into a reason to look down on people. Both attitudes corrupt relationships and prevent gratitude.

The next description shows one way arrogance becomes public harm: the arrogant person hoards what could benefit others.

### Stinginess Is More Than Saving

Stinginess, *bukhl*, is not responsible budgeting or providing for one’s dependants. It is refusing obligations, withholding known rights, and treating Allah’s gifts as though they exist only for the self. It can involve wealth, knowledge, time, influence, opportunities, or emotional support.

A person may have little and still be generous, while a wealthy person may be stingy if he refuses what Allah requires.

### Promoting Stinginess

The verse also condemns those who promote stinginess among people. They may mock charity, discourage fair wages, normalise corruption, or tell others that compassion is foolish. Such speech creates a culture in which everyone protects private advantage while vulnerable people are abandoned.

Leaders and influencers have a special responsibility not to glorify greed.

### Withholding Allah’s Bounties

Allah’s bounties are meant to be used with gratitude. Withholding them includes refusing obligatory charity, concealing beneficial knowledge, obstructing opportunities, and denying that a blessing should serve the common good. The person does not own provision independently; he is a steward of what Allah provided.

### Humiliating Punishment

The punishment is humiliating because the stingy person sought honour through possession and superiority. In the Hereafter, the wealth and status he used to elevate himself cannot protect him. The verse’s mention of disbelievers warns of a settled rejection of Allah’s authority, while individual judgements remain with Allah.

### The Cure: Gratitude and Generosity

Gratitude, *shukr*, is shown by acknowledging the Giver and using gifts in ways He approves. Generosity does not require recklessness. It means fulfilling rights, sharing voluntarily, and refusing to let fear of poverty turn into injustice.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:38

> Likewise for those who spend their wealth to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. And whoever takes Satan as an associate —what an evil associate they have!

**Commentary:**

### Spending for Appearance

The verse warns against spending wealth to show off, *riyāʾ*, rather than seeking Allah’s pleasure. The outward action may look generous, but the inner aim is reputation, influence, admiration, or control. Such spending can still produce temporary benefit for people, yet it loses the reward of sincere worship when the motive is deliberately directed to display.

### Charity and Intention

Islam encourages public charity when it serves a genuine need, inspires good, or provides accountability. The problem is not that people see a good deed; it is that the giver seeks their praise or uses the gift to humiliate and control recipients. Secret charity is often safer for sincerity, but public service is not automatically insincere.

A person should renew his intention before, during, and after giving.

### Disbelief and Moral Direction

The verse connects this behaviour with refusing belief in Allah and the Last Day. Without accountability before Allah, wealth may become a tool for status rather than a trust. Belief in the Hereafter reminds the giver that the real reward is not the audience’s applause.

The verse should not be used to declare the faith of every person whose charity contains a human motive. Allah knows the heart and calls people to sincerity.

### Satan as an Associate

Satan becomes an associate when a person follows his invitations: pride, deception, waste, envy, and using blessings for self-glorification. The association is not worship in the ordinary sense; it is obedience to Satan instead of Allah.

Such companionship is evil because it turns a potentially righteous deed into a means of spiritual loss and social harm.

### Giving with Dignity

True charity protects the dignity of the recipient. It does not publicize private hardship without consent, exaggerate the giver’s role, or make the poor feel indebted to a powerful benefactor. The aim is service, not personal branding.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:39

> What harm could have come to them if they had believed in Allah and the Last Day and donated from what Allah has provided for them? And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of them.

**Commentary:**

### A Better Alternative

Allah asks what harm would have come to those people if they had believed and spent from what He provided. The question exposes the emptiness of their resistance. Faith would not deprive them of true good, and generosity would not destroy the wealth Allah had placed in their hands.

Their refusal came from pride, fear, or attachment—not from a genuine loss that generosity would cause.

### Wealth Is Provided by Allah

The phrase “what Allah has provided” corrects the illusion of absolute ownership. Human beings earn through effort, but the body, intelligence, opportunity, resources, and circumstances that make earning possible are gifts from Allah. Recognizing the Provider makes spending an act of gratitude rather than a humiliating loss.

### Belief and Giving

Belief in Allah and the Last Day transforms economic behaviour. The believer knows that wealth is a trust and that every transaction will be judged. Giving becomes an investment in the Hereafter, while withholding another person’s right becomes a serious danger.

### Showing Off versus Sincere Charity

The previous verse condemned spending for display. This verse points to the right alternative: faith and giving from Allah’s provision. Sincere charity is not a performance for an audience; it is an act of worship and care for creation.

A person may give publicly when wisdom requires it, but his heart should seek Allah’s pleasure and the recipient’s welfare.

### Allah Knows Them

Allah has perfect knowledge of their motives, resources, opportunities, and excuses. He knows whether a person could have given, whether he was genuinely unable, and whether a gift was made from sincerity or vanity. Human beings may judge the amount; Allah judges the complete reality.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:40

> Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight.[197] And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace.

**Commentary:**

### Absolute Divine Justice

Allah never wrongs anyone, even by the weight of the smallest particle. No good deed is overlooked, no innocent person is punished for another’s crime, and no victim’s suffering disappears from the record. Divine justice is more exact than any human court can be.

The verse follows warnings about wealth and showing off, reminding both the wealthy and the deprived that Allah knows the smallest hidden reality.

### The Smallest Deed Matters

The reference to an atom’s weight teaches that a deed does not need to be publicly impressive to matter. A truthful word, a restrained anger, a quiet donation, a rescued animal, or a private prayer can be preserved by Allah even when people never notice it.

Likewise, a seemingly small act of deceit or cruelty should not be treated as irrelevant when it harms another person.

### Multiplication Is Grace

Allah multiplies good deeds many times over. This multiplication is not a wage demanded from Allah; it is *faḍl*, gracious generosity. The servant offers a limited act, and Allah gives a reward far beyond the act’s worldly appearance.

Sincerity, sacrifice, timing, need, and benefit can all affect the reward in ways only Allah fully knows.

### Hope for the Sincere

The verse gives hope to people whose service seems small or unrecognized. Allah’s scale is not controlled by publicity, wealth, or status. A deed performed for Allah may become great because of its sincerity, while a celebrated deed may be empty if it was performed only for praise.

### Justice and Generosity Together

Allah’s justice means that wrongdoing is not ignored; His generosity means that good is rewarded beyond strict equivalence. Believers should fear harming people and remain hopeful when doing good. This balance produces careful, compassionate action.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:29–40 on life, wealth, charity, showing off, justice, and Allah’s grace.
- Qur’an 18:49 and 99:7–8 on the record of every deed, however small.
- Qur’an 6:160 on the multiplication of good deeds and the exact recompense of evil.
- Qur’an 2:261 and 57:18 on Allah multiplying charity and rewarding sincere giving.
- Qur’an 10:44 and 18:49 on Allah never wronging people.
- Qur’an 2:286 and 23:62 on Allah’s justice and not burdening a soul beyond its capacity.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports that a good deed is multiplied from tenfold upward and that even a small act of charity is valued by Allah.
- For *miṯqāl*, *dharrah*, *ḥasanah*, *yuḍāʿif*, *faḍl*, and divine justice and generosity, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:40.
