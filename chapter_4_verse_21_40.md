## Verse 21

Verse 21 says: *And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment?*

In simple terms, the verse closes the dowry discussion with an even stronger argument than the previous verse: how could a man take back the dowry after he and his wife have been intimate with each other, and after she has taken from him a firm, solemn commitment? The intimacy and the covenant make the return of the gift morally impossible.

The verse continues the sequence that began at 4:19. There, the husband was forbidden to pressure his wife into returning part of her dowry. In 4:20, the Qur'an said that even if the dowry was a stack of gold, not the least bit may be taken back when a man replaces one wife with another. Now 4:21 asks the question that seals the argument: after you have been privily united with her, what claim have you to a refund? Al-Jalalayn states the logic plainly: "How shall you take it — that is, by what right — when each of you has been privily with the other through sexual intercourse, which validates the dowry, and they have taken from you a solemn covenant, a binding pledge; and that is what God commanded, namely that they should be retained honourably or set free virtuously." The intimacy is itself the validation of the dowry; a gift given for a union that has taken place cannot be reclaimed when the union ends.

The second argument is the covenant. The verse calls it *mīthāqan ghalīẓan* — in the translation, "a firm commitment"; classical renderings say a "solemn covenant," a "binding pledge," or a "strong compact." This is the same expression the Qur'an uses for the covenant of marriage elsewhere, and commentators note that the marriage contract itself is described in the Qur'an as a solemn covenant. The marriage vow is not a private arrangement between two people; it is a pledge made before Allah, and it is the foundation of the rights — including the financial rights — that arise from marriage. Al-Muntakhab captures the same point from the other side: "How could you reclaim it when you have already entered into matrimony and communion with one another, and your vows and solemn promise of allegiance and good will have been pledged to them before Allah." The covenant, like the intimacy, belongs to the past; the union and the promise have both happened, and the husband's claim to a refund is dissolved by each of them separately and by both together.

Ibn Kathir's presentation of the verse comes within his composite discussion of 4:19–23, and it frames the whole passage as one continuous brief: the wife is not to be inherited, not to be pressured into returning her dowry, not to be treated harshly; she is to be lived with honourably; and if a man wishes to replace her, the former wife's qintar remains hers. The verse then adds the Quranic reason: "And how could you take it back while you have gone in unto each other, and they have taken from you a firm and strong covenant?" The rhetorical question is an interrogative of rebuke and disavowal, and it is designed to make the reader see the absurdity of taking back what was freely given for what has already been freely received.

There is a legal dimension that the classical jurists built on this verse. The dowry becomes the wife's property at the contract, and its full settlement is confirmed at consummation; the husband's obligation is not conditional on the durability of the marriage. Thus a man cannot say, "She left me, so I want my money back," or "I divorced her, so the gift is dissolved." The verse's logic also protects the wife from a subtle form of abuse: the man who marries with the intention of reclaiming the dowry later is not merely breaking a financial promise, he is breaking the covenant before Allah. Al-Jalalayn's closing line ties the whole matter to divine command: the covenant is what God commanded — that wives should be retained honourably or released virtuously.

For the modern reader, the verse has implications beyond the literal case. It teaches that marriage is a covenant with rights that survive the relationship's end, and that a gift given out of love cannot be treated as a loan that is recoverable when the relationship cools. It also teaches the Quranic method of moral persuasion: instead of simply restating the prohibition, the verse makes the prohibition self-evident by appealing to what actually happened between the two people. Anyone who has known the intimacy of marriage and made a solemn promise inside it will instantly understand the question: how could you? The answer is that he cannot, because the union and the promise both stand in the way.

The verse also points to the difference between the Quranic view of marriage and a transactional view. In a transactional framework, the dowry is a price and marriage is a purchase; when the purchase is reversed, the price returns. The Qur'an dismantles that framework: the dowry is a gift with right attached, marriage is a covenant, and the woman is a partner in the covenant, not a commodity in a transaction. That is why the verse's two arguments — the intimacy and the covenant — together render the idea of reclaiming the gift not merely unlawful but unthinkable.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 22

Verse 22 says: *Do not marry former wives of your fathers—except what was done previously. It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice.*

In simple terms, the verse forbids a man from marring a woman whom his father had married — whether the father is deceased or has divorced her. Marriages contracted in pre-Islamic times before the revelation are forgiven, but the practice itself is condemned as shameful, despicable, and an evil path.

This is the first in the sequence of marriage prohibitions, and it introduces a problem the pre-Islamic Arabs had normalised: a son could inherit his father's widow — or even a woman his father had divorced — and treat her as part of the estate. The prohibition is therefore a direct extension of 4:19's abolition of inheriting women against their will. Ibn Kathir's discussion of 4:19–22 treats them as one coherent unit, and his narration from Ibn 'Abbas (Al-Bukhari) describes the old practice: when a man died, his male relatives had the right to do whatever they wanted with his wife — marry her, give her in marriage, or prevent her from marriage, because they claimed more right to her than her own family. The verse replies: that right does not exist, and the specific case of the father's wife is the most stark example.

Al-Jalalayn gives the grammatical and theological details. The particle *mā* in *mā nakaḥa ābā'ukum* means "man" — that is, the women whom your fathers married — and the prohibition applies whether the father is deceased or divorced. The exception, "except what was done previously," is the Quranic pardoning of pre-Islamic conduct: "unless it be a thing of the past, which is forgiven you." Al-Muntakhab is explicit: "Do not marry the women whom your fathers (deceased or divorced) have married before; Allah shall drop into oblivion what happened in the past."

The verse then names the practice with three escalating terms. *Fāḥishah* — immorality, obscenity. *Maqt* — severe hatred; Al-Jalalayn notes *maqt* literally means "hate from God," and the point is that the practice is not merely disliked but detested. *Sā' sabīl* — an evil way, an evil path. Al-Muntakhab renders the three: "an immoral act, a degrading vice and an evil line of conduct." The tripling is meaningful: the practice is wrong in itself (immorality), wrong in its standing before Allah (hatred), and wrong in its consequences for the family and the community (a path that leads to further corruption). The prohibition is not presented as a mere social taboo or a cultural preference; it is a moral verdict issued by the Lawgiver.

The "except what was done previously" clause is important for understanding how the Qur'an treats the transition from Jahiliyyah to Islam. The verse does not say that the people who contracted such marriages in the past were punished retroactively, nor does it demand that existing unions be dissolved. It draws a line at the revelation: "bygone shall be bygone; the new era shall begin" (Al-Muntakhab). The new age of Islam starts with a clean slate before the law, while the law itself now stands for the future. This is a general Quranic principle — the harshness of the past is forgiven, and the obligation begins with the prohibition.

For the scholar, the juristic question of what becomes of a marriage contracted before the prohibition has an established answer: such marriages were left intact because the Qur'an explicitly pardons the past, while the parties were instructed not to repeat the practice. The jurists extend the same logic to any marriage contracted in ignorance of the law: the past is forgiven, the future is governed. Ibn Kathir notes the whole passage — 4:19 through 4:23 — is one continuous statement of the laws of marriage, and the reader should notice the order: first the woman's dignity and property (19–21), then the father's wife (22), then the full list of permanent prohibitions by blood, suckling, and marriage (23). The order suggests the Qur'an's priority: before the long legal list comes the identification and abolition of the practice that treated women as inheritable property.

For the modern reader, the verse speaks both to a historical practice and to a permanent moral principle. The historical practice — a son taking his father's wife — is condemned outright, and the condemnation is built on wider principles: the family relationship creates boundaries that cannot be crossed; a woman is not a possession to be passed between men of the same family; and a marriage creates a network of relations — including the father's wife — that the law must respect. The verse is also a model of how divine law changes communities: the old can be forgiven, the new must be obeyed, and the line between the two is drawn by revelation, not by social convenience.

## Verse 23

Verse 23 says: *˹Also˺ forbidden to you for marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother’s daughters, your sister’s daughters, your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters, your mothers-in-law, your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers—but if you have not, then you can marry them —nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together at the same time —except what was done previously. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.*

In simple terms, the verse lists the women a man may never marry: his blood relatives (mothers, daughters, sisters, aunts, nieces), his relatives by suckling (foster-mothers and foster-sisters, who rank equal to blood), his relatives by marriage (mothers-in-law, stepdaughters after consummation with their mothers, and wives of his own sons), and the prohibition on marrying two sisters at the same time. Marriages made pre-Islam are forgiven.

This is the foundational verse of the Quranic law of prohibited degrees, and the classical scholars treated it as establishing three distinct categories: prohibition by blood (*nasab*), prohibition by suckling (*raḍā'*), and prohibition by marriage (*muṣāharah*). Ibn Kathir reports from Ibn 'Abbas (At-Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim): "I have prohibited for you seven types of relatives by blood and seven by marriage," and Ibn 'Abbas then recited the Ayah. The "seven by blood" are the mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal aunts, maternal aunts, brothers' daughters, and sisters' daughters. The "seven by marriage" are the wife's mother, the stepdaughter (after consummation), the son's wife, and the four categories of foster relationship that follow.

The verse itself opens the list and the commentators broaden each term according to the rule of the Qur'an's own language. Al-Jalalayn explains: mothers "includes the paternal and maternal grandmothers"; daughters "including their children"; sisters from fathers and mothers; paternal aunts are the sisters of your fathers and grandfathers; maternal aunts the sisters of your mothers and grandmothers; brothers' and sisters' daughters "including the children of these daughters." The principle is that the prohibited degree extends in every generation along the line of descent and ancestry, and the Prophet's early instruction to his community — "Be careful of what is forbidden for you by genealogy" — is reflected in the same principle. The full family tree, not only the immediate family, is protected.

The category of suckling is the one that surprised the early Muslims most, and the classical sources give it extensive treatment. 'A'ishah reports the Prophet's hadith in Al-Bukhari and Muslim: "Suckling prohibits what birth prohibits," and, in Muslim's wording, "Suckling establishes prohibited degrees just as blood does." The consequences are identical: a foster-mother and her daughters are as prohibited as a birth mother and her sisters. The jurists detail the quantity and timing: Al-Jalalayn specifies "five instances of suckling within the first two years, as pointed out in a hadith," and Ibn Kathir reports Muslim's narration from 'A'ishah that "Among the parts of the Qur'an that were revealed is the statement, 'Ten incidents of suckling establishes the prohibition,'" which was later abrogated with five, and the Prophet died while the five-version was still recited in that form. The two-year limit is drawn from 2:233, where the Qur'an fixes the full term of suckling at two years. The case of Sahlah bint Suhayl, who was ordered to suckle Salim — the freed slave of Abu Hudhayfah — with five instances, shows the practical application.

The marriage categories are equally precise. The wife's mother is prohibited from the moment of the contract, whether or not the marriage was consummated — Al-Jalalayn states this, and Ibn Kathir reports the consensus (Al-Hasan bin Muhammad, Tawus, Ibrahim, Az-Zuhri, Makhul) that the prohibition of the mother-in-law and the son's wife applies regardless of consummation. The stepdaughter, by contrast, is prohibited only if the stepfather consummated the marriage with her mother; if he divorced the mother before consummation, he may marry the daughter. Al-Jalalayn notes the phrase "under your guardianship" (*allātī fī ḥujūrikum*) is an adjectival qualifier reiterating the obvious — because, per the majority and the Umm Habibah hadith, the stepdaughter is prohibited whether or not she was actually brought up in his house. "Wives of your own sons" is restricted to "those from your loins" — biological sons — and the adopted son is expressly excluded, since adoption was a Jahiliyyah practice the Qur'an abolished; the Prophetic marriage to Zaynab after Zayd's divorce (33:37), and the revelation of 33:4 and 33:40, settled the point.

The final prohibition is combining two sisters in marriage at the same time — and the Sunna extends the same rule to a woman and her paternal or maternal aunt. Al-Jalalayn notes it is permissible to marry each separately, or to own two sisters as handmaidens together, but only to have intercourse with one of them at a time. The verse closes, as it opens, with mercy: "except what was done previously" — pre-Islamic unions are forgiven — and "Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."

For the modern reader, the verse is a complete map of the family's boundaries, and its structure teaches the philosophy behind them: blood creates identity, so blood boundaries protect the family's moral core; suckling creates the same bonds as birth, so the nursing relationship is treated as fully family; marriage creates new relations, so a spouse's mother and daughter belong to the same circle of protection. The exceptions, meanwhile, show a law of precise mercy: what was done in ignorance is forgiven, and the prohibition itself is a shield for the family, not a burden upon it.

## Verse 24

Verse 24 says: *Also ˹forbidden are˺ married women—except ˹female˺ captives in your possession. This is Allah’s commandment to you. ˹But˺ lawful to you are all beyond these—as long as you seek them with your wealth in a legal marriage, not in fornication. Give those you have consummated marriage with their due dowries. It is permissible to be mutually gracious regarding the set dowry. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the list of forbidden women by adding married women: a woman who is already married may not be married by another man. The one exception is the female captive taken in war, who becomes lawful to her captor after the issue of pregnancy is cleared. Beyond these prohibitions, all women are lawful — but only through marriage with a dowry, not through fornication. The dowry is due to the wife, and the parties may be generous with each other after it is fixed.

The first clause prohibits marriage to a woman who already has a husband, and it applies whether she is Muslim, free, or of another religion — as Al-Jalalayn notes, the prohibition holds until she has left her spouse. This is the protection of the marriage bond from outside: no man may intrude on another's marriage, and the Qur'an therefore places the married woman in the same category as the blood relatives — permanently forbidden to other men. The exception for captured women is the only opening, and it is tied to the realities of war in the period of revelation. Ibn Kathir records the occasion from Abu Sa'id Al-Khudri (Imam Ahmad; also At-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i, Ibn Jarir, Muslim): "We captured some women from the area of Awtas who were already married, and we disliked having sexual relations with them because they already had husbands. So we asked the Prophet about this matter, and this Ayah was revealed. Consequently, we had sexual relations with these women." The revelation settled the question: the marriage they had in the enemy camp was dissolved by capture, and after the required waiting period — one menstrual cycle, per Al-Jalalayn, to clear the possibility of pregnancy — the captive becomes lawful.

The verse then says "this is Allah's commandment to you" — *kitāba Allāhi ʿalaykum* — and Ibn Kathir draws the practical conclusion: "Therefore, adhere to Allah's Book, do not transgress His set limits, and adhere to His legislation and decrees." The whole passage of 4:23–24 is a list of limits, and the verse describes itself in exactly those terms: it is a *kitāb* (written prescription) laid upon the believers. The reader is being told that these are not social conventions subject to negotiation but divine boundaries.

The second half of the verse opens the positive side: "lawful to you are all beyond these." Ibn Kathir notes 'Ata' and others stated this: every woman not named in the prohibitions is lawful in marriage. But the permission is framed with conditions: *an tabtaghū bi-amwālikum muḥṣinīn ghayra musāfiḥīn* — "as long as you seek them with your wealth in a legal marriage, not in fornication." The wealth is the dowry; *muḥṣinīn* means desiring chastity, entering lawful marriage; *ghayra musāfiḥīn* means not fornicating. The Qur'an closes off the only alternative a man might imagine: the women who are lawful are lawful through marriage, not through "relationships." The dichotomy is total, and it is the doctrinal rejection of the pre-Islamic and later practices of concubinage-without-contract and prostitution.

The verse then states the financial rule: *fa-mā istamtaʿtum bihi minhunna fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna farīḍah* — "give those you have consummated marriage with their due dowries," rendered *farīḍah* (an obligation, a fixed due). Al-Jalalayn interprets: "Such wives as you enjoy thereby and have had sexual intercourse with, give them their wages — the dowries you assigned them — as an obligation." The final clause allows mutual generosity after the obligation: "you are not at fault in agreeing together, you and they, after the obligation is waived, decreased or increased." This is the same principle as 4:4 — the wife's voluntary waiver of part of her dowry is valid — and Al-Muntakhab states it as "give her more than what you have dutifully paid, or take back from her what she wishes to remit of her own accord." The mutual concession belongs to the couple; the due itself belongs to the wife.

This verse was also one of the proof texts in the classical debate about temporary marriage (mut'ah); Ibn Kathir's reading — "use your money to marry up to four wives and for the purchase of as many female slaves" — follows the majority position that the verse regulates permanent marriage with dowry, and the well-known later consensus (and the Prophet's own prohibition in the authentic Sunna) excluded mut'ah; the commentary tradition for this verse generally reads *ujūrhunna* as the dowry of a regular, recorded marriage. The commentary should be read in that frame, without suggesting the verse itself establishes mut'ah.

For the modern reader, the verse's principles are transferable: marriage is the only lawful channel of sexual relations; a woman in a marriage belongs to that marriage and no other man may claim her; war-time and exceptional circumstances are governed by specific rules, not by general license; the dowry is the wife's due; and within marriage — where each side is secure — generosity and concession are encouraged. The verse ends with the two divine names that frame all of it: Allah is All-Knowing of every situation and All-Wise in every rule.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 25

Verse 25 says: *But if any of you cannot afford to marry a free believing woman, then ˹let him marry˺ a believing bondwoman possessed by one of you. Allah knows best ˹the state of˺ your faith ˹and theirs˺. You are from one another. So marry them with the permission of their owners, giving them their dowry in fairness, if they are chaste, neither promiscuous nor having secret affairs. If they commit indecency after marriage, they receive half the punishment of free women. This is for those of you who fear falling into sin. But if you are patient, it is better for you. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.*

In simple terms, the verse opens a concession for a man who cannot afford the dowry of a free believing woman: he may marry a believing bondwoman owned by another believer, with her owner's permission, with a fair dowry, and only if she is chaste. If she commits adultery after marriage, her punishment is half that of a free woman. The concession is for those who fear falling into sin by remaining unmarried — but patience is better.

The verse begins with the condition. *Ṭawl* — rendered "cannot afford" — means the financial capability for the dowry and support of a free woman. Ibn Kathir explains that "free faithful, chaste women" is what *al-muḥṣanāt al-mu'mināt* means here. Al-Shafi'i's position, reported by Al-Jalalayn, is strict: when a man has the means to marry a free woman, it is unlawful for him to marry a slavegirl instead; and "believing" maids precludes marrying unbelieving slavegirls, even in distress, because the verse's allowance is conditioned on faith. Maududi's note on the same point is that all Muslims are alike — "the difference between the status of people is relative. If there is any true distinction, its basis is a person's faith... so it is possible for a slavegirl to be superior, in respect of her faith and morals, to a woman belonging to the elite of a society."

The verse's reassurance about faith — "Allah knows best the state of your faith and theirs" — is a profound correction of status-based thinking. Al-Jalalayn explains: "God knows very well your faith, so suffice yourself with its outward manifestation and leave the innermost matters to Him... many a slavegirl may be more excellent in faith than a free woman, and this is meant to encourage marriage with slavegirls; the one of you is as the other, being equal in religion, so do not disdain to marry with them." The marriage is a real marriage, not a lesser arrangement: it requires the owner's permission (the owner is responsible for the slavegirl, and the hadith recorded by the scholars states that a slave who marries without his master's permission is a fornicator), a fair dowry paid directly, and moral conditions.

The moral conditions are stated in three terms. *Muḥṣanāt* — chaste, women of wedlock; *ghayr musāfiḥāt* — not women of open prostitution; *wa-lā muttakhidhāt akhdān* — not taking secret lovers. Ibn 'Abbas explains the middle term as "the whores, who do not object to having relations with whomever seeks it," and the last as "taking boyfriends"; Ibn Kathir reports the same from Abu Hurayrah, Mujahid, Ash-Sha'bi, Ad-Dahhak, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Yahya bin Abi Kathir, Muqatil bin Hayyan, and As-Suddi. The verse is not merely describing the woman's status; it is selecting the kind of woman the believer may marry — one whose chastity is established, not one who is merely owned.

The punishment clause — half that of free women — was later the subject of a famous doctrinal dispute, and the classical answer is important. Al-Jalalayn: the half-punishment is fifty lashes and banishment for half a year; male slaves by analogy receive the same; and "here God has not made wedlock the precondition for the prescribed punishment, to show that stoning does not apply in their case." Ibn Kathir: "the type of punishment prescribed here is the one that can be reduced to half — lashes in this case, not stoning to death." Maududi notes that a superficial reading has misled some (including the Khawarij) into denying stoning entirely: *muḥṣanāt* appears in two senses — "married women" and "family-protected women" — and when the slavegirl is married (*fa-idhā uḥṣinna*), she acquires the protection of wedlock and the halved punishment applies to the divisible penalty (lashes) only.

The verse closes with the moral of the whole concession. *Dhālika li-man khashiya al-'anata minkum* — "this is for those of you who fear falling into sin" — where *al-'anat* is the distress of fornication: the verse is a rescue valve for the man who genuinely fears zinā if he remains unmarried, not a loophole for indulgence. And then the verse's own preference: *wa-an taṣbirū khayrun lakum* — "but if you are patient, it is better for you." Ibn Kathir explains the reason: "it is better to refrain from marrying slavegirls and to observe patience, for otherwise the offspring will become slaves to the girl's master." The child of a slavegirl-mother followed her legal status; the verse weighs the risk against the sin, and finds that for most people, patience with lawful celibacy — marriage being unavailable — is the better path.

For the modern reader, the verse is doubly relevant. Historically, it shows the Qur'an's graded approach to a difficult social reality: rather than either forbidding or encouraging one path, it regulates the lower path with strict conditions and recommends the higher. Conceptually, it teaches that faith, not social rank, is the measure of human worth; that marriage — even in its most reduced form — is a contract of rights, permission, dowry, and chastity, never an unregulated arrangement; and that the believer's standard in any allowance is the same: does this path keep me from sin and preserve the rights of the other person?

## Verse 26

Verse 26 says: *It is Allah’s Will to make things clear to you, guide you to the ˹noble˺ ways of those before you, and turn to you in mercy. For Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse explains the intention behind the laws just given — especially the marriage and financial rulings: Allah wills to make the lawful and unlawful clear, to guide His servants to the exemplary ways of the righteous communities that came before, and to turn toward them in mercy and forgiveness. He is All-Knowing of what they need and All-Wise in every ruling He gives.

The verse is a pivot. The previous verses laid out the marriage prohibitions, the rules of the dowry, and the concession regarding slavegirls. Now the Qur'an steps back and gives the *why*: these are not arbitrary rules, but mercy in action. Al-Jalalayn explains: "God desires to make clear to you the laws of your religion and what is in your best interests, and to guide you in the ways — the paths of those prophets before you — in the way of what is lawful and what is unlawful, so that you might follow them, and to turn in forgiveness towards you, bringing you back from the disobedience which you practised to obedience to Him." Each of the three divine purposes is therefore tied to the laws: clarity, guidance, and mercy.

The second purpose — "guide you to the noble ways of those before you" — is the most interesting theologically. *Sunan alladhīna min qablikum* is usually rendered "the ways of those before you," and Ibn Kathir explains it as "their righteous ways and how to adhere to the commandments that He likes and is pleased with." The Qur'an is saying that the moral and social law now being revealed is not an innovation; it is the continuation of the eternal principles followed by the prophets and the righteous from the beginning. Maududi's note adds the historical point: "These directives reveal those lasting principles observed by the Prophets and their followers from the earliest times. It is merely out of His grace and benevolence that God liberated them from their state of Ignorance (Jahiliyah) and opened to them the way of life of the righteous." The laws of inheritance, marriage, and property are therefore presented as the restoration of an ancient, universal moral order — not as the custom of one nation imposed on another.

The third purpose is *yatūbu ʿalaykum* — "to turn to you in mercy." The same verb is used for repentance: Allah turns toward the servant who turns toward Him. Here the meaning is the act of divine acceptance and forgiveness for what has passed. The pre-Islamic exceptions in the previous verses — "except what was done previously" — fit exactly: the law is clear for the future, and the past is met with divine mercy. Al-Jalalayn renders the sense: "bringing you back from the disobedience which you practised to obedience to Him."

The closing names are the guarantee of the whole statement. *ʿAlīm* — All-Knowing: He knows the human condition, the weaknesses being addressed, the situations in which the law must be applied. *Ḥakīm* — All-Wise: His rulings are not random but purposive, designed for human wellbeing in this world and the next. Ibn Kathir: "All-Knower, All-Wise in His commands, decrees, actions and statements." The doubling of the names at the end of a passage of law is a standard Quranic closing: the lawgiver identifies Himself by the two attributes that make His law trustworthy — He knows, and He is wise.

For the modern reader, the verse is a defense of the law's rationality. People often complain about religious law: it seems arbitrary, out of touch, or punitive. The Qur'an answers in advance: the will behind every ruling is clarity, guidance, and mercy. And the verse gives a standard by which any interpretation of the law may be tested: does this ruling make the lawful and unlawful clearer, does it guide to the right path, and does it open the door to mercy? A reading of the law that achieves none of the three has missed the will behind it.

The verse also teaches the believer's response to divine legislation. The laws are not obstacles to personal freedom; they are gifts of clarity in a world of confusion, guidance in a world of competing paths, and mercy in a world of guilt. The All-Knowing knows exactly what the human being needs, and the All-Wise has placed it in precisely the right form.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 27

Verse 27 says: *And it is Allah’s Will to turn to you in grace, but those who follow their desires wish to see you deviate entirely ˹from Allah’s Way˺.*

In simple terms, the verse contrasts two wills: Allah wills to turn toward the believers in mercy and forgiveness, while those who follow their desires — the partisans of their own lusts — wish only that the believers would veer entirely away from the right path. The verse names the enemy of the law: not reasoned disagreement, but desire-driven opposition.

The verse continues directly from the previous one. In 4:26, Allah stated His three purposes — clarity, guidance, mercy. Now He adds: the same mercy is His will, but there is an opposite will working on the believers. *Yurīd Allāhu an yatūba ʿalaykum* — "Allah wills to turn to you in grace." The repetition of the divine will (*yurīd Allāhu*) is emphatic: the law's mercy is not incidental, it is intended, and the believer should receive the law as an act of divine kindness.

The contrast is then drawn. *Wa-yurīd alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-shahawāt* — "but those who follow their desires wish..." — and the word for "desires" is *shahawāt*, the plural of *shahwah*: appetites, passions, lusts. Ibn Kathir identifies them concretely: "the followers of Shaytan among the Jews, Christians and the adulterous," and states their goal: "wish that you would take the horrendous path of falsehood instead of the truth." The verse is not about abstract philosophy; it is about identifiable forces in society — people whose desires are served by changing or abolishing the moral law, and who therefore want the believers to *tamīlū maylan ʿaẓīman* — to incline with a great, tremendous deviation, away from the straight path.

The structural point deserves attention. The Qur'an does not say that these people are merely wrong; it says they *wish* the believers to deviate. They do not want to leave the believers alone; they want the believers to change course. This describes a historical pattern that runs from the Qur'an's own opponents to the present: the moral law's critics are often most insistent that the law itself be abolished, rather than merely choosing a different path for themselves. And the verse's psychology is precise: desire is the engine. When a person's appetites are at war with a commandment, he is tempted not to reform the appetite, but to reject the commandment — and to want others to reject it too, so that his choice looks normal.

There is also a pastoral lesson in the verse. The believers are being warned that opposition to the law will always exist, and that it will be framed as liberation. The believer's test is to recognize the difference between guidance and desire — between those who want the best for humanity and those who want their own way. The mercy of Allah (26) and the desire of the soul (27) are the two forces before every believer, and the verse places them side by side so that the choice is transparent.

For the modern reader, the verse speaks to the age of moral redefinition. When the question is asked, "Why must we keep these limits?" the Qur'an's answer is already here: because the one who wants you to abandon them is often not offering you freedom, but only his own desire, dressed in the language of liberation. The verse is also a call to self-inspection — the believer must ask of every moral position: is this desire or is this guidance? — and a comfort: the forces of desire can wish for the deviation, but they cannot make it happen; the will of Allah toward the believers is mercy.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 28

Verse 28 says: *And it is Allah’s Will to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the trilogy of 4:26–28 with the reason for the law's realism: Allah wants to ease the believers' load — the laws have been designed to be bearable — because the human being was created weak.

The word *yukhaffif* — "to lighten" — is the same word used in the Qur'an for the easing of the fast in Ramadan and for the gradual removal of hardship: *yurīd Allāhu bikum al-yusra wa-lā yurīd bikum al-ʿusr* (2:185). Ibn Kathir explains: "Allah wishes to lighten the burden for you" — His legislation, orders, what He prohibits and what He decrees for you — and he connects the easing directly to the previous concession: "This is why Allah has allowed free men to marry slave girls under certain conditions, as Mujahid and others have stated." The law of 4:25, then, is itself an example of the principle: rather than commanding absolute celibacy for the man who cannot afford marriage and leaving him to fall into sin, the law opens a regulated lower path. The Shari'ah's famous principle — *mashaqqah tajlubu taysīr*, hardship brings ease — is visible here in the law's own structure.

The reason given is anthropology: *wa-khuliqa al-insānu ḍaʿīfan* — "and the human being was created weak." The weakness is not merely physical; it is the weakness of desire, of will, of endurance. Al-Jalalayn: "it is suitable in his case that the commands are made easy for him, because of his weakness and feebleness." Ibn Abi Hatim records Tawus explaining the verse: "Concerning women" — the immediate context being the sexual weaknesses the marriage law addresses — and Waki' saying, "Man's mind leaves when women are involved." The two reports point to the same truth: the law knows that the human being is not an angel, and it is written accordingly. Islam speaks to the whole human being — a being with appetites, circumstances, and limits — and its law is drawn to fit that being.

The theological point is even deeper: the easing of the law is itself an expression of divine mercy, not a concession to human weakness in the sense of a lowering of the standard. The standard is preserved — the prohibitions remain absolute — but the path is graded. The human being is weak, so the law opens the door of repentance (4:26–27), the concession of the lower path (4:25), and the guidance of the full truth (4:26). None of these weakens the law; all of them make it livable.

The scholars of the objectives of the law built much of their science on verses like this one. If the lawgiver's purpose is ease for a weak creature, then every ruling must be understood in the light of that purpose: the law's commands exist to serve the human being's wellbeing, not to burden him; its prohibitions exist to protect him, not to restrain him pointlessly. This is why the jurisprudential rules of concession — the sick may not fast, the traveller may shorten the prayer, the terrified may pray with gestures, the one who cannot wash in extreme cold may perform tayammum — are all rooted in the verse's principle. And it is why the classical jurists held that a ruling which produces hardship where the law intended ease has been misapplied: the application has drifted from the purpose of the Lawgiver.

The verse also answers a common criticism of religious law — that it is "too much." The Qur'an's answer is that the law has been designed specifically for this creature, with his weakness fully known, and the One who designed it is the One who created him (the closing names of 4:26 — All-Knowing, All-Wise — apply to the whole passage). The burden is real, but it is a burden the shoulder can carry, and the law itself is the evidence that the Maker measured the load.

There is a further lesson in the verse's placement. It comes immediately after the verses about mercy, desire, and grace (4:26–27) and immediately before the warnings about unlawful wealth and killing (4:29–30). The middle verse is the hinge: the law is easy because the creature is weak, and that ease is precisely why the creature has no excuse for devouring wealth or shedding life unlawfully. The same mercy that lightens the command also heightens the accountability — the person who was given the easy path and then chooses the hard one has no ground to stand on. The verse is therefore not only a comfort to the weak but a measure for the strong: the one who finds the law easy to follow cannot claim that his violation was beyond his strength.

For the modern reader, the verse is a principle of self-understanding: to be human is to be weak, so the believer need not be ashamed of the difficulty; the law is built around it. And it is a principle of law-application: wherever a ruling is being applied in a way that crushes rather than guides, the application has left the spirit of the verse, which is ease, clarity, and mercy.

## Verse 29

Verse 29 says: *O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but rather trade by mutual consent. And do not kill ˹each other or˺ yourselves. Surely Allah is ever Merciful to you.*

In simple terms, the verse forbids the unjust consumption of other people's property — by fraud, usury, theft, or any wrongful means — and permits wealth-transfer only through trade carried out by mutual agreement. It also forbids killing one another or oneself, and it grounds both prohibitions in Allah's mercy.

The verse opens with the address "O believers," and the prohibition is *lā taʾkulū amwālakum baynakum bil-bāṭil* — "do not devour your wealth among yourselves in falsehood." The word *bāṭil* covers every means that is unlawful, null, or fraudulent. Al-Jalalayn: "consuming your goods between you wrongly — unlawfully according to the Law — through usury or usurpation." Al-Muntakhab lists the practical forms: "usury, gambling, cheating, adulteration, treachery, monopoly, bribery, embezzlement, stealing and such vices; this leads to a gradual self-destruction." Ibn Kathir's summary is equally broad: "various dishonest methods such as Riba, gambling and other wicked methods that appear to be legal, but Allah knows that, in reality, those involved seek to deal in interest."

The exception is precisely drawn: *illā an takūna tijāratan ʿan tarāḍin minkum* — "except it be trade amongst you, by mutual consent." Ibn Kathir explains the meaning: "do not revert to illegal ways and means to acquire money. However, there is no harm in commercial transactions that transpire between the buyer and the seller with mutual consent, so that money is legally earned from these transactions." Mujahid extends "trade" to include buying and selling, or giving someone a gift. The crucial qualifier is *tarāḍ* — mutual consent. Ibn Kathir devotes a section to the meaning: mutual agreement is attained when both parties have the right to uphold or dissolve the agreement before they part, and he cites the hadith in the Two Sahihs: "The seller and the buyer retain the right to change their mind as long as they have not parted." Consent in Islamic commerce is therefore not a formality of signature; it is a freedom of decision that the law protects until the transaction is genuinely concluded.

The historical context adds a vivid detail. Ibn Kathir reports from 'Ali bin Abi Talhah from Ibn 'Abbas: when this verse was sent down, "some Muslims said, 'Allah has forbidden us from eating up each other's property unjustly, and food is our best property. Therefore, none among us is allowed to eat from anyone else's food.' After that Allah sent down" the verse of 24:61 — the ruling that the blind, the lame, and the sick may eat from the houses of their families without restriction. The early community's over-scrupulous reaction was itself corrected by revelation: trade and mutual hospitality are lawful; only wrongful consumption is forbidden.

The second prohibition is *wa-lā taqtulū anfusakum* — "and do not kill each other or yourselves." Ibn Kathir reads it broadly: "by committing Allah's prohibitions, falling into sin and eating up each other's property unjustly" — so the "killing" includes not only literal homicide and suicide but the self-destruction that follows from sin. The literal reading is also fully in view. He reports Abu Hurayrah's hadith (Ibn Marduwyah; also in the Two Sahihs): the one who kills himself with an iron will keep stabbing himself with it in the Fire forever; the one who kills himself with poison will keep drinking it in the Fire forever; the one who throws himself from a mountain will keep falling in the Fire forever. And Thabit bin Ad-Dahhak's hadith (collected by the Group): "Whoever kills himself with an instrument will be punished with it on the Day of Resurrection."

The closing reason is mercy: *inna Allāha kāna bikum raḥīman* — "surely Allah is ever Merciful to you." Ibn Kathir: "Most Merciful to you in what He commanded you and prohibited you from." The prohibition is not a divine restriction on human freedom but a protection of human life and property — and Imam Ahmad's famous hadith of 'Amr bin Al-'As is the perfect illustration: on the very cold night of Dhat As-Salasil, 'Amr had a wet dream and feared that bathing would kill him; he performed tayammum and led the prayer, and the Prophet — told of it — smiled and did not reproach him. The "do not kill yourselves" verse was the very reason he gave, and the Prophet endorsed the reasoning: the law itself provided the escape.

For the modern reader, the verse is the foundation of Islamic commercial ethics: no fraudulent means, no deception, no exploitation, and genuine mutual consent as the core of every transaction. And it is the foundation of the sanctity of life: the individual's life is not his own to destroy, and the community's life — its members — may not be harmed by its own members. The two prohibitions are joined in one verse because the same mentality destroys both: the person who will take another's property unjustly will also take the other's life, and the person who cannot restrain himself from either has forgotten the mercy of the One who forbade both.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 30

Verse 30 says: *And whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire. That is easy for Allah.*

In simple terms, the verse delivers the consequence: whoever commits the forbidden acts — devouring wealth illegally or killing — in transgression and injustice will be cast into the Fire. And the verse adds a clause that is itself a warning: that outcome is easy for Allah.

The verse's two qualifiers are precise: *ʿudwānan wa-ẓulmā* — "in transgression and injustice." Ibn Kathir explains: "whoever commits what Allah has forbidden for him — transgression and injustice — while knowing that it is forbidden for him, yet still dares to commit it." The two words are not redundancy. *ʿUdwān* (transgression) describes the breaking of the boundary — the act of overstepping the limit Allah set; *ẓulm* (injustice) describes the wrong done to the other — taking what belongs to another, harming what belongs to another. The verse's punishment therefore attaches not to every mistake but to the deliberate crossing of the boundary combined with the violation of another's right. A person can make an error in business without incurring this verdict; the verdict belongs to the one who knowingly and unjustly devours.

The punishment is *nāru* — the Fire. The verb *nuṣlīhi* — "We will burn/cast into" — is the same verb used for the fire of Hell elsewhere. Ibn Kathir: "This Ayah carries a stern warning and a truthful promise. Therefore, every sane person should beware of it, those who hear the speech with full comprehension." The "truthful promise" is significant in the Quranic frame: Allah's warnings are not threats designed to frighten; they are statements of what will actually happen, and the believer is to take them as fact.

The final clause is the most striking: *wa-kāna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīran* — "and that is easy for Allah." The clause can be read in two ways, and both are taught. First, it is a reassurance of the *certainty* of the punishment: the Fire is not a matter of Allah's effort, and nothing will prevent it — what is "easy for Allah" cannot be frustrated by human evasion. Second, it is a statement of divine power: the same ease that makes the punishment effortless also makes the reward effortless; the Lord who says "Be" to the Fire is the Lord who says "Be" to the Gardens. The clause appears in the Qur'an as a refrain precisely because the human being tends to doubt either the punishment's certainty or its sincerity. The verse removes both doubts.

Structurally, 4:29–30 and 4:31 form a moral triad: the prohibition (29), the warning (30), and the mercy of the promised absolution (31). The reader who takes the warning seriously is immediately given the door: avoid the major sins, and the minor ones will be forgiven. The Fire is real, but the escape is offered before the sentence is explained.

For the modern reader, the verse teaches three things. First, the moral law is not optional commentary on social behavior; it is a boundary with a consequence, and the consequence is presented in terms no reader can misunderstand. Second, the boundary is about *rights* — the prohibition is "injustice," so the person who has committed no injustice to another need not live in terror of this verse; the verse is aimed at the wrongdoer. Third, the ease of the divine action is both a fright and a comfort: the ease of the punishment calls the sinner to fear; the ease of the power calls the believer to trust a Lord who is able to do all things.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 31

Verse 31 says: *If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour.*

In simple terms, the verse announces a mercy attached to the law's warnings: whoever avoids the major sins that Allah has forbidden will have his minor misdeeds wiped away and will be admitted into a place of honour — Paradise.

The verse is the promise that follows 4:29–30. After the prohibitions of unlawful wealth and killing, and after the threat of the Fire for the deliberate wrongdoer, the Qur'an now opens the door of mercy: *in tajtanibū kabāʾira mā tunhawna ʿanhu* — "if you avoid the major sins which you are forbidden." Al-Jalalayn: "the grave sins for which the threat of punishment has been prescribed — like murder, fornication, or theft — according to Ibn 'Abbas these number as much as seven hundred." The *kabāʾir* (major sins) are distinguished from the *ṣaghāʾir* (minor sins), and the verse's structure is the basis of the classical doctrine of major and minor sins: the major sins are those with a specific threat of punishment in the Qur'an or Sunna, and the minor ones are the rest.

The verse's promise is that the minor sins are *nukaffir* — erased, expiated. The classical commentary holds that this happens through the avoidance of the major sins in combination with acts of obedience, repentance, and the mercy of Allah; the principle is the same as the hadith of Salman Al-Farisi, recorded by Imam Ahmad and in a similar form by Al-Bukhari: the person who takes a bath, prepares himself, attends Friday prayer and listens quietly has his sins between that Friday and the next forgiven, "as long as major sins were avoided." Obedience itself wipes away minor lapses, and the verse generalizes the principle: the believer's careful distance from the great sins is the condition of the mercy that covers the small ones.

What are the major sins? The authentic hadiths give the list. Abu Hurayrah's hadith in the Two Sahihs: "Avoid the seven great destructive sins" — associating partners with Allah, killing the life Allah has forbidden except by right, magic, consuming riba, consuming an orphan's wealth, fleeing from the battlefield, and accusing chaste believing women. Anas's hadith (Two Sahihs): shirk, killing a life, and undutifulness to parents, and the greatest: false statement or false testimony. 'Abdur-Rahman bin Abi Bakrah's hadith (Two Sahihs): shirk, undutifulness to parents, and false testimony — repeated until the Companions wished he would stop. Ibn Mas'ud's hadith (Two Sahihs): making a rival to Allah, killing one's offspring for fear of sharing food, and adultery with the neighbor's wife. 'Abdullah bin 'Amr's hadith (Al-Bukhari, At-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i): shirk, undutifulness to parents or taking a life, and the false oath — and the hadith of cursing one's parents by cursing another man's parents.

The final reward is *mudkhalan karīman* — "a place of honour," "a noble entrance." Al-Jalalayn notes the variant readings *mudkhalan* and *madkhalan* — an honourable admittance or location — and identifies it: Paradise. The word *karīm* is the same root as the Qur'an's descriptions of epithets of dignity: noble, generous, honoured. The reward is not merely a location; it is an entrance that honours the one who enters. The whole verse, then, is a bargain of mercy: the believer is not asked to be sinless, but to avoid the grave sins — and the small failings of a lifetime are erased, and the door of honour opens.

For the modern reader, the verse is one of the Qur'an's most realistic moral teachings. It does not require perfection; it requires the avoidance of the catastrophic. The believer who sins daily in small ways is not therefore condemned; the believer who rationalizes the great sins is not therefore saved. And the verse's psychological wisdom is evident: the fear of the Fire (30) is immediately followed by the promise of forgiveness (31), so that the moral law produces neither complacency nor despair but the precise combination the Qur'an wants — vigilance toward the major sins, hopefulness about the minor ones, and a movement toward the place of honour.

The verse also touches the doctrine of intercession and mercy that the classical tradition developed: the one who avoids the kabāʾir is promised the covering of the ṣaghāʾir; the one who falls into the kabāʾir and repents is promised forgiveness through repentance (4:17); and the one who persists is exposed to the promise of 4:14. The verse's placement — between the warning (30) and the envy-admonition (32) — makes it the hinge of the passage: the law's whole structure is meant to produce a people who avoid the destructive and are honoured by the Merciful.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 32

Verse 32 says: *And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.*

In simple terms, the verse forbids coveting what Allah has given to others — whether wealth, ability, or station — and instead points each person to his own pathway: men and women alike will be recompensed for their own deeds; the believer's hope should be directed to asking Allah for His bounty, not to envying what He has apportioned.

The verse's first word is *tamannaw* — "do not crave" or "do not wish for" — and the prohibition is aimed at the attitude of envy and covetousness. Al-Jalalayn explains the prohibition: "Do not covet that in which God has preferred some of you above others, in the way of worldly affairs or religion, lest it lead to mutual envy and hatred." The verse is not forbidding ambition, aspiration, or striving; it is forbidding the *tamannī* — the longing for another's share as such. The classical distinction is essential: the believer may ask Allah for bounty and work for it; he may not resent the allocation that Allah has made to others.

The occasion of revelation shows the verse's practical aim. Ibn Kathir records Imam Ahmad from Umm Salamah: she said, "O Messenger of Allah! Men go to battle, but we do not go to battle, and we earn one-half of the inheritance (that men get)." At-Tirmidhi also records it, and Al-Jalalayn gives her words as, "Would that we were men so that we could join the struggle and receive the reward they receive!" In response, the verse was revealed: each person will receive according to his own deeds — men have a share from what they earn by their struggle, and women have a share from what they earn by their own obedience and acts.

The second half of the verse is therefore a doctrine of individual recompense: *li-al-rijāli naṣībun mimmā iktasabū, wa-li-al-nisāʾi naṣībun mimmā iktasabna* — "for men is a share of what they have earned, and for women is a share of what they have earned." Ibn Kathir notes Ibn Jarir's reading: each person will earn wages for his works — reward if his deeds are good, punishment if evil — and he also reports the reading of Al-Walibi from Ibn 'Abbas that the verse speaks of inheritance: each person gets his due share. Both readings are within the verse; the common principle is that no person is judged by another's share, and no one's share is the measure of his worth in the sight of Allah. The verse's "equally" is a Quranic insistence: the woman's deeds are her own, the man's are his own, and neither is credited to the other.

The positive instruction follows: *wa-as'alū Allāha min faḍlihi* — "and ask Allah of His bounty." Ibn Kathir's comment is the verse's whole message: "Do not wish for what other people were endowed with over you, for this is a decision that will come to pass, and wishing does not change its decree. However, ask Me of My favor and I will grant it to you, for I Am Most Generous and Most Giving." The believer's energy is redirected: not toward envy of the allocation, but toward petition to the Allocator. Maududi's note adds the social insight: the diversity of human endowment — beauty, voice, strength, abilities, circumstances, resources — "gives variety to human civilization, and hence serves a useful purpose," and "by heeding it, man would be able to achieve a great measure of peace and tranquillity."

The closing name — *ʿAlīm* (All-Knowing) — is the guarantee: Allah knows "who deserves this life, and so He gives him riches, and whoever deserves poverty, He makes him poor. He also knows who deserves the Hereafter, and He directs him to the deeds that will help him succeed in it" (Ibn Kathir). The allocation is not arbitrary; it is knowledge-based, and the believer's trust in the All-Knowing is the antidote to envy.

For the modern reader, the verse is a cure for one of the most corrosive emotions of the age: comparison. Social media has made comparison a permanent activity, and the verse names the disease and the cure — the disease is *tamannī*, craving what another has; the cure is *suʾāl*, asking Allah for what He has to give, and *kash*, earning one's own share. The verse also settles the question of gendered recompense definitively: the woman is not judged by the man's deeds nor the man by the woman's; each has a share of his own earning, and the doors of both prayer and labor are open to both.

## Verse 33

Verse 33 says: *And We have appointed heirs to what has been left by parents and next of kin. As for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things.*

In simple terms, the verse confirms that inheritance belongs to the appointed heirs — parents and next of kin — and addresses the old Arab custom of covenant-inheritance: those with whom a man made a pact of alliance or brotherhood were to be given their share. The verse is the historical bridge between the pre-Islamic customary law and the Quranic fixed shares.

The opening clause is *wa-likullin jaʿalnā mawālī* — "and for everyone We have appointed heirs." *Mawālī* is the plural of *mawlā*, and the commentators explain it here as "heirs." Ibn Kathir lists the authorities: Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Abu Salih, Qatadah, Zayd bin Aslam, As-Suddi, Ad-Dahhak, Muqatil bin Hayyan, and others — "Heirs." Ibn 'Abbas is also reported to have said *mawālī* means relatives. Ibn Jarir comments: "The Arabs call the cousin a Mawla," and explains the verse to mean: "To all of you, O people, We appointed relatives (such as children) who will later inherit what you inherited from your own parents and relatives." The verse therefore establishes that inheritance flows through kinship — parents and next of kin — and that the appointment of heirs is a divine act, not a family preference.

The second clause is the historical one. *Wa-alladhīna ʿāqadat aymānukum* — "and those with whom your right hands/oaths have made a covenant." Al-Jalalayn explains *ʿāqadat* (also read *ʿaqadat*): "those allies with whom before the coming of Islam you made covenants of mutual assistance and inheritance; so give them now their share — their portions of the inheritance, which is a sixth." Ibn Kathir's frame: "Those with whom you have a pledge of brotherhood, give them their share of inheritance, thus fulfilling the ratified pledges that you gave them. Allah has witnessed all of you when you gave these pledges and promises."

The historical background is crucial. When the emigrants arrived in Madinah, the Prophet established brotherhood between the Muhajirun and the Ansar, and the early practice — as Ibn Kathir reports from Ibn 'Abbas in Al-Bukhari — was that "the emigrant would inherit from the Ansari, while the latter's relatives would not inherit from him because of the bond of brotherhood." This was the custom of the first period of Islam, mirroring the pre-Islamic pact system in which alliances and adoption created inheritance claims. The verse before us, Ibn 'Abbas says, cancelled the brotherhood-inheritance, and the clause "give them their share" remained in force only for cooperation and mutual advice, while inheritance went to the kin. And it became permissible to assign something in one's will to the former pledge-partner.

The abrogation is stated by Al-Jalalayn: the verse "was abrogated by His words, 'But those related by blood are nearer to one another' (8:75 and 33:6)." Maududi gives the full historical picture: "According to Arab customary law, those who concluded compacts of alliance and friendship also became mutual heirs. Likewise, an adopted son inherited from his foster-father. While abrogating this customary law, this verse reveals that inheritance goes to one's kin according to the rules for the distribution of inheritance laid down by God Himself. However, if a man has made commitments to people, he has the right to give away to them whatever he wishes during his lifetime." The verse, then, is not a permanent command to give inheritance to covenant-partners; it is the record of the transition, and its permanent teaching is that kinship, not compact, determines inheritance.

The closing phrase — *wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīdan* — "Allah is over all things a Witness" — is the verse's moral anchor: Allah witnessed the pledges made and witnesses all the affairs of inheritance, trusts, and covenants; nothing done in secret, and nothing done in the name of brotherhood, escapes His record.

For the modern reader, the verse teaches the primacy of blood kinship in inheritance over all constructed relations — friendship pacts, adoption, business partnerships, or loyalty contracts. It also teaches the difference between law and goodwill: a man may give freely during his lifetime to anyone he loves, but he may not override the divine inheritance shares. And it teaches the Quranic principle of loyalty within limits: the pledge remains binding in its moral dimension — cooperation, advice, and assistance — but it cannot rewrite the law. Allah is the Witness of both the promise and the allocation.

## Verse 34

Verse 34 says: *Men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially. And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with. And if you sense ill-conduct from your women, advise them ˹first˺, ˹if they persist,˺ do not share their beds, ˹but if they still persist,˺ then discipline them ˹gently˺. But if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them. Surely Allah is Most High, All-Great.*

In simple terms, the verse establishes the husband's responsibility for the household: men are the caretakers and maintainers of women because Allah has given men qualities suited to that role and has tasked them with financial support. Righteous wives are obedient and protect what Allah has entrusted to them. If a wife displays ill-conduct, the husband is to proceed in a graded sequence — advice, then separation from the marital bed, then gentle discipline — and once she changes her ways, all means of pressure must end.

The opening term is *qawwāmūn* — "caretakers," "protectors and maintainers." Ibn Kathir: "the man is responsible for the woman, and he is her maintainer, caretaker and leader who disciplines her if she deviates." Maududi defines *qawwam* (or *qayyim*) as "a person responsible for administering and supervising the affairs of either an individual or an organization, for protecting and safeguarding them and taking care of their needs." The position is not a rank of honour but a burden of responsibility.

The verse gives two reasons. First, *bimā faḍḍala Allāhu baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍ* — "because Allah has made one of them to excel over the other." Ibn Kathir: "men excel over women and are better than them for certain tasks. This is why prophethood was exclusive of men, as well as other important positions of leadership" — and he cites the hadith (Al-Bukhari): "People who appoint a woman to be their leader will never achieve success," applying it to judges and similar positions. Maududi is careful to correct a possible misreading: "The verb used here — a derivative of the root *fdl* — is not used to mean that some people have been invested with superior honour and dignity. Rather, it means that God has endowed one of the sexes with certain qualities which He has not endowed the other sex with, at least not to an equal extent." Second, *bimā anfaqū min amwālihim* — "because they spend from their wealth": the dowry, the maintenance, and all the expenses ordained in the Book and Sunna. The authority is, in the Quranic structure, purchased by responsibility: the one who carries the financial responsibility carries the headship, and the structure exists so that the woman is protected, not so that the man is elevated.

The wife's qualities are the verse's second theme. *Qānitāt* — "devoutly obedient" — Ibn 'Abbas and others explain: obedient to their husbands. *Ḥāfiẓāt lil-ghayb* — "protective of what Allah has entrusted them with" — As-Suddi and others: she protects her honour and her husband's property when he is absent; Ibn Kathir adds "the protected husband is the one whom Allah protects." The Prophet's description (Ibn Jarir, Ibn Abi Hatim; cited by Maududi) is the classic portrait: "The best woman is she who when you look at her she pleases you, when you command her she obeys you, and when you are absent she protects her honour and your property." And Imam Ahmad records 'Abdur-Rahman bin 'Awf's hadith: a woman who prays her five, fasts her month, protects her chastity and obeys her husband is told: "Enter Paradise from any of its doors you wish." The obedience is qualified, as Maududi stresses: "obedience to God has priority over a woman's duty to obey her husband. If her husband asks her to disobey God or prevents her from a duty God imposed, she refuses."

The third theme is the treatment of ill-conduct (*nushūz*). Ibn Kathir defines it: the woman who acts as if above her husband, disobeys, ignores, or dislikes him. The sequence is a *graded escalation* with strict limits. First: *faʿiẓūhunna* — advise, remind her of Allah's torment and the enormity of the husband's right (the hadith: "If I were to command anyone to prostrate before anyone, I would have commanded the wife to prostrate before her husband"). Second: *wa-uhjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿ* — abandon her in the bed — Ibn 'Abbas: no intercourse, lie with his back to her; As-Suddi, Ad-Dahhak, 'Ikrimah, and another narration of Ibn 'Abbas add: do not speak to her (Mu'awiyah bin Haydah's hadith: feed, clothe, do not strike the face, do not curse, do not abandon except in the house). Third: *wa-ḍribūhunna* — discipline, without severe beating; Muslim's Farewell-hajj hadith of Jabir: "Fear Allah regarding women, for they are your assistants... you may discipline them lightly... they have a right on you that you provide them with their provision and clothes in a reasonable manner"; Ibn 'Abbas and others say the beating is not violent; Al-Hasan Al-Basri: not severe. The classical scholars emphasized that the third step is a disciplinary, non-harmful measure, never a licence for violence, and subsequent jurists (and the consensus in modern application) treat it as a last resort limited by explicit prophetic prohibitions against striking the face and causing injury.

The final clause is the verse's safeguard: *fa-in aṭaʿnakum fa-lā tabghū ʿalayhinna sabīlan* — "if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them." Ibn Kathir: "when the wife obeys her husband in all that Allah has allowed, then no means of annoyance from the husband are allowed against his wife. Therefore, in this case, the husband does not have the right to beat her or shun her bed." And the closing names — Most High, Most Great — "reminds men that if they transgress against their wives without justification, then Allah, the Ever Most High, Most Great, is their Protector, and He will exert revenge on those who transgress against their wives and deal with them unjustly."

For the modern reader, the verse is among the most discussed in the Qur'an, and its correct frame is captured by the classical sequence itself: the primary commands are maintenance, kindness, and protection; authority is a responsibility, not a privilege; the wife's obedience is conditioned by her higher obedience to Allah; and the whole passage is bounded by the assurance that Allah is the protector of the wife against injustice.

## Verse 35

Verse 35 says: *If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers. If they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware.*

In simple terms, the verse moves from the case of the wife's ill-conduct to the case of conflict between the spouses: if a breach is feared, two mediators are appointed — one from the husband's family, one from the wife's — to examine the situation and seek reconciliation. If they sincerely desire reform and reconciliation, Allah will bring the spouses back together.

The verse's term is *shiqāq* — a breach, a split, a state of alienation between the two. Al-Jalalayn: "if you fear a breach — a dispute between the two, the married couple — send forth for them an arbiter, a just man, from his folk, and an arbiter from her folk." The appointment is with the consent of the parties, and the verse's structure recognizes the reality that family conflict is often best handled by people who know both the spouses and their families: the husband's mediator knows his family's concerns and temperament; the wife's mediator knows hers.

The role of the mediators is developed by the classical jurists. Ibn Kathir says the Fuqaha' rule that when estrangement occurs, the judge refers them to a trusted person who examines the case to stop wrongs; if the matter continues or worsens, a trustworthy person from each family is sent to meet with them and determine whether it is best for them to part or remain together. Al-Jalalayn gives the delegation details: "the husband delegates to his arbiter the matter of divorce or the acceptance of compensation in its place, while she delegates to her arbiter the matter of separation. The two arbiters do their best and bid the one guilty of the injustice to desist, or they suggest separation if they see fit."

The findings of the mediators have legal weight. Ibn Kathir reports 'Ali bin Abi Talhah from Ibn 'Abbas: the mediators find out who among the spouses is in the wrong — if the man, they prevent him from his wife and he pays restitution; if the wife, she remains and he pays nothing. If the arbitrators decide the marriage should remain or be dissolved, their decision is upheld. And a fine detail: if they decide the marriage remains but one spouse disagrees while the other agrees, and one later dies, the spouse who agreed to the arbitration inherits from the other while the objecting spouse does not — collected by Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Jarir. Ibn Kathir also records Abu 'Umar bin 'Abdul-Barr's summary: the scholars agree that when the two arbitrators disagree, the opinion that dissolves the marriage is not adopted; the decision of the arbitrators is binding even if the spouses did not appoint them as agents; and the majority holds the decision binding even for separation.

The heart of the verse is the divine condition: *in yurīdā iṣlāḥan yuwaffiqi Allāhu baynahumā* — "if they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them." Al-Jalalayn explains "they" as the two arbiters: "if they desire to set things right, God will grant them the married couple success, determining for them what constitutes an act of obedience, be it reconciliation or separation." Ibn Kathir: "Allah gives preference to staying together." The verse teaches a profound spiritual law: reconciliation is not merely a human technique; it is a divine work, and it is granted when the parties *want* it. The mediators may be skilled, but the harmony is from Allah; and the condition is the will — the sincere intention of the parties to mend the marriage.

The closing names — *ʿAlīm* (All-Knowing), *Khabīr* (All-Aware) — are the warrant: Allah knows the hidden causes of the conflict and the state of both hearts; nothing about the marriage is concealed from Him, and His knowledge is available to the sincere mediators who seek it.

For the modern reader, the verse establishes the institution of family mediation as divinely prescribed. Before deadlines, courtrooms, and the hardening of positions, the Qur'an prescribes: bring people who care about both sides, examine the real causes, identify the one in the wrong, and work for reconciliation — and if the couple's own will to reform is present, the harmony will come. The verse also teaches the priority of the union: the law prefers the marriage to continue, and the mediation process is built to serve that preference, with separation as a last resort rather than a first option.

## Verse 36

Verse 36 says: *Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him. And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, ˹needy˺ travellers, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession.*

In simple terms, the verse gathers the whole of the moral law into a single command: worship Allah alone, and then be good to the entire circle of people around you — parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, both kinds of neighbour, companions, travellers, and those in your service. The verse is the Quranic summary of worship-plus-ethics.

The verse opens with the foundation: *waʿbudū Allāha wa-lā tushrikū bihi shayʾan* — "worship Allah and associate nothing with Him." Ibn Kathir explains: "Allah orders that He be worshipped Alone without partners, because He Alone is the Creator and Sustainer Who sends His favors and bounties on His creation in all situations and instances. Therefore He deserves to be singled out, without associating anything or anyone from His creation with Him in worship." And he records the Prophet's famous conversation with Mu'adh: "Do you know what Allah's right on His servants is?" — that they worship Him and do not worship anything with Him — "and do you know what the right of the servants on Allah is if they do that? He should not punish them." The verse is the doctrinal pillar of the whole passage: monotheism, not as a formula but as the root of all human obligations.

The first of the human obligations is the parents. Ibn Kathir: "Allah made parents the reason for the servants to come to existence, after they did not exist," and Allah "joins the order to worship Him with being dutiful to parents in many places" — 31:14 ("give thanks to Me and to your parents") and 17:23 ("your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and that you be dutiful to your parents"). The verse's placement is deliberate: the right of Allah and the right of the parents are inseparable in the Quranic order, and the reward for kindness to parents is treated as part of the worship of Allah.

Then the wider circle opens. Relatives: Ibn Kathir quotes the hadith, "Charity given to the poor is Sadaqah, while charity given to relatives is both Sadaqah and Silah (keeping the relations)." Orphans: "because they lost their caretakers who would spend on them. So Allah commands that the orphans be treated with kindness and compassion." The poor (*masākīn*): "who have various needs and cannot find what sustains these needs; Allah commands they should be helped in acquiring their needs in a sufficient manner that will end their inadequacy" — elaborated in 9:60.

The neighbour is named in two forms. *Al-jār dhī al-qurbā* — the near neighbour, which 'Ali bin Abi Talhah reports from Ibn 'Abbas as "the neighbour who is also a relative" (with the same from 'Ikrimah, Mujahid, Maymun bin Mihran, Ad-Dahhak, Zayd bin Aslam, Muqatil bin Hayyan, and Qatadah); and *al-jār al-junub* — the distant neighbour, "who is not a relative"; Mujahid also explained it as "the companion during travel." The neighbor's rights are among the most emphatic in the Sunna. Ibn 'Umar's hadith (Imam Ahmad, Two Sahihs): "Jibril kept reminding me of the neighbor's right until I thought that he was going to give him a share of the inheritance" — the same in Ibn 'Amr's narration (Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi, Hasan Gharib). "The best companions according to Allah are those who are the best with their friends, and the best neighbours are the best with their neighbours" (Ahmad, At-Tirmidhi Hasan Gharib). Al-Miqdad's hadith (Ahmad): adultery with the neighbour's wife is worse than with ten women, and stealing from the neighbour's house is worse than stealing from ten houses; Ibn Mas'ud's greatest-sins hadith (Two Sahihs) places adultery with the neighbour's wife third after shirk and infanticide. And 'A'ishah's hadith (Al-Bukhari): when she has two neighbours, give the gift to the one whose door is closest.

The "companion at your side" (*al-ṣāḥib bi al-janb*) — Al-Jalalayn: "a travelling companion or a colleague at work, and it is also said one's wife." Maududi: "those with whom one has friendly relations of an abiding nature as well as those with whom one's relationship is transient: for instance, either the person who walks beside one on the way to the market or who sits beside one while buying things from the same shop or one's fellow traveller. Even this temporary relationship imposes certain claims on every refined and decent person — that he should treat him, as far as possible, in a kind and gracious manner and avoid causing him any inconvenience." The wayfarer (*ibn al-sabīl*) is the stranger cut off on a journey, and those your right hands possess — the bondspeople — are the last and the weakest. Ibn Kathir's collection of hadiths here is striking: the Prophet's final illness advice repeated "The prayer! The prayer! And those whom your hands possess" until his tongue was still; Al-Miqdam's hadith (An-Nasa'i, authentic) that feeding yourself, your children, your wife, and your servant are each Sadaqah; 'Abdullah bin 'Amr's — "It is enough sin for someone to prevent whomever he is responsible for from getting their food" (Muslim); and Abu Hurayrah's: "The slave has the right to food, clothing and to only be required to perform what he can bear of work" (Muslim); and Al-Bukhari's: when your servant brings food, if you do not seat him with you, give him a mouthful or two, for he has prepared it.

The classical commentary connects the verse's end to the character named in the following passage: the proud and boastful heart is the opposite of the life the verse describes. Ibn Kathir reports — through Ibn Jarir from 'Abdullah bin Waqid Abu Raja' Al-Harawi — that one who is mean is also proud and boasting, and that one who is undutiful to parents is also arrogant (with 19:32), and the Prophet's counsel: avoid lengthening the dress below the ankles, for it is from arrogance, "and verily, Allah does not like arrogance." The one who serves parents and neighbours has no room for pride, and the one who is proud has missed the verse's own spirit. For the modern reader, the verse is the Qur'an's ladder of care: Allah first, then the family, then the helpless, then the neighbour, then the stranger, then the servant — and the whole ladder is worship.

## Verse 37

Verse 37 says: *those who are stingy, promote stinginess among people, and withhold Allah’s bounties. We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse names the opposite of 4:36's generosity: those who are stingy with what Allah gave them, who urge others to be stingy, and who conceal the bounties Allah has bestowed. For such people — and for the disbelievers — a humiliating punishment is prepared.

The verse is the counterpart of the previous one. In 4:36, the believers were commanded to be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, poor, neighbours, companions, travellers, and servants. Now the Qur'an identifies the character that makes all of that impossible: the *bakhīl* — the miser. Ibn Kathir's frame: "Allah chastises the stingy behavior of those who refuse to spend their money for what Allah ordered them, such as being kind to parents and compassionate to relatives, orphans, the poor, the relative who is also a neighbor, the companion during travel, the needy wayfarer, the slaves and servants. Such people do not give Allah's right from their wealth, and they assist in the spread of stingy behavior." The verse's three verbs are a moral profile. First: *yabkhalūn* — they are stingy in their own duty. Second: *wa-yaʾmurūna al-nāsa bil-bukhl* — they command the people with stinginess; their stinginess is not private but propagandistic, and they urge others toward the same vice. Third: *wa-yaktumūna mā ātāhum Allāhu min faḍlihi* — they conceal what Allah has given them of His bounty; the bounty's effect does not appear on them, in food, clothing, or giving, and they hide the grace of Allah both in wealth and in knowledge.

Ibn Kathir reports the Prophet's two sayings on the vice: "What disease is more serious than being stingy?" and "Beware of *shuḥḥ* (avarice), for it destroyed those who were before you, as it encouraged them to cut their relations and they did, and it encouraged them to commit sin and they did." The point is that stinginess is not merely a private character flaw; it is a sociologically destructive force — it severs kinship, breeds resentment, and leads to the abandonment of duties.

The linguistic point is profound. Ibn Kathir: "*Kufr* means to cover something. Therefore, the *bakhīl* covers the favors that Allah has blessed him with, meaning he does not spread those favors. So he is described by the term *Kafir* (ungrateful) regarding the favors that Allah granted him." The verse's last word is *li-al-kāfirīn* — "for the disbelievers" — and the commentator explains that the miser is *kāfir* in the sense of ingratitude and concealment of the favor, even where he is not a disbeliever in religion. In this Quranic usage, the same root covers both: the one who denies the Giver and the one who hides the Gift. Ibn Kathir also cites the hadith: "When Allah grants a servant a favor, He likes that its effect appears on him." The effect of the bounty on the believer is gratitude in action, and the concealment of the bounty is concealment of the Giver's grace.

The classical tradition adds the case of knowledge. Some of the Salaf said the verse describes the Jews who hid their knowledge of the description of Muhammad, and Ibn Kathir notes the general meaning includes this: "miserly conduct with knowledge is also included." The verse, indeed, is often read as covering both wealth and knowledge: the scholar who hoards what would guide people, and the wealthy who hoards what would sustain them, are both described by the same three verbs.

The punishment is *ʿadhābun muhīn* — "a humiliating punishment." The word *muhīn* is the same one used for the humiliating punishment repeatedly in the Qur'an: the punishment that brings low, exposes, and disgraces. The divine recompense is fitted to the fault: the miser sought to elevate himself by withholding; he is brought low by the withholding. And the phrase "We have prepared" — *aʿtadnā* — indicates that the punishment is already arranged, certain, awaiting.

For the modern reader, the verse is the corrective to the culture of accumulation for its own sake. The verse teaches that wealth is a trust to be circulated — the Qur'an's word *faḍl* (bounty) implies a flow — and that the person who stops the flow is not a wise saver but a concealer of Allah's grace. The verse also teaches the social dimension of vice: stinginess is not private when it is preached, recommended, or normalized — and the person who promotes it shares in its destruction. And the closing names, as the verse's link to the next, show the two faces of the moral life: the proud miser and the ostentatious spender are two variations of the same disease — the heart turned away from Allah.

## Verse 38

Verse 38 says: *Likewise for those who spend their wealth to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. And whoever takes Satan as an associate —what an evil associate they have!*

In simple terms, the verse names the opposite error: not the miser who will not spend, but the spender who spends for show — for the praise of people — while believing neither in Allah nor the Last Day. Such a person has taken Satan as a companion, and Satan is the worst of companions.

The verse is a deliberate pairing with 4:37. Ibn Kathir's frame: "Allah first mentions the punished misers who do not spend, then He mentions those who spend to show off to gain the reputation that they are generous, not for the Face of Allah." The two verses together close the circle: the vice is not in the amount of spending but in the heart behind it — withholding what is owed (37) and giving for reputation (38) are both forms of the same idolatry of self.

The key phrase is *riʾāʾ al-nās* — "to be seen by the people" — the root *riʾā* (ostentation, showing off), the opposite of *ikhlāṣ* (sincerity). Al-Jalalayn identifies the subjects: "the likes of the hypocrites and the Meccans." Al-Muntakhab: "those who spend of their wealth ostentatiously under the garb of benevolence only to attract notice and to win the admiration of people while in fact they believe neither in Allah nor in the Last Day." Ibn 'Abbas (Tanwir al-Miqbās, as Ibn Kathir's page presents it): "the Jewish leaders who spend their wealth in order to be seen of men — by showing off to people so that they say about them that they follow the practice of Abraham and that they give and spend of their money — and believe not in Allah (Muhammad or the Qur'an) nor the Last Day." The verse therefore covers both hypocrisy and outright disbelief: the internal hypocrite who performs religion for the crowd, and the external denier who performs charity for the crowd; the common denominator is the audience — people — instead of Allah.

The hadith of the three who enter the Fire first — recorded by Ibn Kathir and in the authentic Sunna — gives the doctrine its sharpest edge: the first three on whom the Fire feeds are a scholar, a fighter, and a spender, each of whom did his deed for reputation. The wealthy says: "I did not leave any area that You like to be spent on, but I spent on it in Your cause." Allah says: "You lie — you only did that so that it is said, 'He is generous.' And it was said." Ibn Kathir's comment: "meaning, you acquired your reward in the life, and this is indeed what you sought with your action." The verse's own logic is the same: the one who spent for people was paid by people; nothing remains for the Hereafter because nothing was intended for it.

The clause "and believe not in Allah or the Last Day" is the verse's theological root. Ibn Kathir: "it is Shaytan who lured them to commit this evil action, instead of performing the good deed as it should be performed. Shaytan encouraged, excited and lured them by making the evil appear good." And the closing statement is the verdict on the relationship: *wa-man yakun al-shayṭānu lahu qarīnan fa-sāʾa qarīnan* — "and whoever takes Satan as a companion — what an evil companion." Al-Jalalayn: "whoever has Satan for a comrade, for a companion whose command he follows as these do, then an evil comrade has he." The companion (*qarīn*) is not a casual association; it is the one who accompanies, advises, and directs — and the Qur'an's closing exclamation is almost a shudder: what an evil friend to have chosen. Al-Muntakhab: "he who associates himself with the Devil has in fact espoused evil — and evil indeed is the companion."

For the modern reader, the verse addresses the most pervasive religious disease: performance. The believer who gives, prays, or serves while scanning for the audience has made the audience his god, and the verse's verdict on that choice is the same in every age: the applause is the reward, and the reward is over. The verse also corrects the opposite error — the idea that the size of the giving is the measure of faith; the Qur'an asks not how much but *for whom*. And the final image — Satan as a *qarīn* — is the Qur'an's warning about the company of the heart: every deed is done in the company of someone, and the one who chooses the audience of people has chosen the companion of Satan.

## Verse 39

Verse 39 says: *What harm could have come to them if they had believed in Allah and the Last Day and donated from what Allah has provided for them? And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of them.*

In simple terms, the verse turns the censure of 4:38 into an appeal: what harm would it have done them — these ostentatious spenders — if they had believed in Allah and the Last Day and given from what Allah provided? The answer is in the question: no harm at all; only good. And Allah knows them completely.

The verse is a rhetorical question with the answer built into it. Ibn Kathir's frame: "what harm would it cause them if they believe in Allah, go on the righteous path, replace showing off with sincerity, have faith in Allah, and await His promise in the Hereafter, for those who do good and spend what He has given them on what He likes and is pleased with?" The Arabic *mādhā ʿalayhim* — "what is against them?" or "what harm would it be to them?" — is the language of persuasion: the Qur'an is not presenting the alternative as a sacrifice but as a discovery. The spenders are asked to see that the only thing they were giving up by believing was their false audience, and the only thing they were gaining was the true Recipient.

The verse's threefold alternative is precise: *āmanū billāh* — faith in Allah (the answer to the show-off's empty belief); *wal-yawm al-ākhir* — faith in the Last Day (the answer to their short-horizon calculation — the one who believes in the Day knows the true ledger, and the verse's whole punishment/forgiveness frame depends on it); *wa-anfaqū mimmā razaqahum Allāh* — and spent from what Allah provided them (the answer to their ostentation: the same spending, redirected from people to Allah).

The verse also contains a subtle mercy. It does not say "they have lost everything" and leave it; it leaves the door open in the form of a question — the door that the previous verses, with their threats of punishment and their identification of the evil companion, had closed in appearance. The rhetorical question is the Qur'an's way of inviting the one who hears it to reconsider: even the spender who has been spending for the crowd can change the direction of his spending and the object of his belief. Ibn Kathir's note on the verse's closing words completes the picture: *wa-kāna Allāhu bihim ʿalīman* — "He has perfect knowledge of their intentions, whether good or evil. Indeed, Allah knows those who deserve success, and He grants them success and guidance, directing them to perform righteous actions that will earn them His pleasure. He also knows those who deserve failure and expulsion from His great mercy." The verse's last word is therefore both a warning and a guarantee: no one can hide the true intention, and no true intention is lost.

Structurally, 4:37–39 form one complete moral unit across three verses: the miser withholds and conceals (37); the show-off spends and exhibits (38); both are asked the same question (39) — what would it have cost you to believe and to give for the right reason? The two vices are mirrored: one hides Allah's bounty, the other displays his own generosity; one withholds the right, the other spends the right for the wrong. And the answer to both is the same: faith in Allah and the Last Day, and giving from what Allah provided.

For the modern reader, the verse is a mirror for self-examination. The believer who gives must ask: is my giving for the face of Allah or for the faces of people? The believer who does not give must ask: am I concealing the bounty, or is my withholding founded on need? And the verse's rhetorical method is a model of Quranic persuasion: rather than condemning and closing, it asks a question that the reader must answer with his own heart — what harm could have come to you, if you had believed and given?

## Verse 40

Verse 40 says: *Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight. And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace.*

In simple terms, the verse establishes the justice and generosity of Allah's accounting: He never wrongs anyone — not even by the weight of an atom. And beyond not wronging, He multiplies good deeds many times and adds a great reward from His own grace.

The verse's first half states the *absolute justice* of Allah's reckoning. *Inna Allāha lā yaẓlimu mithqāla dharratin* — "Allah does not wrong even the weight of an atom." Al-Jalalayn explains the particle: "the weight of the smallest ant — by diminishing thereby a person's good deeds or increasing thereby his evil deeds." The atom (*dharrah*) is the smallest measure in the Arabic imagination — the dust mote in the light, the wing of a fly — and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly to stress the precision of the scale (99:6–8: "whosoever does good equal to the weight of a speck of dust shall see it"). Ibn Kathir's frame: "Allah states that He does not treat any of His servants with injustice on the Day of Resurrection, be it the weight of a mustard seed or a speck of dust." The doctrine is the ground of the believer's trust: no good is lost, no evil is added beyond its due, and the accounting is exact. Ibn Kathir also connects the verse to the intercession hadith of Abu Sa'id Al-Khudri in the Two Sahihs: Allah says, "Go back and take out of the Fire everyone in whose heart you find the weight of a mustard seed of faith," and in another narration "whosoever had the least, least, least speck of faith"; Abu Sa'id then said, "Read, if you will: 'Surely Allah wrongs not even of the weight of a speck of dust.'" The verse's precision is therefore a doctrine of hope: even the smallest good is not lost.

The second half states the *generosity beyond justice*. *Wa-in taku ḥasanatan yuḍāʿifhā* — "and if it is a good deed, He multiplies it." Al-Jalalayn notes the readings *yuḍāʿifuhā* / *yuḍaʿʿifuhā* and the range: "from ten times up to more than seven hundred times." The multiplication is not a mechanical formula; the Qur'an elsewhere specifies ten-fold for its general rule (6:160) and the countless-fold for those who spend and give (2:261: a seed that yields seven ears, each with a hundred grains — seven hundred-fold). The verse's final phrase is *min ladunhu ajran ʿaẓīman* — "a great reward from Him/from His own presence." Ibn Kathir reports from Abu Hurayrah, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, and Ad-Dahhak that the "great reward" is Paradise. Ibn Abi Hatim records the famous report of Abu 'Uthman An-Nahdi, who travelled to Hajj to question Abu Hurayrah about the claim that he had said "Allah rewards the good deed with a million deeds" — and Abu Hurayrah affirmed he had heard the Prophet say "with two million good deeds," reciting 9:38 ("Little is the enjoyment of the life of this world as compared to the Hereafter."). Ibn Kathir presents the report with the questioner's own caution and the two-number variants, and the reader is best served by the verse's own frame: the exact number is not the point; the point is that the multiplication is beyond estimation and the reward is a gift. Al-Jalalayn: "a great wage that no one can estimate."

There is a further dimension the commentators draw from the verse's two halves. Justice (no wronging) is the *minimum* the creature could expect; generosity (multiplication and grace) is what the creature actually receives. The verse's structure is therefore an argument for hope: if the scale were merely just, the believer with more sins than goods would have no hope; but the scale is biased — as the hadiths say, it is tilted toward mercy — because the multiplication of good and the reward "from His own presence" are acts of *faḍl* (grace), not of *ḥaqq* (right). Ibn Kathir's citation of Sa'id bin Jubayr on the disbeliever (whose punishment is lightened but who never leaves the Fire, as Abu Talib's shallow area in Hell-fire — "were it not for me he would have been in the deepest depths") and Anas's hadith (the believer is rewarded in this life and the Hereafter; the disbeliever is fed in this world, and on the Day has no good deed) sharpen the difference: the reward of 4:40 belongs to the believer's account.

For the modern reader, the verse is one of the Qur'an's most powerful assurances about the afterlife. The person who believes his small deeds are worthless is answered: the atom is weighed. The person who believes his sins are too many is answered: the multiplication. And the person who doubts whether his efforts matter in a vast universe is answered: nothing is lost, everything is recorded, and the reward is not the wage of a transaction but the gift of a gracious Lord. The verse also functions as the closing frame of the passage 4:29–40: after the prohibitions of unlawful wealth and killing (29–30), the promise of absolution for the avoidance of major sins (31), the direction to ask Allah's bounty (32), the law of kinship and covenant inheritance (33), the order of family authority and equity (34–35), the ladder of moral kindness (36–37), the censure of miserliness and ostentation (37–38), the invitation to faith and giving (39), and now the final assurance: the One who imposes the law is the One who never wrongs by an atom's weight and multiplies the good beyond measure. The law is severe in its prohibitions and generous in its reward, because its Author is just and gracious, and both attributes are the believer's security.
