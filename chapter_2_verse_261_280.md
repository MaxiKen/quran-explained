## Verse 261

Verse 261 says: *"The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the parable of the spending in Allah's cause: a grain that grows into seven ears, each with a hundred grains — the reward multiplied by Allah's will — and He is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.

"The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains" — the verse's parable: the grain and the seven hundred. Ibn Kathir: this is a parable Allah made of the multiplication of rewards for those who spend in His cause, seeking His pleasure — Allah multiplies the good deed ten to seven hundred times. The verse's frame: the spending scaled as the seed: the small seed becomes the big harvest — the charity is the seed of the Hereafter. The verse's *fi sabil Allah* — in the cause of Allah — the seeking of His pleasure, not the praise of people: the cause is the field.

"And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills" — the verse's multiplication: the will beyond the seven hundred. Ibn Kathir: Allah gives manifold increase to whom He wills — the seven hundred is the measure's example; the multiplication is the grace's measure: beyond the countable, by the will.

"For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing" — the verse's close: the All-Bountiful (Wasi') — the vast bounty — the All-Knowing ('Alim) — the knowledge of the spender and the deed. Ibn Kathir: Allah is All-Sufficient for His creatures' needs, All-Knower.

The verse's place: after the command to lend to Allah (2:245) — the parable of the interest's counterpart: the charitable spending multiplies where the greed of the hoarding does not. The verse's frame: the believer's economics: the expenditure is the investment; the seven-hundred-fold is the divine yield.

The verse's lesson: the charity is the seed; the reward is the harvest; the multiplication is Allah's will. And the reader's part: give — for the giving is the planting, and the crop is with the All-Bountiful, the All-Knowing.

## Verse 262

Verse 262 says: *"Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words—they will get their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, the verse rules on the charity's purity: the spenders who do not follow their giving with reminders of the favor or hurtful words — their reward is with their Lord; no fear for them, nor grief.

"Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words" — the verse's two prohibitions: the mann — the reminder of the favor — and the adha — the injury, the hurtful word. Ibn Kathir: Allah praises those who spend from their money in His cause and then refrain from reminding those who received the charity of that fact — whether these hints take the form of words or actions; "or with injury" indicates that they do not cause harm to those to whom they gave the charity — "for this harm will only annul the charity." The verse's frame: the charity is not the deed only; the charity is the deed plus the silence — the giving without the branding.

"they will get their reward from their Lord" — the verse's reward: from their Lord — the reward of the righteousness. Ibn Kathir: their reward is with their Lord — indicating that Allah Himself will reward them for these righteous actions. The verse's frame: the reward with the Lord — not the people's praise, not the recipient's gratitude: the Lord's account.

"and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" — the verse's peace: the no-fear of the Day of Resurrection's horrors, the no-grief for what they leave behind. Ibn Kathir: no fear regarding the horrors of the Day of Resurrection, nor shall they grieve regarding the offspring they leave behind and the adornment and delights of this world — they will not feel sorry, because they will acquire what is far better for them.

The verse's companion hadith (Muslim, from Abu Dharr): the Prophet said — three persons whom Allah shall neither speak to on the Day of Resurrection, nor look at, nor purify — and they shall receive a painful torment: he who reminds people of what he gives away, he who lengthens his clothes below the ankles, and he who swears an oath while lying to sell his merchandise (recorded by Ibn Kathir in the commentary of 2:262–264). The verse's frame: the mann is the belittling of the gift — the reminder erases the deed's face.

The verse's lesson: give — and then let it go: the reward is with the Lord, and the reminder and the injury are the charity's annulment. And the reader's part: the pure charity is the generous hand and the silent tongue.

## Verse 263

Verse 263 says: *"Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing."*

In simple terms, the verse compares the two: a kind word and forgiveness are better than a charity followed by injury — and Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing.

"Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury" — the verse's comparison: the qawl ma'ruf — the kind word, the compassionate address — and the maghfirah — the forgiveness — better than the sadaqah followed by the adha. Ibn Kathir: "Kind words" — compassionate words and a supplication for Muslims — "and forgiving" — forgiving an injustice that took the form of actions or words — are better than charity followed by injury. The verse's frame: the word is a deed; the forgiveness is a charity — the kind refusal is better than the hurtful gift.

"And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing" — the verse's close: the Ghani — the Rich, free of all needs — the Halim — the Most Forbearing, who forgives, releases, and pardons. Ibn Kathir: Allah is Rich — not needing His creation — Most Forbearing — forgives, releases and pardons them. The verse's frame: the Self-Sufficient does not need your charity's interest — and the Most Forbearing's forgiveness is the model of your forgiveness: He forgives the injury, so forgive the injury of the asker.

Maududi (Tafhim): it is a thousand times better to offer some reasonable and appropriate excuse to the person who asks, if the giver feels unable to give — and if the asker's impolite behavior angers the giver, it is also a thousand times better to forgive than to give charity followed by pain. The verse's frame: the dignity of the asker is the charity's part — the refusal with the kind word preserves the honor that the hurtful gift destroys.

The verse's lesson: the kindness of the word and the forgiveness of the fault are the charity's greater part. And the reader's part: when you cannot give, give the kind word — and when you are wronged, forgive — the Self-Sufficient's bounty and the Most Forbearing's pardon are the model.

## Verse 264

Verse 264 says: *"O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people."*

In simple terms, the verse warns against nullifying charity by reminders and injury — like the show-offs who donate to be seen and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day; their example is the barren rock: the rain washes away the thin soil, leaving it bare — no reward preserved — and Allah does not guide the disbelieving.

"O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words" — the verse's command: the la tubtilu — do not nullify, do not waste — the sadaqah with the mann and the adha. Ibn Kathir: "Do not render in vain your sadaqah by reminders of your generosity or by injury" — the charity will be rendered in vain if it is followed by harm or reminders — in this case, the reward of giving away charity is not sufficient enough to nullify the harm and reminders. The verse's frame: the annulment: the given gift is erased by the reminder — the repeated "I gave you" is the charity's cancellation.

"like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day" — the verse's likeness: the riya' — the showing off — the one who spends to be seen of men. Ibn Kathir: "like him who spends his wealth to be seen of men" — do not nullify your acts of charity by following them with reminders and harm, just like the charity of those who give it to show off to people — the boasting person pretends to give away charity for Allah's sake, but in reality seeks people's praise and the reputation of being kind or generous, or other material gains of this life — all the while he does not think about Allah or gaining His pleasure and generous rewards. Ad-Dahhak: the example fits one who follows his acts of charity with reminders or harm. The verse's frame: the show-off's charity is the display, not the deed — the appearance of the giving, the absence of the belief.

"Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone" — the verse's image: the safwan — the smooth rock — with a little dust, the heavy rain leaving it bare. Ibn Kathir: heavy rain left the rock completely barren of dust — such is the case with Allah's action regarding the work of those who show off — their deeds are bound to vanish and disappear, even though people think these deeds are as plentiful as specks of dust. The verse's frame: the thin soil over the stone: the appearance of the garden in the dust — the rain (the exposure, the testing) reveals the stone — the earth's layer was never the root.

"Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity" — the verse's loss: la yaqdirun — they are not able, they can do nothing with what they have earned. The verse's frame: the reward's preservation: the earned reward scattered by the rain of the exposure — the show-off's harvest is the bare stone.

"Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people" — the verse's close: the guidance of the disbelieving — withheld. Ibn Kathir: Allah does not guide the disbelieving people.

The verse's lesson: the reminder and the injury are the charity's ruin; the showing off is the deed's emptiness; the reward is preserved by the sincerity. And the reader's part: give in secret from the eye — the soil that the rain washes is the appearance; the root that the rain feeds is the sincerity.

## Verse 265

Verse 265 says: *"And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah's pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the counterpart example: the donors who seek Allah's pleasure and are certain of the reward — their spending is like a garden on a fertile hill: the heavy rain doubles the produce; the drizzle alone suffices — and Allah is All-Seeing.

"And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah's pleasure and believing the reward is certain" — the verse's subject: the ibtigha' mardat Allah — the seeking of Allah's pleasure — and the tathbit — the certainty, the assurance of the reward. Ibn Kathir (with 2:265): the garden on the height — the believer's spending with sincerity and certainty. The verse's frame: the two conditions the show-off lacks: the seeking of the pleasure (not the praise) and the certainty of the reward (not the doubt): the garden of the sincere.

"is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce" — the verse's image: the rabwah — the elevated, fertile ground — struck by the wabil — the heavy rain — yielding the double produce. Ibn Kathir: "Wabil falls on it" — heavy rain — so it produces its yield of harvest — fruits, produce — doubled as compared to other gardens. The verse's frame: the elevation and the rain: the secure ground (the faith's certainty) and the abundant watering (the reward's rain): the double yield of the sincere.

"If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient" — the verse's second measure: the tall — the drizzle, the light rain. Ibn Kathir: ad-Dahhak said the tall is light rain — the ayah indicates that the garden on the rabwah is always fertile — for if heavy rain does not fall on it, light rain will suffice for it. The verse's frame: the little giving and the great giving both suffice — the believer's spending, whether small or great, is multiplied (Tanwir, Ibn 'Abbas): the sincere little is the garden's drizzle; the sincere great is the garden's downpour.

"And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do" — the verse's close: the All-Seer — the seeing of the sincerity and the deed. The verse's frame: the seen by the Seer: the hidden intention, the private giving — the All-Seeing knows the certainty of the heart that the people cannot see.

The verse's lesson: the two parables side by side (2:264 and 2:265): the bare rock of the show-off and the fertile garden of the sincere — the same rain, the two yields. Maududi (Tafhim): the contrast of the two conditions — the show-off's deeds wash away; the sincere's garden bears twice. And the reader's part: plant on the high ground of the certainty — the reward's rain will come — and the drizzle of the small giving will suffice.

## Verse 266

Verse 266 says: *"Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up? This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect."*

In simple terms, the verse poses the heart-searching question: would you like a flourishing garden — palms, vines, every fruit, rivers beneath — and then, in your old age with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind burns it all? So Allah makes the revelations clear — so that you may reflect.

"Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath" — the verse's picture: the complete garden — the palms, the vines, the fruits of every kind, the rivers. Ibn Kathir: the garden with the date palms and vines, rivers flowing underneath, all kinds of fruits — the full wealth. The verse's frame: the picture of the complete blessing: the garden at its best, the wealth at its summit.

"and as they grow very old with dependent children" — the verse's moment: the old age and the weak offspring. Ibn Kathir (from al-'Awfi, from Ibn 'Abbas, via Ibn Abi Hatim): he lost all this in his old age — while his offspring and children are weak, just before the end of his life. The verse's frame: the neediest moment: the one who owns the garden is the one who needs it most — the old age's weakness, the children's dependence: the time when the loss is the ruin.

"a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up?" — the verse's catastrophe: the i'sar — the whirlwind — the wind with the fire — the burnt garden. Ibn Kathir: "struck with a whirlwind" — with heavy wind — that is fiery, so that it is burnt — its fruits were burnt and its trees were destroyed — "therefore, what will his condition be like?" The verse's frame: the whirlwind of the loss: the complete blessing burnt by the wind — the question's point: no one wishes this for the garden of the world.

"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect" — the verse's close: the clarification and the reflection. Ibn Kathir (from Ibn 'Abbas): Allah has set a good parable, and all His parables are good — this is the condition of the disbeliever on the Day of Resurrection when he returns to Allah — he will not have any good deeds to provide an excuse or refuge for him, just as the man in the parable had no strength to replant the garden — and he will not find anything to resort to for help, just as the offspring of the man in the parable did not provide him with help — so he will be deprived of his reward when he most needs it, just as the man in the parable was deprived of his garden when he most needed it. The verse's frame: the parable of the nullified deeds: the charity destroyed by the mann and the adha (2:264) is the garden burnt at the old age — the reward lost at the need.

Ibn Kathir's heading: "The Example of Evil Deeds Nullifying Good Deeds" — the verse's aim: the deeds of the disbeliever and the show-off are the burnt garden. Ma'arif: spending in charity against given conditions is like hoping to be investing profusely in the Hereafter — but with Allah this investment comes to naught.

The verse's lesson: the reward's loss at the need is the worst loss — the good deeds preserved are the garden guarded against the whirlwind. And the reader's part: reflect — the garden you guard is the deeds you keep; the whirlwind of the nullification is the reminder, the injury, the show-off.

The verse's lesson: the stinginess is the devil's bidding; the spending is the trust in the promise. And the reader's part: when the whisper of the poverty comes with the giving — answer with the promise: the forgiveness and the bounty are Allah's, and His bounty is wider than the poverty's fear.

## Verse 271

Verse 271 says: *"To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse rules on the charity's manner: to give openly is good; to give to the poor secretly is better — and it will absolve you of sins — and Allah is All-Aware.

"To give charity publicly is good" — the verse's openness: the in tubdu — if you disclose — the sadaqat — the charities — "it is well" — the good — the public giving has its good. Ibn Kathir: "If you disclose the charity, it is good" — the public charity is good — with the note: if there is an apparent wisdom behind disclosing the charity, such as the people imitating this righteous act, then disclosing it becomes better than concealing it (in the virtue's balance). The verse's frame: the public giving is not forbidden: the good of the good — the encouragement, the example, the public good.

"but to give to the poor privately is better for you" — the verse's preference: the concealment — the giving to the poor in secret — the better for you. Ibn Kathir: "if you conceal them and give them to the poor, that is better for you" — this indicates that concealing charity is better than disclosing it — because it protects one from showing off and boasting. Ma'arif: the secrecy has the religious merits — it is far from the possibility of riya', more graceful for the receiver who can feel shy about receiving charity in public, and beneficial because it does not disclose the amount of one's wealth. Maududi (note 311): if the charity is of an obligatory nature, it is preferable to dispense it openly; non-obligatory charity should preferably be dispensed secretly — the principle: obligatory acts openly, non-obligatory acts of goodness secretly. The verse's frame: the better: the private giving — the protection of the giver and the dignity of the receiver.

"and will absolve you of your sins" — the verse's absolution: the yukaffir — the expiation — the removal of the sins. Ibn Kathir (and Ma'arif): "This will write off part of your sins" — the expiation is the result of the charity — with the note (Ma'arif): it does not mean the expiation of evil deeds is confined to the charity made in secret only — in fact, a charity made in public also carries the same benefit — but the reference to expiation here is in the context that even if someone feels a secret charity is useless in this world, he should not feel depressed — Allah will forgive his sins, and that is a great gain for him. The verse's frame: the secret's wage: the removal of the sins — the hidden charity's hidden reward.

"And Allah is All-Aware of what you do" — the verse's close: the Khabir — the All-Aware. The verse's frame: the All-Aware of the public and the secret — the awareness that makes the secret giving known to the Lord: the secrecy from the people is the openness to Allah.

The verse's lesson: give — the open is good and the secret is better; the secret absolves the sins and guards the honor. And the reader's part: the charity of the night is the sin's eraser — give the open when the open teaches, give the secret when the hand should not be seen.

## Verse 272

Verse 272 says: *"You are not responsible for people's guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills. Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah. Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged."*

In simple terms, the verse frees the Prophet of the guidance's responsibility — it is Allah who guides whom He wills — and teaches: whatever you spend in charity is for your own good, seeking Allah's pleasure — and it will be repaid in full, and you will not be wronged.

"You are not responsible for people's guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills" — the verse's release: the laysa 'alayka hudahum — the guidance is not upon you. Ibn Kathir: you are not responsible for their guidance — Allah guides whom He wills. Maududi (Tafhim, note 313): in the beginning Muslims tended to hesitate in helping either their non-Muslim relatives or other non-Muslims who were in need — they thought that helping Muslims only constituted "spending in the way of Allah" — this verse rejects this attitude: the Muslims are not responsible for forcing true guidance down the throats of people; conveying the message of truth to people absolves them of the obligation — it is for God either to favor the recipients of the message with true perception or not — and Muslims should not shrink from helping their relatives in the affairs of the world on the ground that they are not following the true guidance — they will be rewarded by God for whatever help they render to needy persons for the sake of God. The verse's frame: the guidance and the giving are separated: the Prophet's task is the message, not the compulsion; the helper's task is the help, not the conversion: the giving is judged by the giver's intention.

"Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good" — the verse's benefit: the li-anfusikum — for your own selves. Ibn Kathir: whatever you spend in charity is to your own benefit. The verse's frame: the spent wealth is not the loss: the charity is the self's good: the seed of the self's reward.

"as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah" — the verse's condition: the ibtigha' wajh Allah — the seeking of Allah's face. Ibn Kathir: you spend merely to please Allah. The verse's frame: the condition of the benefit: the seeking of the Face: the help to the needy relative who does not believe — the help seeking Allah's pleasure, not the convert's thanks.

"Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged" — the verse's repayment: the yuwaffa — the full payment — the la tuzlamun — the no wrong. Ibn Kathir: whatever you spend will be repaid to you in full and you shall not be wronged. Maududi: they will be rewarded by God for whatever help they render to needy persons for the sake of God. The verse's frame: the full payment from the One who does not wrong: the charity's wage is the complete, the unjust is absent: the recompense's justice.

The verse's lesson: guide whom you can, give to whom you can — the guidance is Allah's, the giving is yours: the seeking of the Face makes the giving the self's good. And the reader's part: never withhold the help by the religion of the helped — the return is from the One whose pleasure is the seeker's aim — and the payment is in full.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 273

Verse 273 says: *"˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg. You can recognize them by their appearance. They do not beg people persistently. Whatever you give in charity is certainly well known to Allah."*

In simple terms, the verse directs the charity to the needy who are occupied in the cause of Allah and cannot travel for work: the stranger thinks them rich because they do not beg — you know them by their appearance; they do not ask people persistently — and whatever you give, Allah knows it.

"˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺" — the verse's recipients: the fuqara' — the needy — the uhsurū fi sabil Allah — the confined in the cause of Allah, unable to travel. Qurtubi (via Ma'arif): the word al-fuqara' — the needy who need support for their physical sustenance — covers all those who cannot engage in other jobs because of their religious preoccupation. The verse's frame: the hidden poor: the ones occupied in the cause — the scholars, the learners, the mujahid, the devoted — their work is the cause, and the cause is their livelihood's block.

"Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg" — the verse's misjudgment: the ignorant — the one who does not know their situation — supposes them free of need — because of their abstinence. Ma'arif: an ignorant person takes them as free of need because of their abstinence. The verse's frame: the dignity that hides the need: the one who does not ask is mistaken for the one who has — the self-respect is the poor's mask.

"You can recognize them by their appearance" — the verse's recognition: the ta'rifuhum bi-simahum — the marks: the appearance's signs. The verse's frame: the knowers read the poverty in the face — the trace of the need, the modesty of the dress, the worn state — the signs the asker-free poor cannot hide.

"They do not beg people persistently" — the verse's abstinence: the la yas'aluna al-nas ilhafan. Qurtubi (via Ma'arif): on the surface, the verse gives the sense that they do not solicit importunately — but in accordance with the consensus of commentators, it means they just do not ask — they totally refrain from asking. The verse's frame: the begging's absence is the dignity's price: the helped must be sought out: the charity is the seeker's duty when the asker's tongue is silent.

"Whatever you give in charity is certainly well known to Allah" — the verse's close: the All-Aware: the 'alim — the knowing. The verse's frame: the given to the hidden poor — the one who does not ask — is known to Allah: the hidden need and the hidden giving meet in the divine knowledge.

The verse's lesson: the charity's worthiest is the dignity that does not ask — seek out the hidden poor; the appearance's signs and the silence of the asking are their marks. And the reader's part: the one you think has enough may be the one who is confined in the cause — give to the silent, for the known and the hidden are both in Allah's knowledge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 274

Verse 274 says: *"Those who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly—their reward is with their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, the verse commends the constant spenders: day and night, secretly and openly — their reward is with their Lord; no fear for them, nor grief.

"Those who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly" — the verse's spenders: the all-times, the all-manners. Ibn Kathir: those who spend their wealth — day and night — secretly and openly — the complete giving: the time's constancy and the manner's both. The verse's frame: the day and the night — the constancy of the giving; the secret and the open — the wideness of the manner: the giving is not the occasion's flash, it is the life's habit.

"their reward is with their Lord" — the verse's reward: the ajruhum 'inda Rabbihim — the reward with their Lord. The verse's frame: the reward with the Lord: the accounting of the day-and-night, the secret-and-open — the ledger that men cannot see, the Lord who records all.

"and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" — the verse's peace: the no-fear, the no-grief. The verse's frame: the same promise of 2:262 and 2:277: the no-fear of the Day, the no-grief for the left — the constant giver's constant peace.

The verse's lesson: the giving's completeness: the everywhere and the always. Ibn Kathir (in the verse's flow): the charity's reward is with the Lord; the constant spenders are the peaceful. And the reader's part: give in the day and in the night, in the open and in the secret — the reward is with the Lord, and the fear and the grief are the givers' distance.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 275

Verse 275 says: *"Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan's touch. That is because they say, "Trade is no different than interest." But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest. Whoever refrains—after having received warning from their Lord —may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah. As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."*

In simple terms, the verse rules on the interest (riba): its consumers will rise on Judgment Day like the one driven mad by Satan's touch, because they say trade is like interest — but Allah permitted trade and forbade interest; whoever desists after the warning keeps the past gains and the case is Allah's; those who persist are the Fire's residents forever.

"Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan's touch" — the verse's condition: the akilun al-riba — the consumers of the riba — rising like the one beaten by the devil into madness. Ibn Kathir: "Those who eat riba will not stand (on the Day of Resurrection) except like the standing of a person beaten by Shaytan leading him to insanity" — the description of the rising: the madness' staggering. The verse's frame: the rise's condition: the resurrection's balance — the one who consumed the interest rises staggering like the maddened: the punishment mirrors the intoxication of the greed.

"That is because they say, "Trade is no different than interest." " — the verse's claim: the innama al-bay' mithl al-riba. Ibn Kathir: "That is because they say: 'Trading is only like riba'" — indicates that the disbelievers claimed that riba was allowed due to the fact that they rejected Allah's commandments — not that they equated riba with regular trade. Ma'arif: the disbelievers did not recognize that Allah allowed trade in the Qur'an — for if they did, they would have said "riba is trade" — rather, they said "trading is only like riba" — meaning, they are similar, so why did Allah allow this but not that — they asked in defiance of Allah's command. Tanwir (Ibn 'Abbas): the increase at the end of selling once the term is due is like the increase at the beginning of the transaction when the sale is deferred. The verse's frame: the equation's defiance: the trade and the riba are not the same — the trade is the risk and the work; the riba is the surplus without the counter-value.

"But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest" — the verse's ruling: the halal of the bay' and the haram of the riba. Al-Jalalayn: riba is the excess levied in transactions of money or foodstuffs either on their value or on credit. Ibn Kathir: Allah has permitted trading and forbidden riba — the decisive ruling: the claim is answered by the Command: the difference is the legislation's wisdom (the trade's shared risk against the loan's guaranteed surplus). The verse's frame: the ruling of the Lord: the permitted and the forbidden: the Wisdom's line between the trade and the usury.

"Whoever refrains—after having received warning from their Lord—may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah" — the verse's mercy: the man jaa'ahu maw'izah — the admonition — the intaha — the desists — the lahu ma salafa — the past gains — the amruhu ila Allah — the case to Allah. Al-Jalalayn: he shall have his past gains — those made before the prohibition and which cannot be reclaimed from him — and his affair, with regard to pardoning him, is committed to God. Ibn Kathir: "So whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and stops eating riba shall not be punished for the past; his case is for Allah (to judge)" — meaning those who have knowledge that Allah made usury unlawful. The verse's frame: the mercy's turn: the past before the warning is not reclaimed; the case after the desisting is committed to the Forgiver.

"As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever" — the verse's end: the 'ada — the return — the ashab al-nar — the dwellers of the Fire — the khulud — the eternity. Ibn Kathir: but whoever returns (to riba) — such are the dwellers of the Fire — they will abide therein. The verse's frame: the persistence after the clear warning is the Fire's residence — the eternity of the consumption of the unjust increase.

Abu Dawud recorded from Jabir (Ibn Kathir's note): when the ayah of the consumers of riba was revealed, the Messenger of Allah recited it to the people... (the narration's beginning about the revelation's occasion). The verse's frame: the revelation's occasion and the warning's clarity.

The verse's lesson: the trade and the riba are not alike: the permitted and the forbidden are the Command's line; the warning is the mercy; the persistence is the Fire. And the reader's part: desist — the past is the warning's pardon, the persistence is the Fire's residence: take the warning for the case is committed to the Merciful.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 276

Verse 276 says: *"Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer."*

In simple terms, the verse states the divine law of the increase: Allah blights the interest and grows the charity — and He does not love the ungrateful evildoer.

"Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful" — the verse's two actions: the yamhaq — the blights, the destroys, the makes fruitless — the riba; the yurbi — the increases, the makes fruitful — the sadaqat. Ibn Kathir: Allah states that He destroys riba — either by removing this money from those who eat it, or by depriving them of the blessing, and thus the benefit of their money — because of their riba, Allah will torment them in this life and punish them for it on the Day of Resurrection. The verse's frame: the law of the increase: the interest's apparent growth is the blessing's removal; the charity's apparent loss is the increase's grant: the growth is in the measure of the Giver, not the count of the gain. Ibn Kathir (the companion hadith from the book of zakah): whoever gives in charity what equals a date from honest resources, and Allah only accepts that which is good and pure — Allah accepts it with His right (Hand) and raises it for its giver, just as one of you raises his animal — until it becomes as big as a mountain. And (the cross-reference): "And that which you give in gift (to others), in order that it may increase your wealth by expecting to get a better one in return from other people's property, has no increase with Allah" (30:39).

"And Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer" — the verse's close: the kaffar athim — the ungrateful, the evildoer. Ibn Kathir: Allah does not like every sinning disbeliever — the one who combats the law of the growth with the hunger of the increase — the ungrateful for the blessing, the persistent in the sin. The verse's frame: the displeasure's object: the ungrateful evildoer — the one who takes the interest and calls it the growth — the refusal of the Giver's law.

The companion hadith (Bukhari, Muslim): the Prophet said — abstain from the seven disasters — they asked, what are they, O Messenger of Allah? — he replied: to ascribe partners to Allah, to practice sorcery, to unjustly kill one whom Allah has declared inviolable, to take riba, to exploit the property of an orphan, to escape at the time of war, and to slander the chaste women who are believers, unwary (recorded by Ibn Kathir's commentary of 2:276). Muslim (and others): the Prophet cursed the one who consumes riba, the one who pays it, the one who writes it and the one who witnesses it — and said they are all alike. The verse's frame: the seven disasters and the curse's circle: the riba is the named disaster — the consuming, the paying, the writing, the witnessing are alike.

The verse's lesson: the blessing's law: what grows by Allah is blessed; what grows by the take is blighted. And the reader's part: the charity's small is the mountain in the Hand's raising; the interest's large is the fruitless — the ungrateful evildoer is not loved, and the blighting is the interest's end.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 277

Verse 277 says: *"Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax will receive their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, the verse names the believers' signs — the belief, the good deeds, the prayer, the alms-tax — and their reward from the Lord: no fear, no grief.

"Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax" — the verse's four: the iman — the belief — the 'amal al-salih — the good deeds — the iqam al-salat — the establishment of the prayer — the ita' al-zakat — the paying of the alms-tax. Ibn Kathir (in the verse's flow): after Allah mentioned the righteous believers who give charity, pay zakat — the verse's description of the believers' complete practice: the faith, the deeds, the prayer, the zakat — the pillars' living. The verse's frame: the charity's place: after the riba's prohibition — the believers' wealth-purification: the zakat's payment against the interest's consumption — the growth of the giving against the blight of the take.

"will receive their reward from their Lord" — the verse's reward: the ajrahum 'inda Rabbihim — the reward with their Lord. The verse's frame: the four-pillar believers' reward: with the Lord — the giver of the zakat's reward and the prayer's wage.

"and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" — the verse's peace: the no-fear, the no-grief — the same phrase of 2:262 and 2:274. The verse's frame: the believers' security: the no-fear of the Day's horror, the no-grief of the world's loss: the four signs, the two peaces.

The verse's lesson: the riba's opposite is the zakat: the believers' wealth is purified by the giving, established by the prayer — their reward is with the Lord. And the reader's part: believe, do good, pray, give the alms-tax — the reward is with the Lord, and the fear and the grief are the believers' distance.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 278

Verse 278 says: *"O believers! Fear Allah, and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺ believers."*

In simple terms, the verse commands: fear Allah and give up the outstanding interest — the remaining riba — if you are true believers.

"O believers! Fear Allah" — the verse's command: the ittaqu Allah — the fear of Allah. Ibn Kathir (with 2:279's heading): "O you who believe! Fear Allah and give up what remains of riba" — the address to the believers: the fear first — the command's root. The verse's frame: the fear of the Lord before the law of the wealth: the one who fears Allah leaves the forbidden increase.

"and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺ believers" — the verse's command: the dharu ma baqiya min al-riba — the give up what remains of the riba — the outstanding interest — the ma baqiya — the remaining: the sums still owed, the contracts still running. Ibn Kathir: the command to the believers to abandon the remaining riba — the desisting now, after the warning (2:275). The verse's frame: the "if you are true believers" — the condition's test: the true faith is the leaving: the belief that does not leave the interest is the claim without the proof — the faith is proven by the forsaking.

The verse's place: after the warning and the mercy (2:275), the blight and the growth (2:276), the believers' four signs (2:277) — the address: the fear, the leaving: the believers whose faith is challenged by the outstanding gain. The verse's frame: the "outstanding" — the ma baqiya — the existing balances: the command is not the past's reckoning but the present's leaving: the case of the past is Allah's (2:275); the remaining is the believer's leaving.

The verse's lesson: the true faith gives up the forbidden remaining. And the reader's part: fear Allah — the fear's proof is the leaving: the interest's outstanding is the faith's test: the true believer gives it up.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 279

Verse 279 says: *"If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger! But if you repent, you may retain your principal—neither inflicting nor suffering harm."*

In simple terms, the verse threatens the refusal with war from Allah and His Messenger; and the repentance's path: you keep your principal — neither wronging nor being wronged.

"If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger!" — the verse's threat: the harb — the war — with Allah and His Messenger. Ibn Kathir (the heading): "Riba Constitutes War Against Allah and His Messenger" — the ayah serves as a stern threat to those who continue to deal in riba after Allah revealed this warning. The verse's frame: the only sin with the war's threat: the one who continues the interest after the clear command is at war with the Lord Who blights the interest and the Messenger who forbade it: the war is the sword drawn against the one who refuses the leave.

"But if you repent, you may retain your principal" — the verse's mercy: the tubtum — the repent — the falakum ru'us amwalikum — the principal, the heads of your wealth — the capital is yours. Ibn Kathir: if you repent, you may retain your principal — the capital, without the increase. The verse's frame: the repentance's economics: the principal is the right; the increase is the war: the repentant keeps what is his own, and leaves what is the other's.

"neither inflicting nor suffering harm" — the verse's justice: the la tazlimun wa la tuzlamun — you neither wrong nor are wronged. Ibn Kathir (and the well-known hadith of the Farewell Pilgrimage): the Prophet said — the riba of the Jahiliyyah is abolished, and the first riba I abolish is the riba of al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib — and you may keep your principal — do not wrong and you will not be wronged. The verse's frame: the justice's balance: the lender does not take the increase (the wrongdoing) and the borrower does not suffer the unjust claim (the wronged): the principal's return is the fairness.

Ibn 'Abbas (reported in the commentary): regarding the verse, the one who consumed the riba will be told on the Day of Judgment: take your weapon — (i.e., the summons to the war he waged, which he cannot fight). The verse's frame: the war's day: the summons to the war with the One who cannot be fought.

The verse's lesson: the refusal after the command is the war; the repentance is the principal: the justice is the neither-wrong-nor-wronged. And the reader's part: leave the increase — the principal is yours and the increase is the war: repent and keep your own; wrong no one and no one will wrong you.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 280

Verse 280 says: *"If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease. And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you, if only you knew."*

In simple terms, the verse rules on the debtor's difficulty: postpone it until the ease — and the waiver of the debt as charity is better for you, if only you knew.

"If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease" — the verse's command: the in kana dhu 'usrah — if he is in difficulty — the fa-nazirah ila maysarah — the postponement until the ease. Ibn Kathir: "And if the debtor is having a hard time, then grant him time till it is easy for him to repay" — Allah commands creditors to be patient with debtors who are having a hard time financially. The verse's frame: the ease's wait: the debt's postponement is the command, not the favor: the grace to the debtor in the difficulty: the time until the maysarah — the ability, the ease.

"And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you" — the verse's better: the an tasaddaqu — the charity's waiver: the better for you. Ibn Kathir: "but if you remit it by way of charity, that is better for you if you did but know" — the waiver is the charity's better: the canceling of the debt is the sadaqah's best. The verse's frame: the two graces: the postponement is the mercy's minimum; the waiver is the mercy's full: the canceling of the owed is the charity whose growth the Lord increases (2:276).

"if only you knew" — the verse's close: the in kuntum ta'lamun — if you knew: the knowledge's appeal: if you knew the reward of the mercy, you would waive. The companion hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim, from Abu Hurayrah): a man used to lend money to people and would say to his employee: if the debtor is in difficulty, grant him respite — perhaps Allah will grant us respite — and when the man met Allah, Allah granted him respite (the hadith of the merciful creditor). The verse's frame: the mercy's reward: the mercy for the debtor is the mercy from Allah.

The verse's lesson: the debt's law is the mercy's law: the difficulty delays the claim; the waiver is the charity; the reward is better than the repayment. And the reader's part: when the debtor is in the difficulty, wait — and when you can, waive — the charity's waiver is the better for you, if only you knew.

- Ibn Kathir on 2:277 — the believers' four signs (belief, good deeds, prayer, zakat) and the reward with the Lord; no fear, no grief. https://quran.com/en/al-baqarah/277-282
- Ibn Kathir on 2:278–279 — "Riba Constitutes War Against Allah and His Messenger": the stern threat to those who continue after the warning; the repentance and the principal; "neither wronging nor being wronged"; the Farewell Pilgrimage announcement — the riba of the Jahiliyyah abolished, the first: the riba of al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib; Ibn 'Abbas — the summons on the Day of Judgment: "Take your weapon." https://quran.com/en/al-baqarah/278-282
- Ibn Kathir on 2:280 — "And if the debtor is having a hard time, then grant him time till it is easy for him to repay; but if you remit it by way of charity, that is better for you." https://www.alim.org/tafsir/ibn-kathir/2/
- Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim (Abu Hurayrah) — the hadith of the merciful creditor: the merchant who told his employee to grant the debtor in difficulty respite — "perhaps Allah will grant us respite" — and Allah granted him respite. https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-280-sora-2.html
