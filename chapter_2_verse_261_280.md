## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:261

> The example of those who spend their wealth in Allah’s cause is like a grain that grows seven ears, each ear bearing a hundred grains. Allah multiplies for whom He wills, and Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### A Seed That Multiplies

The Qur’an compares spending in Allah’s cause to a single grain that produces seven ears, with a hundred grains in each ear. The image begins with something small and hidden, then shows growth far beyond the original. Charity may seem to reduce a person’s immediate wealth, but Allah can place blessing in what remains and multiply the reward in ways no human calculation can contain.

The metaphor does not promise that every donation produces visible financial profit. It promises divine multiplication of reward, and that reward may include forgiveness, contentment, protection, benefit to descendants, social repair, and everlasting recompense. Allah’s accounting is wider than the balance sheet.

### Spending for Allah’s Cause

“Allah’s cause”—*sabīl Allāh*—includes every lawful effort that serves worship, justice, mercy, and human welfare. It includes zakāh, support for relatives and orphans, feeding the hungry, education, healthcare, refuge, debt relief, and legitimate defence of the oppressed. The cause must itself be lawful; giving money to aggression, terrorism, fraud, or corruption is not a righteous expenditure.

The giver should verify the organization, the purpose, and the likely benefit. Sincerity does not excuse negligence. A charity that cannot account for donations should not be trusted merely because it uses religious language.

### Allah Multiplies as He Wills

The closing words—*yuḍāʿifu liman yashāʾ*—show that multiplication is according to Allah’s wisdom. The amount is not the only measure. A small gift from a poor person may be greater than a large gift from wealth, especially when the sacrifice and sincerity are greater. A private donation may outweigh a public one made to gain reputation.

The Qur’an’s parable also protects the giver from pride. The seed grows by Allah’s permission. The servant plants and waters, but Allah creates the increase. He thanks Allah for enabling the charity and asks Him to accept it.

### The Conditions of a Good Gift

The reward is connected to the ethics of giving. Wealth should be lawful, the intention directed to Allah, and the recipient treated with dignity. Reminding people of a gift, insulting them, seeking control, or giving contaminated or worthless property can destroy the spiritual benefit, as the following verses explain.

A gift should meet a real need. Food should be edible, clothing suitable, and financial support delivered in a way that preserves privacy. A person does not give away what his dependants urgently require and then call the resulting neglect charity.

### The Seed of Social Repair

A single act of giving can produce many effects. Food restores strength, education opens opportunity, medical care preserves life, and debt relief frees a family from fear. Those helped may then help others. Like a seed, charity can grow through relationships and institutions beyond what the original giver sees.

This is why Islam joins personal generosity to public systems such as zakāh. Individual gifts express mercy, while organized distribution can address structural need. Both should be transparent and accountable.

### The All-Encompassing and Knowing Lord

Allah is *Wāsiʿ*, All-Encompassing in generosity, knowledge, and ability. His resources do not run out when servants give. He is *ʿAlīm*, knowing the giver’s intention, the recipient’s need, and every consequence of the gift. People may overlook quiet charity, but Allah does not.

The two names also teach balance. The giver does not demand that Allah multiply according to his schedule, and he does not despair when the worldly result is invisible. The seed may be growing in a realm he cannot yet see.

- Qur’an 2:245 and 2:261–274 on the good loan, charity, sincerity, and multiplication.
- Qur’an 2:215 and 59:9 on priorities of spending and preferring others.
- Qur’an 9:103 and 64:16 on charity as purification and spending according to ability.
- Qur’an 2:264–267 on avoiding reminders, injury, and inferior gifts.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that charity does not decrease wealth and the hadith on hidden charity and sincerity.
- For the seed parable, *sabīl Allāh*, multiplication, *wāsiʿ*, and *ʿalīm*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:261.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:262

> Those who spend their wealth in Allah’s cause and do not follow what they spend with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words will have their reward with their Lord. They will have no fear, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

### Charity Must Remain a Gift

This verse gives the conditions that preserve the reward of charity. The giver spends for Allah and does not follow the gift with *mann*, reminding the recipient of what was given, or *adhā*, words and actions that cause hurt. A donation can be physically helpful yet spiritually corrupted if the giver later turns it into a chain around the recipient’s neck.

“Did you not remember what I did for you?” “Without me you would have nothing.” “Everyone should know that I supported you.” Such words can destroy dignity and create dependence. The Qur’an calls believers to give in a way that leaves the recipient grateful to Allah rather than permanently humiliated before the donor.

### The Reward Is with Allah

The reward is “with their Lord.” People may forget the gift, reject the giver, or criticize the way it was offered. Allah does not lose the sincere act. The phrase also teaches the giver not to demand repayment from the recipient. He seeks his account with Allah, whose reward is safer and more generous than human gratitude.

This does not mean that a charity may be hidden when public reporting is needed or that a donor cannot keep lawful records. Transparency protects trust. The danger is publicity or reminder used to establish superiority and control rather than accountability.

### No Fear and No Grief

The promise of no fear and no grief belongs to the Hereafter and can also bring tranquillity in this life. The giver who trusts Allah is less fearful of poverty and less grieved when worldly recognition does not come. He knows that the real transaction has not been lost.

The promise does not guarantee that a generous person will never face anxiety or sadness. Believers can experience both while remaining sincere. It promises that Allah will protect them from the final fear and grief of abandonment when they meet Him.

### Respecting the Recipient

The recipient has dignity before he receives the gift. He is not a project for the donor’s image or a prop in a publicity campaign. A Muslim asks what form of assistance preserves privacy and independence. A cash transfer may be better than a public food distribution; a loan may be appropriate in one case and a grant in another; training may be more beneficial than a one-time payment.

The Qur’an’s guidance is adaptable, but the moral rule is firm: do not make a person pay emotionally for receiving help. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the upper hand is better than the lower hand while also protecting the recipient from humiliation.

### The Donor’s Intention

Sincerity—*ikhlāṣ*—is not a feeling that needs to be announced. A giver can examine whether he would still give if no one knew, whether he is angry when the recipient cannot praise him, and whether he uses charity to gain political or personal influence. If the intention becomes mixed, he renews it rather than abandoning all good.

Public charity can be good when it encourages others, funds a visible project, or fulfils a public duty. It should be accompanied by humility and accurate reporting, not by boasting or pressure.

### After-Gift Conduct

The verse also covers what happens months or years after a gift. A donor may later use past charity to demand obedience, win a family dispute, or silence criticism. Such conduct reveals that the gift was treated as a purchase. The recipient remains free to make lawful choices and offer advice; charity does not make him the donor’s property.

If hurtful words have already been spoken, repentance includes apologizing, stopping the reminders, and restoring the recipient’s dignity. Allah’s mercy remains open.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:261–264 on multiplication, sincere charity, reminders, and hurtful speech.
- Qur’an 2:215 and 76:8–9 on giving to those in need without seeking repayment or thanks.
- Qur’an 9:60 and 59:7 on recipients of zakāh and circulating wealth.
- Qur’an 2:274 and 92:18–21 on secret spending and seeking only Allah’s face.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on the seven shaded by Allah, including hidden charity, and the hadith on the upper hand and lower hand.
- For *mann*, *adhā*, *ikhlāṣ*, dignity, and the reward being with Allah, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:262.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:263

> A kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by hurt. Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing.

**Commentary:**

### Kindness Can Be Better than Money

A kind word—*qawl maʿrūf*—and forgiveness are better than charity that is followed by hurt. The verse does not diminish material assistance. A hungry person needs food and a debtor may need money. It teaches that the spiritual and moral manner of giving matters so much that an apparently generous gift can become worse than a respectful refusal if the gift humiliates and injures the recipient.

Sometimes a person cannot give financially. A gentle explanation, sincere apology, useful information, prayer, or referral to someone able to help can preserve dignity. The one who asks should not be mocked or treated as a burden. The one who cannot give should say so kindly rather than making promises he cannot keep.

### Forgiveness as a Gift

Forgiveness—*maghfirah*—can mean overlooking an offensive request, declining to shame someone who cannot repay, or forgiving a person’s manner while addressing the underlying need. It does not require surrendering a legitimate right or remaining in an unsafe situation. A victim can seek justice and still refuse the desire to humiliate the wrongdoer.

The pairing of kindness and forgiveness shows that charity is relational. Wealth can solve an immediate problem, but mercy can protect the person’s heart and future. The recipient should leave with hope rather than a new wound.

### Charity and Human Dignity

A person who gives may unconsciously assume superiority. The verse corrects this by reminding him that the recipient is not less valuable before Allah. The donor is being tested through wealth, while the recipient may be tested through need. The roles can reverse quickly, and both are servants of the same Lord.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah does not look at bodies or wealth but at hearts and deeds. This should shape charitable institutions: no degrading photographs, no public queues designed for spectacle, no insulting language, and no conditions that force people to praise the donor.

### Allah Is Self-Sufficient

Allah is *Ghaniyy*, Self-Sufficient. He does not need the charity, praise, or obedience of His servants. This name removes the donor’s illusion that he has done Allah a favour. Allah commands charity for the benefit of people and the purification of the giver; His dominion is not increased by a donation.

Because Allah is free of need, He can reward a small gift enormously. The giver is not competing with Allah or reducing divine wealth. He is using a provision that Allah entrusted to him.

### Allah Is Forbearing

Allah is *Ḥalīm*, Forbearing. He delays punishment for those who give arrogantly, injure recipients, or use His name for reputation. This forbearance is an opportunity to repent, not evidence that hurtful charity is acceptable. A person should correct his intention and conduct before the hidden damage becomes a pattern.

The name also teaches the giver to be patient with those he helps. A recipient may be slow to repay, anxious, disorganized, or unable to express gratitude. Patience does not excuse fraud, but it prevents a donor from turning assistance into constant complaint.

### Refusal with Grace

There are times when giving money would enable addiction, corruption, or dependence. A person can refuse while offering a safer alternative. A parent may pay a bill directly instead of handing over cash; a charity may provide treatment rather than fund destructive conduct. The refusal should still be kind and free of humiliation.

The verse’s ideal is not indiscriminate giving. It is beneficial assistance administered with wisdom, honesty, and mercy.

### The Social Culture of Iḥsān

A community guided by this āyah makes help accessible without forcing people to expose their poverty. It trains volunteers to speak respectfully, funds professional services, and ensures that those who ask are not punished for their need. It also teaches recipients that accepting help does not remove their agency or right to make lawful decisions.

Charity becomes *iḥsān* when the giver seeks Allah, the recipient is honoured, and the assistance produces genuine benefit.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:261–264 on charity, kind words, forgiveness, and hurtful giving.
- Qur’an 17:53 and 49:11–12 on good speech and avoiding humiliation and suspicion.
- Qur’an 76:8–9 and 92:18–21 on giving without seeking repayment or thanks.
- Qur’an 24:22 on forgiving and overlooking while continuing generosity.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that Allah looks at hearts and deeds rather than wealth and appearance; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim on gentle speech and generosity.
- For *qawl maʿrūf*, *maghfirah*, *Ghaniyy*, *Ḥalīm*, and the superiority of dignified kindness over injurious charity, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:263.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:264

> O believers, do not nullify your charity with reminders of your generosity and hurtful words, like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in Allah and the Last Day. His example is like a smooth stone covered with dust that is struck by heavy rain and left bare. They have no power over anything they earned, and Allah does not guide the disbelieving people.

**Commentary:**

### Charity Can Be Spiritually Cancelled

The verse warns believers not to destroy the reward of charity through *mann* and *adhā*: reminding recipients of the gift, demanding submission or praise, and hurting them with words. The physical donation may have helped for a moment, but the spiritual reward can be nullified when the giver turns it into a tool of humiliation.

This warning is directed to believers because even a person who begins with faith can corrupt an act through later behaviour. Sincerity is not secured merely by making the initial payment. The giver protects the charity after giving by remaining humble, avoiding reminders, and seeking Allah’s acceptance rather than the recipient’s dependence.

### Showing Off

The verse compares injurious charity with spending to be seen—*riyāʾ*. The person who gives for reputation may receive worldly praise, photographs, business connections, or political influence, but he has not prepared a lasting account with Allah. The problem is not that people happen to notice a good deed; the problem is making their notice the purpose of the deed.

Public charity can be valid and sometimes beneficial when it encourages others, reports a public trust, or funds a visible project. The giver should renew intention and protect recipients from being displayed. If applause comes unexpectedly, he thanks Allah and does not let it become the aim.

### The Smooth Stone

The image is of a smooth stone covered with a thin layer of dust and soil. Rain falls heavily and washes everything away, leaving the stone bare. The charity and outward appearance are like the dust; the rain of judgement reveals that no rooted reward remains. The person expected a harvest but had not planted in sincere soil.

The metaphor also warns against relying on appearance. A large donation can be spiritually empty, while a small private gift can be immense before Allah. The heart is the ground in which the deed grows. If arrogance and showmanship cover it, the outward act may not survive the test of the Hereafter.

### No Power over What Was Earned

“They have no power over anything they earned.” The giver who sought only human praise cannot rely upon that praise before Allah. The social status, photographs, or influence obtained from charity cannot purchase forgiveness. He may have gained worldly advantage, but he has no control over the eternal reward.

The phrase does not mean that the recipient received no practical benefit. A meal can feed someone even if the giver’s intention was corrupt. It means the donor has no claim to the spiritual harvest he destroyed through hypocrisy and injury.

### Guarding the Recipient’s Dignity

Charity should not expose a person’s poverty unnecessarily. Donors and organizations must seek consent before using names, images, stories, or personal details. Children and refugees are especially vulnerable to being turned into publicity. The Qur’an’s concern with hurtful words includes the subtle humiliation of making a recipient feel permanently indebted.

A gift also should not be used to buy political loyalty, silence abuse, or pressure a person into an unlawful relationship. The recipient remains free before Allah and retains his rights.

### Hypocrisy in Worship

The verse links showing off with not believing in Allah and the Last Day. A person may speak religiously but act as though human approval is the final judgement. Belief in the Hereafter should make Allah’s evaluation more important than a temporary audience.

The Prophet ﷺ warned that the first people judged may include a scholar, a fighter, and a charitable person whose deeds were performed for reputation, in the hadith recorded by Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith is a terrifying reminder that impressive deeds require sincere intention.

### Repentance after Showing Off

Riyāʾ can be subtle and can enter after a sincere beginning. A person should not abandon a good deed simply because people notice it; he should correct the intention and continue where the deed is beneficial. If he has injured someone, he apologizes and restores dignity without making the apology another performance.

Allah’s guidance is sought through hidden worship, remembrance, honest self-examination, and asking for acceptance. The believer fears hypocrisy without becoming paralysed by obsessive doubt.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:261–264 on the multiplication of charity, kind speech, reminders, injury, and showing off.
- Qur’an 2:271–274 on hidden and public charity and Allah’s knowledge of deeds.
- Qur’an 107:1–7 on religious claims without care for the needy.
- Qur’an 18:103–106 on deeds that appear impressive but are lost through misdirection.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith of the first three judged for showing off; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim, reports on intention, hidden charity, and protecting the recipient.
- For *mann*, *adhā*, *riyāʾ*, the stone parable, and the loss of reward, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:264.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:265

> The example of those who spend their wealth seeking Allah’s pleasure and strengthening their own souls is like a garden on a hill. Heavy rain falls upon it and it produces twice its fruit; if heavy rain does not reach it, then a light rain is sufficient. Allah sees all that you do.

**Commentary:**

### A Rooted Charity

After the image of a smooth stone whose dust is washed away, this verse gives the opposite image: a fertile garden on elevated ground. The garden represents a charitable act rooted in sincerity—*ibtighāʾ marḍāt Allāh*—and in the strengthening or purification of the giver’s soul. Its reward grows because the foundation is sound.

A garden on a hill receives rainfall and benefits from good drainage, sunlight, and protection from stagnant water. The image suggests a deed that is well-placed, healthy, and able to produce repeatedly. Sincere charity can benefit the recipient immediately and continue through education, healing, family stability, and the good practices that grow from it.

### Seeking Allah’s Pleasure

The giver seeks Allah’s pleasure rather than public praise. His intention does not have to be perfectly free from every passing thought; he renews it and resists the desire to be admired. He gives because Allah loves generosity and because people have rights, not because he wants to own the recipient’s loyalty.

A private gift often protects sincerity, but public giving can also be good when it encourages others or fulfils a public trust. The test is whether the giver would still want the good done if his name were removed. Allah sees the intention and the conduct more fully than people do.

### Strengthening the Soul

The phrase *tathbītan min anfusihim* can mean seeking to strengthen the soul, confirm sincerity, or establish the heart in obedience. Spending weakens the grip of miserliness and trains the servant to trust Allah. It also turns concern for the poor into a settled character rather than an occasional emotional reaction.

A person strengthens himself through regular charity, even if small, and through spending in ways that require patience and responsibility. He learns that his identity is not secured by accumulation. The heart becomes more stable when it is attached to Allah rather than to possessions.

### Heavy Rain and Light Rain

Heavy rain produces abundant fruit, while light rain is sufficient. This illustrates that sincere deeds bear fruit according to the conditions Allah knows. A wealthy person may give much, while a poor person gives little but with a greater sacrifice. A large project may produce public benefit, while a small meal quietly given to a hungry neighbour may be immense in Allah’s account.

The verse does not make small charity insignificant. If the soil is fertile, even light rain sustains it. A person who cannot donate large amounts can give time, food, expertise, advocacy, or a kind word. What matters is sincere rootedness and beneficial effect.

### Giving and Capacity

The garden image also teaches planning. A charity must be maintained rather than launched with enthusiasm and abandoned when attention moves elsewhere. Donors should choose sustainable projects, monitor funds, and listen to the people being served. The best assistance helps people become stable rather than keeping them permanently dependent upon unpredictable gifts.

The giver must still support his family and fulfil debts. Strengthening the soul does not mean destroying the household’s legitimate provision. Allah loves balanced obedience.

### Allah Sees Everything

The verse closes with Allah’s vision—*baṣīr*—of all that people do. He sees the gift, the intention, the recipient’s condition, the sacrifice involved, and the harm or benefit that follows. A public donation may be praised while a hidden gift is unknown; Allah sees both without confusion.

This sight also warns institutions. They may publish attractive reports while misusing funds or harming communities. Allah sees what the auditors and donors do not. Transparency, independent oversight, and truthful reporting are forms of taqwā.

### A Community of Gardens

If many believers give sincerely, the community becomes like a landscape of gardens: provision circulates, knowledge grows, and people shelter one another. Charity is not merely emergency relief; it can build schools, clinics, housing, safe water systems, and opportunities for dignified work.

The projects must remain subordinate to people’s rights. A beautiful building that leaves the poor hungry is not automatically a garden of righteousness. Fruit is measured by benefit and sincerity.

- Qur’an 2:272–274 and 92:17–21 on seeking Allah’s pleasure and giving without seeking repayment.
- Qur’an 3:92 on spending what one loves and 59:9 on preferring others.
- Qur’an 2:286 and 64:16 on capacity and responsible generosity.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on consistent deeds, hidden charity, and Allah’s acceptance of sincere intention.
- For *ibtighāʾ marḍāt Allāh*, *tathbīt*, the hill garden, and the different degrees of reward, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:265.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:266

> Would any of you wish to have a garden of palm trees and grapevines with rivers flowing beneath it, containing every kind of fruit, then old age overtakes him while he has weak children, and a fiery whirlwind strikes it and burns it? Thus Allah makes the revelations clear to you so that you may reflect.

**Commentary:**

### A Terrifying Picture of Lost Provision

This verse presents a vivid parable about a person who possesses a flourishing garden and depends upon it in old age while children are weak and unable to support him. A fiery whirlwind destroys it at the moment he needs it most. The image follows the discussion of charity and the loss of reward through showing off, reminders, and injury. A deed may appear valuable for years, then be destroyed by a final act of hypocrisy or harm.

The parable is emotionally powerful because the garden represents security, family, livelihood, and accumulated effort. Its destruction is not a minor reduction in comfort. It leaves the elderly person and dependent children exposed. The Qur’an asks whether anyone would wish for such a fate, inviting the heart to recognize the horror of losing the Hereafter’s harvest after believing it was secure.

### Age and Dependants

Old age and weak children represent vulnerability. A person may need the fruits of his past work when his physical strength declines and his dependants cannot replace him. In the same way, a believer needs the reward of his deeds when he stands before Allah unable to return and act again.

The image also teaches responsibility toward parents and children. Wealth should not be accumulated only for personal prestige while dependants are left insecure. Charity and family support must be balanced. A person who gives publicly to impress people but neglects those under his care has misunderstood both generosity and worship.

### The Fiery Whirlwind

The destructive wind contains fire—*iʿṣār fīhi nār*—a sudden force that combines movement and burning. It evokes the way a hidden spiritual disease can destroy the reward of an outwardly impressive life. The person may have cultivated charity, prayer, or service, but the fire of showing off, arrogance, and injury can consume the harvest.

The image does not mean that every mistake erases every good deed. It warns about deliberate corruption and about the danger of assuming that a deed is safe merely because it was large or long ago. Sincere repentance can repair what was damaged while life remains.

### Fruits of the Garden

The garden contains every kind of fruit, suggesting abundant opportunity. A person may have been given wealth, health, education, time, influence, and access to many forms of good. These are gardens planted by Allah. The question is how they are cultivated and whether the harvest is protected from destructive conduct.

A wealthy person can use assets to support family, create employment, fund education, relieve debt, and care for the poor. If he uses them for vanity, corruption, or harm, the apparent garden becomes a source of loss. The fruit is judged by its moral use.

### Sudden Loss and False Security

The whirlwind is sudden. A person cannot assume that he will have years to correct his intention or repair a wrong. Death, illness, economic collapse, public scandal, and the loss of opportunity can arrive without warning. The wise servant reviews his deeds regularly and does not postpone repentance.

This urgency should not produce panic or obsessive fear. It should produce practical habits: hidden charity, honest apologies, clear wills, lawful earnings, and consistent worship. The believer asks Allah to accept deeds and to protect them from corruption.

### Reflection as the Purpose

Allah makes the revelation clear so that people may reflect—*yatafakkarūn*. Reflection connects the image with personal life. What garden has Allah entrusted to me? Who depends upon its fruit? What kind of fire could destroy it? Is my charity nourishing people or feeding my ego?

The mind that reflects does not stop at the image. It changes behaviour. It protects the rights of recipients, controls speech, and seeks a sincere ending.

### Care for the Vulnerable

The parable also demands communal compassion. Elderly people, orphans, single parents, and children should not be left at the mercy of sudden loss. Zakah, social support, fair inheritance, and responsible institutions can preserve the “garden” of a family when its provider is gone.

The person who helps protect another family’s provision may find that Allah protects his own harvest. Charity is a network of mercy, not only a private transaction.

- Qur’an 2:180–215 and 4:2–10 on wills, dependants, orphans, and protecting family wealth.
- Qur’an 18:32–44 on gardens, wealth, pride, and sudden loss.
- Qur’an 59:18 and 63:10–11 on preparing deeds before death and not delaying generosity.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on hidden charity, the rights of dependants, and the sudden nature of death and loss.
- For *jannah*, *iʿṣār*, the fiery whirlwind, the weak children, and the parable of destroyed reward, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:266.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:267

> O believers, spend from the good things you have earned and from what We have brought forth for you from the earth. Do not deliberately choose the inferior of it to spend, when you would not accept it yourselves except with closed eyes. Know that Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

**Commentary:**

### Give from Good Provision

Allah commands believers to spend from the good things they have earned and from the produce He brings forth from the earth. The sources include wages, business profits, crops, livestock, minerals, and all lawful provision. Charity should be drawn from *ṭayyib*, what is lawful, wholesome, and worthy—not from stolen money or goods that the giver would never accept for himself.

The command links human work with divine provision. A person earns through effort, but the ability to work, the land, the market, and the resources all come from Allah. Gratitude directs a portion back toward the needy and the public good.

### Do Not Give What You Would Reject

The verse asks the giver to imagine receiving the item he is offering. Would he accept damaged food, worn-out clothing, defective equipment, or money acquired through deception? If he would take it only while overlooking its defects, he should not choose it for charity. The question exposes a double standard in which the donor protects his own comfort while treating the poor as undeserving of quality.

This does not prohibit giving used clothing, surplus food, or second-hand goods. Such items can be valuable when clean, safe, useful, and offered with dignity. The prohibition concerns deliberately selecting the inferior because the recipient is poor.

### Charity and Human Worth

The recipient’s poverty does not reduce his worth before Allah. A person may lack money while possessing faith, knowledge, skill, courage, and honour. Giving good food and useful goods acknowledges this dignity. The donor does not bestow value; he fulfils a responsibility with wealth Allah entrusted to him.

Institutions should apply the same principle. Relief distributions should not become a way to dispose of expired products, broken equipment, or goods that would violate safety standards. The poor deserve protection from harm and the right to receive what genuinely benefits them.

### Lawful Wealth

The phrase “what you have earned” excludes wealth obtained through theft, fraud, bribery, gambling, or other forbidden means. Unlawful property must be returned to its owner where possible. Donating stolen money does not transform theft into charity. If the owner cannot be found, the person seeks qualified advice about removing the unlawful benefit without claiming spiritual credit.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah is good and accepts only what is good. The hadith links the quality of wealth to the quality of worship. A person should examine contracts, wages, taxes, debts, and business practices, not only the item placed in a donation box.

### Agricultural and Natural Produce

The earth’s produce includes crops and fruits that nourish people. Zakah on agricultural production has its own rulings, while voluntary charity can be given from any harvest. Farmers and businesses should avoid waste, protect soil and water, and pay workers fairly. The verse does not permit exploiting land or labour in the name of charitable output.

A person can share from what he grows, cooks, or makes. Goodness is not limited to cash. A nutritious meal, a repaired appliance, a textbook, or professional service can be a valuable gift.

### Allah Is Self-Sufficient

Allah is *Ghaniyy*, Self-Sufficient. He does not need inferior goods or the donor’s wealth. The command is for the giver’s purification and the recipient’s welfare. A person should not imagine that Allah is enriched by his donation; rather, Allah enriches the servant by allowing him to participate in mercy.

Because Allah is self-sufficient, He can accept a small sincere gift and multiply it. The giver must not use charity to claim superiority over Allah’s servants. The poor person is not indebted to the donor in a way that permits control.

### Allah Is Praiseworthy

Allah is *Ḥamīd*, Praiseworthy in His names, actions, and judgement. He deserves praise whether people give or withhold. The verse’s ending shifts the heart from the recipient’s reaction to Allah’s approval. The donor gives for Allah and trusts Him with the reward.

Praising Allah also means praising Him through the use of blessings. Wealth becomes an act of *ḥamd* when it feeds people, protects children, supports knowledge, and removes harm.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:268

> Satan threatens you with poverty and commands you to immorality, while Allah promises you forgiveness and bounty. Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### The Fear behind Miserliness

When the Qur’an commands generous spending, Satan responds by threatening poverty—*al-shayṭān yaʿidukum al-faqr*. He whispers that charity will leave a person without security, that supporting others is foolish, or that one must accumulate indefinitely before giving. Responsible planning is not wrong, but fear that prevents every act of generosity is one of Satan’s recurring strategies.

A person may possess enough for his needs yet feel permanently threatened. The more he gathers, the more afraid he becomes of loss. Satan turns a real concern about provision into a refusal to trust Allah, a neglect of dependants, or the hoarding of wealth while others suffer.

### Commands to Immorality

Satan commands *faḥshāʾ*, indecency and shameful conduct. Misers may be led to exploit workers, steal inheritance, take bribes, consume interest, or humiliate the poor. The fear of poverty becomes an excuse for actions that destroy dignity and community. Satan also invites people to spend extravagantly on desire while claiming that they cannot afford charity.

The Qur’an’s word “commands” does not remove human choice. Satan suggests and beautifies; the person decides. Seeking refuge in Allah, questioning the whisper, and taking lawful means break the cycle. A believer can budget carefully without obeying the demand to become unjust.

### Allah Promises Forgiveness

Allah promises *maghfirah*. Charity, repentance, and responsible generosity can be means of forgiveness, not because money purchases Allah’s mercy, but because sincere giving reveals faith and purifies attachment. The believer who has been miserly can return to Allah without waiting until he feels completely generous.

Forgiveness also covers mistakes in financial judgement when a servant acts sincerely. A person should consult, learn, and correct errors, but he need not be paralyzed by the fear of never giving perfectly. Allah sees the intention and effort.

### Allah Promises Bounty

Allah promises *faḍl*, bounty and increase. The increase may be material provision, blessing in what remains, good relationships, contentment, protection from calamity, or the multiplied reward of the Hereafter. The promise does not turn charity into a guaranteed investment scheme. A believer gives for Allah, not as a technique for forcing a worldly return.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that every morning two angels supplicate for replacement for the one who spends and destruction for the one who withholds, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith encourages trust while reminding people that withholding rights has consequences.

### Planning and Reliance

Islam balances charity with responsibility. The believer pays debts, supports dependants, prepares for genuine needs, and avoids waste. He then gives according to ability. The Prophet ﷺ advised Saʿd not to leave his heirs dependent upon people, showing that generosity does not mean abandoning family.

Satan may use either extreme: fear to prevent charity or vanity to cause irresponsible giving. Wisdom identifies the true need, the lawful amount, and the most beneficial form of support.

### Allah’s Vastness and Knowledge

Allah is *Wāsiʿ*, vast in provision, mercy, and knowledge. His stores do not shrink when servants give, and His knowledge encompasses the giver’s circumstances and the recipient’s need. He knows whether a person is genuinely unable or hiding behind an exaggerated fear of poverty.

This knowledge is a comfort to the poor giver. Allah does not demand what he cannot afford. It is a warning to the wealthy who call miserliness prudence while spending freely on luxuries.

### Replacing the Whisper

The believer counters Satan’s threat with the Qur’anic promises: “Whatever you spend of good, He will replace it” (34:39), and “If you are grateful, I will surely increase you” (14:7). He begins with small consistent gifts, gives privately where possible, and observes how generosity changes the heart.

A person facing actual financial distress should seek assistance, renegotiate debts, and protect dependants. Asking for help is not a failure of tawakkul. Communities should ensure that the poor are not shamed for needing support.

- Qur’an 4:37 and 59:9 on miserliness, generosity, and preferring others.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:269

> He gives wisdom to whom He wills, and whoever is given wisdom has certainly been given abundant good. None will take heed except people of sound understanding.

**Commentary:**

### The Gift of Wisdom

*Ḥikmah* is more than information. It includes sound understanding, placing matters in their proper context, making the right decision, and acting at the right time and in the right manner. A person may know many facts yet lack wisdom, while another may possess modest information but use it with exceptional judgement and benefit.

In this passage about charity, wisdom includes recognizing who has a right, how much can be given, how to preserve dignity, when public reporting is useful, and how to balance generosity with family responsibility. It also includes knowing when a kind word is better than money and when a material gift is essential.

### Knowledge Joined to Action

The Qur’anic wisdom links learning to obedience. A person who understands that wealth is a trust gives sincerely, avoids reminders, and protects the poor. A person who knows the harms of miserliness resists Satan’s threat. Knowledge that remains only a source of debate has not yet become the abundant good described here.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that when Allah wants good for someone, He gives him understanding of the religion, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This understanding includes knowing rulings and recognizing their purposes, limits, and ethical application. It should produce humility and mercy rather than contempt for people who are still learning.

### Allah Gives Wisdom as He Wills

Allah gives wisdom to whom He wills, and this gift may appear in scholars, parents, workers, leaders, or people whose formal education is limited. It is not restricted to social status or institutional title. At the same time, a person does not claim wisdom without learning. He seeks teachers, verifies evidence, and accepts correction.

The phrase “to whom He wills” does not teach arbitrary favoritism. Allah knows who is sincere, who is capable, and how a person will use knowledge. The servant asks for wisdom, takes the means of learning, and thanks Allah for any understanding he receives.

### Abundant Good

Whoever receives wisdom receives *khayran kathīran*, abundant good. Wisdom benefits the person and those around him. It protects from waste, prevents injustice, reconciles families, guides charitable spending, and helps a community respond to changing circumstances without abandoning principles.

Wisdom also protects from extremes. A generous person without wisdom may give irresponsibly or enable harm. A cautious person without wisdom may use planning as an excuse for miserliness. A wise believer joins generosity with discernment and firmness with mercy.

### The People of Sound Understanding

“People of sound understanding”—*ūlū al-albāb*—are those who reach the core of matters. They reflect on revelation, distinguish appearance from reality, and allow truth to change them. In the charity passage, they see that the value of spending depends on intention, source, recipient, and conduct after giving.

They also learn from parables. The seed, garden, stone, fire, and Satan’s threat are not decorative images. They train the mind to see invisible spiritual realities through familiar forms. Reflection becomes successful when it leads to gratitude and action.

### Wisdom in Disagreement

Family disputes, financial decisions, and religious questions often contain competing rights. Wisdom means listening before judging, separating facts from emotion, and seeking reconciliation where possible. It does not mean avoiding firm decisions when a right is clear. A judge may be compassionate without becoming partial; a donor may be generous without surrendering accountability.

The Prophet ﷺ combined wisdom with clarity. He spoke according to the listener’s understanding, repeated important matters, and chose the appropriate time. Teachers and leaders should imitate this rather than overwhelming people with information or using knowledge to dominate them.

### Asking for Wisdom

The believer can ask Allah for beneficial knowledge and sound judgement. He reads the Qur’an with reflection, studies the Sunnah, consults experienced people, and examines his own motives. Before giving, he asks whether the gift helps, whether it is lawful, and whether it preserves dignity. Before speaking, he asks whether the words are true and useful.

Wisdom also grows through repentance. A person who learns from a financial or relational mistake may gain insight that protects others. Allah can turn a failure into abundant good when the servant responds with humility.

- Qur’an 16:125 and 17:39 on wisdom, good counsel, and the purposes of revelation.
- Qur’an 31:12 and 38:20 on wisdom, gratitude, and sound judgement.
- Qur’an 39:18 and 3:190–191 on listening, reflection, and understanding.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “When Allah wishes good for someone, He gives him understanding of the religion,” and the Prophet’s teachings on wise counsel.
- For *ḥikmah*, *khayran kathīran*, *ūlū al-albāb*, and the relation between knowledge and action, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:269.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:270

> Whatever you spend or whatever vow you make, surely Allah knows it. The wrongdoers will have no helpers.

**Commentary:**

### Nothing Is Hidden from Allah

This verse gathers charity and vows under Allah’s complete knowledge. He knows the amount spent, the source of the wealth, the recipient’s need, the intention of the giver, and the words and circumstances of every vow. A public donation and a private payment are equally visible to Him, as are promises made in a moment of fear or bargaining.

The reminder protects both sincerity and justice. A person who gives quietly but receives no praise should know that Allah has recorded it. A person who displays generosity while concealing fraud should know that appearance cannot deceive Allah. The final account includes the inner motive and the outward deed.

### Spending as Worship

The preceding verses have described several qualities of charity: it must be lawful, sincere, beneficial, and free from reminders and injury. This verse confirms that every expenditure falls under divine knowledge. Spending on dependants, relatives, or the needy can be worship when done for Allah, while spending on vanity, oppression, or forbidden causes can become a source of sin.

A believer should therefore review both the amount and the purpose. He pays obligatory maintenance and zakāh, settles debts, and gives voluntary charity according to ability. He does not neglect family while seeking public praise, and he does not use family responsibility as an excuse to refuse every claim of the poor.

### Vows and Their Limits

A *nadhr* is a vow by which a person binds himself to an act of worship or obedience. If the vow concerns a lawful act of devotion, fulfilling it is required according to the rules of fiqh. The Qur’an praises those who fulfil their vows (76:7). If a vow concerns sin or an impossible act, it must not be fulfilled; the person repents and follows the relevant ruling on expiation.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that vows do not bring forward or delay anything Allah has not decreed. A vow should therefore not be used as a bargain: “If Allah gives me this, I will worship Him,” as though the servant can compel divine action. Worship is owed to Allah before the requested outcome arrives.

### Do Not Make Unnecessary Vows

The Sunnah discourages making vows because people may bind themselves to a burden they cannot sustain, or they may fulfil it resentfully and then imagine they have purchased a favour. A person who wants to give charity should give without waiting to make a dramatic vow. Sincere voluntary action is better than turning worship into an emotional contract.

If a vow has been made, the person should record it and learn its ruling. Families should not pressure someone into a vow or use a public promise to extract money. Allah knows whether the vow was deliberate, coerced, conditional, or intended as a sinful act.

### No Helpers for Wrongdoers

The wrongdoers—*ẓālimūn*—will have no helpers. A person who uses charity or vows to oppress people cannot rely on wealth, relatives, officials, or public reputation to save him before Allah. Created helpers may assist in the world, but no one can cancel divine accountability.

The warning also applies to those who help wrongdoing: an executor who hides assets, an institution that launders corrupt money, or a group that uses a vow to manipulate the vulnerable. Cooperation in righteousness is commanded; cooperation in sin is forbidden (5:2).

### Repentance and Repair

If a person spent unlawfully or made a vow that harmed someone, repentance includes stopping the wrong, restoring property, fulfilling lawful rights, and seeking qualified guidance about expiation. A public vow that misled people may require a public clarification. Private regret alone is not enough when others were harmed.

Allah’s knowledge is also a comfort to the sincere person who made a mistake without intending sin. He corrects the error and hopes in Allah’s mercy rather than remaining trapped by obsessive guilt.

### Charity without Bargaining

The believer gives because Allah deserves worship and because people have rights, not because he wants to control Allah or the recipient. He does not demand an immediate worldly return, though he may ask Allah for good in this life and the Hereafter. The “good loan” metaphor honours the giver, but Allah remains the Lord, not a debtor compelled by a transaction.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:271

> If you disclose your charitable giving, it is good; but if you conceal it and give it to the poor, that is better for you, and He will erase some of your sins. Allah is fully aware of what you do.

**Commentary:**

### Public and Private Charity

The verse gives a balanced judgement about the visibility of charity. Public giving is good when it encourages others, reports a public trust, or makes an important project possible. Concealing charity and giving it to the poor is better for the giver’s sincerity and for the recipient’s dignity. The verse does not declare every public donation corrupt or every private donation automatically sincere.

The deciding question is purpose and effect. A public campaign may feed thousands and inspire a culture of generosity, while a private gift may be used to control or harm. A hidden gift may protect the heart from showing off, while a public zakāh payment may provide transparency. Allah knows the full circumstance.

### Concealing Charity

Hidden charity—*tukhfūhā*—can protect both sides. The giver is less likely to seek praise, and the recipient is less likely to feel exposed. A quiet transfer, anonymous payment, or discreet delivery can preserve a person’s dignity. The donor should not later reveal the secret to win an argument or demand loyalty.

The Prophet ﷺ mentioned the person who gives charity so secretly that the left hand does not know what the right hand has given among those shaded by Allah’s mercy on the Day of Resurrection, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The image emphasizes sincerity rather than prescribing secrecy in every case.

### Public Charity with Sincerity

Public giving can be necessary. An institution may need published accounts, donors may need to verify funds, and a visible project may encourage others to contribute. A person who gives publicly should protect the recipient from being made a spectacle and should renew his intention when praise arrives.

Leaders should also avoid using charity to build a personality cult. A public donation that makes the poor chant the donor’s name or suppresses criticism is no longer simple generosity. Transparency should serve accountability, not domination.

### Erasing Sins

Allah promises that charity can erase some sins. This does not erase debts or rights owed to people without restitution, and it does not make deliberate injustice harmless. Repentance, restoration, and lawful settlement remain necessary. The promise refers to Allah’s forgiveness of shortcomings and the purification produced by sincere giving.

The person who gives should not become confident that charity permits him to continue fraud. A gift from stolen wealth does not cleanse theft. The best charity is accompanied by lawful earning, repentance, and a change in conduct.

### Giving to the Poor

The verse specifically mentions giving to the poor—*al-fuqarāʾ*. Charity should reach those with genuine need, and the giver should learn how to identify need without humiliating people. Not every poor person asks, and not every person who asks lacks resources. Trustworthy institutions and discreet inquiry can help.

A gift should be appropriate. Food, rent, medical treatment, education, debt relief, and tools for work may serve different needs. The giver asks the recipient what would help rather than imposing an unwanted project for the sake of public appearance.

### Allah’s Complete Awareness

Allah is fully aware—*khabīr*—of the gift, the source, the intention, the recipient’s condition, and the public or private consequences. A charity may be celebrated by people while Allah knows that it was accompanied by manipulation. Another may be unknown in the world while Allah knows every sacrifice.

This awareness gives the poor giver comfort and the public donor accountability. No good deed is lost, and no spiritual corruption is hidden.

### Charity and Community Systems

The preference for hidden voluntary charity does not abolish organized zakāh. Zakāh often requires records, collection, and distribution. The administrators should preserve confidentiality as far as possible and report finances honestly. The recipients should not be shamed for receiving an obligatory right.

The community can combine anonymous assistance with public education about poverty, fair wages, disability, housing, and debt. Good charity relieves immediate need while seeking to remove the causes of preventable deprivation.

- Qur’an 9:60 and 59:7 on zakāh recipients and circulating wealth.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:272

> Their guidance is not your responsibility, but Allah guides whom He wills. Whatever good you spend is for your own benefit, and you spend only seeking the Face of Allah. Whatever good you spend will be repaid to you in full, and you will not be wronged.

**Commentary:**

### Charity Is Not a Tool of Conversion

The verse tells the Messenger ﷺ that he is not responsible for forcing people onto guidance. His duty is to convey the message; guidance of the heart belongs to Allah. The immediate context includes questions about giving charity to people whose faith or attitude may differ from the Muslims. Believers are encouraged to give for Allah’s sake rather than making material assistance conditional upon a person’s conversion.

This does not mean that every charitable decision must ignore safety, accountability, or the recipient’s conduct. A charity may refuse to fund violence, addiction, fraud, or exploitation and may require proper documentation. The principle is that the deserving poor should not be denied help merely because the donor cannot control their faith.

### Guidance Belongs to Allah

Allah guides whom He wills through wisdom, evidence, circumstances, and the person’s response. The Prophet ﷺ loved guidance for his relatives and grieved when people refused it, but he could not place faith in their hearts (28:56). A donor likewise cannot purchase belief by giving money or punish disbelief by withholding basic mercy.

Charity can express the moral beauty of Islam, but it should not be a deceptive sales tactic. The believer gives because Allah commands compassion and because people have rights. He explains faith with wisdom when appropriate, without using hunger or vulnerability to force a declaration.

### Spending Benefits the Giver

“Whatever good you spend is for your own benefit.” The recipient receives practical help, but the spiritual reward returns to the donor through purification, forgiveness, and nearness to Allah. Allah is not enriched by charity. The giver is the one who needs the good deed.

This perspective protects against resentment. A person who gives for Allah does not constantly calculate whether the recipient thanked him or whether the social return was sufficient. He evaluates the benefit responsibly but leaves the reward with Allah. If the gift is misused through no fault of his own, Allah knows the intention and effort.

### Seeking the Face of Allah

“Seeking the Face of Allah”—*ibtighāʾ wajh Allāh*—is a Qur’anic expression for seeking His pleasure and acceptance. It does not mean imagining Allah in a created direction. The believer purifies his intention from reputation, political influence, control over recipients, or the desire to appear compassionate.

Mixed motives can enter. The servant renews his intention and continues beneficial charity rather than allowing a passing desire for praise to cancel all good. He also chooses privacy when it protects sincerity and public transparency when it protects people’s rights.

### Full Recompense

Allah promises that whatever good is spent will be repaid in full. The repayment may be forgiveness, blessing, protection, a good name that benefits others, or reward in the Hereafter. It is not necessarily a direct financial return. A person may give money and receive contentment, a protected family, or a reward that remains after death.

“Without being wronged” assures that no sacrifice is undervalued. The poor person who gives a small amount, the caregiver who gives time, and the wealthy person who gives much are all judged by intention, ability, and obedience. Allah does not deprive a person of the reward he deserves.

### The Ethics of Helping People of Other Faiths

The verse supports charitable kindness beyond the Muslim community. Qur’an 60:8 commands justice and kindness toward those who do not fight Muslims because of religion. A Muslim may feed a hungry neighbour, support disaster relief, fund healthcare, or protect a refugee without treating the help as proof that every belief is the same.

Faith remains clear while mercy remains broad. The donor does not assist oppression, but he does not allow sectarian hostility to make him indifferent to human need.

### Charity and Prophetic Duty

The Prophet ﷺ was responsible for conveying guidance, not controlling hearts. This distinction protects religious teachers from burnout and manipulation. They teach clearly, model goodness, and pray for people, but they do not measure the value of the message solely by immediate conversions.

Likewise, parents and community leaders advise without claiming ownership of another person’s faith. Allah knows the path by which a person may eventually return.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:273

> Charity is for those needy people who are restricted in Allah’s cause and cannot travel through the land. An unaware person might think they are self-sufficient because of their restraint. You will recognize them by their signs; they do not ask people persistently. Whatever good you spend, surely Allah knows it.

**Commentary:**

### Hidden Poverty

This verse directs charity toward people whose need is not obvious. They are restricted in Allah’s cause—*uḥṣirū fī sabīl Allāh*—and cannot travel through the land to earn a livelihood. Classical exegetes connected the first audience to the poor Muhājirūn who devoted themselves to Allah’s cause and lacked the freedom, property, or security to pursue ordinary work. The principle extends to anyone whose devotion, disability, displacement, caregiving, study, or other lawful responsibility restricts earning.

The verse does not define all poor people as fighters or require poverty as proof of devotion. It teaches donors to notice people whose circumstances limit income and whose dignity prevents them from public begging. A refugee unable to work, a caregiver, a disabled person, a student of beneficial knowledge, or a family trapped by conflict may all need discreet support, depending on the circumstances.

### Poverty Hidden by Restraint

An unaware person may think they are rich because they do not ask. Their clothing, speech, and dignity conceal deprivation. This is a challenge to shallow charity systems that help only the person who can stand in a queue and tell a dramatic story. The Qur’an asks believers to look carefully and respectfully for signs of need.

Restraint—*taʿaffuf*—is not pride or refusal of all assistance. It is the effort to preserve dignity and avoid dependence upon people. The community must not exploit this restraint by assuming that quiet people need nothing. Relatives, neighbours, and responsible institutions should learn to offer help without forcing a person to expose private hardship.

### Recognizing Signs without Intrusion

“You will recognize them by their signs” invites attentive compassion, not spying or humiliation. Signs may include inadequate housing, unpaid medical needs, lack of food, inability to travel to work, worn tools, interrupted education, or a sudden change in circumstances. A donor should verify responsibly but protect privacy.

No one should be accused of fraud merely because his need is not visible. Allah knows the truth, and human decisions should be based on reasonable evidence and good manners. A person may receive help and later become able to work; the gift is not a permanent label.

### They Do Not Beg Persistently

“They do not ask people persistently”—*lā yasʾalūna al-nāsa ilḥāfan*—does not prohibit asking for help when genuine need exists. It praises those who do not harass, pressure, manipulate, or make begging their preferred livelihood while able to work. The needy person may ask clearly and honestly, and the community must respond without contempt.

The Prophet ﷺ explained that the truly poor person is not simply the one who goes around asking, but the one whose need is not recognized and who does not persistently beg, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This hadith trains believers to search for hidden need.

### The Duty of the Community

The verse places a responsibility on donors to be observant. Zakah administrators should develop discreet assessment, and charities should not rely only on public self-referral. Neighbours can check on elderly people, single parents, refugees, students, and disabled people without turning care into surveillance.

Support can be financial, medical, educational, legal, or vocational. The best help may remove the restriction itself—transport to work, equipment, childcare, a safe home, or documentation for a displaced person. Charity should seek dignity and independence where possible.

### Spending in Allah’s Knowledge

The verse ends by assuring the giver that Allah knows every good expenditure. A donor may never know the full benefit of a discreet gift, and a recipient may not be able to express gratitude. Allah knows the hidden sacrifice, the careful search for a deserving person, and the kindness that prevented humiliation.

The reminder also warns against giving to display one’s ability to identify hidden poverty. The goal is Allah’s pleasure, not the reputation of being perceptive or generous.

### Poverty and Structural Responsibility

Individual charity cannot replace just wages, accessible healthcare, protection from exploitation, and responsible public policy. The verse calls for immediate help while the wider Qur’an addresses economic justice. A society that praises the poor for not asking but refuses to create fair opportunities has misunderstood taqwā.

Those who can work should be supported toward dignified work, while those unable to work retain a right to care. The categories must be assessed with mercy and expertise rather than suspicion.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:274

> Those who spend their wealth by night and by day, secretly and publicly, will have their reward with their Lord. They will have no fear, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

### Generosity at Every Time

This verse praises those who spend by night and day, secretly and publicly. The fourfold description suggests continuity and variety. Charity is not limited to a yearly campaign or a public emergency. A believer remains ready to help when a need appears, whether people see the gift or not.

The wording does not require giving money constantly or neglecting family needs. It praises a settled disposition of generosity. The person gives according to ability, fulfils obligations, and looks for lawful opportunities to benefit others. A small regular gift can be more sincere and sustainable than a large donation made once for reputation.

### Secret and Public Giving

Secret giving often protects sincerity and the recipient’s dignity. Public giving can encourage others, support a visible institution, or demonstrate that a public trust is being fulfilled. The verse includes both, showing that the form is not the only measure. Intention and effect determine whether public visibility serves good or vanity.

The donor should avoid turning recipients into publicity. If a project requires names, photographs, or stories, consent and privacy matter. Public accounts should report funds accurately without making the poor bear the cost of the donor’s reputation.

### Spending by Night and Day

Night and day also represent ease and difficulty, private and public life, and times of prosperity and scarcity. A person who gives only when he has excess may never learn sacrifice. A person who gives beyond capacity may harm dependants. The Qur’an’s balance is to give from what is lawful and available while preserving rights.

A gift at night may be especially hidden from people, while a public gift during the day may mobilize a community. Allah knows the intention in both. The believer asks Him to make generosity a character rather than an occasional performance.

### Their Reward Is with Allah

“Their reward is with their Lord” assures the giver that no good is lost. People may reject, forget, or misuse the gift, but Allah records the intention and effort. The phrase also protects the donor from expecting gratitude. He may evaluate a charity’s effectiveness and adjust future giving, but he does not transform aid into a debt of personal loyalty.

The reward belongs to Allah’s generosity. The servant does not claim that his charity obligates Allah, nor does he despair when the worldly outcome is unseen. The seed and garden parables earlier in the passage teach that reward can grow beyond human observation.

### No Fear and No Grief

The promise of no fear and no grief is especially meaningful for those who worry that giving will leave them insecure. Allah can place blessing in what remains and reward what was sent ahead. In the Hereafter, the generous servant is protected from the fear of losing the eternal account and the grief of discovering that his wealth was wasted.

This promise does not guarantee a life without financial anxiety. Believers can face poverty, illness, or loss. It gives them a deeper security and teaches them to seek lawful help when needed rather than being ashamed of dependence during hardship.

### Charity as Social Infrastructure

Continuous generosity supports more than emergency relief. It can fund schools, hospitals, water systems, shelters, food programmes, debt relief, and legal aid. A community that spends night and day develops structures that protect people before a crisis becomes catastrophic.

Such institutions require governance. Donors should verify accounts, trustees should disclose conflicts, and recipients should be treated with dignity. Generosity without accountability can unintentionally enable corruption.

### The Spiritual Opposite of Hoarding

The preceding verses warn against Satan’s threat of poverty. This verse presents the opposite heart: a person who trusts Allah enough to give at different times and in different forms. He does not worship wealth or measure every relationship by profit. His generosity expresses certainty that provision ultimately belongs to Allah.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that charity does not decrease wealth in the true sense and that the upper hand is better than the lower hand, while he also protected the giver from leaving dependants destitute. Generosity is strongest when joined to wisdom.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:275

> Those who consume interest will not stand except like one driven to madness by Satan’s touch. That is because they say, “Trade is only like interest,” while Allah has permitted trade and forbidden interest. Whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists may keep what is past, and his matter is with Allah. But whoever returns to it—those are the companions of the Fire; they will remain there forever.

**Commentary:**

### The Moral Disorder of Riba

This verse condemns *ribā*, an unjust increase attached to a debt or exchange, and rejects the claim that it is identical to ordinary trade. The Qur’an presents the person who consumes ribā as standing in a disordered way, like someone struck by Satan. The image conveys moral and economic confusion: the person sees exploitation as commerce and treats a vulnerable debtor as an opportunity for guaranteed gain.

The phrase is not a diagnosis of mental illness or a licence to stigmatize people with psychological conditions. Mental illness is a medical reality that deserves care and compassion. The verse uses a vivid image of disorientation to describe the spiritual and social disorder produced by riba.

### Trade Is Not the Same as Interest

“Allah has permitted trade and forbidden interest.” Trade involves an exchange of goods, services, or productive risk by mutual consent. The seller may profit because he owns an item, provides labour, accepts risk, or adds value. Ribā in its prohibited forms guarantees an increase on a debt because time passes, often placing the borrower under a growing burden regardless of whether the underlying activity succeeds.

A profit is not automatically lawful merely because it is called a sale, and a loan is not automatically ribā merely because money changes hands. Contracts must be examined in their substance, risk, ownership, timing, and conditions. Modern mortgages, credit cards, business finance, inflation adjustments, and emergency loans require qualified scholars and financial experts who understand both Sharīʿah and the contract.

### Why the Analogy Fails

The claim “trade is only like interest” ignores the difference between risk-bearing exchange and extracting increase from need. In a genuine sale, both parties may benefit from the asset and accept the terms. In a debt, the lender may demand more simply because the debtor needs time. The poor can become trapped, and wealth can accumulate among those already able to lend.

Islam permits *qarḍ ḥasan*, a good loan without exploitative increase, and encourages partnership, leasing, and trade structured around real assets and risk. The goal is not to prevent investment or profit but to protect people from unjust compounding and the commodification of desperation.

### The Human Cost of Ribā

Ribā can produce debt spirals, family breakdown, homelessness, economic inequality, and dependence upon predatory lenders. Even when a contract is formally agreed, consent may be distorted by urgent need. Islamic law therefore looks beyond paperwork to fairness, transparency, and the harm likely to result.

The prohibition also protects the lender’s heart. Guaranteed gain without productive service can cultivate greed and indifference to the borrower’s suffering. Wealth should circulate through trade, partnership, charity, and fair investment rather than becoming a permanent claim over another person’s future labour.

### Repentance after the Warning

“Whoever receives an admonition and desists may keep what is past.” This gives mercy to the person who stops after learning the ruling. He does not need to unravel every historical transaction in a way that creates impossible harm, while future dealings must be corrected and unlawful claims abandoned. The precise treatment of existing contracts and gains requires qualified advice and attention to other people’s rights.

The phrase does not authorize a person to keep money stolen from a known victim. Repentance requires returning identifiable rights, settling obligations, and ending the unlawful practice. “His matter is with Allah” places the unseen judgement of his past within divine mercy and knowledge.

### Returning to Ribā

“Whoever returns to it” describes a person who persists after the warning and treats the prohibition as something to evade. The severe warning concerns deliberate continuation, not a person who is trapped in a complex contract, seeks advice, or is trying to exit without causing greater harm. Muslims should distinguish ignorance, compulsion, hardship, and deliberate defiance.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against ribā and cursed the one who consumes it, the one who pays it, the one who records it, and the witnesses to it, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith shows that the prohibition reaches the network that facilitates exploitation, while legal responsibility still depends upon knowledge and circumstances.

### Economic Alternatives

A community that wants to leave ribā must build alternatives: interest-free emergency loans, zakāh funds, cooperative savings, fair partnerships, ethical investment, debt counselling, and support for people escaping predatory contracts. Telling the poor to avoid interest without providing any lawful route can become a form of cruelty.

The prohibition is joined to generosity. The next verses call for giving up remaining interest and provide relief for debtors in hardship. The Qur’anic economy protects both the borrower and the lender through justice and mercy.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:276

> Allah destroys interest and causes charities to grow. Allah does not love every ungrateful sinner.

**Commentary:**

### Destruction and Growth

This verse contrasts *ribā* and *ṣadaqāt*. Ribā may appear to increase wealth because the lender receives more than the principal, but Allah removes its blessing and ultimate benefit. Charity may appear to reduce wealth because the giver releases property, but Allah grows it through blessing, social repair, forgiveness, and eternal reward.

“Allah destroys interest” does not mean that every riba-based business collapses immediately or that every charitable person becomes wealthy in this life. Some exploiters prosper for a time, and some generous people remain poor. The verse describes the deeper reality: unlawful increase corrupts the heart and society, while sincere charity produces enduring good that may not be visible at once.

### The Difference between Increase and Blessing

A numerical increase is not the same as *barakah*. Wealth can expand while bringing anxiety, family conflict, legal exposure, illness, and spiritual emptiness. Another person may possess less but enjoy contentment, trustworthy relationships, and reward with Allah. The Qur’an asks people to judge wealth by its lawful source and beneficial use, not merely by its amount.

Ribā can also be destroyed through inflation, default, litigation, or economic instability. But even when it survives materially, its spiritual destruction remains. Charity can grow through the recipient’s recovery, the education of children, a community strengthened, or a good practice continued after the donor’s death.

### Charity Grows in Many Forms

Allah grows charity through direct reward and through its effects. A meal may give someone strength to work; a loan without exploitation may save a family from predatory debt; education may benefit generations; and a medical treatment may preserve a life. The giver may see only the first act, while Allah sees the whole chain of benefit.

The growth also occurs in the giver’s character. Generosity weakens miserliness, increases empathy, and teaches reliance upon Allah. The recipient’s dignity is preserved, and social trust grows when wealth is shared without humiliation.

### Riba and Social Concentration

Ribā can concentrate wealth among lenders and make the poor pay more for access to necessities. The borrower carries the risk of illness, unemployment, or business failure, while the lender demands a predetermined increase. This can turn temporary hardship into permanent dependency.

Islamic finance is not a promise that every partnership succeeds or that all trade is fair by default. It requires transparency, shared risk where appropriate, lawful assets, and protection from deception. The moral objective is an economy in which profit is connected to real benefit rather than guaranteed extraction from need.

### The Ungrateful Sinner

The verse ends by describing the person who consumes ribā as *kafūr athīm*, ungrateful and sinful. Ingratitude appears when a person receives Allah’s provision but refuses the limits that protect others. Sin appears when he knowingly uses wealth to exploit or when he treats the prohibition as an obstacle to clever circumvention.

The description is not a licence for ordinary Muslims to declare every borrower or employee outside Islam. It warns about a pattern of deliberate ingratitude and sin. People trapped in complex systems require knowledge, help, and a path out, not casual condemnation.

### Repentance and Repair

A person who recognizes riba should stop entering new exploitative transactions, seek reliable advice about existing obligations, and protect the rights of all parties. If he has taken identifiable unlawful gain from a person, he must return it. If he is a borrower trapped by a contract, he seeks lawful ways to settle without creating greater harm.

Charity can accompany repentance, but it does not replace restitution. The wronged person’s right must be restored where possible. Institutions should provide debt counselling and interest-free assistance so that repentance is practical.

### Allah’s Love and Moral Economy

Allah does not love the ungrateful sinner, while He loves the charitable and those who do good. The contrast invites the believer to align financial choices with divine love. He earns lawfully, gives generously, avoids waste, and supports systems that protect the vulnerable.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah accepts only what is good. The quality of the money matters before it is given, and the manner of giving matters afterward. Charity grows when it is rooted in lawful provision and sincere intention.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:277

> Surely those who believe, do righteous deeds, establish prayer, and give zakāh will have their reward with their Lord. They will have no fear, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

### The Righteous Alternative

After warning against ribā, the Qur’an presents the path of faith, righteous action, prayer, and zakāh. These qualities restore the balance that exploitative wealth destroys. Faith directs the heart to Allah, righteous deeds benefit creation, prayer maintains the relationship with the Lord, and zakāh purifies wealth and supports the vulnerable.

The list is not a claim that believers never sin. It describes the orientation of people who believe, repent, and establish their obligations. A person may struggle financially or spiritually while sincerely returning to Allah. The promise belongs to those who do not make sin and exploitation their settled method.

### Establishing Prayer

“Establish prayer”—*aqāmū al-ṣalāh*—means more than performing occasional movements. It includes guarding the obligatory times, purification, direction, recitation, humility, and the ethical effect of ṣalāh. Prayer reminds the wealthy lender and the vulnerable borrower that both stand equally before Allah.

A person who prays but uses his financial power to oppress must allow prayer to correct him. The Qur’an says that prayer restrains indecency and wrongdoing (29:45). Its benefit appears when worship reshapes contracts, wages, debt collection, and treatment of family.

### Giving Zakāh

Zakāh is a structured obligation on eligible wealth, calculated and distributed according to the rules of Islamic law. It is not merely a voluntary donation or a substitute for honest business. By requiring the wealthy to share a portion, zakāh challenges the idea that wealth is absolute ownership and creates a social bond between people with different financial conditions.

The giver must calculate honestly, avoid delaying payment, and deliver it to eligible recipients. Administrators must be trustworthy and transparent. A person may also give voluntary charity beyond zakāh, especially where urgent need exceeds the formal collection system.

### Faith and Economic Ethics

The placement after the ribā verses shows that economic conduct is part of belief. A person cannot separate spiritual identity from the way he lends, borrows, hires, prices, and pays. Faith requires refusing exploitation even when it is common or profitable.

The believer seeks lawful trade, interest-free help where possible, fair partnerships, and debt relief. He does not assume that religious vocabulary makes a contract ethical. The substance and consequences matter.

### Reward with the Lord

“Their reward is with their Lord” assures believers that lawful restraint is not wasted. A person may refuse a profitable but exploitative contract, give zakāh, forgive a debtor, or support a poor family without receiving public recognition. Allah records the sacrifice and returns it in a form better than the servant can calculate.

The phrase also removes dependence on worldly approval. The believer seeks the reward with Allah, not from the borrower, recipient, employer, or audience. This protects charity from becoming a claim of ownership over others.

### No Fear and No Grief

The promise of no fear and no grief concerns ultimate security in the Hereafter. The righteous may still experience anxiety, illness, poverty, and loss in this world. They are not promised exemption from every trial; they are promised that faith and obedience will lead to Allah’s protection and reward.

In financial life, this promise helps believers resist Satan’s threat of poverty. They plan responsibly but do not let fear make them unjust. They give, pay, and forgive while trusting that Allah knows what they need.

### A Community of Mutual Care

Prayer and zakāh together build a community in which worship and social responsibility reinforce one another. The mosque should not be separated from the poor, the marketplace from ethics, or religious teaching from economic justice. Leaders should make zakāh accessible and ensure that recipients are not humiliated.

A society that establishes prayer but ignores hunger has preserved an outward symbol while losing a central purpose. A society that gives charity but abandons worship loses the source of lasting accountability. The Qur’an joins both.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:278

> O believers, be mindful of Allah and give up whatever remains of interest, if you are truly believers.

**Commentary:**

### The Final Call to Abandon Ribā

After explaining the moral difference between trade and ribā and contrasting the growth of charity with the destruction of interest, Allah addresses believers directly. They must fear Allah and abandon whatever remains of ribā. The command concerns outstanding increases attached to debts, not the lawful principal that was originally lent.

The phrase “whatever remains”—*mā baqiya min al-ribā*—requires stopping the continuing claim rather than collecting every last increment promised by a contract. A creditor may recover the principal legitimately owed, but he may not continue demanding an unlawful increase because it was written into an agreement.

### Taqwā and Faith

The verse links abandonment of ribā to *taqwā* and genuine faith. Taqwā is not only a feeling of fear; it is protecting oneself from Allah’s displeasure through action. A person who knows that a financial practice is prohibited but continues because it is profitable places money above obedience.

The wording “if you are believers” is a severe moral challenge, not a permission for ordinary people to declare others outside Islam. It shows that financial ethics are part of living faith. The believer’s testimony must appear in contracts, lending, employment, and debt collection.

### Principal versus Increase

Islam does not forbid a lender from recovering the principal. The Qur’an’s next verse says, “For you is your principal; you do not wrong, nor are you wronged.” This balance protects both parties. A creditor should not lose the money genuinely lent, and a debtor should not be charged an increase merely because time passed.

The exact classification of modern financial products can be complex. Some contracts labelled interest-free may hide fixed increases, while some charges compensate for genuine services or administrative costs. Muslims should present the full contract to qualified scholars and financial experts rather than relying on marketing language.

### Exiting Existing Contracts

A person already involved in a ribā-based contract should seek advice about how to exit without causing greater injustice. He should not enter new arrangements, renegotiate toward further exploitation, or conceal the issue. If he has taken unlawful increase from a known person, he should return the right. If he is a borrower facing penalties, he seeks lawful relief, restructuring, or charitable support.

Repentance is practical. It may involve changing banks, refusing predatory credit, building emergency savings, using partnerships, joining cooperative finance, and supporting interest-free loan funds. Individuals cannot always transform the entire financial system immediately, but they can move toward obedience step by step.

### Protecting the Debtor

The command especially protects vulnerable debtors. A person may borrow because of illness, unemployment, education, housing, or emergency. Adding a guaranteed increase can turn temporary difficulty into a permanent burden. Islamic ethics require compassion, transparency, and reasonable repayment arrangements.

The creditor’s power is a test. He should not use a written contract to justify humiliation, harassment, seizure of essential property, or public exposure. The next verse and 2:280 call for limiting the claim to principal and giving relief to those in hardship.

### Community Alternatives

Abandoning ribā requires institutions, not only personal slogans. Communities can create *qarḍ ḥasan* funds, mutual aid, ethical investment, cooperative housing, debt counselling, and transparent charity. Employers can provide fair wages and emergency assistance, reducing workers’ dependence upon predatory lenders.

Scholars and finance professionals should collaborate. A legal form that avoids the word “interest” but reproduces the same unjust burden does not fulfil the Qur’anic purpose. Substance, risk, ownership, and real benefit matter.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:279

> If you do not, then be informed of a war from Allah and His Messenger. But if you repent, then you may have your principal—neither wronging nor being wronged.

**Commentary:**

### The Most Severe Economic Warning

This is one of the strongest warnings in the Qur’an. Those who refuse to abandon ribā after the command are told to expect a war from Allah and His Messenger. The language shows that exploitative interest is not a minor technical violation. It attacks the foundations of justice, burdens the weak, and turns lending into a mechanism of domination.

“War from Allah and His Messenger” is not a permission for individuals to commit violence against lenders, bankers, or borrowers. It is a declaration of divine opposition and a warning of grave punishment and social consequence. Any worldly legal enforcement belongs to legitimate authority and due process. Vigilantism is itself transgression.

### Repentance Restores the Balance

The verse immediately gives the path of repentance: leave ribā and retain the principal. The creditor is not required to surrender the amount genuinely lent, and the debtor is not required to pay an unlawful increase. “Neither wronging nor being wronged”—*lā taẓlimūna wa-lā tuẓlamūn*—is the governing balance.

The creditor wrongs the debtor by demanding more than the principal under an exploitative debt. The debtor wrongs the creditor by refusing to repay the principal when able. Repentance creates justice for both parties rather than turning opposition to ribā into a licence for theft.

### What Counts as the Principal

The principal is the amount genuinely advanced or the lawful value owed, not hidden fees, compounding penalties, or invented charges. Some costs may compensate for actual administrative service, inflation, collateral, or loss, but the classification must be assessed carefully. A creditor cannot relabel a guaranteed increase as a service charge merely to evade the prohibition.

Because modern financial arrangements are complex, a person should show the full documents to a qualified scholar and a competent financial adviser. The Qur’anic rule is clear, but its application to mortgages, business finance, late penalties, credit cards, and currencies requires expertise.

### Repentance Is Not Impossible

The severe warning is followed by an accessible path. A lender who stops collecting unlawful increase, returns identifiable gains, and restructures debts can repent. A borrower who was compelled by circumstances seeks relief and avoids new contracts where possible. Allah knows the difference between deliberate exploitation and a person trapped in a system while trying to escape.

Repentance must not be used to collapse a vulnerable family or to abandon every lawful obligation. Mediation, refinancing through permissible means, charitable support, and gradual settlement may be necessary. The goal is to end the injustice while preserving life and rights.

### Economic War and Social Trust

Ribā damages trust because it treats another person’s hardship as a guaranteed revenue stream. It can widen the distance between those who own capital and those who must borrow for necessities. The Qur’an’s language of war reflects not only the individual sin but the social conflict created by a system that enriches one party through another’s vulnerability.

The remedy includes fair wages, cooperative finance, charitable loans, ethical investment, bankruptcy protections, and relief for people facing illness or unemployment. A society cannot demand that individuals avoid exploitation while providing no humane alternatives.

### No Wronging and No Being Wronged

The double negation is a concise principle of economic justice. No creditor may take more than his right, and no debtor may consume another’s property without repayment. It also applies to contracts, inheritance, employment, and public finance. Justice is not achieved by reversing who is exploited.

The lender should document the debt, give reasonable time, and refrain from harassment. The debtor should communicate honestly, pay according to ability, and not use the prohibition of ribā to justify deliberate default. Both should remember Allah’s final account.

### Public Responsibility

Authorities have a duty to regulate predatory lending, protect consumers, disclose costs, and provide fair avenues for dispute. Religious leaders should not issue simplistic rulings without understanding financial reality, and financial professionals should not hide exploitation behind technical complexity.

The verse calls the entire society to reform. The warning is addressed to the people who persist in ribā, but the remedy requires institutions that make justice possible.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:280

> If the debtor is in hardship, then grant him a delay until ease. But if you remit it as charity, that is better for you, if only you knew.

**Commentary:**

### Mercy toward a Distressed Debtor

After prohibiting ribā and limiting the creditor to the principal, this verse gives a direct rule for debtors in hardship: grant time until ease. A creditor may have a legitimate claim, but he may not demand payment from someone who genuinely cannot pay in a way that destroys food, housing, medical care, or the basic needs of dependants.

The command applies to real hardship, not to a person who hides wealth or refuses repayment while able. The debtor should communicate honestly, disclose what is relevant, and make a reasonable plan. The creditor should investigate without humiliation and distinguish inability from deliberate evasion.

### Delay Is an Obligation of Mercy

*Naẓirah ilā maysarah* means waiting until the debtor reaches ease. The period may require restructuring, reduced instalments, a temporary pause, or another arrangement that preserves both parties’ rights. No additional interest may be charged for the delay; extending time is not an opportunity to increase the debt.

The Prophet ﷺ praised the person who gives a debtor time or reduces the claim. He told of a man who used to instruct his servants to be lenient with the one in difficulty, and Allah forgave him, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Mercy in debt is therefore an act of worship, not merely a business concession.

### Remitting the Debt Is Better

“If you remit it as charity, that is better for you.” A creditor who forgives some or all of a debt may receive a reward greater than the financial return. The recommendation is not an instruction to force every lender to forgive every debt regardless of his own ability. A poor creditor may need repayment, and the law protects his property. The verse praises voluntary remission when the creditor can afford it.

Forgiveness can be partial: reducing the amount, cancelling penalties, accepting a lower settlement, or giving a longer period. It must be documented clearly so that the debtor is not later harassed and the heirs understand what was forgiven.

### The Debtor’s Responsibility

Mercy does not make debt meaningless. A person who borrows with no intention to repay, hides assets, spends extravagantly while pretending hardship, or takes repeated loans through deception commits a serious wrong. The Prophet ﷺ warned that whoever takes people’s wealth intending to repay it will receive Allah’s help, while whoever takes it intending to destroy it will be destroyed.

The debtor should avoid unnecessary borrowing, record the agreement, prioritize repayment, and seek counselling early. Asking for a delay is more honest than taking a new predatory loan to cover the old one. Family and community support can prevent a temporary crisis from becoming a cycle.

### Protecting the Creditor

The creditor also has rights. He may request documentation, collateral, a guarantor, or a realistic repayment schedule, provided the terms are lawful and not exploitative. If the debtor is genuinely able but refuses, the creditor may pursue a lawful remedy. He may not use threats, public shaming, violence, or unlawful seizure.

The Qur’anic balance is neither “all debt is forgiven” nor “the lender may demand anything.” It protects property and mercy together. A just court or mediator can help when trust has broken down.

### Charity and Economic Solidarity

The command to remit debt builds a culture in which wealth serves people rather than enslaving them. Zakah may assist debtors who are genuinely burdened, and communities can establish interest-free loan funds, emergency grants, and debt counselling. A person who has been helped should later help others when able.

The reward of remission can be greater than the money retained because it repairs a life. A family spared eviction may recover, a student may finish education, and a sick person may receive treatment. Allah knows these consequences even when the creditor sees only a cancelled balance.

### “If Only You Knew”

The closing phrase teaches that the creditor’s immediate calculation is incomplete. He sees the amount he could collect; Allah sees the reward, the preserved family, the blessing in what remains, and the mercy shown. The servant asks Allah for wisdom before choosing between full collection, delay, reduction, and forgiveness.

This wisdom must be joined to boundaries. Forgiveness should not be used to enable addiction or repeated fraud, and a debtor should not be shamed for accepting lawful assistance. The aim is relief, responsibility, and restored stability.
