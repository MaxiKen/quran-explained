## Verse 21

Verse 21 says: *Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse pronounces a verdict on the worst of the People of the Book's history: those who deny the signs of Allah, kill prophets without any right, and kill ordinary people who command justice — announce to them the good news of a painful punishment.

The verse is a rebuke addressed directly to the situation of the Jews of Madinah. Ibn Kathir frames the whole passage as "Chastising the Jews for Their Disbelief and for Killing the Prophets and Righteous People": this ayah chastises the People of the Book for the transgression they committed in the past and in more recent times, by their denials of Allah's ayat and the Messengers. The reason, he says, was their defiance and rejection, their denial of the truth, and their refusal to follow it. The verse does not list three unrelated crimes; it lists the genealogy of one crime: the same rejection that leads a people to deny the signs is the rejection that leads them to kill the prophets — and the same arrogance that kills the prophets is the arrogance that kills the ordinary people who stand up and command justice.

"kill the prophets unjustly" — the phrase *bi ghayr haqq* (without right, without cause) is essential. Ibn Kathir clarifies: they killed many prophets when those prophets conveyed what Allah legislated for them, "without cause or criminal behavior committed by these Prophets, for they only called them to the truth." The killing was not a punishment for crime; it was the crime itself. The prophets were the most innocent of people — they brought guidance and received death. Al-Jalalayn adds the tradition reported of the Jews: they are reported to have killed forty-three prophets, and to have been forbidden from doing so by one hundred and seventy devout worshippers among them, each of whom was killed immediately. The numbers may vary in the reports, but the pattern is the same in all of them: the community that kills its prophets also kills its reformers, one after the other.

"and kill people who stand up for justice" — *al-qist* is justice, equity, fairness. The ordinary people who commanded justice, who told the powerful the truth, who stood at the side of the prophets — they were killed too. This, says Ibn Kathir, "demonstrates the worst type of arrogance," and he cites the prophetic saying: "Kibr (arrogance) is refusing the truth and degrading people." The link is exact: the person who kills the one who commands justice refuses the truth, and the person who refuses the truth and kills its bearers degrades people. That is why, Ibn Kathir continues, "when they rejected the truth and acted arrogantly towards the creation, Allah punished them with humiliation and disgrace in this life, and humiliating torment in the Hereafter."

"give them good news of a painful punishment" — the command uses the word "good news" (*bashshir — to give glad tidings*) for the worst possible news. Both Al-Jalalayn and Maududi identify this as a sarcastic remark, a bitter irony. Maududi: "Its purpose is to bring home to them that the misdeeds about which they are so jubilant, and which they regard as their proud achievements, will ultimately lead them to a painful end." Al-Jalalayn calls it "sarcastic ridicule of them." The irony is the point: the people who celebrate the murder of prophets as a victory are being "congratulated" on a defeat. Al-Jalalayn reads the word *fa-bashshirhum* as part of the predicate: "if they disbelieve… then give them good tidings…" — the announcement follows the condition.

The verse matters to the surah's argument because the Christians of Najran and the Jews of Madinah both claimed descent from the chosen families (3:33) and special standing with Allah. The Qur'an's answer: lineage does not excuse history. If your people's record is the murder of the prophets and the killers of those who command justice, then the claim "we are the chosen" is a claim against the very message the chosen ones brought. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings agree that the "good news" is the bitter announcement that pride in the crimes will end in the punishment. For the reader, the verse is a warning about the pattern that appears in every era: the society that silences its truth-tellers is the society that has begun to kill its prophets, whether by the sword or by the character assassination, the marginalization, and the mockery that accomplish the same thing in other forms.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 22

Verse 22 says: *They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers.*

In simple terms, the verse states the consequence of the conduct described in the previous verse: the deeds of such people are made worthless — in this world and the Hereafter — and they will have no one to save them.

The verse continues the verdict of 3:21 and completes it with the two losses: the loss of the deeds and the loss of the helpers. Ibn Kathir relates the verse directly to the previous one: these are "those whose works will be lost in this world and in the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers."

"whose deeds are wasted" — the Arabic verb is *habitat*, from *hubut*: the falling, the collapsing, the becoming void. It is the word the Qur'an uses for deeds that have been performed but have lost their value — things that looked like investments and turned out to be nothing. The classical exegetes explain the two horizons of the loss. In this world: the deeds yield no real benefit — no security, no honor that lasts, no satisfaction that survives; the killing of the prophets produced nothing for the nation that killed them except the judgment of heaven, the humiliation, and the disgrace that Ibn Kathir describes as their punishment in this life. In the Hereafter: whatever the outward quantities of their record, the books of deeds are empty of weight because the foundation — faith in the signs — was lacking. A building without a foundation is not a smaller building; it is nothing. The Qur'an's principle throughout is that deeds are weighed not by their bulk alone but by their faith and intention; the deeds of those who denied the signs, killed the prophets, and killed the just are precisely the deeds whose foundation was absent.

"they will have no helpers" — *ma lahum min nasirin* — they will have no supporters, no allies, no intercessors on their side. This answers the greatest source of their confidence. The people being described had helpers in the world: wealth (3:10), children (3:10), arms, nations, and alliances. The verse declares that when the accounts are settled, all of those helpers will be gone. Ibn Kathir's cross-references in the wider tafsir pull in the same theme: 9:55 — "So let not their wealth nor their children amaze you"; 3:196–197 — the brief enjoyment, then the abode of Hell. The helpers they counted on were themselves the fuel for the Fire (3:10) and the brief enjoyment (3:197).

For the reader, the verse presents one of the central doctrines of the Qur'an about the worth of deeds: deeds can be wasted, and the wasting is not random. The deeds are wasted when the heart's foundation is the denial of the truth and the rejection of those who command it. The man who kills the truth-teller and then builds a large record of activity will find that the record is void — activity is not the same as substance. And the second half answers the human fear of standing alone: on that Day, no one stands alone before Allah without his deeds being what he has — and those who trusted in helpers other than Allah will find that the helpers were not there. The reverse is also implied and is stated in the same surah (3:195): for those who truly believe, Allah does not waste the deeds of the doers.

There is also a precise point in the order of the three elements that the Qur'an presents: the signs denied, the prophets killed, the justice-commanders killed — and then, in the next breath, the deeds wasted and the helpers absent. The three crimes and the two losses are one logical sequence. A person whose standing in the world is built on the murder of the truth and its bearers has built his whole account on the thing that Allah weighs against him; and when the account is taken, the "helpers" — the armies, the alliances, the wealth, the children, the men of the tribe, the lineage of which they boasted — are the same things the earlier verses of this surah have already declared unable to avail (3:10). The Qur'an is being consistent: the world's props, which the disbelievers count on, are precisely the props that fail. The verse therefore also serves as a warning to every generation that equates the survival of a cause with the survival of its helpers: the nation that kills its prophets and its just ones in the belief that this secures its position is destroying the very record it will be judged by — and it will find that the helpers it kept were not the help it needed.

The verse should also be read against the background of the People of the Book's own self-understanding. They regarded themselves as the heirs of the prophets and the bearers of the covenant; the Qur'an's verdict is that the deeds of those who rejected the truth are void even in the lineage that claims the prophets. Ibn Kathir's framing in the following verse (3:23) completes the picture: these are the same people who, when called to the Book of Allah to judge between them, turn away. Their history is not a story of keeping the covenant but of breaking it — and the broken covenant is the voided deed. For the Muslim reader, the verse is a standing mirror: the claim of the community is not the evidence of the community; the deeds are the evidence, and the deeds are voided by the denial of the signs, not by the grandeur of the name. The religion's true wealth is the deeds that survive the weighing — and the deeds survive only where the foundation of faith is intact.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 23

Verse 23 says: *Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures? Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly.*

In simple terms, the verse points out the contradiction in the behaviour of the People of the Book: they claim to follow their Scripture, yet when they are invited to let the Book of Allah judge between them, a party of them turns away.

The verse begins the second movement of the surah's argument with the People of the Book: the critique of their refusal to refer to their own Book. Ibn Kathir states it plainly: "Allah criticizes the Jews and Christians who claim to follow their Books, the Tawrah and the Injil, because when they are called to refer to these Books where Allah commanded them to follow Muhammad, they turn away with aversion. This censure and criticism from Allah was all because of their defiance and rejection."

"those who were given a portion of the Scriptures" — *nasiban min al-Kitab* — a share, a portion of the Book. The phrase is precise: not "those who were given the Book" but "those who were given a portion" — the Jews and the Christians had received the Torah and the Gospel; they had a share of the divinely revealed heritage. Maududi clarifies: the expression here refers to the Jewish and Christian religious scholars, and "the Book of God referred to here is the Torah and the Injil." The criticism is not that they lacked the Book but that they had it and would not submit to it.

"Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes" — Al-Jalalayn records the historical occasion: the verse was revealed concerning the Jews, two of whom had fornicated. They asked the Prophet to adjudicate the case. He ruled that they be stoned, but they refused. When the Torah was brought and consulted, the same verdict was found — and so the two were stoned, but the Jews became wrathful. Maududi generalizes the point: "They are asked to acknowledge the Book of God as the final arbiter in all matters, and to submit to its judgment, accepting as right whatever this Book holds to be right, and as wrong whatever it holds to be wrong." The irony is the structure of the verse: people who say "we follow the Book" are invited to the Book — and they turn away.

"some of them turn away heedlessly" — *thumma yatawalla* — then a party of them turns away, and they are *mu'ridun* — averse, turning aside, refusing. The two verbs are progressive: the invitation comes, the turning happens, and the refusal is a settled state. It is not an argument against the Book that is given; we are not told they raised a reasoned objection. The verse says they turned away — the gesture of the person whose mind was made up before the hearing.

For the reader, the verse is one of the Qur'an's sharpest lessons about the misuse of Scripture. The People of the Book were not guilty of ignorance of the Book; they were guilty of selective allegiance to it — they accepted what suited them and refused what confronted them. This is the universal religious failure: to treat the revealed text as a library to be browsed rather than a judge to be obeyed. The phrase "the Book of Allah to settle their disputes" is the key: Scripture is not primarily a source of identity or prestige; it is an arbiter — the final reference when people differ. The community that uses its Book to divide rather than to adjudicate, to claim rather than to obey, reproduces the exact problem this verse condemns. And the verse's unspoken question to every reader — including the Muslim reader — is: when the Book of Allah is brought to judge between you and your opponent, do you submit, or do you turn away?

## Verse 24

Verse 24 says: *This is because they say, “The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.” They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying.*

In simple terms, the verse exposes the root of the People of the Book's arrogance: they believed the Fire would not touch them except for a few days — a comfortable lie of their own invention, which deceived them.

"This is because they say" — the verse gives the reason behind the turning away of 3:23. The refusal to submit to the Book was not a coincidence of temperament; it had a doctrine behind it: the doctrine of their own immunity from the Fire. Ibn Kathir: "what made them dare to challenge and defy the truth is their false claim that Allah will only punish them for seven days in the Fire, a day for every one thousand years in this life." The exegetes report the tradition: the Jews claimed that the fire would touch them only for the number of days the generations they had worshiped the calf — or in the count of the days of their wandering in the wilderness — and then they would be released. Classical references (cf. 2:80) record the claim: "The Fire will not touch us except for a few days" — and the Qur'an answers it there as here. The essential point, as Ibn Kathir says, is that it is "their false claim" — and "it is they who have invented this notion, and Allah did not grant them authority to support this claim."

"The Fire will not touch us except for a few days" — the doctrine is a perfect religious self-deception: it keeps the idea of accountability (there is a Fire) while removing the weight of accountability (but only for a few days). Al-Jalalayn and the classical commentators note that the "few days" (*ayyaman ma'dudat*) was counted variously — the days of the calf-worship, or a day for each thousand years of the world — the exact count differing among the reports, which itself shows the flimsiness of the doctrine: the foundation was unverified tradition, not revelation.

"They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying" — *gharra* — they were deceived, deluded; *ma kanu yaftarun* — what they used to fabricate, invent. Ibn Kathir: "what caused them to remain on their false creed is that they deceived themselves, believing that the Fire will only touch them for a few days for their errors." The deception is self-deception, and the lying is their own: the invention was not from Allah, not from their scripture, and not from a prophet; it was the product of wishful thinking dressed as doctrine. The verse's language is exact: the thing that deceived them in their *religion* was the thing they *fabricated* in their religion. The lie is internal: they did not merely believe a lie; they built, shaped, and cultivated the lie until it became the ceiling of their faith.

For the reader, the verse is a lesson in the most dangerous of all religious errors: the doctrine of exemption. The People of the Book believed they were exempt from the full consequences of their conduct — not because of repentance, not because of obedience, but because of identity and invented privilege. When a community — or a person — believes that the Fire "will not touch" it except lightly, it loses the very thing that keeps faith honest: the fear of consequence and the humility before the judge. The verse does not say they denied the Fire; it says they counted it cheap. The Qur'anic alternative is stated at the end of the surah (3:185): every soul will taste death, and rewards are full on the Day of Judgment — no one's account is a "few days" — the full settlement belongs to all, and its burden is removed only by those who are drawn near to the Fire and saved from it (3:185, 3:187).

## Verse 25

Verse 25 says: *But how ˹horrible˺ will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt—when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged!*

In simple terms, the verse confronts the comfortable doctrine of the previous verse with the reality of the Day: what will their condition be when Allah gathers them on the Day about which there is no doubt, and every soul is paid exactly what it earned — with no injustice?

"But how ˹horrible˺ will it be" — the verse asks the question that the People of the Book had never asked themselves. Ibn Kathir asks it in the voice of the tafsir: "What will their condition be like after they have uttered this lie about Allah, rejected His Messengers and killed His Prophets and their scholars who enjoined righteousness and forbade evil? Allah will ask them about all this and punish them for what they have done." The rhetorical question is a shattering of the doctrine of 3:24: you think the Fire will touch you for a few days — but what will it be like when the gathering happens and the accounts are exact? Al-Jalalayn's framing of the same question: the gathering cancels the "few days" and replaces it with the unbounded reckoning.

"when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt" — the phrase is the one used in 3:9 for the prayer of the well-grounded in knowledge: *yawman la rayba fihi* — a Day about which there is no doubt. The same Day that the believers confess and pray for is the Day the deniers will face. The "no doubt" is the anchor: this is not a symbol, a myth, or a possibility — it is a certainty stated in the same grammatical form as the faith, and Ibn Kathir adds: "there is no doubt that this Day will come."

"when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged" — *wuffiyat kullu nafs* — every soul will be paid in full, delivered the complete amount — *ma kasabat* — for what it earned — *wa hum la yuzlamun* — and they will not be wronged. Two things are emphasized. The payment is complete: not reduced, not approximated, not discounted — the full amount. And the settlement is just: there is no wrong — no soul bears another's burden, no deed is misrecorded, no good deed is lost. The doctrine of the "few days" is answered by the doctrine of the exact account: the count is not a few days; the account is the full sum, and the sum is what each one earned.

The verse is the Qur'an's standard answer to every religious claim of exemption based on identity. The Jews said: we are the chosen, the Fire will touch us lightly. The Qur'an answers: the gathering is sure, and the payment is full. The Christian of Najran might say: we are the people of the covenant; the Qur'an will make the same answer. And the Muslim who claims the ummah is exempt from the consequences of its own sins is being warned by the same verse: the Day is sure, the account is complete, the wrong is none. The comfort of the believer is not the doctrine of exemption but the mercy of the One who accounts — who says, in the same passage, "Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants" (3:30) and that the deeds of the doers are not wasted (3:195). The believer's hope is not that the Fire will be cheap but that the Lord, in His mercy, will forgive — and that the forgiven account is better than the cheap account.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 26

Verse 26 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please; You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please—all good is in Your Hands. Surely You ˹alone˺ are Most Capable of everything.*

In simple terms, the verse turns the whole argument into a prayer: say, O Allah, Owner of all sovereignty — You give the kingdom to whom You will and take it from whom You will; You honor whom You will and humiliate whom You will; all good is in Your hand; You are capable of everything.

The verse is a turning point in the surah. Ibn Kathir titles the section "Encouraging Gratitude": "Say, O Muhammad, while praising your Lord, thanking Him, relying in all matters upon Him and trusting in Him." The argument against the People of the Book is not left as abstract doctrine; it is turned into the acknowledgment of the one who holds all authority — and therefore the one who gave the prophethood and the kingdom to this ummah can take them from another and give them to this one.

Allah says *al-Malik* — the Owner, the King, the Sovereign — *al-Mulk* — the sovereignty, the kingdom, the dominion. Ibn Kathir: "meaning, all sovereignty is Yours." The verse enumerates the acts of the Sovereign in four pairs: You give the sovereignty to whom You will; You remove it from whom You will; You honor whom You will; You humiliate whom You will. Ibn Kathir's commentary: "You are the Giver, You are the Taker, it is Your will that occurs, and whatever You do not will does not occur." The verses refute those who thought they could decide for Allah. Ibn Kathir cites 43:31 (they said, "Why is not this Qur'an sent down to some great man of the two towns?") and 43:32 ("Is it they who would portion out the Mercy of your Lord?"), and 6:124 ("Allah knows best with whom to place His Message") — the authority to appoint messengers, to give kingdoms, to honor and humiliate, is Allah's alone, and His choice is made with perfect wisdom.

The verse has a specific occasion recorded in Al-Jalalayn: when the Prophet promised his community sovereignty over the lands of Persia and Byzantium, the hypocrites said, "How preposterous!" — and this verse was revealed. Ibn Kathir takes the historical point further: this ayah encourages gratitude for the favors Allah granted His Messenger and his Ummah: "Allah transferred the prophethood from the Children of Israel to the Arab, Qurashi, Makkan, unlettered Prophet, the Final and Last of all Prophets and the Messenger of Allah to all mankind and Jinn." He notes the Prophet was endowed with the best qualities of the prophets before him, with more knowledge of Allah, His law, and the matters of the past and the future — and that the Ummah was allowed to reach the eastern and western parts of the world, with his religion and law given dominance over all others.

"all good is in Your Hands" — *bi yadika al-khayr* — the good is in Your hand. Al-Jalalayn notes the reading "in Your hand is good, that is, as well as evil" — meaning the question of good and evil is one of the ordering of the decree, with the good being the dominant and ultimate theme. Ibn Kathir's reading places the whole verse in the context of the divine perfection: Allah's will occurs; nothing can resist it. And the end of the verse: *innaka 'ala kulli shay'in qadir* — Surely You are capable of everything. The sovereignty, the honor, the humiliation, and the good are all within the capacity of the One addressed.

For the reader, the verse is both doctrine and comfort. Doctrinally: the distribution of power in the world — the rise and fall of kingdoms, the honor of one community and the humiliation of another, the appointment of the prophets and the transfer of the message — is Allah's will, not the product of human scheming, ancestry, or effort alone. Comfortably: the believer who feels weak, outnumbered, and humiliated in the world can say this prayer and mean it — the One who gives the kingdom to whom He wills is the One who gave this ummah its place, and the One whose hand holds the good is the One who will decide the outcome of the dispute with the People of the Book. The verse is also the beginning of the famous Qur'anic du'a that Muslims recite on Fridays and in times of trial: the acknowledgment that no authority exists and no honor or disgrace occurs except by the Owner of all authority, in whose hand is all good.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 27

Verse 27 says: *You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit.”*

In simple terms, the verse continues the prayer with the evidence of Allah's power in creation: He merges the night into the day and the day into the night; He brings the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living; and He provides for whom He wills, without measure.

The verse is the second half of the du'a that begins in 3:26. After the doctrine of sovereignty in the realm of kingdoms, now the doctrine of sovereignty in the realm of nature — the daily and annual cycles, the biological cycles, the mechanism of provision. Ibn Kathir: "You make the night enter into the day, and You make the day enter into the night — meaning, You take from the length of one of them and add it to the shortness of the other, so that they become equal, and take from the length of one of them and add it to the other so that they are not equal. This occurs throughout the seasons of the year: spring, summer, fall and winter." The daily cycles and the lengthening and shortening days through the seasons are the regular demonstration of the One who merges and divides at will.

"You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living" — the phrase covers the whole range of creation: the plant from the dry earth, the drop from the cloud (as the exegetes explain), the living creature from the dead sperm and ovum — and in reverse, the dead from the living: every living being's death, the dry earth from the once-growing land. The classical readings include the seed-and-harvest example, the egg-and-bird example, the believing-offspring-from-disbelieving-parent and the reverse, and the day and night themselves as the "dead" and "living" of the cycle. The point is the same in every reading: the living and the dead, the growing and the withered, the emerging and the passing — all are in the hands of the One addressed in 3:26.

"And You provide for whoever You will without limit" — *bi ghayr hisab* — without reckoning, without measure, without count. The phrase is the same one used in 3:37 about Maryam's provision, and in 2:212 and 24:38 about the provision that has no arithmetic. It means provision measured not by the recipient's merit alone but by the Giver's will: He provides for whom He will, without limit, without calculation, without prior deserving. Ibn Kathir's treatment ties it to the whole verse: the One who manages the seasons, the cycles, the living and the dead can provide whom He will without measure.

The verse is the natural completion of 3:26's argument. The People of the Book rested their privilege on ancestry and their own claim; the Qur'an answers: the same hand that turns the day and night, the same hand that produces the living from the dead, the same hand that gives provision without count is the hand that gives and takes the sovereignty, the honor, and the prophecy. If Allah can do all of that, He can give the kingdom to the believers, transfer the message from one lineage to another, and honor whom He wills — and no one's ancestry can block Him. For the reader, the verse is the daily evidence: the dawn that lengthens and shortens, the harvest from the dead earth, the provision that arrives from unexpected places — the believer sees the Owner of the kingdom in the ordinary cycles of the world, and reading 3:26–27 together is reading the same sovereignty in both the affairs of nations and the affairs of nature.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 28

Verse 28 says: *Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah— unless it is a precaution against their tyranny. And Allah warns you about Himself. And to Allah is the final return.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the rule of loyalty: believers should not take disbelievers as guardians and allies in place of fellow believers; whoever does so cuts himself off from Allah — except in a case of real precaution against their harm. Allah warns you against Himself, and to Him is the final return.

The verse is the Qur'an's central teaching on the boundary of the Muslim community's loyalty. Ibn Kathir titles the section "The Prohibition of Supporting the Disbelievers": "Allah prohibited His believing servants from becoming supporters of the disbelievers, or to take them as comrades with whom they develop friendships, rather than the believers." The word *awliya'* (guardians, allies, protectors, patrons) carries both legal and emotional weight: the person whose protection, support, counsel, trust, and loyalty the believer relies on. The verse does not forbid ordinary civil interaction, trade, or fair dealing with non-Muslims; it forbids the *walayah* — the allegiance — that makes the disbeliever the believer's protector in the sense that the believer's fate is tied to him.

"whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah" — the phrase is severe: *fa laysa min Allah fi shay'* — he has nothing from Allah, he is not of Allah's side, he is cut off from the help of Allah. Ibn Kathir: "whoever commits this act that Allah has prohibited, then Allah will discard him." The severity is the logic of the matter: the believer's help comes from Allah; if he places his loyalty, protection, and dependence in the hands of the enemies of Allah's cause, he has chosen another lord in the place of Allah's help, and the two loyalties cannot stand together. He cites 60:1 — "Take not My enemies and your enemies as friends, showing affection towards them" — and 4:144 — "Do you wish to offer Allah a manifest proof against yourselves?" — and 5:51 — "Whoever befriends them, then surely he is one of them" — and 8:73.

"unless it is a precaution against their tyranny" — *illa an tattaqu minhum tuqat* — except that you fear from them a danger, a harm. The exception is the famous *tuqyah*: the outward precaution of a believer who is in a position of weakness and fears for his safety. Ibn Kathir: "except those believers who in some areas or times fear for their safety from the disbelievers. In this case, such believers are allowed to show friendship to the disbelievers outwardly, but never inwardly." Al-Jalalayn: "unless you fear something, in which case you may show patronage to them through words but not in your hearts — this was before the hegemony of Islam, and the dispensation applies to any individual residing in a land with no say in it." Al-Bukhari recorded that Abu ad-Darda said, "We smile in the face of some people although our hearts curse them," and Al-Bukhari quotes Al-Hasan: "The Tuqyah is allowed until the Day of Resurrection." The exception is narrow, situational, and measured: it is precaution of the tongue and the outward manner to protect life and limb, never the inward loyalty of the heart; it does not legalize friendship with the enemies of Allah as a settled choice.

"And Allah warns you about Himself" — *wa yuhadhdhirukum Allah nafsahu* — He warns you concerning Himself, His own self. Ibn Kathir: "He warns you against His anger and the severe torment He prepared for those who give their support to His enemies, and those who have enmity with His friends." The warning is not against an abstract principle but against the Person whose favor is the only thing that matters: do not trade My help for theirs. The phrase is repeated in 3:30 — the same warning about Himself — and it is one of the Qur'an's strongest expressions of the personal relationship: the believer is warned not to offend the Lord directly, not through a rule, but against His very self.

"And to Allah is the final return" — *ila Allah al-masir* — to Allah is the destination, the end of the journey. The return resolves the matter: whatever alliances a person makes in the world, the journey ends at Allah — and He will reward or punish each person according to their deeds (Ibn Kathir). The verse's last sentence is the believer's answer to the fear that drives him to the wrong alliance: the safety you seek from people will not follow you to the destination; the safety that matters is the One to whom the journey ends.

For the reader, the verse is the standard for the modern believer's loyalties: the test is not superficial contact but the structure of dependence. Who is your protector in the deepest sense? Whose side are you on when the cause of Allah is contested? The verse permits caution and even outward tact in a position of weakness, but it does not permit alliance as a settled way of life — and the warning at its heart is that the One who warns you is the One to whom you are returning.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 29

Verse 29 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”*

In simple terms, the verse closes the loophole that the previous verse's exception could have opened: whether you hide what is in your hearts or show it, Allah knows it; He knows everything in the heavens and the earth; and He is capable of everything.

The verse is the internal correction to 3:28. The tuqyah was permitted — the outward precaution of the tongue against harm — but the verse immediately defines its limit: the outer precaution may be concealed from people, but it is not concealed from Allah. Ibn Kathir: "Allah tells His servants that He knows the secrets and apparent matters and that nothing concerning them escapes His observation. Rather, His knowledge encompasses them in all conditions, time frames, days and instances." Al-Jalalayn makes the connection to the previous verse explicit: "Say to them, 'Whether you hide what is in your breasts of patronage to them, or disclose it, God knows it, and He knows what is in the heavens and what is in the earth; and God is Able to do all things — and this includes punishing those who patronise them.'" The verse is the counterweight: the person who shows outward friendship in fear while his heart remains loyal to the believers is safe — because the heart is the standard, and Allah knows the heart. But the person who uses the outward precaution as a cover while his heart secretly leans to patronage of the disbelievers is not fooling Allah: the cover is visible to the One who knows the breasts.

"whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah" — the verse's grammar is exhaustive: *in tukhfu* (whether you hide) *aw tubduhu* (or you manifest it) — the two poles of the human capacity for secrecy, and both are null in the presence of divine knowledge. The word used is *sudur* — the breasts, the chests — the seat of the inner person, the intentions, the affections, the hidden loyalties. The Qur'an's characteristic word for the deepest interior: Allah knows what is in the breasts.

"For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth" — the scope expands from the human heart to the total creation. Ibn Kathir: "His knowledge encompasses all that is in heaven and earth, and nothing, not even the weight of an atom, or what is smaller than that in the earth, seas and mountains, escapes His observation." The heart's secret and the atom's weight are both inside the same Knowledge; there is no scale at which the knowledge is absent.

"And Allah is Most Capable of everything" — the verse ends with the same *qadir* as 3:26. The knowledge is connected to the power: the One who knows the hidden loyalty is the One who is capable of acting on it. Ibn Kathir: "This Ayah alerts Allah's servants that they should fear Him enough to not commit what He prohibits and dislikes, for He has perfect knowledge in all they do and is able to punish them promptly. And He gives respite to some of them, then He punishes them, and He is Swift and Mighty in taking account."

For the reader, the verse is the completion of the doctrine of the previous verse and the foundation of the whole surah's teaching on sincerity. The believer's inner condition is not private; it is the primary object of the divine knowledge. The outward posture can be managed, and in a case of genuine precaution it should be managed — but the inward loyalty is the one that will be weighed. This is also the verse the Muslims of the surah's era needed most: they were a small community in a city of tribes, often in the position of 3:28's exception, forced to smile at the powerful. The verse reassures them: Allah knows the hearts; the outward smile does not compromise the inner faith; and the One who knows the heart is the One who will judge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 30

Verse 30 says: *˹Watch for˺ the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done. And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off. And Allah warns you about Himself. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the section of warnings with the Day: a day when every soul finds its good deeds present before it and its evil deeds present before it — and it will wish the evil were a great distance away. Allah warns you about Himself, and Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants.

"the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done" — the phrase *muhdaran* — present, brought forward, placed before. Ibn Kathir: "On the Day of Resurrection, Allah brings the good and evil deeds before the servant," and he cites 75:13 — "On that Day man will be informed of what he sent forward and what he left behind." The verse does not say the deeds are *recounted*; it says they are *presented* — the good and the evil are given a presence, a visibility, a concreteness that the person will face. The classical description: when the servant sees the good deeds, "he becomes happy and delighted"; when he sees the evil, "he becomes sad and angry."

"It will wish that its misdeeds were far off" — the wish is the strongest psychological statement in the verse: *tawaddu law anna baynaha wa baynahu amadan ba'idan* — it would wish that between it and that (evil) were a far distance. Ibn Kathir: "Then he will wish that he could disown his evil work and that a long distance separated it from him." The person will not be able to deny it, will not be able to disown it, will not be able to return it — so the only wish left is the wish of the impossible: distance. Al-Jalalayn: "a great distance, an extremely lengthy distance, so that the evil could never reach it." Ibn Kathir connects it to 43:38 — the confession on that Day to the devil: "Would that between me and you were the distance of the two easts — a horrible companion indeed!"

"And Allah warns you about Himself" — the repetition of 3:28's warning, now placed between the Day's scene and the mercy's statement. Ibn Kathir: "He warns you against His punishment." The warning is the surah's recurring contrast: the same section that shows the Day's horror closes with the Lord's grace.

"And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants" — *Rauf* — Ever Gracious, Kind, Compassionate, full of tenderness. Ibn Kathir records the interpretations: Al-Hasan al-Basri said, "Allah is so kind with them that He warns them against Himself"; others commented, "He is merciful with His creation and likes for them to remain on His straight path and chosen religion, and to follow His honorable Messenger." Maududi: "It is out of sheer goodwill that God warns people against deeds likely to have devastating consequences for them." The order of the verse is the doctrine: the warning is itself the act of grace; the One who warns is the One who is kind; the warning of the Day is not the announcement of a cold cage but the kindness of the One who opens the eyes of the traveler to the cliff before the fall.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's first full statement of the Day in vivid terms and its first full pairing of the warning and the mercy. The warning: the good and the evil both arrive, given presence, and the only wish of the evil is the distance that cannot be granted. The mercy: the warning itself is the kindness — the verse arrives in this life, before the Day, precisely so that the person can act. The whole section 3:21–30 has moved from the sins of the past (the killing of the prophets) to the deception of the present (the "few days" doctrine) to the reality of the future (the Day of the exact account) — and the surah has placed next to the horror of the Day the One who warns in order to save. The believer's response to the verse is both fear and hope: fear of the presented deeds, hope in the Gracious One who warned before the presentation.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 31

Verse 31 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the test and the reward of love: if you truly love Allah, then follow me — and Allah will love you and forgive your sins; Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse is one of the most important verses in the Qur'an for the religion's structure of authority, and Ibn Kathir frames the whole surah from here. "This honorable Ayah judges against those who claim to love Allah, yet do not follow the way of Muhammad. Such people are not true in their claim until they follow the Shari'ah (Law) of Muhammad and his religion in all his statements, actions and conditions." The claim to love Allah is a claim that the Qur'an itself tests: the condition of the love of Allah is the following of the Messenger.

Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: "When they said 'We only worship idols out of our love for God, that they might bring us close to Him,' the following was revealed." The verse's first audience was the polytheists of the era and the people who claimed devotion while keeping invented intermediaries. Al-Hasan al-Basri and several scholars among the Salaf commented: "Some people claimed that they love Allah. So Allah tested them with this ayah." The claim of love is not accepted at face value; it is tested by the evidence of following.

"If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me" — the conditional is the test: *in kuntum tuhibbuna Allah* — if you love Allah — *fattabi'uni* — then follow me. The word *ittiba'* is the following that is submission and imitation: following in practice, in method, in obedience. Ibn Kathir: the following of his Shari'ah and religion "in all his statements, actions and conditions." The claim of love without the following is a self-description that contradicts its own evidence; the following is the proof of the love, and it is the way the love becomes real.

"Allah will love you" — the reward is greater than the claim. Ibn Kathir: "what you will earn is much more than what you sought in loving Him, for Allah will love you." The structure of the exchange is the Qur'an's deepest spiritual teaching: the human love of Allah is not the possession of an object but the entry into a relationship; the one who loves Allah enters into the divine response — and the divine love is the return of the love of the believer. The believer seeks His love; Allah's love is given to the one who follows the Messenger. Al-Jalalayn and the classical reading tie the "Allah will love you" to the reward of the following and the forgiveness of sins.

"and forgive your sins" — *wa yaghfir lakum dhunubakum* — He will forgive you your sins. The reward of the following is double: the love of Allah and the removal of the burden. Ibn Kathir: "by your following the Messenger, you will earn all this with the blessing of his mission." The forgiveness is the mercy's removal of what stands between the believer and the relationship: the sins that made him unworthy of the love are removed, and the relationship is restored.

"For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — the two names close the verse as the guarantee: the One who commands the following is the One who forgives — *Ghafur* (All-Forgiving, the One who covers the faults) and *Rahim* (Most Merciful, the One who wishes the good for the servant). The command of the verse is not a legalistic test but the setting of the mercy's path: follow, and the mercy will meet you.

The verse's significance in the surah is polemical and structural. Polemically: the People of the Book claimed the love of Allah and a special relationship with Him while rejecting the messenger. The Qur'an's answer: the claim is tested by the following — if the love is real, it follows the one whom Allah sent; if it does not, the claim is not the love. Structurally: the verse is the foundation of the religion's authority — the sunnah of the Prophet is not a supplement to the Qur'an or an optional expression of culture; it is the concrete form of the love of Allah and the visible evidence of the following. The Muslim reader of the verse understands why the Prophet's example carries the centrality it does: the love of Allah is not an abstraction; it is embodied in the following of His Messenger.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 32

Verse 32 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Obey Allah and His Messenger.” If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers.*

In simple terms, the verse advances from following to obedience: say — obey Allah and His Messenger; if they turn away, then Allah does not like the disbelievers.

The verse is the command form of 3:31's condition. The previous verse said "follow me"; this verse says "obey Allah and His Messenger." Ibn Kathir connects them: "Allah next commands everyone: 'Say: Obey Allah and the Messenger.'" The two verbs — *ittiba'* (follow) and *ta'a* (obey) — are the two sides of the relationship: the following of the example and the obedience of the command. The verse keeps them together: obedience is to Allah and to the Messenger; the two coordinates of the believer's action are in a single line.

"Obey Allah and His Messenger" — the phrase is the Qur'an's standard pairing: *ati'u Allah wa ati'u al-Rasul* — obey Allah and obey the Messenger; the repetition-form makes the command emphatic. The classical doctrine that this verse anchors is the inseparable authority of the two: obedience to the Messenger is the form of obedience to Allah, and there is no obedience to Allah that bypasses the Messenger. Ibn Kathir's treatment states it in the strongest terms: "testifying that defiance of the Messenger's way constitutes Kufr. Indeed, Allah does not like whoever does this, even if he claims that he loves Allah and seeks a means of approach to Him, unless, and until, he follows the unlettered Prophet, the Final Messenger from Allah to the two creations: mankind and the Jinn."

"If they still turn away" — *fa in tawallaw* — if they turn away, they withdraw, avert. The verse does not postulate a category of people who reject Allah but obey the Messenger, or who obey Allah but reject the Messenger; the turning away is the turning from the command as a whole. Ibn Kathir: the condition is "by defying the Prophet."

"then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers" — the sentence is the negative of the love of 3:31. The verse does not say Allah will not forgive them, that He will punish them, or that their deeds are void — it says He does not like them: *fa inna Allah la yuhibb al-kafirin*. The *kafirin* — the deniers, the disbelievers — are the ones who turn away from the command after the call. The "not liking" is the Qur'an's way of stating the edge of the relationship: the one who turns away from the love-test of 3:31 and the obedience-command of 3:32 places himself outside the divine approval — however much he claims the love.

For the reader, the verse completes the pair: 3:31–32 is the two-step statement of the believer's relation to the Messenger — follow his example, obey his command. The verse's strength is in the name of the turning-away: the person who "claims that he loves Allah and seeks a means of approach to Him" (Ibn Kathir's wording for the claim without the following) is counted among the disbelievers in the sense of the verse's verdict: the denial of the Messenger's authority is the denial of the relationship the verse defines. The surah's polemic with the People of the Book is here at its sharpest: they claimed the love of Allah and His covenant; the Qur'an says the claim is tested by the obedience, and the turning away from the messenger is the evidence against the claim.

The verse is also one of the Qur'an's anchor texts for the doctrine of *ta'a* — obedience in Islam — and the classical scholars read it as the command that encompasses the whole religion: the obedience to Allah in His commands and prohibitions, and the obedience to the Messenger in the transmission and explanation of the commands. The believer who accepts the Qur'an as the book of Allah necessarily accepts the Prophet as the explainer of the book; the two acceptances are one acceptance.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 33

Verse 33 says: *Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺.*

In simple terms, the verse begins the second discourse of the surah with the statement of divine election: Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of 'Imran above the worlds — the people of their time.

"Indeed, Allah chose" — *istafa* — chose, selected, preferred, purified, singled out. The verb is from *safw* — purity, the picking of the best. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that He has chosen these households over the people of the earth." Al-Jalalayn: "He preferred their selves, Abraham and 'Imran, above the worlds by making prophethood reside in them and their progeny." The election is the Qur'an's answer to the People of the Book's claim of a special relationship: the election is real, and it is Allah's — and the elected houses are listed precisely.

"Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of 'Imrân" — the four chosen: Adam, the father of mankind; Noah, the first messenger to the earth; the family of Abraham, the father of the prophets; and the family of 'Imran, the house of Maryam and 'Isa. Ibn Kathir expands each: Allah chose Adam — created him with His Hand, blew life into him, commanded the angels to prostrate before him, taught him the names of everything, allowed him to dwell in Paradise, but then sent him down out of His wisdom. Allah chose Nuh and made him the first Messenger to the people of the earth, when the people worshipped idols and associated others with Allah — he called his people day and night, in public and in secret, for a very long time; his calling only made them shun him more; he supplicated against them, and Allah caused them to drown, none saved except those who followed the religion Allah sent with him. Allah chose the household of Ibrahim, including the master of all mankind and the Final Prophet, Muhammad. And Allah chose the household of 'Imran — the father of Maryam bint 'Imran, the mother of 'Isa. And 'Isa is from the offspring of Ibrahim.

"above all people ˹of their time˺" — *'ala al-'alamin* — above the worlds, above the peoples of their respective eras. The phrase moderates the claim: each house was elevated above the peoples of its time, not all peoples of all times in a single unqualified ranking. The election is the elevation of the bearers of the message: the prophets among them and the continuous line of guidance.

Maududi provides the historical frame: the verse marks the beginning of the second discourse of the surah, revealed about 9 A.H., when a delegation from the Christian republic of Najran visited the Prophet. Najran lay between the Hijaz and Yaman, and comprised seventy-three towns and villages; an estimated one hundred and twenty thousand men could bear arms; the entire population was Christian, under three chiefs — the 'aqib (head of the community), the sayyid (collective and political affairs), and the usquf (bishop, religious leader). When the Prophet annexed Makka, the whole of Arabia became convinced the future of the area was bound up with him, and deputations began to visit him. The three chiefs of Najran came to Madina with sixty people — not prepared to go to war, the alternatives were to embrace Islam or to live as dhimmis (protected non-Muslim subjects of the Islamic state). It was on this occasion that Allah revealed this discourse — an invitation to the people of Najran to accept Islam. And Maududi notes the identification: 'Imran was the father of Musa and Harun, mentioned in the Bible as Amram — though the 'Imran of this surah's story is the father of Maryam, and the exegetes note the two 'Imrans or the family line connecting them (Ibn Kathir's discussion; cf. the note in most editions of the tafsir).

For the reader, the verse is the surah's pivot from the argument against the People of the Book to the story of the chosen family — and the two are connected. The People of the Book claimed the election; the Qur'an grants the election and names it — Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, the family of 'Imran. The election is not a blanket property of a people; it is the elevation of particular houses for the carrying of the message, and the message is the same message the surah has been preaching: Tawhid, the following of the messenger, and the submission. The story that follows (3:35–64) is the story of the house of 'Imran — the mother of Maryam, Maryam herself, Zachariah, Yahya, and 'Isa — and the surah will use the story to show the People of the Book that the message of their own chosen families is the message of the Messenger, and that the disputes over 'Isa are disputes over the very sign of the chosen lineage.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 34

Verse 34 says: *They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.*

In simple terms, the verse links the chosen families: they are a single line of descent — one from the other — and Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

"They are descendants of one another" — *dhurriyyatan ba'duha min ba'd* — offspring, one from another, a seed-line in which each is the descendant of the previous. Ibn Kathir: "Offspring, one of the other." The chosen families are not four disconnected names; they are one continuous chain: the message is carried by descent of spirit and blood from Adam to Noah to Abraham to 'Imran — and the chain continues through the families to the prophets within them and ultimately to the Messenger from the offspring of Abraham. Al-Jalalayn: the election was making prophethood reside in them and their progeny — the continuity is the point. The chosen are a lineage, not a list.

The meaning is double: biological and spiritual. Biologically, the families are one line — the prophets are blood-related, and 'Isa is from the offspring of Ibrahim (Ibn Kathir). Spiritually, the line is the continuity of the message: the same Tawhid preached by Adam (in his repentance and recovery), by Noah (in the call against idolatry), by Abraham (in the call of the pure creed), and by the family of 'Imran (in the story of Maryam and 'Isa). The descent is the descent of the *din* — the way — as much as of the blood.

"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — *Sami' 'Alim* — All-Hearing, All-Knowing. The verse's attribute-closing does the work of the whole passage: the One who chose the chain is the One who heard the prayers of the chosen — the prayer of the wife of 'Imran in 3:35, the prayer of Zachariah in 3:38 — and the One who knows the hearts, the intentions, the circumstances of each link. Ibn Kathir and the classical readings place the names at the point where they carry the narrative: the All-Hearing hears the du'a of the barren woman and the aging prophet; the All-Knowing knows the child in the womb, the name, the future, the refuges, and the provisions. The verse is the bridge from the doctrine of election to the story of the family of 'Imran — and the two names are the guarantee that the story is heard and known by the One who tells it.

For the reader, the verse teaches the continuity of revelation and the continuity of the chosen. The religion is not a series of disconnected events; it is one line — the line of the prophets — and the believer who reads the surah is being given the family tree of the message: Adam, Noah, Abraham, 'Imran — and within the family of 'Imran, Maryam and 'Isa — and beyond 'Imran, through Abraham's line, to Muhammad. The "one from another" also answers the People of the Book's separatism: the chosen families are not separate communities with separate religions; they are one line of the same din, and the message of the final Messenger is the continuation of the family's message. The verse's last two names remind the reader that the One who chose the line is the One who heard every prayer in it and knows every heart in it — the story that follows is a story of prayers heard and knowledge revealed.

There is a further point in the connection between the two halves of the verse. The "descendants of one another" is not only a statement about the past — Adam to Noah to Abraham to 'Imran — but a structure of the surah's whole argument. The People of the Book grounded their claims in their descent: they were the children of Abraham, the heirs of the chosen. The Qur'an grants the descent and immediately turns it into a criterion rather than a privilege: the descent is a line of transmission — the one message, the one din, the one submission — and the value of a descendant is measured by the carrying forward of what the line carried. Al-Jalalayn's reading makes the criterion explicit: the election was "making prophethood reside in them and their progeny" — the chosen are chosen to transmit, and the transmission is the test. The Qur'an is therefore not disputing the People of the Book's genealogy; it is showing them what the genealogy was for. And the attribute that closes the verse — the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing — is the attribute of the One who can distinguish the true transmission from the false claim: the descent that carries the message is honored; the descent that rejects the messenger has the genealogy but not the election.

There is also a balance worth seeing in the four names. Adam is the individual chosen and tested and sent down; Noah is the chosen one of the era of the flood, the first messenger after the idolatry of the peoples; the family of Abraham is the chosen household of the father of the prophets, the one who purified the creed and the one from whose line the final Prophet comes; and the family of 'Imran is the chosen household of the mother of Maryam and the grandfather of 'Isa — the household whose story the surah is about to tell. The four names span the whole history of the guidance and each one leads to the next: Adam's fall and recovery, Noah's call and deliverance, Abraham's creed and lineage, 'Imran's family and the Messiah — and then, in the verses that follow, the Messiah's story itself. The reader who tracks the line is following the surah's own movement from the first chapter of revelation to the story of Maryam, 'Isa, and the dispute with the Christians — which is precisely the argument the surah is building: the chosen lineage ends in the family the Christians dispute about, and the dispute is about what the lineage means.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 35

Verse 35 says: *˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”*

In simple terms, the verse begins the story of the family of 'Imran: the wife of 'Imran, when she conceived, dedicated the child in her womb entirely to Allah's service and asked her Lord to accept it — for He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

The verse introduces the story that the surah uses to answer the Christians of Najran: the story of Maryam and 'Isa — the house of 'Imran — as the sign of the chosen lineage and the truth about the Messiah. The woman is the mother of Maryam, and Ibn Kathir gives her name: Hannah bint Faqudh. Al-Jalalayn: Hanna said this after she had reached old age and longed for a child, supplicated to Allah, and sensed she was carrying a child.

"when the wife of 'Imrân said" — the verse opens with the remembered moment: *idh qalat* — when she said. The narrative's frame is the surah's own address: remember, O Prophet, when she said. The story is told not as an ancient anecdote but as a sign for the present dispute. Ibn Kathir's account: "Hannah could not have children, and one day, she saw a bird feeding its chick. She wished she could have children and supplicated to Allah to grant her offspring. Allah accepted her supplication, and when her husband slept with her, she became pregnant." The longing of a barren woman who watches a bird feed its young, the supplication, the acceptance, and the vow — the story opens at the level of the ordinary human desire for children and moves to the extraordinary.

"I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service" — *nadhartu laka ma fi batni muharraran* — I have vowed to You what is in my womb, as *muharrar* — dedicated, consecrated, set free from the distractions of the world for the service of Your Holy House in Jerusalem. The *nadhr* — the vow — is a serious religious act: the mother pledged the child before it was born to the exclusive service of the Temple. Ibn Kathir: "She vowed to make her child concentrate on worship and serving Bayt al-Maqdis, when she became aware that she was pregnant." Al-Jalalayn: "one liberated and delivered from the distractions of this world, for the service of Your Holy House in Jerusalem." The child was dedicated to the service of Allah before it drew its first breath — the story begins with a parent's total dedication of the unborn child to Allah.

"so accept it from me" — *fa-taqabbal minni* — so accept it from me. The request of acceptance follows the vow: the dedication is the human act; the acceptance is the divine. Al-Jalalayn notes: 'Imran died while she was still pregnant — the mother of Maryam faced the dedication alone, as a widow, carrying the child she had pledged.

"You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — *innaka anta al-Sami' al-'Alim* — the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing: the two names from 3:34 now perform their narrative function. Ibn Kathir: "meaning, You hear my supplication and You know my intention." The All-Hearing hears the prayer of the barren woman; the All-Knowing knows the intention behind the vow. The verse's ending is the assurance that the prayer's two halves — the hearing and the knowing — are both with Allah.

For the reader, the verse is the model of dedication: the parent who offers the child to Allah before the child's birth, with the request of acceptance. The Qur'an does not present this as a ritual to be copied literally — the dedication of a child to lifelong temple service belongs to the law of the earlier people — but as the spirit of the commitment: the parent's life is not the parent's own; the child is a trust; and the deepest word a parent can speak over the unborn is the word of dedication. And the verse's last line is the reader's own frame: the All-Hearing hears; the All-Knowing knows — and what is heard and known can be accepted. The mother asked the accept; the next verse answers.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 36

Verse 36 says: *When she delivered, she said, “My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—“the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.”*

In simple terms, the verse tells the outcome of the vow: when the wife of 'Imran gave birth, she discovered it was a girl — and Allah knew exactly what she had delivered. She said the male is not like the female, named the girl Mary, and sought Allah's protection for her and her offspring from Satan the accursed.

"When she delivered, she said, "My Lord! I have given birth to a girl"" — the verse's drama is the difference between the vow and the outcome. The mother had pledged a *muharrar* — a dedicated servant — expecting (as Al-Jalalayn and Ibn Kathir note) a male, for in the law of the earlier people only males served in the Temple in that manner. She was old, she had waited, she had vowed — and the child was a girl. Ibn Kathir: "She did not know then what she would give birth to, a male or a female." The verse records the moment of discovery: the mother's own words — "My Lord! I have given birth to a girl."

"—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered" — the parenthetical is the Qur'an's most important comment in the verse: *wa Allah a'lamu bima wada'at* — and Allah knew better what she bore. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings treat the parenthetical as addressed to the reader: the mother's first word was disappointment at the gender; the Qur'an corrects the reader's assumption — Allah knew what she bore, and what she bore was the woman who would carry the Messiah. The "girl" was the greater gift. The surah's hierarchy of the sexes is here stated against the background of the era's valuation: the birth of a girl was to the Arabs of the pre-Islamic era a shame; the Qur'an's story of the chosen family begins with the birth of the girl, and the birth of the girl is the birth of the mother of the Messiah.

"the male is not like the female" — *laysa al-dhakar ka al-untha* — the male is not like the female. Ibn Kathir: "in strength and the commitment to worship Allah and serve the Masjid in Jerusalem." The mother's statement is the acceptance of the difference: the girl who was born was not the son she had envisioned, and the service she had pledged would be served by a girl. The verse's context makes the statement rich: the male is not like the female — in the world's valuation of 3:14 the sons and the desire for children are the world's ornaments; the Qur'an's chosen line is carried by the daughter.

"I have named her Mary" — *innami sammaytuha Maryam* — I have named her Maryam. Ibn Kathir draws a legal and prophetic point: "thus testifying to the fact that it is allowed to give a name to the newly born the day it is born, as is apparent from the Ayah, which is also a part of the law of those who were before us." He cites the Sunnah: the Prophet said, "This night a son was born for me and I called him by my father's name, Ibrahim" (al-Bukhari and Muslim), and the narration of Anas that the Prophet chewed a date, put it in the newborn's mouth, and called him 'Abdullah. The naming is the mother's act of identification: the nameless girl is given her name — Maryam.

"and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed" — the mother's second prayer after the birth: *u'idhuha bika wa dhurriyyataha min al-Shaytan al-rajim* — I seek refuge for her and her offspring from Satan. Ibn Kathir: "she sought refuge with Allah from the evil of Shaytan, for her and her offspring, i.e., 'Isa, peace be upon him. Allah accepted her supplication." He cites the hadith recorded by 'Abd ar-Razzaq from Abu Hurayrah: the Prophet said — there is no newborn child who is not touched by Satan at birth, except Maryam and her son; and Abu Hurayrah invited: "Recite if you wish: 'I seek refuge with You for her and her offspring from Satan the accursed.'" The prayer of the mother was accepted: the protection from the accursed was granted to the mother of the Messiah and to her son.

For the reader, the verse is the turning of the story: the mother's "disappointment" at the birth of a girl is the Qur'an's demonstration that the valuation of the world is not the valuation of Allah. A girl was born to a woman who had vowed a son; Allah knew what she bore — and the girl became the mother whose story the angels will witness, chosen above the women of the worlds (3:42). The verse also contains the mother's wisdom: she did not withdraw the vow with the change of the child; she committed the girl, named her, and placed her under the protection of Allah. The lesson of the parent: the outcome of the dedication may not match the plan; the dedication continues — and the protection is sought before the child begins the journey.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 37

Verse 37 says: *So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” She replied, “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.”*

In simple terms, the verse records the acceptance: the Lord accepted Mary with gracious acceptance, caused her to grow in a good manner, placed her under the care of Zachariah; every time he entered the sanctuary he found provisions with her; he asked from where, and she answered: from Allah — for Allah provides for whom He wills without limit.

"So her Lord accepted her graciously" — *fa-taqabbalaha rabbuha bi qabulin hasan* — accepted her with goodly acceptance. The request of 3:35 ("accept it from me") is answered: the dedication was accepted, and the acceptance was *hasan* — good, beautiful, complete. Al-Jalalayn: "Her Lord accepted the child — He received Mary from her mother with gracious acceptance." The mother's vow, the mother's naming, the mother's refuge — all were received; the girl born to the woman who had longed for a son was accepted with the goodly acceptance.

"and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing" — *wa ambataha nabatan hasanan* — grew her, caused her to grow, with a good growing. Al-Jalalayn: "made her grow up with excellent character. She would grow in a day by as much as a new-born grew during a year." Ibn Kathir: "made her conduct becoming, her mannerism delightful and He made her well liked among people. He also made her accompany the righteous people, so that she learned righteousness, knowledge and religion." The growing is total — character, conduct, popularity with people, the association of the righteous: the upbringing of Maryam was the work of the One who accepted her.

"entrusting her to the care of Zachariah" — *wa kaffalaha Zakariyya* — and He gave her in care to Zakariyya. Al-Jalalayn's narrative: her mother took her to the priests, the keepers of the Holy House of Jerusalem, and said, "This here before you is the dedication I offered." They competed for guardianship of her, because she was the daughter of their religious leader; Zachariah said, "I am most worthy of her for her maternal aunt lives with me." The others said, "Not until we have cast lots." All twenty-nine of them departed to the River Jordan, where they cast their quills, agreeing that the one whose quill remained fast and floated to the surface would be most worthy to be guardian. Zachariah's quill remained fast and surfaced. He took charge of her and built for her a gallery-room with a ladder in the Temple, and none apart from him went up to her; he used to bring her food, drink, and oil. Ibn Kathir also notes the tradition that Zakariyya was the husband of her maternal aunt — quoting the hadith of the night journey, "I saw John and 'Isa, who are maternal cousins" — and the hadith from the Two Sahihs that the Prophet decided 'Amarah, the daughter of Hamzah, be raised by her maternal aunt, the wife of Ja'far bin Abi Talib, saying: "The maternal aunt is just like the mother." The guardianship of Maryam was the sign of her honor: the chosen girl was placed in the care of a prophet.

"Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions" — *kullama dakhala 'alayha Zakariyya al-mihrab* — every time he entered upon her the mihrab, the prayer chamber, the most noble seat of the Temple. The mihrab is her private place of worship, and in it he found *rizqan* — provision, sustenance. Ibn Kathir records the classical readings: Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Abu al-Sha'tha, Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, Ad-Dahhak, Qatadah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas, 'Atiyah al-'Awfi, and as-Suddi said — "He would find with her the fruits of the summer during winter, and the fruits of the winter during summer." Al-Jalalayn: "He would find her with summer fruits in winter and winter fruits in summer." The provision was out of season — the ordinary order of provision reversed, the sign of the One who provides without the means.

"He exclaimed, "O Mary! Where did this come from?"" — the question of the prophet to the girl: *anna laki hadha* — where is this from for you? The question is the natural response to the sign: the guardian prophet's own supplies were ordinary; the girl's supplies were from a source he could not see.

"She replied, "It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit."" — the answer is the verse's center: *huwa min 'indi Allah* — it is from Allah — *inna Allah yarzuqu man yasha' bi ghayr hisab* — Allah provides for whom He wills without reckoning. The girl's answer is the creed in one line: the provision that has no visible source has the Source; the provision that has no calculation has the One who does not need the calculation. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn (on the parallel 3:27) tie the *bi ghayr hisab* to the same phrase in 3:27 — the provision without limit. The "little" girl at the Temple answered the prophet with the doctrine the whole surah teaches: the provision, like the sovereignty and the honor, is in the hand of the One who gives without measure.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's demonstration of the relationship between dedication and provision: the mother dedicated the child, the Lord accepted the dedication, and the provision followed the accepted one. It is also the verse of the child's faith: the only speech the Qur'an records directly from the young Maryam before the story of 'Isa is the statement of tawhid — "It is from Allah." The girl raised in the sanctuary under the care of a prophet, supplied with out-of-season fruits, answered the prophet's question with the doctrine that the whole passage teaches: the provider without limit. The believer who reads the verse and wonders about his own provision — where it comes from, how long it will last, how it will reach him — is being taught that the provision has one Source and no arithmetic.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 38

Verse 38 says: *Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the prophet's response to the sign he witnessed: immediately, Zachariah prayed to his Lord — grant me from Your grace pure offspring; You are truly the Hearer of supplication.

"Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord" — *hunalika da'a Zakariyya rabbahu* — at that point, there, at that place and moment, Zachariah called upon his Lord. Al-Jalalayn: "Then when Zachariah had seen this and realized that the One with power to bring something about in other than its natural time is able to bring about a child in old age and with those of his family line all deceased, he prayed to his Lord." The trigger was the sign of the out-of-season provisions: the One who gives summer fruits in winter is the One who can give a child to an old man — the same logic by which the whole surah's argument has run. The prophet was not amazed into silence; he was moved to supplication.

"when he entered the sanctuary to pray in the middle of the night" — Al-Jalalayn places the prayer: when he entered the sanctuary to pray in the middle of the night. Ibn Kathir: "When Zakariyya saw that Allah provided sustenance for Maryam by giving her the fruits of winter in summer and the fruits of summer in winter, he was eager to have a child of his own. By then, Zakariyya had become an old man, his bones feeble and his head full of gray hair. His wife was an old woman who was barren. Yet, he still supplicated to Allah and called Him in secret." The one who saw the provision of the girl in the sanctuary prayed for a child in the same sanctuary, in the middle of the night, in secret.

"Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring" — *hab li min ladunka dhurriyyatan tayyibah* — grant me from You, from Your very self, good offspring. The *min ladunka* — from Yourself — is the key phrase: not from my effort, not from my capacity, not from the natural order — from You. And *tayyibah* — good, pure, excellent: not any offspring, but the good one. Ibn Kathir: "A good offspring — meaning a righteous offspring." The request came from the man whose wife was barren and whose age was past — the request was for exactly what the natural order could not give, from the One who had just overridden the natural order in the sanctuary.

"You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers" — *innaka Sami' al-du'a'* — You are the Hearer of supplication, the One who hears the call. The name is the same as 3:34's *Sami'* and 3:35's *al-Sami' al-'Alim* — the chain of hearing: the All-Hearing heard the barren woman in the story before; the same All-Hearing is addressed by the old prophet in the story now. The two prayers of the house of 'Imran's story are paired: the woman's dedication-prayer in 3:35 and the prophet's offspring-prayer in 3:38; both end in the hearing.

For the reader, the verse is the model of the believer's response to the signs of Allah: to see the provision and to ask; to witness the power over the natural order and to turn the witness to the need; to pray in secret at the middle of the night; and to ask from Allah, not from the means. The verse also answers the believer who has concluded that his situation is beyond hope: the man in the verse was old, his bones feeble, his wife barren, his family line deceased — the verse's point is precisely that this was the man who prayed, and the prayer was answered. The natural order is the thing the Qur'an repeatedly shows overridden by the One who set it. The believer's part — as the middle of the night, the secret prayer, and the "from You" show — is to make the request of the One who orders the natural world.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 39

Verse 39 says: *So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary, “Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John who will confirm the Word of Allah and will be a great leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the answer to the prayer: while Zachariah was standing in prayer in the sanctuary, the angels called to him — Allah gives you good news of John, who will confirm a word from Allah, and he will be a leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.

"So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary" — *fa-nadathu al-mala'ikah* — so the angels called him — *wa huwa qa'imun yusalli fi al-mihrab* — while he was standing in prayer in the mihrab. Ibn Kathir: "the angels spoke to him directly while he was secluded, standing in prayer at his place of worship." The multiplicity of the address ("the angels called him") emphasizes the moment: the prayer in the sanctuary was answered in the sanctuary — the standing man received the good news while still standing in the prayer. The answer to the prayer of the middle of the night came in the moment of the prayer itself.

"Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John" — *anna Allah yubashshiruka bi Yahya* — Allah gives you glad tidings of Yahya. The name itself: Ibn Kathir records Qatadah and others: "he was called Yahya (literally, 'he lives') because Allah filled his life with faith." The good news names the child before the child is born — the same structure as the good news of 'Isa — and the name carries the meaning: the one who lives, filled with faith.

"who will confirm the Word of Allah" — *musaddiqan bi kalimatin min Allah* — believing in, confirming, a Word from Allah. Ibn Kathir records the classical identification: Al-'Awfi reported that Ibn 'Abbas said, and Al-Hasan, Qatadah, 'Ikrimah, Mujahid, Abu al-Sha'tha, as-Suddi, ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Ad-Dahhak and several others said — the "Word from Allah" means 'Isa, son of Maryam. Yahya would confirm, believe in, and testify to the truth of the Messiah: the man was sent before 'Isa to prepare and to testify — the same role in relation to 'Isa that the Qur'an assigns Zachariah's son and the Messiah with whom he is a maternal cousin (Ibn Kathir cites the hadith: "I saw John and 'Isa, who are maternal cousins"). The confirmation is the prophecy of the coming Word: Yahya was the sign of the One who would come after him.

"and will be a great leader" — *wa sayyidan* — and a master, a noble, a leader. Ibn Kathir collects the readings: Abu al-'Aliyah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Qatadah and Sa'id bin Jubayr said "a wise man"; Ibn 'Abbas, Ath-Thawri and Ad-Dahhak said "the noble, wise and pious man"; Sa'id bin al-Musayyib said "the scholar and faqih"; 'Atiyah said "the man noble in behavior and piety"; 'Ikrimah said "a person who is not overcome by anger"; Ibn Zayd said "the noble man"; Mujahid said "honored by Allah." The title *sayyid* encompasses the whole: the leading, the noble, the wise, the pious, the learned — a man whose station among his people was the station of the master.

"chaste" — *wa hasuran* — self-restrained, chaste. Ibn Kathir is explicit about the meaning: "does not mean he refrains from sexual relations with women, but that he is immune from illegal sexual relations. This does not mean that he does not marry women and have legal sexual relations with them, for Zakariyya said in his supplication for the benefit of Yahya, 'Grant me from You a good offspring' — meaning, grant me a son who will have offspring." The chastity is the purity from the unlawful — the control of the desire, not the abolition of the natural order.

"and a prophet among the righteous" — *wa nabiyyan min al-salihin* — and a prophet from among the righteous. Ibn Kathir: "delivers more good news of sending Yahya as Prophet after the good news that he will be born. This good news was even better than the news of Yahya's birth." The structure of the good news is the double glad tidings: the child will be born, and the child will be a prophet. Ibn Kathir compares it to the good news given to the mother of Musa: "We shall bring him back to you, and shall make him one of the Messengers" (28:7).

For the reader, the verse is the answer to the prayer of 3:38: the man who prayed in the sanctuary at the middle of the night received the answer in the sanctuary. And the verse's description of Yahya is the portrait the Qur'an returns to in 19:12–15 — the child given wisdom in childhood, the one who was told "O Yahya, take hold of the Book with might," the one given tenderness from Allah in his youth. The verse is also the surah's first announcement of the chain that leads to the Messiah: the good news of John, who will confirm the Word of Allah — the Word who is connected in the same surah to the dispute with the Christians. The good news of Yahya ascends the surah's story and points to the good news of 'Isa: both are signs of the One who does what He wills.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 40

Verse 40 says: *Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the human question and the divine answer: Zachariah asked how he could have a son when he was very old and his wife was barren — and the reply was: so will it be; Allah does what He wills.

"Zachariah exclaimed, "My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?"" — the verse records the prophet's response to the good news. Ibn Kathir: "When Zakariyya heard the good news, he started contemplating about having children at his age. He said, 'O my Lord! How can I have a son when I have reached old age and my wife is barren?'" Al-Jalalayn reads the same: the request for the sign is the natural human response to the impossible — the man who had prayed "grant me from You" now asks from the position of the natural order: the age is past, the wife is barren, the human means are absent.

The question is the question of the whole surah's argument: Zachariah is placed in the position of the questioner who must be taught the answer. The man who saw the summer fruit in winter, who saw the provision without source, who had himself prayed "from You" — he knows in one sense and must be moved in another: the knowledge of Allah's power over the natural order and the natural order's fixed limits must be reconciled. The question is the Qur'an's own rhetorical device: the same human situation as the question of Abraham (11:72 — "How can I have a child when I am old and my wife is barren?"), the same answer; and the same situation as the question the surah is answering about the Messiah: how can a child be born without a father? The answer to both is the same.

"He replied, "So will it be. Allah does what He wills."" — *kadhalika Allah yaf'alu ma yasha'* — thus it will be — Allah does what He wills. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings: the reply is the doctrine — the One who has power to bring something about other than in its natural time (as the prophet had just witnessed in the out-of-season provisions) is the One who does what He wills. The verse's "*yasha'*" — wills — is the hinge of the surah's theology of signs: the natural order is the regular expression of His will; the sign is the direct expression of the same will; both are the actions of the One who does what He will. The barrenness of the wife, the age of the man — these are the laws of the ordinary; the child, the virgin birth, the resurrection — these are the exceptions spoken by the same will that set the laws.

For the reader, the verse is the answer to every human "how can it be?" in the surah — the birth of John to the barren, the birth of 'Isa to the virgin, the day of gathering, the victory of the small army at Badr, the transfer of the kingdom from one people to another. "So will it be. Allah does what He wills" settles the matter of the means: the believer asks the human question, and the answer is the divine will. The verse also resolves the tension of the surah's argument: Zachariah's "how" is the human reason's last word, and Allah's reply is the revelation's first — the same "kun fa-yakun" (Be! and it is) that the surah will use three verses about 'Isa (3:47, 3:59): the One who does what He wills says "Be" and the impossibility exists. The reader who has a "how" is being taught the answer before the answer arrives: the impossible is possible to the One whose will is the cause of all things; the "how" belongs to the order of means, and the means are His.

- Ibn Kathir on 3:38–40 — "The Supplication of Zakariyya, and the Good News of Yahya's Birth": the trigger (the out-of-season provision), the old prophet with feeble bones and gray hair, the barren wife, the secret call; "grant me from You a good offspring"; the angels' call while he stood in prayer in the mihrab; the name Yahya — "he lives" (Qatadah — filled with life by faith); "believing in the Word from Allah" = 'Isa (Ibn 'Abbas, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, 'Ikrimah, Mujahid, as-Suddi, ar-Rabi', Ad-Dahhak and others); *sayyid* (wise, noble, pious — the collected readings); *hasur* — immune from illegal sexual relations, not celibate (contra the reading); "a prophet from among the righteous" as the second, better good news (cf. 28:7); the "how" and the answer "Thus Allah does what He wills." https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-38-sora-3.html
