# Sūrah 8 — al-Anfāl (The Spoils)

*75 verses; Madinan, closely associated with the Battle of Badr. Ibn Kathīr counts 1,631 words and 5,294 letters. The opening answers the dispute over Badr’s spoils; the sūrah then recalls the believers’ formation, God’s aid, and the battle, and lays down principles for loyalty, restraint, and communal solidarity. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī] [Maʿārif]**

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* · **[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** Al-Anfāl means “the spoils” or “war gains.” Its immediate setting is Badr, the first major battle between the Medinan Muslims and Quraysh. The opening does not treat victory as private entitlement: it redirects the Companions’ dispute to God and the Messenger, then names the inward and outward qualities that sustain a community. The later account interprets Badr as a victory granted by God amid human vulnerability, while its instructions on fighting, captives, covenants, and mutual protection turn the event into enduring guidance. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif] [Study Quran]**

---

## 8:1

*“They ask you about the spoils. Say: ‘The spoils belong to God and the Messenger.’ So be mindful of God, set right what is between you, and obey God and His Messenger, if you are believers.”*

**Context.** The verse addresses the disagreement at Badr after the enemy fled. Some Muslims had pursued the retreating army, some gathered the abandoned goods, and others had stayed near the Prophet to protect him; each group argued that its service entitled it to the spoils. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Maʿārif]**

**Meaning.** *Al-anfāl* is understood principally as the spoils of war, though early authorities also used it for special awards or property that came to Muslims outside the ordinary division. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]** “For God and the Messenger” entrusts their disposition to divine law and the Prophet’s judgment rather than private claims; at Badr he distributed them equally. **[Qurṭubī] [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī]** The command to mend relations calls the disputants away from rivalry and toward affection, restraint, and obedience. The later rule of one-fifth is stated at 8:41, so the opening should be read as establishing authority over the distribution, not as a complete account of every later rule. **[Qurṭubī]**

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** Ibn Kathīr relates Saʿd b. Abī Waqqāṣ’s report that he asked for a sword taken at Badr; the Prophet first told him to return it, then, after revelation, gave it to him. Al-Tirmidhī graded the report *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*. **[Ibn Kathīr]**

**Reflection.** The verse makes reconciliation part of faith: even a dispute over a legitimate concern must not be allowed to dissolve the bonds that make a community able to obey together. **[Saʿdī]**

## 8:2

*“The believers are only those whose hearts tremble when God is mentioned; when His signs are recited to them, they increase them in faith; and they put their trust in their Lord.”*

**Meaning.** The description turns from the dispute about spoils to the inner life of believers. Their hearts are awed by God’s greatness and warning, an awe that restrains wrongdoing rather than leaving them in passive fear. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] [Maʿārif]** When revelation is recited, they receive it as true and their conviction grows; reliance on God means that they hope in Him and do not treat other powers as their ultimate support. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Jalālayn]**

**Belief.** The verse presents faith as responsive and capable of increase: attentive engagement with revelation deepens understanding, hope, desire for good, and caution against sin. As-Saʿdī calls this a reason to keep tending one’s faith through reflection on the Qur’an. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

**Cross-references.** The Study Quran connects the trembling heart with 22:35 and 23:60, and the increase of faith with 3:173 and 9:124. The Qur’an also says that hearts find rest in God’s remembrance (13:28); awe and tranquillity are complementary responses to knowing God. **[Study Quran] [Qurṭubī]**

**Reflection.** A useful measure of remembrance is what it changes: a heart made more attentive to God becomes less willing to persist in injustice. **[Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī]**

## 8:3

*“Those who establish prayer and spend from what We have provided them.”*

**Meaning.** The inward trust of 8:2 appears here in two public practices. To “establish” prayer is more than to go through its motions: it is to fulfil its obligations, conditions, and proper reverence. **[Jalālayn] [Maʿārif]** Spending from God’s provision is broad, including required dues and voluntary charity, and is a recognition that wealth is entrusted by God rather than possessed absolutely. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

**Rulings.** The verse names expenditure generally, not one single payment; as-Saʿdī includes zakāh and other obligatory support as well as voluntary giving. **[Saʿdī]**

**Reflection.** Prayer disciplines a person’s relationship with God, while giving disciplines the relationship with other people. Together they make inward reliance visible as worship and generosity. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī] [Study Quran]**

## 8:4

*“Those are the believers in truth. For them are ranks with their Lord, forgiveness, and generous provision.”*

**Meaning.** The pronoun gathers the qualities just named—reverent hearts, growing faith, trust, prayer, and generosity—and calls their possessors believers “in truth,” meaning that outward confession and inward commitment are joined. **[Maʿārif] [Ibn Kathīr]** Their reward has three aspects: elevated ranks proportionate to their deeds, pardon for sin, and a gracious provision in Paradise. **[Jalālayn] [Saʿdī] [Study Quran]**

**Belief.** The description is a standard toward which believers strive, not a warrant for declaring every imperfect Muslim an unbeliever. Maʿārif cites al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī distinguishing basic assent from the complete faith praised in these verses. **[Maʿārif]**

**Reflection.** The verse holds effort and mercy together: rank is associated with the quality of deeds, while forgiveness acknowledges human shortcoming and generous provision is God’s gift. **[Saʿdī] [Jalālayn]**

## 8:5

*“As your Lord brought you forth from your home in truth, though a party of the believers were averse.”*

**Context.** The reference is to the Muslims’ departure from Madīnah toward the Quraysh caravan returning from Syria. When the armed Meccan force came to defend it, the Muslims faced a battle they had not set out to fight. **[Jalālayn] [Study Quran] [Maʿārif]**

**Meaning.** The opening “as” links the command to accept God’s judgment about the spoils with the earlier departure for Badr: in both matters the outcome that first seemed unwelcome proved to be for the believers’ good. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Jalālayn]** The phrase “in truth” can describe the just purpose of the expedition and the divine guidance by which the Prophet went forth. The Quraysh caravan had escaped by the coastal route, leaving the larger armed force at Badr. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** The verse does not deny the believers’ fear; it shows that reluctance and eventual obedience can coexist, and that the wisdom of a difficult command may become clear only afterward. **[Maʿārif] [Saʿdī]**

## 8:6

*“They disputed with you concerning the truth after it had become clear, as if they were being driven toward death while they looked on.”*

**Meaning.** The “truth” is the decision to face the armed Quraysh force after the Prophet had made the situation clear. Some argued from fear, feeling as though they were being led to certain death. **[Jalālayn] [Study Quran] [Ibn Kathīr]** Maʿārif stresses that the Companions were expressing hesitation during consultation, not refusing a command already given; the severe wording conveys how grave the moment felt. **[Maʿārif]**

**Context.** The contrast is between the relatively safe prospect of intercepting a lightly guarded caravan and the danger of confronting a much larger army. The Muslims had expected the former, but God’s decree brought them to the latter. **[Ṭabarī] [Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** Clear guidance may still demand courage. The verse remembers human apprehension honestly while placing it within a story whose outcome the believers could not yet see. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]**

## 8:7

*“When God promised you that one of the two companies would be yours, you wished for the unarmed one; but God willed to establish the truth by His words and cut off the last remnant of the disbelievers.”*

**Meaning.** The two companies were the returning caravan, which was lightly defended, and the Quraysh army, equipped for battle. The believers hoped to capture the caravan; God’s purpose was to establish the truth through the decisive encounter at Badr and to break the power of those who opposed it. **[Jalālayn] [Study Quran] [Qurṭubī]** “By His words” points to God’s promise and command; “cut off the last remnant” describes the defeat of the hostile force at Badr, not a universal instruction to pursue people without limit. **[Qurṭubī] [Ṭabarī]**

**Context.** The episode makes the difference between an immediate gain and a victory with far-reaching consequences: the caravan promised goods, while confronting the army altered the balance between the new Muslim community and Quraysh. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** The verse asks readers to distinguish what seems easiest from what serves a deeper moral purpose, while locating ultimate success in God’s wisdom rather than human preference. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

## 8:8

*“So that He might establish the truth and bring falsehood to nothing, even though the guilty were averse.”*

**Meaning.** This verse completes the purpose announced in 8:7. To establish truth is to make its reality manifest; to annul falsehood is to expose and defeat it. **[Qurṭubī] [Jalālayn]** The Study Quran notes that the Arabic can also be rendered “prove falsehood false” or “render what is vain vain”: the point is not merely a military result but the public vindication of the message. **[Study Quran]**

**Context.** The outcome at Badr contradicted the expectation that numerical advantage would decide the contest. The guilty disliked the result, but their preference could not cancel the truth God brought to light. **[Maʿārif] [Ibn Kathīr]**

**Reflection.** Truth is not made true by popularity, nor falsehood made secure by power. The verse calls for steadfastness when right is resisted. **[Saʿdī] [Study Quran]**

## 8:9

*“When you sought succour from your Lord, He answered you: ‘I shall aid you with a thousand angels, rank upon rank.’”*

**Context.** At Badr, the Prophet and his companions supplicated for help while facing a much larger Quraysh force. Reports describe the Prophet praying earnestly and Abū Bakr reassuring him that God would fulfil His promise. **[Qurṭubī] [Ibn Kathīr]**

**Meaning.** God’s answer is expressed as reinforcement by a thousand angels following one another in ranks; *murdifīn* conveys their succession. **[Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]** Jalālayn notes that 3:124–125 speaks of further promises of three thousand and five thousand in different circumstances; the present verse itself gives the initial promise. **[Jalālayn] [Maʿārif]**

**Belief.** The verse presents supplication and divine aid together: the believers ask, and God answers by means of help that remains wholly under His command. The Study Quran records differing accounts of whether the angels’ assistance was physical or spiritual, and reads 8:10 as emphasizing reassurance and strengthened hearts. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** Turning to God in danger is not an evasion of effort; it places effort within a trust that recognizes the limits of human strength. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]**

## 8:10

*“God made it only as glad tidings, so that your hearts might be at rest thereby; victory comes only from God. God is Mighty, Wise.”*

**Meaning.** The promised angelic aid is a sign that encourages the believers and steadies their hearts, not an independent source of victory. Victory belongs to God alone, whose might is unconquerable and whose wisdom determines the means and outcome. **[Jalālayn] [Saʿdī] [Ibn Kathīr]**

**Belief.** The Study Quran observes that reports differ over whether angels fought bodily at Badr; the verse’s emphasis on glad tidings and inward reassurance supports understanding their aid as strengthening the believers, without claiming that every report describes the same mode of help. **[Study Quran]** Maʿārif relates the successive promises of angelic numbers to the changing circumstances described in 3:124–125. **[Maʿārif]**

**Reflection.** Means can encourage and support, but they do not rival the One who governs them. The verse keeps confidence from becoming either despair at small numbers or self-reliance on apparent strength. **[Saʿdī] [Study Quran]**

## 8:11

*“He covered you with sleepiness as a security from Him, sent down water from the sky to purify you, remove Satan’s defilement, strengthen your hearts, and make your feet firm.”*

**Context.** On the eve of Badr, the believers were anxious and camped on soft, sandy ground, while the Quraysh had better access to water. Sleep gave them rest, and rain supplied water and changed the terrain. **[Maʿārif] [Study Quran]**

**Meaning.** The unexpected sleepiness is called security because it calmed fear and allowed the fighters to recover. The rain purified them bodily for worship and, in the commentators’ reading, removed Satan’s whisper that their hardship proved they were abandoned. It also steadied their hearts and physically firmed the ground beneath their feet. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī] [Jalālayn]** The Qur’an thus joins practical relief to inward reassurance: the water met ritual and battlefield needs at once. **[Study Quran]**

**Readings.** Qurṭubī records variant grammatical readings in which sleepiness is either sent over the believers or is itself said to overcome them; both convey the same divine gift of safety. **[Qurṭubī]**

**Reflection.** Divine care may arrive through ordinary-seeming means—rest, rain, and firmer ground—while also answering fears that cannot be solved by material means alone. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

## 8:12

*“When your Lord revealed to the angels: ‘I am with you, so make firm those who believe. I shall cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieve. Strike above the necks and strike every fingertip of theirs.’”*

**Meaning.** God’s message to the angels is an assurance of aid and a command to strengthen the believers. Their strengthening is described as courage and steadiness; the terror cast into the enemy’s hearts reverses the fear that had weighed on the Muslims. **[Jalālayn] [Saʿdī] [Study Quran]** “Above the necks” and “every fingertip” are battle language for striking the enemy’s heads or necks and extremities in the actual armed encounter at Badr. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr]** The verse does not detach the command from that battle setting.

**Belief.** The Study Quran notes that the angels may be addressed directly, while “I am with you” ultimately reassures the believers: their support and the enemy’s fear alike are under God’s power. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** Courage in the verse is not self-generated bravado; it is steadiness grounded in trust, discipline, and the knowledge that God’s aid is not measured by the enemy’s numbers. **[Saʿdī] [Ibn Kathīr]**

## 8:13

*“That is because they broke with God and His Messenger. Whoever breaks with God and His Messenger—truly God is severe in retribution.”*

**Meaning.** The defeat described in the surrounding verses is attributed to the Quraysh’s opposition to God and His Messenger, not simply to a difference in military strength. To “break with” them is to set oneself against their command and message. **[Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]**

**Context.** The sentence explains the outcome at Badr: the Meccan force had numbers and equipment, but these did not protect it from the consequences of its hostility. It also links the immediate battle to the larger moral claim of the sūrah. **[Maʿārif] [Study Quran]**

**Cross-references.** The Study Quran notes similar language for opposition to God and His Messenger in 2:137, 47:32, and 59:4. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** The verse locates accountability in deliberate opposition to justice and revelation, not in mere weakness or lack of worldly power. **[Saʿdī]**

## 8:14

*“That is your punishment, so taste it; and the disbelievers shall have the punishment of the Fire.”*

**Meaning.** The first punishment is the immediate defeat and loss suffered at Badr; the verse then warns that the consequences of persistent rejection do not end with a worldly battle. **[Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]** “Taste it” addresses those who had fought the Prophet and his community, while the closing clause distinguishes the hereafter’s punishment from the episode just witnessed. **[Study Quran]**

**Context.** Read with 8:13, the verse completes an account of the Quraysh’s defeat as both a historical event and a warning about the moral cost of aggression. **[Maʿārif] [Ibn Kathīr]**

**Reflection.** The warning is not an invitation to gloat over suffering; it turns victory into a reminder that worldly power is temporary and that conduct remains answerable to God. **[Saʿdī]**

## 8:15

*“O you who believe, when you meet those who disbelieve advancing in battle, do not turn your backs to them.”*

**Meaning.** *Zaḥfan* describes two forces drawing near and engaging in battle. The command forbids abandoning one’s companions and the community in the face of an advancing enemy. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] [Jalālayn]** As-Saʿdī explains that steadfastness protects the community and prevents fear from spreading through its ranks. **[Saʿdī]**

**Rulings.** This verse is read together with the exceptions in 8:16 and the numerical qualification later stated at 8:65–66. Qurṭubī discusses the rule in relation to the relative size of the forces; the prohibition is not a command to remain in a position where there is no lawful tactical purpose or prospect of aid. **[Qurṭubī] [Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** The warning is against desertion, not against every movement on a battlefield: it protects mutual responsibility while leaving room for the tactical exceptions the next verse names. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]**

## 8:16

*“Whoever turns his back to them that day—unless maneuvering for battle or joining another company—has incurred wrath from God, and his refuge is Hell; what an evil destination.”*

**Meaning.** Two withdrawals are exempted: a fighter may reposition to attack from a better angle, or move to join another unit and continue the fight. These are tactical actions, not flight from responsibility. **[Ṭabarī] [Jalālayn] [Saʿdī]** Leaving the battlefield without such a purpose is treated as a grave betrayal, and the verse gives a severe warning against it. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī]**

**Rulings.** The exception for joining a *fiʾah*—another company—shows that regrouping is distinct from desertion. The Study Quran reports accounts in which a returning fighter asks whether he has fled, and the Prophet replies that he has returned to his company; the commentators also discuss how the later ratio in 8:65–66 qualifies the rule. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** Courage is not reckless refusal to adapt. The verse makes loyalty and the purpose of one’s movement—not the mere direction of one’s steps—the measure of steadfastness. **[Saʿdī]**

## 8:17

*“You did not slay them, but God slew them; and you did not throw when you threw, but God threw, so that He might test the believers with a beautiful trial. God is Hearing, Knowing.”*

**Context.** Commentators connect the verse with the Muslims’ victory at Badr and with the Prophet’s handful of dust or pebbles thrown toward the enemy, whose reach and effect were beyond an ordinary throw. **[Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]** Qurṭubī also preserves reports associating the throwing with other battles, so not every transmitted account identifies the event in the same way. **[Qurṭubī] [Study Quran]**

**Meaning.** The verse does not erase the believers’ effort; it denies that their strength alone explains the outcome. God enabled their action and brought its effect to the enemy, turning a small force’s struggle into victory. **[Ṭabarī] [Qurṭubī] [Jalālayn]** The “beautiful trial” is also a blessing and test: victory gives the believers an opportunity for reward, but requires humility rather than pride. **[Study Quran] [Maʿārif]**

**Belief.** The joining of “you threw” with “God threw” affirms human action while attributing its enabling and decisive effect to God. **[Ṭabarī]**

**Reflection.** Success should deepen gratitude, not self-congratulation; effort is real, yet its capacity and fruits remain gifts. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

## 8:18

*“That is so; and God makes feeble the schemes of the disbelievers.”*

**Meaning.** “That” points back to God’s aid at Badr. The enemy’s plans are made ineffective, while the believers’ victory is credited to God rather than to a superior strategy or force of numbers. **[Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]** The verse is a concise conclusion to the account of the thrown dust and the believers’ divinely aided victory. **[Study Quran]**

**Cross-references.** The Study Quran points to 3:120, 4:76, and 52:46, where the schemes of those who oppose God are likewise shown to be powerless before Him. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** Planning matters, but no plan can guarantee success against God’s wisdom; believers are called to act without mistaking their means for the source of the outcome. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

## 8:19

*“If you sought a judgment, the judgment has come to you. If you desist, it is better for you; if you return, We shall return, and your company will not avail you, however numerous it may be. God is with the believers.”*

**Context.** One report says that Abū Jahl and the Quraysh called for God to judge between the two sides at Badr; the outcome became the answer to their appeal. **[Jalālayn] [Ibn Kathīr] [Qurṭubī]**

**Meaning.** On the most direct reading, the verse addresses the defeated Quraysh: the judgment they demanded has arrived, and it has vindicated the believers. They are urged to end their hostility; should they renew it, numerical strength will not protect them from God’s aid to the believers. **[Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]** The Study Quran records other readings that take part of the address as directed to the believers or shift its audience mid-verse, but the central warning remains a call to desist from aggression. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** The invitation to stop follows the warning: justice is not vengeance without end, and an adversary is still given a path away from hostility. **[Maʿārif] [Saʿdī]**

## 8:20

*“O you who believe, obey God and His Messenger, and do not turn away from him while you hear.”*

**Meaning.** Obedience means carrying out what God and the Messenger command and refraining from what they forbid. “Do not turn away” warns against hearing revelation and then acting in defiance of it. **[Ṭabarī] [Ibn Kathīr] [Jalālayn]** The verse follows the account of Badr to show that divine support is joined to the believers’ responsibility to obey. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**

**Belief.** Hearing in this context is not merely receiving sound: it entails attention and response. The Study Quran relates the verse to other passages that make obedience to the Messenger a central expression of obedience to God. **[Study Quran]**

**Reflection.** The difference between attentive faith and empty profession is whether what one hears is allowed to shape what one does. **[Saʿdī] [Maʿārif]**
