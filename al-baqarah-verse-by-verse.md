## Overview of This Cluster of Āyāt

This cluster of āyāt, an independent lesson, could be considered a timely follow-up to the eternal classification of people: the muttaqīs, the kufr crowd, and the nebulous and nefarious mobs of munāfiqūn. It is important to note that Allah (الله), the Magnanimous and the Majestic, chose to tell us humans of this pre-human conversation. Besides Himself, the exchange included the angels, Iblīs, and then Adam and Eve (عليهما السلام) after they were brought about and effected as Allah's (الله) beneficiaries of the earth.

Obviously, Allah (الله) did not give us this information so that we close our minds and lock our brains. He had enough confidence in our ability to reason and think that He divulged to us a part of history and existence, to which we would otherwise have no access. It is Allah (الله) who is nurturing our confidence in ourselves and our ability to think and reason when some of us have no confidence whatsoever in our human abilities, our range of understanding pertinent information, our reasoning, our analyzing, and our making sense out of events. Had this type of information been out of intellectual bounds, Allah (الله) would not have advanced it for our faculty of thought. And to ease the meanings into our realm of understanding this whole narration has been couched in a "short story" form.

With enough confidence in our mental capacity and knowing that the Qur'an is for people who think, we who read the Qur'an for guidance should realize that there is a caveat pertaining to this particular lesson about Allah (الله), the angels, Iblis, and our common father and mother — Adam and Eve (عليهما السلام). We are cautioned that these āyāt are mutashābihāt (allegorical or suggestive and evocative). This means that the surface meanings of the āyāt are not exhaustive of the core-to-detail facts. Let us now enter into the realm of these āyāt and behave as rational subjects of Allah (الله).

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> And Lo! Your Sustainer said unto the angels, "Behold, I am about to render on earth a khalifah (successor)." They said, "Will You place on it such as will spread corruption thereon and shed blood; whereas it is we who extol Your limitless glory, and praise You, and hallow Your name?" [Allah] answered, "Verily, I know that which you do not know."

**Commentary:**

The first thing to clarify in understanding this āyah is that Allah (الله) in His address to the angels was not consulting them. His knowledge is infinite and He needs no advice from anyone, angels or humans. The angels' reply, "Will You place on it such as will spread corruption thereon and shed blood…" is not to be construed as a quarrel or argument with Allah (الله). By expressing their thoughts, the angels were not objecting to Allah's (الله) will of having a successor on earth. A feud between Allah (الله) and the angels does not behoove Allah (الله) nor is it appropriate for the angels. This mental twist, which could be found in the Yahūdī interpretation of scripture, has no explanation in the Islamic morality of thought. Angels are not created to bicker or quibble with Allah (الله), their Creator and Sustainer. Angels, in the definition of Allah (الله) and the words of the Qur'an, "…do not disobey Allah in whatever He has commanded them, but [always] do what they are bidden to do" (66:6). The classical mufassirūn captured this same distinction: Ṭabarī and others read the angels' words as *istifhām* — a question asked to learn the wisdom of the divine decree — and not as *iʿtirāḍ*, an objection to it. There is a world of difference between asking in order to know (the way of the angels) and asking in order to defy (the way of Iblīs, as the following āyāt will show).

The second thing requiring clarification in order to understand this and other āyāt is that, by being involved with man, Allah (الله) is not diminished in any way whatsoever. Whenever there is a discussion about Allah (الله), a description of Allah's (الله) will and involvement with man, there are some individuals who try to demote or devalue Allah (الله) because He is involved with man's affairs. In some murky minds that makes Him in one sense or another human! When Allah (الله) says, "Your Sustainer said unto the angels…," these minds that are human-centered cannot imagine that anyone can say anything except that he be human. If they were told Allah (الله) communicated to the angels, some of them would still ask: how was this communication done? And if they were informed that there are no details about how the communication occurred, they may use this missing information as an excuse to relieve themselves of the Qur'anic and Islamic affairs altogether. These types have to understand that Allah (الله) is Allah (الله) and man is man. It is because of Allah's (الله) care and love for human beings that He divulges this information and it is because of some humans' ego and conceit that they refuse to place themselves where they belong in this God-to-man relationship. Man begins to respect himself when he realizes that Allah (الله) is exalted, extolled, and praised while he himself is imperfect, flawed, and inferior. In Allah's (الله) own words, "Limitless in His glory is your Sustainer, the Lord of almightiness, [exalted] above anything that men may devise by way of definition!" (37:180). The *qāla* ("He said") of this āyah is a divine speech befitting His majesty — without modality, without organ, without the limitations that hem human speech; this is the position of the mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah: *bilā kayf* — He speaks as befits Him, however He wills.

Another issue that has to be acknowledged is the human mind's ability to latch on to reason in revelation, or what is referred to in Qur'anic terminology as ta'wil. To explain this, it should be affirmed that the injunctions of Islam have been anchored in rationality. And so when an āyah or authentic and established hadith infer a meaning that is at odds with structured thinking and the rational mind, then it is assumed that the inferred surface meaning of the āyah or hadith is not the exhaustive meaning. And this is where ta'wil is applied by qualified and competent scholars to blend the rational and the reasonable with that which is revealed and reported. It should be reiterated that Allah (الله) did not address man with meaningless words.

In studying this cluster of āyāt from Sūrah al-Baqarah, one encounters the mention of angels. These are a form of creation whose existence Allah (الله) has disclosed together with some of their functions. Even though complete information about their nature has not been revealed, the fact of their existence is a certainty. Concerning angels, man should be pleased to know that Allah (الله) has chosen to provide him the information He has, without demanding to know what He has withheld. When it is said that these angels have "wings," the wings should not be construed to match those of birds with feathers, for if they had such physical characteristics, man would obviously be able to see them. But as to what form these wings do take, there is not enough information to speculate. When it is said that angels are, in some sense, managing particular physical domains, such as the plant kingdom or the oceans, one can infer that there are spheres of Allah's (الله) integrated "laws of nature" that are somehow supervised by particular angels, making it possible for humans to live in an accommodating world rather than a chaotic and discordant habitat. And such an understanding can be accepted without question, for there is every reason to do so and no ground for doubt. The Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim tradition that "the angels were created from light, the jinn from smokeless fire, and Ādam from what has been described to you (clay)" tells us of the difference of substance, not of rank: rank is measured by knowledge and obedience, as the rest of this passage will prove.

"And lo! Your Sustainer said unto the angels, 'Behold, I am about to constitute a khalīfah on earth…'" Allah (الله) informed the angels that He is arranging for a deputy on earth. They understood that Allah (الله) will bring into being entities whose nature will combine a potentially absolute resolve with freedom of will and choice, a combination bound to result in a chain of destructive actions and reactions. The nature of these actions and reactions will depend on man's judgement, which shall be contingent upon the degree of his knowledge and understanding of himself, the world and his Creator. If his actions are not guided by wisdom and the general or public interest, the will of man will have the potential to cause mischief, instability and corruption.

This is a very serious statement, "Allah is about to constitute a deputy on earth." The Arabic word *jaʿala* does not mean *khalaqa* (create). Had Allah (الله) said, "Behold, I am about to create on earth one who shall inherit it," perhaps the angels would not have questioned the intentions of this human being, nor would Iblis have objected to him; Allah (الله) knows best. But the heavenly company raised queries about this earthly khalīfah for a reason. In his individual and collective existence, man was created to become the vessel of Allah's (الله) will on earth. The words *jaʿala* and *khalīfah* in the āyah moved the angels to pose questions about this new form of intelligent life, and were enough for Satan to take exception. *Jaʿala* means not only that Allah (الله) is going to create a human, He is going to cultivate and develop him as well. In another sense, Allah (الله) has given man the capability of infusing his will with the Will of Allah (الله) through the medium of rahmah and tashkhīr (grace and utility), thereby enabling man to fulfill his role on earth, that of Allah's (الله) khalīfah. But he will only be able to execute this role as Allah's (الله) heir and representative on Earth provided his will is subservient to Allah's (الله), that is, when his understanding is guided by Allah (الله) and his actions are drilled by Him. Linguistically, *khalīfah* is derived from *khalafa* — to come after, to succeed — and it is read in two transmitted ways (*khalīfah* and *khalaf*), meaning one who succeeds another who came before: succeeding generations of mankind one after another, and succeeding Allah's (الله) authority on earth in the sense of deputies. The singular noun carries a collective sense: the whole race of Ādam is the khalīfah. Istikhlāf (vicegerency) is therefore a trust, not a license — a theme the Qur'an will state plainly when it asks, "Indeed, We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it and feared it; but man undertook it…" (33:72), and when it says of the believers, "spend out of that of which He has made you successors (istakhlafakum)" (57:7): whatever man is given stewardship over remains Allah's.

The angels could envision the potential of this human second-to-Allah (الله) to misuse the powers and capabilities given him, resulting in conflict, injustice, disorder and bloodshed in the world. This man of flesh and bone would find himself involved in a continual struggle against disaster, both natural and man-made. Natural disasters such as earthquakes or floods emerge from this earthly environment, while man-made ones like war and persecution spring from man's vulnerable human nature and his misuse of the powers granted him by Allah (الله). Just in the last century alone, earthquakes have killed nearly two million people worldwide. From Lisbon in 1755 to China in 1976, earthquakes have taken their toll. Volcanoes, extreme weather and drought have also decimated millions of people over the centuries, despite man's limited successes in building defenses against the cataclysmic repercussions of such natural forces. Man's synchronization with the Will of Allah (الله) will be difficult indeed when he encounters inexplicable natural phenomena, which result not only in the collapse of his physical defenses but also the erosion of his mental and spiritual consolidation.

The angels, in their question "Will You place on it such as will spread corruption…?" were actually asking the same question that occurs to the human mind in response to specific catastrophes, which may be easier to explain for worldly reasons, such as starvation due to famine, crop failure due to disease, displacement due to warfare, and mass contamination due to the misuse of natural resources. However, with man in a trustee position on earth, such a question can also be posed in response to natural calamities. This is to say that natural and man-made disasters are connected. Consider, for instance, the proposition that ancient killers such as cholera, the bubonic plague, pandemic influenza and smallpox, which have apparently been conquered by modern medicine, are not eradicated but rather subdued. One might ask in this context, not unlike the angels, about the wisdom of a khalīfah on earth when widespread malnutrition or civil wars can easily provide the conditions for the return of these silent scourges.

Wars have always left a wake of disease, misery and suffering. Improving technology and the increasing power of states over societies have meant that the deleterious impact of war has increased rather than decreased over time; few will escape entanglement in future conflicts. About 64 million perished in the two "world wars" alone. What makes men visit such cruelty upon their fellow human beings? Whether for profit, pleasure, or persecution of the powerless, human cruelty is often beyond imagination, relatively far worse than "natural" disasters in terms of death and destruction. From the torture of the early "Christians" and "Muslims" to the Zionists' "final solution" for the Palestinians, Arabs, and Muslims, how can man be so inhuman and yet qualify to be Allah's (الله) successor on earth?

"Whereas it is we who extol Your limitless glory, and praise You, and hallow Your name." This was the context in which the angels posed their inquiry — they presented their own ceaseless worship as the contrast case, not as a rival claim to the assignment. And Allah (الله) responded, "Verily, I know that which you do not know" — a single sentence that closes the question without humiliating the questioners. The divine answer points to a hidden good in man's creation that the angels could not see from their vantage point: the capacity to know, to learn, to return after falling, to rise by moral effort — faculties the sinless angels never need and never exercise. Where the angels' perfection is static, man's is dynamic; and Allah (الله) values the climber above the one who never had a mountain to climb. This āyah is thus the foundation-stone of human dignity in the Qur'an — "We have honored the children of Ādam" (17:70) — and of the entire philosophy of khilāfah: power is delegated, stewardship is conditional, and the worth of the deputy is measured by conformity to the One who deputizes him.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> And He imparted unto Ādam the names of all things; and then He exhibited them to the angels and said, "Declare unto Me the names of these [things], if what you say is true."

**Commentary:**

It is interesting to note here that Allah's (الله) response to a seemingly skeptical company of angels about the assignment of humans to stand for Allah (الله) on earth was, "He imparted unto or taught Ādam the names of all things." If Allah (الله) is teaching, then the role of Ādam (عليه السلام) and his descendants is to learn. This simple statement is the answer to the complex questions that surface when insecurity and loss of life become evident. The conventional and traditional Muslim will tell you that the world needs spirituality, as will the conventional and traditional followers of other religions. But Allah (الله) is saying that humans need to know, they need to learn, and they need to understand. What is the value of a spirituality that does not know it is effectively in the service of oppressive powers and tyrannical governments, which, in the larger scheme of things, becomes a spirituality in the service of evil? Man needs proper knowledge of the Qur'an and Sunnah, as well as of science and technology, to move the human condition to the level of khilāfah.

Partial and fragmented knowledge is not going to do it, be it "religious" knowledge or "secular" knowledge. It is only this proper, complete and total knowledge that will induce a robust spirituality and a determined pattern of behavior. Only this combination is capable of gaining and maintaining the status of khilāfah. It is in identifying and defining things accurately that man has come a long, long way from the Qur'anic description, "…man has been created weak" (4:28); and "And Allah has brought you forth from your mothers' wombs knowing nothing…" (16:78). The whole vocation of science — observation, definition, taxonomy, verification — is contained in embryo in this āyah; and so is the whole vocation of revelation, for "the All-Merciful taught the Qur'an; He created man and taught him articulation (*bayān*)" (55:1–4), and "He taught man that which he did not know" (96:5). Knowledge, not muscle, is man's miracle.

When it was demonstrated that Ādam (عليه السلام) knew the definition of "things" and that the angels were unable to show such knowledge, they admitted that Allah (الله) knows what they do not know. The creation of Ādam (عليه السلام) caused the angels to realize the limitations of their knowledge and understanding, something they had not previously been aware of. It is this Allah-given capacity to know, learn, understand and think that is the basis of the status granted to Ādam (عليه السلام) and his descendants. And man's assignment to the position of Allah's (الله) deputy on earth was an eye-opening experience for the angels, as they realized their own shortcomings and the significance of the divine lifeline of information from Allah (الله) to Ādam (عليه السلام) and his successors.

The classical tradition discussed at length what "the names of all things" were. In the reports transmitted through Ibn ʿAbbās and cited by Ibn Kathīr, Ādam was taught the names of everything — the pots, the pans, and every known thing; other reports say the names of the angels, or the names of the descendants of Ādam, or the names of the birds presented in the demonstration. What all the reports agree upon is the principle: Ādam was given the power of designation — language, definition, classification — the intellectual instrument by which the world is managed. To know the *name* of a thing in Qur'anic idiom is to know its nature and its limit (*ḥadd*); he who can define a thing can recognize its proper use and its proper restraint. That is why the knowledge of names, and not the splendor of form, is what qualified man for authority on earth. Note also the sequence of the āyah: the teaching came first, the testing after; knowledge given by Allah (الله) was then demanded back in demonstration — "Declare unto Me the names of these [things]." Allah (الله) does not examine what He did not teach; examination follows endowment, never precedes it. And the challenge is conditional — "if what you say is true" — tying truth-claims, from the very first page of human history, to the ability to produce knowledge on demand.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:32

> They replied, "Limitless are You in Your glory! No knowledge have we save that which You have imparted unto us. Verily, You alone are All-Knowing, Truly Wise."

**Commentary:**

The angels' answer is a model of epistemic humility, and its grammar deserves attention. They begin with *tasbīḥ* — "Limitless are You in Your glory!" — purging the divine majesty of any deficiency before they confess their own deficiency. This is the etiquette the Qur'an teaches on the tongue of every humble speaker before the Divine: Yūnus cries "Limitless are You in Your glory! Indeed, I was of the wrongdoers" (21:87), and the bearers of the Throne open with "Limitless are You in Your glory!" (40:7). Confession of createdness comes only after sanctification of the Creator. Then follows the confession itself: "No knowledge have we save that which You have imparted unto us." In one sentence the angels define the true anatomy of all human and angelic knowledge: it is *ʿaṭāʾ* — a gift — never self-generated. The verse implicitly refutes every later theory of knowledge that makes the creature autonomous in knowing; the knower knows only what the Knower "taught man that which he knew not" (96:5). The Qur'an will state the hierarchy with perfect economy elsewhere: "…above every possessor of knowledge is the All-Knowing" (12:76).

The angels close with two divine names: "You alone are All-Knowing, Truly Wise" — knowledge (*ʿalīm*) married to wisdom (*ḥakīm*). The pairing is precise: knowledge is of facts, wisdom is of ends. Allah's (الله) decree to place a khalīfah on earth, which the angels could not at first fathom, is hereby acknowledged to be grounded not merely in information they lacked but in purposes they could not compute. When the believer does not understand a decree, this āyah trains him to say what the angels said — not to doubt the decree, nor to bluff an explanation of it, but to confess the limits of the creature's knowledge while affirming the perfection of the Creator's.

Set this reply beside the reply of Iblīs in the following verses and the whole moral geometry of the passage appears: the angels, confronted with what they did not know, magnified Allah (الله) and abased themselves; Iblis, confronted with the same decree, abased Allah's (الله) choice and magnified himself. Same test, opposite spirits. The angels' sentence is thus preserved in the Qur'an as a permanent *duʿāʾ* and a permanent school: to say "we know only what You taught us" is the beginning of all sound learning and the end of all arrogance in learning. The creation of Ādam (عليه السلام) caused the angels to realize the limitations of their knowledge, something they had not previously been aware of — and their first response to the discovery was praise, not protest.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:33

> Said He, "O Ādam, convey unto them the names of these [things]." As soon as [Ādam] had conveyed unto them their names, [Allah] said, "Did I not say unto you, verily, I alone know the hidden reality of the heavens and the earth, and know all that you bring into the open and all that you would conceal?"

**Commentary:**

The demonstration is now completed in public. Ādam (عليه السلام) is commanded to *convey* the names — *anbiʾhum*, from the same root as *nabī* (prophet): the first human act recorded in the Qur'an after learning is transmission. Man's role in creation is thus stamped from the outset: he is a teacher-creature, a conveyor of what he has received; knowledge in the Adamic dispensation is not a private possession but a trust to be announced. The scholar who learns and conceals inverts this verse, and will be condemned by its logic.

Then Allah (الله) turns to the angels with the seal of the whole examination: "Did I not say unto you, verily, I alone know the hidden reality (*ghayb*) of the heavens and the earth, and know all that you bring into the open and all that you would conceal?" Two vast truths are stated in one breath. First, the ghayb — the hidden reality of the heavens and the earth — belongs to Allah (الله) alone; whatever keys of the unseen others hold, they hold as gifts, "and He knows what is in the land and the sea; and not a leaf falls but He knows it" (6:59). Second, His knowledge bisects the whole of created being into the open and the concealed — *mā tubdūna wa-mā kuntum taktumūn* — nothing of the creature's display and nothing of the creature's interior escapes Him. The exegetes noted a pointed implication of the closing phrase: among the assembly there was one whose exterior conformed while his interior concealed refusal and pride — Iblīs. The āyah, while answering the angels, is already unmasking the one who stood among them with a hidden objection he never voiced. The first polygraph in history was not a machine but a verse: Allah's (الله) knowledge of what "you would conceal."

For the human reader, this āyah settles the architecture of the passage: authority on earth was awarded on the basis of demonstrated knowledge, before any deed had been done — competence, not ancestry, not even piety-as-yet-untested, qualified Ādam (عليه السلام) for the khilāfah. And it announces the standard of ultimate accountability that will close every account: on the Last Day none will be judged only by his open record; "He knows the fraud of the eyes and what the breasts conceal" (40:19). To live aware that the Concealed is fully Known is the beginning of *murāqabah* — the vigilance of the heart.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:34

> And when We told the angels, "Prostrate yourselves before Ādam!" they all prostrated themselves, except Iblīs, who refused and gloried in his arrogance, and thus he became of the **kuffār.**

**Commentary:**

### The First Kāfir

Allah (الله) shows an affinity with humans through the instrument of knowledge, "and He imparted unto Ādam the names of all [things]" (2:31). Thus humans have a relationship of dependence with Allah (الله), meaning that man is ill-equipped — without help from his Creator — to handle properly his responsibility on earth. Without knowledge, he would not only be weak, but his apparent weakness may lead him to harm himself and others. Allah (الله) says about man's state of being, "…man has been created in a state of frailty" (4:28), "…man is indeed most persistent in wrongdoing, stubbornly ingrate!" (33:72). At this moment of apparent polarization between man and God, He says to the angels, "Prostrate yourselves before Ādam!" And the angels when they realized their lagging knowledge and their subordinate strength, all prostrated themselves. Obedience to Allah (الله) supersedes all intervening factors even if these turn out to be a human destiny of bloodshed, warfare, worldwide instability, and clashes of civilizations. But the apparent discrepancy of an Almighty and Powerful Allah (الله) favoring a fragile and weak humanity stimulates a rationalizing attitude that refuses to accept an Omnipotent Divinity allying itself with an impoverished humanity. Hence, "…Iblis, who refused and gloried in his arrogance, and thus he became one who denies the [worldly] power of Allah" (2:34).

The exegetes are careful to state that this prostration was one of *ikrām* (honor) and greeting, not of *ʿibādah* (worship): the command was Allah's, the direction was toward Ādam, and the worship belonged wholly to Allah (الله) — as when the father and brothers of Yūsuf fell prostrate before him (12:100) in the jurisprudence of their own dispensation. The angels, having just acknowledged that their knowledge was a gift, found no difficulty in bowing to the creature their Lord had honored. Note the precise grammar of the fall: "he refused (*abā*) and gloried in arrogance (*istakbara*)" — two verbs, two stages. Refusal is an act; arrogance is the inner state that fathers it. And the result: "he became of the kuffār." The root *k-f-r* means to cover, to bury: the kāfir is one who covers the truth he knows. Iblīs's kufr was not ignorance — he had stood in the divine presence and received the divine command — it was the suppression of known truth beneath the self's estimate of its own excellence. From this single verse the tradition derived the doctrine that the first sin in creation was pride, and the Prophet (ﷺ) said in the authentic hadith: "None who has an atom's weight of pride in his heart will enter Paradise." It was said: a man loves that his garment be fine and his sandals be fine. He said: "Indeed Allah is Beautiful and loves beauty; pride is the rejection of truth (*baṭar al-ḥaqq*) and the contempt of people (*ghamṭ al-nās*)." And in the hadith qudsī: "Pride is My cloak and greatness is My mantle; whoever contests Me in either, I cast into the Fire."

It is not clear from the Qur'an or Sunnah whether there is an integral and essential difference between the angels and the jinn, and if so, what that difference is. There may be a difference of types, with clear differences between their characteristics. Alternatively, it may simply be that the jinn are a subset of the angels. The Qur'an itself says of Iblīs, "he was of the jinn" (18:50), even while he is pictured standing among the angelic assembly — hence the majority position that he was present among them though not of them in substance: the angels created of light, the jinn of smokeless fire, Ādam of clay. However, the general object here is to expose Shayṭān because of his objection to Allah's (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) designation of this weak and flawed human as His khalīfah on earth. In the satanic mind the oppressed are just that: oppressed. They are created oppressed, they are born oppressed, they deserve to be oppressed, and they should remain oppressed.

This primordial satanic attitude has its contemporary incarnation in the sponsors and maintainers of policies that subdue humanity through institutions of servitude to the strong and mighty in society — the social, economic and political elites of the modern world. The human psychology that cannot imagine a God who favors the poor, the impoverished and the destitute, rationalizes this attitude with arguments of racism, nationalism, ethnic superiority and class supremacy. Long before fascism, imperialism, communism and Zionism began to terrorize the mass of humanity, there came the satanic statement of inspiration for these anti-god systems, "I am better than he: You have created me out of fire, whereas him You have created out of clay" (7:12). Observe the logic closely, for it is the founding syllogism of materialism: worth is measured by the substrate — by what a thing is made of — rather than by the choice of its Maker. By that logic the son of fire outranks the son of clay; by that logic, every later ranking of human beings by blood, color, class, or genotype is a paraphrase of Iblīs's sentence. The Qur'an's answer is the doctrine of *taqwā* as the only aristocracy: "the most honored of you in the sight of Allah is the most conscious of Him" (49:13).

Continuing from the previous āyāt, at the beginning of Sūrah al-Baqarah, which highlighted the classification of people into the three broad categories of mu'mins, kuffār, and munāfiqūn, here the Qur'an introduces the first known kāfir, Iblis. He is the first form of life and intellect who is known to have objected to Allah's (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) power. He objected to it not because he did not know or realize that Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) is the Almighty and the Most-Powerful, for he clearly did. He objected because Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) wanted to designate a khalīfah on earth, by empowering man. Iblis had no shortage of rationalizations for his conduct, as the coming sūrahs reveal. It is also worth noting that even before he was confirmed as a kāfir, Iblis's suspicious behavior had been characterized by questioning and false pride. These are the psychological attitudes of all beings that take issue with the power of Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) on earth. From this scene the believers learn to diagnose the disease wherever it reappears: pride begets refusal, refusal begets rationalization, rationalization hardens into kufr — and each stage is curable by tawbah except the last, when the self has become the standard of truth.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:35

> And We said, "O Ādam, dwell you and your wife in the garden, and eat freely thereof, both of you, whatever you may wish; but do not approach this one tree, lest you become offenders."

**Commentary:**

After the prototypical kāfir is identified, the āyāt go on to speak of the condition of Ādam (عليه السلام), and in this discourse the reader is left with a sense that the angels and Shayṭān somehow have access to the vital core of man. They appear to have some influence over the decisions he makes. Thus the instinct that makes him do what is good and right is angelic; the impulse to do what is evil and wrong is satanic. Man's predisposition to do what is right is tawfīq; and his tendency to do what is wrong is ghiwāyah. A hadith of the Prophet (ﷺ) states that Shayṭān circulates in man the way blood circulates in the body. This refers to the subtle and pervasive presence of Shayṭān in man's life. It is indicated in the Qur'an that Shayṭān procreates but Angels do not, "Will you, then, take him and his offspring for [your] allies instead of Me, although they are your foes…?" (18:50).

These āyāt point out that Ādam and Hawwā (عليهما السلام) — zawj (spouse) — were given the freedom to live and eat from the garden, with just one exception. They were told not to approach a particular tree lest they become guilty of injustice. It can be understood from this āyah that human nature has a fascination with the prohibited. It is this fascination that becomes the playground of the devil. The Qur'an's wording is surgical in this respect: "do not approach (*lā taqrabā*) this one tree" — the fence is drawn not around the act but around the approach to it; the sin is fenced at its first threshold, at the look, the stroll, the thought of it. This is the preventive ethics of the sharīʿah: "do not even approach fornication" (17:32), not merely "do not commit it." Whoever grazes the perimeter will eventually pasture inside.

Note too what the āyah does not name: the tree. The garden is unnamed and the tree is unnamed because the lesson is not botanical but anthropological — it is about prohibition as such, about the creature's relation to the single limit drawn by the Creator amid total permission. The classical exegetes did record the older identifications — wheat, the fig, the vine, the "tree of the immortality of kingship" — but the soundest observation is ʿAlī's reported remark that Allah tested Ādam with a tree and forbade him a tree, and many trees did not lead him astray; what led him astray was the failure to fulfill the covenant, and Allah mentions the tree only to make the story intelligible. And when Allah willed to test, He tested by the tree — an unremarkable object — teaching that the weight of a command lies in the Commander, not in the commanded thing. In the same spirit: the entire garden is licensed ("eat freely thereof, both of you, whatever you may wish"), one tree is fenced — the architecture of the sharīʿah in miniature: the ḥalāl vast and hospitable, the ḥarām pinpointed and few; whoever magnifies the fenced few until they eclipse the open whole has misread the map.

It should be noted that the motivation of Adam and Eve (عليهما السلام) to err (not sin) against Allah (ﷻ) came from their ambition to become supermen and superwomen with everlasting life. "Thereupon Shaytān insinuated unto the two with a view to making them conscious of their nakedness of which (hitherto) they had been unaware; and he said, 'Your Sustainer has but forbidden you this tree lest you two become [as] angels, or lest you live forever'" (7:20). Instead of having the human, through self-denial, blend into the divinity of Allah (ﷻ) through His decree, the ego in man sought to have it all: confirmed heavenly status, eternity, and the manifestation of an individualistic absolutism. The societal embodiment of the Adamic mistake would ultimately evolve into Banū Isrā'īl, who would multiply and socialize what was in generations past a personal trait; the Adam-and-Eve ego was promoted in Israeli history from those individualistic moments of a vulnerable persona of Ādam (عليه السلام) to a premeditating clan of Israel. This new feature that is diametrically and doggedly opposed to the divine Will is the communal individualism of Banū Isrā'īl. This communal individualism that is fed by a lesser individual absolutism has become the test of any human concentration of power. When Ādam (عليه السلام) was empowered to be Allah's (ﷻ) khalīfah he "grew an ego." When the Israelis were empowered to be pioneers of a divine system on earth they also "grew an ego," from which emerged such notions as "God's chosen race," "God's chosen people," the gentiles, the goyim, and other analogous concepts. As the Israelis have sought to be God's chosen race, Ādam and his wife (عليهما السلام), in a sense, wanted to be God's chosen individuals. In both cases the concept and the handling of power has gone to the ego instead of to the heart. Other nationalisms and racism are but an imitation or a mutation of this serious mistake and manifest deviation from the human-to-divine relationship.

For completeness of the moral record, the Qur'an's narration of the garden is scrupulously fair between the spouses: the address is dual throughout — "dwell you (dual) and eat (dual)" — the permission is for both, the prohibition is for both, and in the event both slipped and both repented (7:22–23). The Qur'an knows nothing of a primeval woman whose vanity undid the race; the burden of the fall is laid on the pair and, decisively, on the whisperer. And the warning-clause "lest you become of the ẓālimīn" teaches the Qur'an's first definition of injustice: ẓulm of the self — to wrong oneself by transgressing the limit, before any wrong is done to another.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:36

> But Shaytān swayed them both therein, and thus ousted them from their erstwhile state. And so We said, "Down with you, [to become henceforth] enemies unto one another; and on earth you shall have your abode and your livelihood for a while!"

**Commentary:**

Through the satanic connection that foments latent ambitions and greed, Ādam (عليه السلام) — the human — and his mate stumbled. They lost their balance and hence their presence in the eternal garden. In other words, true to their human nature, they succumbed to their human susceptibility and weakness through Shayṭān's contact with them. Thus, they (Ādam, Hawwā (عليهما السلام), and Shayṭān) were told to descend as "enemies of each other." Earth was to be their new domicile and abode for a time.

The Arabic verb is *azallahumā* — "he made the two of them slip": the noun from its root is *zallah*, a stumble. The fathers of humanity fell; they did not apostatize. This lexical precision is the Qur'an's own verdict on the gravity of the event, and it grounds the later doctrine that what occurred in the garden was a lapse of the first believers, not the inheritance of an original guilt — in Islām no child inherits Adam's fall; each inherits his freedom. Compare the twin descriptions of Shayṭān's method in the sister-āyāt: he "swayed them by delusion" here, he "whispered to them" in 7:20, and the Qur'an names his whole craft *waswasah* (114:4–5) — insinuation, the exploitation of a desire already latent: ambition for permanence and status. He creates no appetite; he only misdirects the existing one. That is why the defense against him is not the amputation of desire but its reorientation by dhikr, for he withdraws from the heart that remembers: "the scheme of Shayṭān was ever weak" (4:76).

The sentence pronounced is threefold, and each clause defines the human condition on earth. "Down with you" — the descent (*ihbiṭū*) is addressed, per the plain sense of the passage, to Ādam, Ḥawwāʾ and Iblīs together: the tempter is exiled with the tempted, and the stage of history is shared by the two adversaries. "Enemies unto one another" — the minimum meaning is man versus Shayṭān; the exegetes add man versus his own nafs, the impulse against the conscience, and — tragically — man versus man: the first child of this first couple will prove the point when Qābīl kills Hābīl (5:27–31), the first blood shed on the new earth foreshadowing the angels' very question about bloodshed. And "on earth you shall have your abode and your livelihood for a while" — the dunyā is defined by two limits: it is a dwelling (*mustaqarr*), a stage and not the destination; and an enjoyment until a term (*matāʿ ilā ḥīn*), a lease that expires. Whoever mistakes the lodging for the homeland has misread his own address; and the roof of the whole arrangement is mercy, not vengeance, for the very next āyah opens with the promise of guidance — the expulsion is wrapped, before it ends, in the guarantee "there shall most certainly come unto you guidance from Me" (2:38).

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> Thereupon Ādam received words [of guidance] from his Sustainer, and He accepted his repentance: for, verily, He alone is the Acceptor of Repentance, the Dispenser of Grace.

**Commentary:**

Then Ādam (عليه السلام) was forgiven his transgression as he repented his error. Ādam (عليه السلام) learned the error of his actions through experience, and Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) embraces those who learn, even if it is by their mistakes, as long as their egos do not thrive on their sins. Ādam (عليه السلام) knew that he had made a mistake; he knew that errors entail consequences. He regretted what he had done. Notice that the feelings of regret and shame follow the realization of error. Out of this comes a determination not to repeat the mistake. This is the atonement that gains Ādam (عليه السلام) and his descendants absolution and grace.

The "words" (*kalimāt*) which Ādam received from his Sustainer are identified by the exegetes with the prayer the Qur'an itself records in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf: "They said: 'Our Sustainer! We have wronged ourselves; and unless You grant us forgiveness and bestow Your mercy upon us, we shall most certainly be among the lost'" (7:23) — thus the first recorded human supplication in revelation is a confession, not a defense. Other reports, following Ibn ʿAbbās, connect it to the word of distress that the Qur'an quotes on the tongue of Yūnus: "There is no deity but You; limitless are You in Your glory! Indeed, I was of the wrongdoers" (21:87). In either case the anatomy of return is identical, and from it the scholars distilled the conditions of every valid tawbah: cessation of the sin, regret for it, resolve not to return to it — and, where the wrong touched another, restoration of his right. Tawbah in Arabic is "return": the sinner turns back to the station he abandoned; and the Qur'an crowns the verb with a divine name of intensiveness — *at-Tawwāb*, the One who accepts repentance again and again: not a one-time amnesty but a standing disposition of mercy paired with *ar-Raḥīm*.

The Prophet (ﷺ) said: "Every son of Ādam errs, and the best of those who err are those who repent," and again: "The one who repents from sin is like the one who has no sin." Set beside the fate of Iblīs, this āyah completes the Qur'an's masterclass in moral psychology. Both erred in the garden's orbit; compare only their two sentences. Ādam: "Our Sustainer, we have wronged ourselves" — the self is indicted, the Lord is petitioned. Iblīs: "Grant me respite until the Day they are raised" (7:14) — no confession, only a request for time to pursue the grudge. One plea sought pardon; the other sought a postponement to do more harm. Error joined to humility becomes an ascent — Ādam fell from the garden and rose to prophethood; error joined to pride becomes a descent beneath even the fallen state — Iblīs stood in the heavenly assembly and ended as the accursed. On this contrast the tradition grounded the maxim: error is human; persistence in error is satanic. And in the phrase "words from his Sustainer" lies a quiet mercy for all the descendants: man does not invent his own apology — the very words of return are taught by the One to whom he returns.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:38

> [For although] We did say, "Down with you all from this [state]," there shall, nonetheless, most certainly come unto you guidance from Me, and those who follow My guidance need have no fear, and neither shall they grieve.

**Commentary:**

So Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) had such compassion for humans that He did not leave them to wander without guidance in a world charged with satanic pulses; instead, He promised to provide them guidance: "There shall, nonetheless, most certainly come unto you guidance from Me, and those who follow My guidance need have no fear, and neither shall they grieve; but those who are bent on denying My power presence and giving the lie to Our messages — they are destined for the fire, and therein shall they abide" (2:38–39).

From these āyāt (2:30–39), it becomes evident that Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) has empowered man and designated him His khalīfah on earth. This places man in the position he should be in, a position of honor, dignity and power. Man should never become an adjunct to a machine, nor should he become mechanized. There is a standard human that Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) has empowered, and he should do his best to live up to his responsibilities. Man cannot forget or conveniently hide the fact that the universe has been regulated to accommodate him and make his life convenient. This was not done by some random event or sequence of events; the odds against that happening are impossible. There is a God who made all this possible with His knowledge and wisdom, and He cares for man so much that He has provided him with scripture and revelation so that he can reason and learn. This is intended to enable him to do what is required. If man errs, then He forgives, provided that he first repents.

The āyah's promise is cast in the Arabic with the certainty of a thing already decreed — guidance from Allah (الله) *shall* come — and history vindicated the promise in an unbroken chain: messengers to every people ("there was no community but a warner passed within it," 35:24; "We raised in every community a messenger," 16:36), scriptures sent down, and the line sealed with the Qur'an and the final Prophet (ﷺ), "a mercy for all the worlds" (21:107). The condition attached to the security is precise and must not be missed: "those who *follow* (yatbiʿ) My guidance" — following, not merely admiring, assenting, or celebrating guidance in the abstract. Guidance in Qur'anic idiom is a road to be walked; al-Fātiḥah, recited in every cycle of every prayer, begs for the walking — "guide us to the straight path" — and the whole of Sūrah al-Baqarah may be read as the map that follows that petition.

The reward of following is stated as a double immunity: "no fear shall fall upon them, neither shall they grieve." Fear projects forward onto a loss to come; grief looks backward onto a loss sustained. The Qur'an's refrain of psychological security cancels both axes of anxiety — the future and the past — for the one whose life is aligned with heaven's guidance (the refrain recurs: 2:62; 2:112; 2:262; 2:274; 2:277; 3:170; 7:49; 10:62; 46:13). And this is the Qur'an's earliest formulation of the theodicy of exile: the descent from the garden is answered, before the sentence of descent has even cooled, with the guarantee of guidance — the earth is not a prison but a stage for khilāfah; "Indeed We established you in the earth and made for you therein means of livelihood; little is it that you give thanks" (7:10). The door Adam left was closed behind him, but this āyah is the door opened in front of him — and in front of every descendant: whoever follows the guidance re-enters, in this world, the neighborhood of the garden he lost — "the ones who believe and do righteous deeds are the companions of the Garden; they shall abide therein" (2:82, and see 20:123–124, where the same promise follows the same descent: "whoever follows My guidance shall neither go astray nor be miserable").

Materialism has triggered the emergence of a class of power-hungry people who have severed the natural human relationship with Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ). They displace this relationship with ideologies and systems of secularism that marginalize or deny Allah's (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) power-presence in human affairs, and in the universe as a whole. The materialistic camps of this world routinely trespass on Allah's (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) prerogatives, and violate the proper heaven-to-earth relationship in every possible way. Out of this mess on earth the only way to redeem mankind is for man to realize that Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) wants to favor him when he is weak — the precise point that Shaytān objected to. However, man is required to maintain a deliberate and conscious relationship of humility and subservience with Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ), and be able and willing to improve this relationship. If, in this struggle between a human who is developing a conscious relationship with Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ), and Shaytān who is determined to thwart this relationship by appealing to the human ego, humans make mistakes and errors, Allah (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) will accept their repentance provided they are genuinely striving to purge their individual and collective selves of the ego. The Prophet of Allah (ﷺ) said, "No person with a scintilla of ego in his heart will enter paradise."

Among the most provocative arguments used against humans who want to fulfill their role of khilāfah on earth is that they are establishing a theocracy, or "playing God," or "holier than thou," or claim to be God's representatives on earth to the exclusion of all other peoples. This is a gross misrepresentation of the role of Muslims who are, and should always be, aware of their unique role of "light unto the peoples of the world." This is not simply an Islamic version of the Western tradition of power monopoly and manipulation. Should Muslims understand their role in such a similarly arrogant manner, or behave in identical ways once in positions of power, they clearly do not understand Allah's (عَزَّ وَجَلَّ) purpose in His empowerment of man and are themselves falling victim to Shaytān's appeals to their egos.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:39

> But those who are bent on denying [Me] and giving the lie to Our message, they are destined for the fire, and therein they shall abide forever.

**Commentary:**

The cluster's closing āyah draws the line that the whole passage has been bending toward: two humanities, two trajectories. "Those who follow My guidance need have no fear, and neither shall they grieve" (2:38); "those who are bent on denying [Me] and giving the lie to Our message — they are destined for the fire, and therein they shall abide." Note the pair of verbs by which the Qur'an describes the losers: they *deny* (yakfurūn) and they *give the lie* (yukadhdhibūn). Kufr is the inward rejection — the covering of known truth; takdhīb is kufr gone public and verbal — the branded lie, the refusal to let the truth stand even as words. Denial hardens into belying, and the hardening is a career: the Qur'an's participles here denote a settled disposition, not a single stumble. A lapse of the disposition kind can still repent — Ādam proved it in this very passage; a life of the disposition kind closes upon itself — Iblīs proved that too, and the fire named here is the destiny his pattern earns. The first kāfir was introduced in 2:34; this āyah is his inheritance deed, and every kufr after his is a reprint of his sequence: pride, refusal, denial, belying.

The symmetry of the two endings is exact and deliberate: security without fear and without grief, versus fire and abiding therein. The one is psychological wholeness extended into eternity; the other is the self's own fire — kindled by its own concealments — made permanent. The Qur'an repeats the sentence elsewhere so that no reader may mistake its gravity: "whoever kills a believer deliberately, his recompose is Jahannam, abiding therein" (4:93); "thus does Allah recompense every stubborn denier" (40:74–76). And the āyah quietly completes the link back to the opening of the sūrah: al-Baqarah began by sorting humanity into muttaqīn, kāfirīn and munāfiqūn; the sorting is now given its narrative root — the categories are as old as the garden, and the archetype of the middle category has just been exhibited. For the believer the lesson lands as consolation and commission: guidance has been guaranteed, following has been commanded, the outcomes belong to Allah (الله) — "you do not guide whom you love; rather Allah guides whom He wills" (28:56) — so the grief of rejection is not the preacher's to carry; the carrying of the message is.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:45

> Seek help in patience and ṣalāh; and truly it is difficult [to do so] except for those who are humble-minded;

Ṣabr in the Qur'an is a threefold strength: the patience that holds a person steady in obedience when obedience is costly; the patience that holds him back from sin when sin is easy; and the patience that endures affliction when affliction cannot be lifted. It is mentioned in the Book well over ninety times — nearly always as the twin of its opposite or the condition of its reward — and the Qur'an couples it with the divine presence itself: "Allah is with the patient" (2:153; 8:46), a companionship announced with the particle *inna* of emphasis and nothing between His name and theirs. The pairing of the two in this āyah is thus the complete therapeutic: ṣabr steadies the soul in the interval between prayers; ṣalāh re-charges the soul's capacity to be steady.

Then comes the āyah's astonishing candor: "truly it is difficult — *innaḥā lakabīrah* — except for the humble-minded." The Qur'an names its own prescription heavy before it promises its fruit: the doubled *kāf-bā-rā* (*lakabīratun*) is the word for a mountain's bulk, for an unbearable load. The honest reader who has tried both knows exactly what the verse means: to hold the ṣabr and keep the ṣalāh when the world presses — that is weight-lifting of the spirit. But the exception-clause explains why it is heavy and for whom it becomes light: *illā ʿalā al-khāshiʿīn* — except for those of khushūʿ. What makes the two anchors heavy is carrying them without the humility; what makes them weightless is carrying them with it.

The verse closes (in the Arabic order, completed by the next) without naming the reward — and that restraint is itself the lesson: the help sought in ṣabr and ṣalāh is not the reward after them, but the strength inside them. The following āyah will define the khushūʿ it appeals to.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:46

> Who know that they will have to meet their Lord, and that unto Him they are returning.

**Commentary:**

This āyah is the definition clause of the one before it: the humble-minded (*al-khāshiʿūn*) of 2:45 are here defined not by a posture but by a piece of knowledge — "who know (*yaẓunnūna*) that they are to meet their Lord, and that unto Him they are returning." The Arabic verb is from *ẓann*, which in ordinary usage means to suppose or conjecture; but here, as the exegetes unanimously explain, it is the *ẓann* of certainty — the conviction so settled that it functions as sight. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the believer's conviction elsewhere precisely because the meeting is not yet visible: it is known the way one knows the sunrise is coming at the end of the night — by evidence, not by eyeballs. Such knowledge is the difference between the one who prays heavily and the one who prays lightly: the lightness has nothing to do with temperament and everything to do with expectation.

The knowledge itself has two arms, and the āyah states both: *mulāqā* — the meeting with the Lord; and *rajūʿ* — the return to Him. The meeting is the appointment: death, then resurrection, then the standing — "the day when every soul will find present what it did of good and what it did of evil" (3:30). The return is the journey's end: all of creation is on loan out of His presence and on schedule back into it — "O tranquil soul, return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing; then enter among My servants, and enter My Garden" (89:27–30). The believer who holds both arms of the knowledge lives in a different geometry from the one who does not: time is not open-ended drift but the interval before an appointment; the self is not autonomous property returning to itself but a trust returning to its Owner; and no injustice, no exile, no power of Pharaoh or Zion, is final — because every ledger is reopenable at the meeting.

This is why the āyah stands where it stands: it is the engine of the ṣabr and ṣalāh commanded in 2:45, and the missing engine is why those duties are heavy for everyone else. The person for whom the prayer is a weight is the person who has forgotten — or shelved — the appointment; the moment the meeting is certain, the prayer becomes correspondence with the One he is about to meet, and the ṣabr becomes the discipline of the interval. The early generations used to prepare for decades for an audience with a king; the Qur'an here tells the believer the audience is fixed, the King is known, and the date is the only unknown. The Qur'an's refrain on the heedless is the mirror of this verse: "they are those who deny the meeting with their Lord" (among others 6:31; 10:45; 18:105; 32:10) — and the denial is never argued so much as wished, because a heart that will not stand for the appointment edits it out of the calendar. The khāshiʿ keeps it on the calendar; that, in the end, is all the khushūʿ ever was.

The āyah must be read whole: "Remember those blessings of Mine with which I graced you, and how I favored you over all people (*al-ʿālamīn*)." The exegetes gloss *al-ʿālamīn* here as the peoples of their era — the world they lived in — for the Qur'an does not confer an open-ended, unconditional supremacy; its favors are always strung to covenants and obligations. What were the favors? The following āyāt will enumerate them at length: deliverance from Pharaoh's slaughter, the parting of the sea, the forgiveness after the calf, the Torah and the criterion, the shade of clouds, manna and quails, the twelve springs, the holy land — above all, an unbroken chain of prophets and scripture. "And We gave the Children of Israel the Book and wisdom and prophethood, and provided them with good things, and preferred them above the worlds" (45:16). But the very next sūrah will state the other edge of the preference with identical clarity: "Moses said to his people: 'O my people, remember the favor of Allah upon you when He raised among you prophets and made you kings and gave you what He gave not to anyone among the worlds. O my people, enter the holy land…'" (5:20–21) — and then, when they refused, the favor was succeeded by forty years of wandering. Preference, in the Qur'an's grammar, is a grant with a duty stapled to it; the Israelite version severed the staple, and the āyah's own wording puts the correction in the possessive: *niʿma* is "Mine" (*blessings of Mine*), not theirs — they were custodians, never owners, of the favor.

And the conditions run in both directions, as the covenant-āyāt state: "if you fulfill My covenant I shall fulfill yours" (2:40), "Allah promised those of you who believe and do right that He will surely establish them as successors on the earth, as He established those before them" (24:55) — where the phrase "as He established those before them" is the whole history of Banū Isrāʾīl compressed into one clause: establishment, forfeiture, replacement. The lesson is not for one people. The Muslim ummah holds a parallel grant — "you are the best community ever raised for mankind: you enjoin right and forbid wrong" (3:110) — and the conditional participle is doing quiet work in that verse too: the rank is for the enjoining, and the ummah that exports sermons while exempting itself (the exact charge of 2:44) should hear in this āyah the sound of its own address. The Qur'an abolished racial election in a single sentence: "the most honored of you in the sight of Allah is the most conscious of Him" (49:13). The favor that cannot be forfeited is the one never tied to blood in the first place.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:48

> And remain conscious of [the coming of] a Day when no human being shall in the least avail another, nor shall intercession be accepted from any of them, nor ransom taken from them, and none shall be supported.

**Commentary:**

"O Descendants of Israel!... guard yourselves against [the consequences of] a Day when one soul shall not relieve another soul, nor shall intercession be accepted from any, nor shall any be able to ransom itself, and none shall be helped." The privileges granted to the Children of Israel were conditional upon their assumption and discharge of power in light of divine guidance. But power can corrupt, and it corrupted the Children of Israel, as history demonstrates. When that corruption set in and the leaders of Israel were no longer capable of fulfilling God's command to administer power with justice and equity, they forfeited His divine favor and fell from grace to treachery. It was their position of honor and reverence, which accrued from the principled exercise of temporal power, that led the Children of Israel to believe they were the only people belonging to God, to believe they were the only humans deserving God's care, and to believe that all other peoples were inferior. Even when Prophets and Apostles (عليهم السلام) were divinely commissioned to rectify this attitude, the Israelis killed these men of God. It is no coincidence that in the course of history, these Israelis fell from the seat of Jerusalemite power into the ghettos and slums of human societies around the world. In the Israeli interpretation of history and humanity, prophets are to be censured if they challenge the deviations and excesses of the Children of Israel. It is little wonder that Allah (ﷻ) condemned these Israelis to a life of ignominy. Their insistence on an Israelist universe has earned them their history of exodus and exile. One might think that a reminder would humble their egos and draw them back into the divine fold. This Qur'an, Allah's (ﷻ) final word to humanity, tries to do precisely that by reminding them of a time when they were dear to Allah (ﷻ) and when Allah (ﷻ) was near to them; but these Israelis will not have it. Their atrophied consciences seem to be beyond the warnings of the Qur'an. They do not want to be reminded.

And so, as they do not want to open their consciences to times past, the Qur'an gives them a vision of the future, a Day when the Israeli individual is ripped from the Israeli clan and he has to stand alone and account for his deeds, "when a soul cannot compensate for another soul." The āyah's Arabic builds a wall of four negations around the standing soul, and each negation closes one of the exits that worldly power knows. *Lā tamliku imru'an li-imri'in shayʾā* — no soul owns anything for another: the first exit closed is influence by proxy, the patron, the fixer, the clan whose name opens doors; on that Day the name opens nothing. *Wa-lā tuqbalu min-hā shafāʿah* — no intercession is accepted: the second exit closed is the intermediary for sale; the verse negates intercession absolutely here, and the Qur'an reconciles the seeming tension with the āyāt that affirm intercession "by His permission" (2:255; 20:109; 53:26) precisely as the exegetes reconciled it: intercession belongs to Allah (39:44), is granted only by His leave, only to whom He is pleased with, and is never for sale, never solicited by rank, never a franchise of any clergy or aristocracy — the intercession denied in this āyah is the intercession the powerful assumed they could arrange in advance, the heavenly lobby. *Wa-lā yuʾkhadhu minhā ʿiwḍ* — no ransom is taken: the third exit closed is the buy-out — wealth, the very instrument in which this passage has already shown them trading the verses of Allah (2:41); the currency that bought everything on earth converts at no rate on that Day: "the unbelievers would gladly ransom themselves from the punishment… though it be with their sons and wives and brothers… but it will not be accepted" (70:10–14). *Wa-lā hum yunṣarūn* — and they are not helped: the fourth exit closed is rescue — no ally descends, no state, no alliance, no arsenal; the help (*naṣr*) that decided every worldly contest is absent from this one, for the Contest-Giver has already judged.

Each human has been given free will and the capacity to distinguish what is right from what is wrong. He should not let himself be swayed by any ideology that violates divine justice, a divine justice that guarantees man's dignity and honor. Now that the Israelis are back in power and repeating their abuse of it from Jerusalem, they have managed to brainwash world public opinion into believing that the Israeli standards are firm and final, and that other people can be killed off with impunity. The psychology of these Israelis is almost unbelievable. When the Qur'an reminds them of their glorious Godly past, they refuse to remember it; and when the Qur'an warns them of the inevitable encounter with God to come, they do not want to know. This is a common feature of those who sell themselves for the trappings of power, including non-Yahūd, and even Muslims, whose behavior shows similar traits. The Qur'an's warning is there for all peoples for all times; it is not going to react to their pitiful power predicament. The Qur'an is going to continue to drill in the lesson until they adjust and behave or show contempt and act with arrogance and aggression.

For the believer, the āyah is the completion of the thought begun in 2:46 — the meeting with the Lord is certain, and this is its agenda: individual accounting, "every soul is pledged for what it earned" (74:38). The Qur'an's immediate audience was a people who trusted in lineage — children of prophets, carriers of scripture — and the verse strips that trust bare: the ancestor will not attend the audit of the descendant. Islām's democratization of destiny is total: no blood screens, no portfolio transfers, no counsel retained. What remains standing when the four exits are sealed is the only thing the believer ever really owned — his own taqwā, his own record, his own relationship with the One before whom he stands. Hence the āyah's opening imperative — *wa-ittaqū* — "and be conscious of" the Day: the taqwā of that Day is not terror but preparation, the one asset not subject to the four negations, "and take provision — and the best of provision is taqwā" (2:197).
