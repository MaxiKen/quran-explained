## Verse 81

Verse 81 says: *"But no! Those who commit evil and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."*

In simple terms, the verse above is the Qur'an's answer to the claim the previous verse recorded — the claim that the Fire would touch them only for a few days. "But no!" is the response: the matter is not as the claimants wished. The verdict is stated generally: whoever commits evil and is engrossed in sin will be a resident of the Fire, forever.

The key expression is "engrossed in sin" — in Arabic, *aḥāṭat bihi khaṭī'atuhu*, literally "his sin surrounds him." The image is of a person whose wrongdoing closes in on him from every side, leaving no room, no exit, and no remnant of good. The classical commentators (Ibn Kathir and Ma'arif al-Qur'an among them) explain that the verse speaks of the person whose sins have taken hold of him completely, so that when he comes before Allah he brings nothing but evil — no good deed that could stand in his favour. Such a person is the resident of the Fire and will remain there: the verdict is unqualified and unending.

The scholars also note an important distinction in reading this verse. Since the verse concerns those whose sin has completely engulfed them, the mainstream understanding across the centuries has been that this describes the state of one who dies upon disbelief — the person whose rejection of Allah nullifies even his apparent good, so that no goodness remains to weigh in his favour. A believer who commits sin does not thereby become a resident of the Fire forever; he is under the promise and the warning of Allah, subject to His judgment, mercy, and the possibility of forgiveness (the principle the Qur'an states in 4:48 and 4:116). The verse's force, then, is not a blanket condemnation of every sinner but a statement of the fate of the person whose sin has become his whole identity — who lives, and dies, in it.

The verse also answers a deeper human tendency: the religion of the discounted verdict. The Jews of the verse had reduced divine justice to a few days because they wanted the certainty of salvation without its conditions. The same pattern persists wherever a person assumes that divine punishment is a formality that will not touch him, or that his identity — his community, his ancestry, his good standing — will stand between him and the consequences of his deeds. The Qur'an answers: no. The criterion is the state of the soul, and the soul that has been engulfed by evil has chosen its own residence.

In modern life, the verse asks each reader to examine the direction of his life: what is closing in on him — the goodness that surrounds him and draws him upward, or the wrong that encloses him and leaves no exit? And it warns against the comfortable assumption — held by people of every religion and none — that the reckoning will be brief, small, and manageable. The verse says the reckoning is as large as the sin that has engulfed the person, and the mercy is as large as the goodness that remains.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 82

Verse 82 says: *"And those who believe and do good will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows completes the pair. If verse 81 describes the fate of the person engulfed in evil, verse 82 describes the fate of the person who believes and does good: the residents of Paradise, forever. The two verses together state the Qur'an's fundamental law of the hereafter: the outcome is determined not by lineage, not by label, not by wish, but by faith and deeds.

The pairing is deliberate and precise. The same word, *aṣḥāb* — "companions," rendered here as "residents" — is used for both: the companions of the Fire and the companions of Paradise. The symmetry is a warning against the assumption of privilege. In the previous section, some Jews claimed that the Fire would touch them only briefly, and in a later verse they will claim that the Home of the Hereafter is exclusively theirs out of all humanity (2:94). The Qur'an answers both claims with the same law: the two categories are not "Jews and others" or "our community and theirs," but those engulfed by evil and those who believe and do good. Anyone can enter either category; no one has a reservation in either.

The verse also defines the content of the second category: belief and good deeds. "Believe" — a living faith in Allah, His messengers, and the Last Day — and "do good" — the deeds that faith produces in the world. The Qur'an never separates the two: faith without deeds is dead, and deeds without faith are rejected. The phrase recurs throughout the Qur'an as the description of true religion, and the same verse that defines the believers' reward here is the verse that resolves the question of salvation for people of every community (2:62): whoever truly believes and does good.

The verse is also an answer to a subtle spiritual error: the error of treating the mercy of Allah as a license to ignore the conditions. Because Allah is merciful, the argument runs, the details do not matter. The Qur'an's answer: the mercy is real — the believers enter Paradise only through it — but the mercy applies through the life of faith and good deeds, not instead of it. The person who hopes for Paradise while living in the category of verse 81 — evil at the centre, sin surrounding him — has not understood either verse.

In modern life, the two verses form a mirror for self-examination, and it is worth noting that they are more difficult than they first appear. Neither says "those who commit no evil" nor "those who never sin." The first describes the person whose sin has engulfed him; the second describes the person whose faith and good deeds define him. The question before every reader is which of the two describes the centre of his life: the sin that surrounds, or the faith and good that surround. Both verses are an invitation to move from one category to the other — and the door between them is the one the Qur'an calls turning back, repentance, and the doing of good.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 83

Verse 83 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, "Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax." But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent."*

In simple terms, the verse now records the covenant that Allah took from the Children of Israel: worship none but Allah; kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, and the needy; kind speech to people; prayer; and alms. The covenant is a complete definition of religion in a single sentence — the vertical relation to Allah, the horizontal relations to humanity, and the ritual acts that train the soul.

The first command is the foundation: "Worship none but Allah." This is the covenant at the heart of every divine message, and it is the first thing ever written on the tablets and the first thing ever addressed to every nation. Everything else in the verse flows from it, because the person who has placed his worship in the right place is the person who can receive the other commands. The rest of the verse is the shape that the worship takes in the world: it is kindness, and it is honest speech, and it is prayer, and it is the giving of a portion of one's wealth.

The social commands are striking in their range. Parents come first — the nearest relation, the first debt. Then relatives, then orphans and the needy — the ones who have no one else. The verse then adds "speak kindly to people" — a command that has no exception: not only to the good, not only to the deserving; the command is to all people. A religion that is measured only by its rituals is incomplete; a ritual that does not change the tongue, the hand, and the treatment of the weakest is hollow. The Qur'an's commentators note that this command — *qūlū lilnāsi ḥusnā* — includes truthfulness and gentleness, and that it is one of the most comprehensive of all the commandments: it makes every conversation an act of worship.

Then the verse records the response: "But you turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent." The Arabic word *tawallaytum* — turned away — is the same word used earlier for the turning from the covenant at the mountain (2:64), and the pattern is the same: the covenant was received, and then it was abandoned. The phrase "except for a few of you" is the Qur'an's repeated qualification throughout this passage — the few who actually kept the covenant, the few who believed, the few who stood in the long history of Israel.

In modern life, the verse serves as a reminder that religion is not a compartment. The person who prays but does not speak kindly, who worships but neglects his parents, who gives alms but is cruel to the weak, has not fulfilled the covenant in the Qur'an's terms. The verse is also a warning against the culture of indifference that follows the abandonment of the covenant: a religion can be formally complete and spiritually dead, and the person who has all the rituals and none of the obligations is closer to the description "turned away" than he realizes.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 84

Verse 84 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other's blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness."*

In simple terms, the verse records a second covenant, more specific than the first: the covenant not to shed each other's blood and not to expel one another from their homes. And it adds the crucial historical detail: they gave their pledge and bore witness. The covenant was not made in their absence; they were parties to it, and they testified to it.

The covenant is remarkable for the sensitivity of its terms: blood and homes. These are precisely the things that human beings are prepared to destroy in the pursuit of power. The first covenant (2:83) had commanded worship, kindness, prayer, and alms; this covenant forbids the most fundamental violations — killing one another and displacing one another. The two belong together: the person who has accepted the worship of Allah has accepted that the life and home of his fellow human being are inviolable.

The phrase "neither shed each other's blood" is translated from the Arabic *lā tasfikūna dimā'akum* — "do not shed your blood" — where "your blood" means the blood of one another. The Qur'an uses the same construction later in the verse: "expel each other from their homes." The covenant is not only between a people and Allah; it is between the members of that people themselves. A community that accepts a covenant with Allah has also accepted a covenant with its own members: their lives are not cheap, and their homes are not disposable.

The verse's final sentence — "you gave your pledge and bore witness" — is the legal image of the covenant. The people were not coerced by an unknown condition; they acknowledged it and became witnesses to it, both to themselves and to their history. The classical commentators note that this covenant follows the general covenant of the previous verse: it is one of the provisions of the Torah that the Children of Israel had confirmed at Sinai, and they knew it, accepted it, and were then to be held to it. They cannot plead ignorance.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the moment when a covenant is made — the moment of the promise, the pledge, the contract, the marriage, the oath, the acceptance of the law. The danger of the covenant is not that it is made but that it is made and then forgotten. The verse stresses the witness: the parties were present, they promised, and they knew. The Qur'an's history is a history of covenants made, witnessed, and then broken — and the verse before us is the record of the promise in its original form, so that the breaking can be measured against the pledge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 85

Verse 85 says: *"But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them—though expelling them was unlawful for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest? Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment? For Allah is never unaware of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse now confronts the covenant with the reality. The people who had pledged not to shed each other's blood and not to expel one another were doing exactly both — and worse, they were doing it with the two sides of their law: they would fight one another, and then they would ransom their own captives. The Qur'an's question is the sharpest in the passage: "Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?"

The occasion is well documented in the classical tafsir. Ibn Kathir explains the historical setting: in Madinah there were three Jewish tribes — Banu Qaynuqa', allies of the Khazraj, and Banu Nadir and Banu Qurayzah, allies of the Aws. When the two Arab tribes fought their pre-Islamic wars, the Jewish tribes fought on opposing sides, killing one another though the Torah prohibited it, and helping the idolatrous Arabs against their own kinsmen. When the wars ended, they would ransom the captives taken from their own side, precisely as the Torah commanded — and they would take blood money. The Qur'an's exposure is devastating: they were careful to perform the part of the law that suited them and careless about the part that cost them. "You ransom them," the verse says, "though expelling them was unlawful for you" — the very law they used to justify the ransom was the law they had already broken by expelling the person they now ransomed.

"Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?" This is one of the most famous questions in the Qur'an, and it names the sin of selective religion in one sentence. The person who takes the verses that confirm his interests and ignores the verses that oppose them has not believed in the Scripture; he has used it. The phrase "reject the rest" is precise: the rejection is not ignorance but choice. The people knew both commands, accepted both in the covenant, and then obeyed one and discarded the other.

The consequence is stated in two parts. In this world: disgrace — and the commentators note that the historical fate of these very tribes fulfilled it: Banu Qurayzah were killed and Banu Nadir were expelled from Madinah, the disgrace of this worldly life. In the next: "the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment." The verse ends: "For Allah is never unaware of what you do" — the entire performance was observed.

In modern life, the verse is the mirror of selective religiosity. It is possible to believe in the verses that are gentle and ignore the verses that are demanding, to accept the commands that protect one's own side and reject the commands that protect the other side, to practice charity while participating in the expulsion of others. The Qur'an's answer is that this is not faith — it is a use of the Scripture, and it carries the double consequence: disgrace among people, and the accounting with Allah, who sees the selection and the rejection.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 86

Verse 86 says: *"These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. So their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped."*

In simple terms, the verse sums up the people of the previous verses in one image: they are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. The image is of a transaction — the most unequal transaction imaginable — and the verse then states its consequence: their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped.

The word "trade" is the key. The Qur'an describes this exchange in several places (2:90 uses the same family of words) and always with the same shocking economy: what they received — a short life, a temporary position, a small price — and what they paid — the eternal. The Jews of the previous verses had fought for the interests of their tribes, ransomed or expelled for power, and placed their worldly position above the covenant of Allah. The verse names the transaction: they traded the Home of the Hereafter for this world.

The verb *ishtaraw* — "they bought" — is used in the Qur'an for the people who sell what is eternal for what is momentary. The tragedy is not that they loved the world; the tragedy is that they thought the world was worth the price. And the deeper tragedy, as the previous verses made clear, is that they did not even know what they were trading: the promise of the covenant, the guidance of the prophets, the favor of Allah — all of it exchanged for the life of the world, which was passing at the very moment they acquired it.

The consequence is stated with finality: "their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped." The two clauses correspond to the two forms of hope that sinners place in the future: the hope that the punishment can be negotiated down, and the hope that someone can rescue them — intercession, ransom, assistance. The Qur'an rejects both at once, as it does in the Day of Judgment verses of this same surah (2:48, 2:123): no reduction, and no help.

In modern life, the verse is a warning about invisible transactions. Every person trades constantly: time for money, comfort for meaning, reputation for truth, the world for the soul — and most trades are never noticed because they are small. The Qur'an's objection is not to the world, which it calls a field for the Hereafter, but to the exchange rate. The person who has paid the eternal for the momentary has made the worst trade in existence, and the verse's prophecy is that the accounting will not be reduced and no one will be able to help.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 87

Verse 87 says: *"Indeed, We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers. And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others?"*

In simple terms, the verse now recalls the long prophetic history of the Children of Israel: Moses was given the Book; after him came messengers one after another; then Jesus son of Mary was given clear proofs and supported with the holy spirit. And the verse asks the question that the history itself answers: why is it that every time a messenger comes to you with something you do not like, you become arrogant — rejecting some and killing others?

The verse's structure is the accusation. The first half lists the gifts: the Book given to Moses, the succession of messengers, the clear proofs of Jesus, the support of the holy spirit. The second half lists the response: arrogance, rejection, killing. Every element of the first half is matched by an element of the second: the more Allah gave, the more they rejected; the clearer the proof, the firmer the refusal.

The "holy spirit" is the subject of an important discussion in the tafsir. The classical commentators — Ibn Mas'ud, Ibn Abbas, Muhammad ibn Ka'b, Isma'il ibn Khalid, al-Suddi, al-Rabi' ibn Anas, 'Atiyah al-'Awfi, and Qatadah, as recorded by Ibn Kathir — explain that the holy spirit is Gabriel, the angel of revelation, through whom Allah supported Jesus. The same angel is called "the trustworthy spirit" in the Qur'an's description of the revelation descending upon the Prophet's heart (26:193). The Qur'an records that Gabriel came to Mary, and that Jesus was supported by him; the verse before us connects the gift to Jesus with the theme of revelation itself — a theme the next verses will take up directly.

"With something you do not like" is the most revealing phrase of the verse. The messengers were not rejected because their messages were false — the reception was determined by preference, not evidence. The Arabic *lā tahwā anfusukum* — "what your souls do not desire" — makes the diagnosis precise: the objection is not in the intellect but in the appetite. The messengers came with demands: release the poor, abandon the exploitation, honor the covenant, worship none but Allah. Each generation found some command that its desires refused, and the refusal was dressed as judgment: the prophet is a liar, the message is old, the law is burdensome.

In modern life, the verse asks a question that every hearer must answer for himself: is my response to truth determined by evidence or by desire? The person who has never found a command that troubled him, who has never encountered a truth that cost him something, may be in the position of the people of the verse: not because his message is false, but because his desire has selected his prophets — and the one who selects his prophets to please himself ends up with no prophets at all.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 88

Verse 88 says: *"They say, "Our hearts are unreceptive!" In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith."*

In simple terms, the verse records the response of the people of the Book to the prophets: "Our hearts are unreceptive!" The claim is a defense — we cannot receive what you say. The Qur'an's reply is sharp: "In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith." The condition they describe is not the cause; it is the consequence.

The Arabic word is *ghulf* — the plural of *aghlaf* — which carries the image of a covering, a sheath, a wrapping. The classical commentators explain the claim in two related ways. Ibn Abbas said the hearts are "screened"; Mujahid said they are "covered"; 'Ikrimah said a stamp is upon them; Abu al-'Aliyah said they do not comprehend. The claim, then, was a claim of enclosure: the message cannot enter. Some early authorities — including a reading reported from Ibn Abbas (via Mujahid and Qatadah) and from 'Ata' — even understood the words as arrogance: "our hearts already contain every kind of knowledge and do not need what you bring." The Qur'anic reply, however, is the same in both readings: the condition of the heart is not a natural barrier that exempts them; it is the product of their own disbelief, and the proof is the verdict — Allah has condemned them.

The verse's logic is one of the Qur'an's most important psychological insights: the closed heart is not the cause of rejection but the result of it. The person who repeatedly refuses the truth acquires a habit of refusal, and the habit eventually becomes a condition: the heart that has refused enough becomes the heart that cannot receive. The Qur'an describes the same process in the verse about the coverings (41:5), and later in the passage about the seal upon the hearts of those who disbelieve (4:155, which repeats their very words and answers that Allah has sealed their hearts for their disbelief).

The final clause — "They have but little faith" — has been read in two ways by the commentators, and both are instructive. Some read it as "few of them believe" — the people of the Book as a whole are in the state of rejection, with a small number of believers among them. Others read it as "little is their faith" — the faith they do have is weak, partial, and without effect, since the little belief that does not carry into the acceptance of the messenger is not faith at all. Either reading confirms the same point: a claim of "unreceptive hearts" is not a neutral description of the mind; it is the symptom of a spiritual condition that has been observed and judged.

In modern life, the verse names a familiar state: the person who has read, heard, and encountered the truth — and has built a wall against it, telling himself that his heart is "not the type" to receive it, or that his mind already contains everything it needs. The Qur'an's answer is that the wall is not a natural feature of his heart; it is a result of his refusals, and the verdict on the condition belongs to Allah. The heart that has become unreceptive can be unsealed — but only by the direction the person has been refusing: turning back, and asking.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 89

Verse 89 says: *"Although they used to pray for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists, when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it. So may Allah's condemnation be upon the disbelievers."*

In simple terms, the verse records one of the strangest and most painful episodes in the history of Madinah: the people who had been praying for the coming of the Prophet to gain victory over their enemies were the same people who rejected him when he came. They recognized the Book — the verse says so — and they rejected it anyway.

The occasion is documented in the classical tafsir. Before the Prophet's arrival, the Jews of Madinah — the scholars tell us, notably Ibn Abbas in the report recorded by Ibn Kathir — used to pray for victory over the polytheists by means of the messenger they expected. They would say: a prophet is about to be sent, and we will be with him to exterminate you, as the nations of 'Ad and Iram were exterminated. Abu al-'Aliyah's report is the same: the Jews asked Allah to send the Prophet so that they could torment and kill the disbelievers alongside him. When the Prophet was sent, however, he was sent to the Arabs, and the Jews recognized him — the Qur'an uses the word *'arafū*, "what they recognized" — and they rejected him out of envy and fear of losing their position. One of their scholars, Salam ibn Mushkim, said simply: "He did not bring anything we recognize."

The verse's sequence is the accusation: they prayed for victory by him, the Book came which they recognized, the Scripture confirmed what they had in their hands, and then came the word — "they rejected it." Each clause moves from promise to fulfillment to refusal. And the refusal is not of the Book but of what the Book assigned: the grace of Allah given to whom He wills. The recognition was not the barrier; the recognition made the rejection worse, because they knew exactly what they were doing.

The curse at the end is general: "So may Allah's condemnation be upon the disbelievers" — not "upon the Jews" but upon the disbelievers: the category of everyone who does the same: knows and refuses. The Qur'an distinguishes between the person who has not yet found the truth and the person who has found it and turned away. The first is not cursed; the second is — and the verse applies the verdict to the category, not the nation.

In modern life, the verse is the most powerful statement of the difference between knowledge and acceptance. A person can know that a message is true — can recognize it, confirm it, even pray for its fulfillment — and still refuse it when it arrives in a form he did not choose, or at a cost he is unwilling to pay. The Jews of the verse had prayed for the Prophet they wanted; they were given the Prophet Allah chose. The difference is everything. The verse warns every reader to examine his prayers: are we praying for the truth, or for a truth shaped to our liking? And it warns of the state in which recognition itself becomes evidence against the one who refuses.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 90

Verse 90 says: *"Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for—denying Allah's revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants! They have earned wrath upon wrath. And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment."*

In simple terms, the verse now names the crime in the sharpest form yet: "Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for." The language is of a transaction — souls sold for a price — and the price is then described: the denial of Allah's revelation, and the resentment of Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills. The verse ends: wrath upon wrath, and a humiliating punishment.

The verb *ishtaraw* — "they bought" — is the same word used in the previous verse, and the image is consistent: the people of the Book had put their souls into a transaction. The price they received was the denial of the revelation; what they paid was their souls. The verse's opening phrase — *bi'sa mā ishtaraw bihi anfusahum* — is the Qur'an's judgment on the exchange rate: miserable is the price. The phrase is used elsewhere for the same crime (2:41, 3:187), and always with the same effect: the seller of truth for the world is not even getting the world — he is getting the disappearance of both.

The motive is then named: "resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants." The Arabic *baghyan* — here "resenting" — carries the meaning of envy, transgression, and oppression. The classical commentator al-Mizan notes that the word states the reason for their disbelief: they denied the Prophet not because of any doubt but because of envy — that the favor of revelation, which they expected for themselves, had been given to an Arab. The objection is not to the revelation but to its recipient. This is the most profound form of unbelief: it does not deny that the gift is from Allah; it objects to the Giver's right to give it where He wills.

"Wrath upon wrath" is one of the Qur'an's most terrible expressions. The classical commentators explain the two wraths: the first for the history of transgression that the surah has already recounted — the breaking of covenants, the killing of prophets, the worship of the calf, the corruption of the Scripture; and the second, added to it, for the denial of the final Messenger whom they recognized and rejected (2:89). The wrath is cumulative: each refusal adds to the account. The end of the verse — "a humiliating punishment" — is the result: not merely punishment, but punishment with disgrace, the exposure of the position they tried to defend by bargaining.

In modern life, the verse exposes the envy that wears the mask of religious concern. It is possible to speak much about the truth while being animated by the resentment that the truth has been entrusted to someone else — a rival school, a different family, an unexpected messenger. The verse names the resentment as the motive of disbelief: the person who can accept the truth from anyone but the one Allah chose has not accepted the truth at all; he has accepted only the flattery of his own position. And the verse's warning is that the account is cumulative: wrath upon wrath.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 91

Verse 91 says: *"When it is said to them: "Believe in what Allah has revealed," they reply, "We only believe in what was sent down to us," and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures! Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, "Why then did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are ˹truly˺ believers?""*

In simple terms, the verse now records the standard reply of the people of the Book to the call of Islam: "We only believe in what was sent down to us." The Qur'an's answer has two parts. First, the denial of the new revelation is the denial of the old: the Qur'an is "the truth confirming their own Scriptures" — the one who truly believes in the Torah must believe in what confirms the Torah. Second, the claim of belief in their Scripture is tested against their history: why did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are believers?

The claim "We only believe in what was sent down to us" appears to be piety — fidelity to the original. The Qur'an replies that it is something else: selectivity dressed as loyalty. The new Book confirms the old; therefore the person who rejects the new has, in fact, rejected the old — because he has rejected what it pointed to and confirmed. One cannot claim to believe in a message while rejecting the messenger whom that message promised. The Qur'an's phrase is exact: the denial of the *afterwards* is not a separate denial; it is the denial of the confirmation.

The second part of the answer is a historical question with an unanswerable implication: "Why then did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are believers?" The question exposes the pattern, not merely the episodes. The people who claimed to believe in the revelation given to them had a history — recorded in their own scripture and known to all — of rejecting the prophets who came with demands they did not like. The Qur'an records the same pattern in the previous verse: "rejecting some and killing others." The question is devastating in its logic: your ancestors did to the prophets exactly what you are doing to this one; if that was faith, then faith is what you are doing now; and if it was not faith, then your claim of belief in "what was sent down to us" has always been false.

The verse also engages a subtlety: the people of the Book did not deny the principle of prophecy; they denied the particular prophets. They believed in a message in the past tense — a message that had already been completed, a line of prophets that had already ended with the prophets they approved. The Qur'an's reply is that the message is not a museum; it is a living sequence, and the person who believes in the sequence must accept the next messenger to arrive in it. The claim to believe in the prophets of the past while rejecting the prophet of the present is not faith in prophecy; it is the selection of a past that demands nothing.

In modern life, the verse is the mirror of every religion of the past tense. The person who honors the founders while ignoring the successors, who believes the first generation while rejecting the last, who is loyal to the Scripture he has not read while dismissing the message that confirms it — is in the position of the people of the verse. And the question of the verse is addressed to him as it was addressed to them: if your faith is true, what does it produce in the present?

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 92

Verse 92 says: *"Indeed, Moses came to you with clear proofs, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully."*

In simple terms, the verse continues the argument against the claim of belief: Moses himself came with clear proofs, and the very people who claim to believe in the Torah worshipped the calf in his absence. The verse is a single historical fact presented as the answer: the people who say "we believe in what was sent down to us" are the people of the calf.

The force of the verse is in the phrase "in his absence." Moses had gone to the appointed meeting with Allah; the people had seen the sea parted, the manna descend, and the cloud of guidance; and within the period of his absence they made a calf. The absence of the prophet was the test, and they failed it. The verse's word order is itself the argument: "Moses came to you with clear proofs" — and then, "you worshipped the calf." The proofs and the worship are placed in the same sentence so that the contrast cannot be missed: the evidence was clear, and the response was idolatry.

The "clear proofs" — *bayyināt* — are the signs that accompanied Moses: the staff, the hand, the parting of the sea, the manna and quails. The people had not merely heard of them; they had witnessed them. And yet — the verse's *thumma* — "then" — records what followed: the worship of the calf. The Qur'an names the state precisely: "acting wrongfully" — *wa antum ẓālimūn* — "while you were wrongdoers." The worship was not an error of interpretation or a misunderstanding of Moses' teaching; it was a wrongdoing, done knowingly.

The classical commentators connect this to the same theme as the previous verses: the calf was the first great apostasy of the Children of Israel, and the Qur'an returns to it repeatedly as the proof that the claim of fidelity to Moses was false from the beginning. The people who now tell the Prophet "we only believe in what was sent down to us" are the descendants of the people who, in the presence of Moses' own proofs, turned to an idol. The faith they claim as inheritance was already broken by their ancestors — and the verse's point is that the break has continued to the present.

In modern life, the verse is about the distance between proofs and obedience. It is possible to have witnessed the clearest signs of truth and still to worship what one has made with one's own hands — the calf being the image of every idol we construct: the success, the pleasure, the self, the position, the desire that we place where the revealed command should be. The verse's test is the absence: the moment when the prophet is not present, when the teacher is away, when no one is watching — what is worshipped then? The people of the verse had the clearest proofs and the longest record, and in the absence of the witness they made a calf. The believer is asked the same question about his own absences.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 93

Verse 93 says: *"And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, "Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey," they answered, "We hear and disobey." The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!""*

In simple terms, the verse records the covenant scene a second time — the mountain raised above them, the command to hold firmly and obey — but now with the answer: "We hear and disobey." The Qur'an then names the condition of the heart: the love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. And the verse closes with the devastating question: "How evil is what your belief prompts you to do, if you actually believe!"

The answer "We hear and disobey" is the Qur'an's most condensed image of spiritual resistance. It is the exact opposite of the response that the same covenant commanded, and it is the exact opposite of the response that the believers gave to their covenant: the Qur'an records the believers' answer as "We hear and obey" (2:285). The two answers are the two ways of receiving revelation: the submission that accepts the command, and the resistance that acknowledges it in words and rejects it in action. The people of the verse heard the words "hold firmly" and "obey" and answered with the words of refusal. The refusal is not ignorance: it is the failure of the will that has heard everything.

"The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief" — the translation renders the Arabic *ushribū fī qulūbihim al-'ijl* — literally "they were made to drink the calf into their hearts." The classical commentators explain the image: Qatadah (via Ma'mar from 'Abdur-Razzaq) said their hearts absorbed the love of the calf until the love resided within them; Abu al-'Aliyah and al-Rabi' ibn Anas said the same. The image of drinking is the image of absorption — the love was not a surface preference but something ingested, internalized, settled into the deepest part of the person. The reason is then stated: "because of their disbelief" — the condition of the heart and the love of the calf were not two separate things; the disbelief was the ground, and the love was what grew from it.

The closing question — "How evil is what your belief prompts you to do, if you actually believe!" — is ironic in the sharpest way. The claim of the people was that they believed in the Torah; the verse says: if you believe, then look at what your belief produced — the broken covenant, the worship of the calf, the answer of disobedience. A belief that produces this is not belief; it is the name the person gives to his refusal. The same irony runs through the whole section: the people who claim faith in the previous prophets are the people whose historical record is the record of the opposite of faith.

In modern life, the verse names the condition of the divided heart: the person who has heard the command clearly and answers with the words of a person who has also heard it — but whose heart has already been absorbed by something else. The "calf" of every age is whatever has been drunk into the heart: the love of money, of status, of a person, of an ideology, of the self. The verse's diagnosis is precise: the refusal is not the cause of the love; the love is the cause of the refusal. And the verse's encouragement is hidden in the same structure: the heart that has been absorbed by the calf can be freed only by the same process in reverse — the love of what is true, drank into the heart, until it resides there.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 94

Verse 94 says: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death if what you say is true!""*

In simple terms, the verse now moves from history to a direct challenge. If the Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively yours — as the people of the Book claimed — then wish for death, if you are truthful. The challenge is one of the Qur'an's most effective arguments: it takes the claim of privilege and tests it against desire.

The argument works on the logic of the claim itself. If the Hereafter is exclusively for a people, and if that Home is the eternal bliss of Allah, then death is the door to it — and the people who truly believe in their exclusive claim should be the least afraid of death, indeed the most eager for it. The fact that they will not wish for it (as the next verse says) is the proof that the claim is false: the people who believe the Home is theirs would leave this world for it without regret. The Qur'an's test is not a riddle; it is a psychological experiment, and it was performed in front of everyone.

The classical commentators explain that the "wish for death" here means invoking Allah against the lying party — the practice known as *mubahalah* — the mutual invocation of Allah's curse upon the one who is lying. Ibn Kathir explains that the "wish" is so called because every just person who is certain of his evidence wishes that the unjust opponent who denies him be destroyed; the Prophet ﷺ made this offer to the Jews, and they declined it. The same challenge appears in Surah Al-Jumu'ah (62:6-8), where the claim is friendship with Allah, and the same logic is used: wish for death if you are truthful — and they never will.

The verse's deeper point is about the relationship between belief and the world. A person who truly believes in the Hereafter holds this world with an open hand, because the better thing is ahead. A person who claims belief in the Hereafter but clings to this world with both hands has the psychology of a person who does not believe. As the commentators note, this is why the challenge was a test of the claim: the person who is certain that the best is coming does not beg to stay. The one who begs to stay reveals that he does not think the best is coming.

In modern life, the verse is a diagnostic instrument that has not grown old. Anyone — individual, community, religion — who claims that the eternal Home is privileged for himself can be asked the question: then why are you so attached to this transient one? The claim of exclusive salvation has always coexisted with the most intense attachment to the world, and the coexistence is the contradiction. The verse is not asking a person to wish for death; it is asking him to examine what his life reveals about what he actually believes. And for the believer, the challenge is inverted into an instruction: let your life show that the Hereafter is what you are living for.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 95

Verse 95 says: *"But they will never wish for that because of what their hands have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers."*

In simple terms, the verse records the outcome of the challenge: they will never wish for death, and the reason is stated — because of what their hands have done. The verse then closes with the knowledge of Allah: He knows the wrongdoers.

"Because of what their hands have done" is the verdict. The people could not wish for death because their own deeds had already placed them in a position where death was not a door to bliss but a door to the accounting. They knew the content of their history — the broken covenants, the killing of prophets, the calf, the rejection of the messenger they recognized — and they knew what that history meant for the Home they claimed. Their refusal to wish for death was not a failure of the challenge; it was the confession the challenge was designed to extract. The man who will not demand the door knows that the house behind it is not his.

The verse's logic is worth stating precisely: the claim was "the Home is exclusively ours." The test was "wish for death." The outcome — they will never wish for it — is the falsification of the claim. A person whose hands are clean of the wrongs would have passed the test without fear; the person whose hands have sent ahead what they know to be wrong cannot even attempt it. The Qur'an does not need to describe their secrets; their refusal describes them.

"And Allah has perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers" — the clause is not a threat in the ordinary sense; it is the ground of the whole argument. The verse does not say "Allah knows" as a vague acknowledgment; it says that the One who knows everything has observed the deeds — the expulsions, the shedding of blood, the selective belief, the rejection of the messenger, the insistence on the claim. The knowledge is complete, and the accounting will be based on it: not on the claim, not on the lineage, not on the wish, but on the knowledge.

In modern life, the verse gives a quiet criterion of self-knowledge: the things a person avoids reveal what he knows. The person who cannot bring himself to face a question — the question of death, of the accounting, of the wrong he has done — has already answered it. The Jews of the verse would not say "death, if we are truthful" because their hands had answered before their tongues. The verse's invitation is to the opposite: to the life in which a person can face the door without fear — not because he is sinless, but because his hands have been turned toward the One who knows, and the wrong has been exchanged for the right.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 96

Verse 96 says: *"You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do."*

In simple terms, the verse now explains why the challenge of the previous verses was refused: the people who claimed the exclusive Home of the Hereafter are the people most clinging to this life — more than any other people, even more than the polytheists. Each of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even that would not save them from the punishment, and Allah sees what they do.

The comparison with the polytheists is the key. The polytheists hold to life for the obvious reason: they do not believe in a resurrection, so this life is all they have. The People of the Book, however, claim to believe in the Hereafter — and still cling to life more greedily than those who have no Hereafter at all. The verse exposes the contradiction: the people who believe in the better Home act as though this world were the only one. The classical commentators explain the reason: the Jews know what awaits them — the humiliation and the Fire they have earned — and so they want to delay the meeting as long as possible. The polytheist clings to life because he has nothing better; the one who was given the Book clings to life because he knows what is worse.

"Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years" — the Qur'an does not say all of them wish for exactly a thousand years; it says each one wishes for a very long life, and the thousand is the figure of the wish. Ibn Kathir notes that this longing is a matter of degree: they are the most eager for life, and even the polytheists who merely enjoy the world do not match them. The scholars also point to the irony: a thousand years is not even a day in the eternal account, and the wish itself — the enormous desire for length of life — is the sign of a soul that has nothing to look forward to in the direction it is going. Ibn Kathir compares it with Iblis, who was given a long life and was not helped by it.

"But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment." The verse closes the argument: the postponement is not escape. The person who clings to life to delay the reckoning has not avoided the reckoning; he has only delayed the unavoidable and lost the time of life. The thousand-year wish is the image of the religion of delay — the faith that always intends to repent, the obedience that always begins tomorrow. The verse's answer is that the delay does not move the accounting; it only moves the person closer to it while he is still unprepared.

In modern life, the verse is the diagnosis of the culture of postponement. The desire for longer life, the obsession with health and youth, the fear of aging, the dread of death — the verse does not condemn the love of life, which is natural; it condemns the love of life that has no confidence beyond it. The person who lives as though this world were everything is living by the standard of the polytheists; the person who lives with the Hereafter in view is the one genuinely free of the fear of death.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 97

Verse 97 says: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah's Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.""*

In simple terms, the verse now takes up the objection of the people of the Book to the very messenger of revelation. They claimed that Gabriel was their enemy. The Qur'an's answer is a single logical chain: the person who is an enemy of Gabriel is an enemy of the One who sent Gabriel, and of the message Gabriel carried. Gabriel revealed the Qur'an to the Prophet's heart by Allah's Will, confirming the previous Scriptures, and it is a guide and good news for the believers.

The occasion is well documented. Imam al-Tabari states plainly that the scholars of tafsir agree the verse was revealed in response to the Jews who claimed that Gabriel was their enemy and that Michael was their friend. The reports preserved in the tafsir describe the exchange: some Jewish scholars asked the Prophet which angel brought him the revelation; he answered Gabriel; they replied that Gabriel is the angel of war, hardship, and punishment, while Michael is the angel of rain, mercy, and provision; and they said that had it been Michael, they would have accepted the message. Ibn Abbas is reported to have named a Jewish scholar, 'Abdullah ibn Suriyyah, as the one who made the claim. The Qur'an's response defeats the objection at its root: the angel does not act on his own; he brings only what Allah commands, and the message he brought is the confirmation of what the objectors themselves hold.

The phrase "to your heart" is significant. The revelation descended not merely to the Prophet's tongue but to his heart: the deep, inner place where certainty is formed and where the soul is changed. The verse describes the Qur'an's descent as the act of the One who wills it — "by Allah's Will" — and the message as triple: it confirms what came before it, it guides, and it brings good news. The objection to Gabriel is therefore an objection to every part: the confirmation, the guidance, and the glad tidings. There is no way to be its enemy while claiming loyalty to what it confirms.

The deeper lesson of the verse is about messengers and messages. The Jews claimed to judge the message by its messenger: Gabriel is our enemy, therefore we reject what he brings. The Qur'an reverses the method: judge the messenger by the message. The message Gabriel brought confirms the Torah, guides to what is true, and brings glad tidings to the believers. If the message is true, the messenger who carried it must be honored. The prejudice against the carrier cannot stand as an argument against the content — and the content, confirmed by their own Scripture, gave them no room.

In modern life, the verse remains a test of intellectual honesty: the person who rejects a truth because he dislikes its carrier, its community, or its form is doing what the Jews did with Gabriel. The message is judged by itself; the messenger is judged by what he carries. And the reverse lesson is also present: the person who claims to love the message while abusing its messenger has not understood what he claims to love — because the message and the honest reception of it are inseparable.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 98

Verse 98 says: *"Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, and Michael, then ˹let them know that˺ Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers."*

In simple terms, the verse completes the argument by expanding it. The person who is an enemy of Gabriel is not merely an enemy of one angel; he is an enemy of Allah, of the angels, of the messengers, and of the two named angels — Gabriel and Michael — together. And the verse returns the verdict: Allah is the enemy of the disbelievers.

The verse's expansion is deliberate. The Jews had made a division: Gabriel was their enemy, Michael was their friend. The Qur'an refuses the division: the two angels are named together, and the person who takes one as an enemy and the other as a friend has taken a position against both — because they are both servants of the same Lord, both under the same command. The Ibn Kathir commentary notes that Gabriel and Michael are mentioned specifically, although they are included among the angels, because this verse was meant to support Gabriel, the emissary between Allah and His prophets, and to answer the claim that Michael was the Jews' friend. The division is the error: one can no more be a friend of Michael and an enemy of Gabriel than one can be a friend of one prophet and an enemy of another, or believe in part of the revelation and reject the rest — the very error the surah has been exposing since verse 85.

The names are also significant. Gabriel and Michael are the two angels most closely associated with revelation and provision: Gabriel with the message, Michael — as the reports of the Jews described him — with rain and sustenance. The Qur'an's answer is that both are angels of Allah: the one who brings the Book and the one who brings the mercy are equally under the command of the Lord, and enmity toward one is enmity toward the order of the whole. The person who says "I love the mercy but hate the command" has not understood that the command is the mercy.

The final clause — "Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers" — is not a display of divine anger in the human sense. Allah is not harmed by enmity; the meaning, as the commentators explain, is that Allah will punish and be hostile to the disbelievers on the Day of Judgment — the enmity is the verdict of the Hereafter, not the vulnerability of the Divine. The verse's structure is exact: it moves from the small claim — Gabriel is our enemy — to the great verdict — Allah is the enemy of the disbelievers. The person who began by insulting a servant ends by standing against the Lord.

In modern life, the verse is a warning against partial religion. It is possible to love some aspects of revelation and hate others, to be devoted to one part of the faith while hostile to another, to split the message according to temperament. The Qur'an says the parts are one order, from one Lord. And the verse teaches the moral of the whole section: a religion of selection is not religion; it is the self placed in judgment over the revelation — and the final enmity the self will face is the enmity of the One it tried to select against.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 99

Verse 99 says: *"Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious."*

In simple terms, the verse now states the standing of the revelations given to the Prophet: they are clear. And it states the standing of those who deny them: none denies them except the rebellious. The verse is a compact answer to the whole section — the people who kept saying "we cannot recognize," "the message is unclear," "we do not understand" — the revelations are clear, and the denial is a matter of rebellion, not confusion.

"Clear revelations" — *āyāt bayyināt* — the same word that the surah used for the proofs of Moses (2:92) and for the signs by which Allah makes His guidance legible. The Qur'an's claim is not that its verses are simple in every respect, but that they are clear in what matters: the call to worship, the description of the Judgment, the criteria of salvation, the confirmation of the prophets, the correction of the people of the Book. The objection of the previous verses — "our hearts are unreceptive" — was answered here: the obscurity is not in the revelation but in the heart that refuses to open.

"None will deny them except the rebellious" — the word is *fāsiqūn*, from *fisq*, the act of leaving the path. The denial of clear revelation is not a position of uncertainty; it is an act of departure. The Qur'an repeatedly distinguishes between the person who seeks and has not found, and the person who has found and turns away. The one who rejects the clear signs after they have been made clear is not a seeker; he is a rebel — he has left the path, not missed it. This is the verdict that the whole section has been building: the people of the Book did not fail to recognize the Prophet; they recognized him and rejected him (2:89), and the verse now names the category of that act.

The classical commentators read the verse as a general law: the clear signs are never denied by the honest but only by the rebellious; the objections are not intellectual. And the same law applies in both directions: the person who finds himself tempted to deny what is clear should examine not the evidence but his own relation to it. The obscurity in such cases is produced by the desire that has taken up residence, not by the evidence.

In modern life, the verse addresses the most common form of spiritual confusion: the claim of ambiguity. The person who has not understood may be honest; the person who has understood and refuses is in another category. The verse's challenge to the modern reader is to distinguish the two honestly: where the revelation is clear and my life is not, the problem is mine. And where the revelation is clear and I deny it, the verse names me by what I have done: I have not reasoned; I have rebelled — and the cure of rebellion is not more information, but the turn of the heart.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 100

Verse 100 says: *"Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? In fact, most of them do not believe."*

In simple terms, the verse brings the section to its conclusion with a question: why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? The question is the summary of the whole history that the surah has been recounting — the covenant at Sinai, the covenant of the mountain, the covenant against bloodshed and expulsion, the covenant confirmed by the prophets — all of them broken, and each time by a group of them. The verse's final clause gives the answer: most of them do not believe.

The pattern of the verse is the pattern of the section: covenant, breaking, covenant, breaking. The Qur'an does not say they rejected the covenants; it says they *cast them aside* — the word *nabadhū* — "they threw them away" — behind their backs. The image is of a person who receives a document, holds it, and then tosses it away: the act is not ignorance but contempt. The image returns in 2:101, where a group of those given the Scripture cast the Book of Allah behind their backs, as if they did not know it. The covenant was not lost; it was discarded.

"In fact, most of them do not believe." The verse closes the section with the verdict on the majority. The Qur'an had earlier said "except for a few of you" (2:83), and "they have but little faith" (2:88); now it states the proportion plainly: most of them do not believe. The verdict concerns those who made the claim and broke the covenant — it is not a verdict on every individual among them, since the small group of believers is recognized throughout. But the majority, the verse says, is not in faith. The reason is the pattern: the person whose covenant is repeatedly cast aside is not in faith; faith is precisely the keeping of the covenant.

The modern application is at once personal and collective. For communities, the verse is a warning that the measure of belief is the keeping of the promise: the organizations, movements, and religions that repeatedly break their own covenants — with Allah, with each other, with their own principles — are described by the verse. For the individual, the verse is the question of consistency: how many times has the same covenant been made and cast aside? The promise to Allah, the commitment to the community, the vow of the marriage, the oath of the profession — every promise kept is an act of belief, and every promise cast aside is an act of the same kind, repeated. The verse's conclusion is that the two are inseparable: the person who cannot be trusted to keep his word with those who can see him is not in a position to claim the covenant with the One who sees everything.
