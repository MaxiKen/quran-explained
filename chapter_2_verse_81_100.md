## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:81

> But no! Those who commit evil and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Commentary:**

### The Answer to False Religious Security

The previous āyah confronted the claim that the Fire would touch some people only for a few days. This verse begins with *balā*—“but no,” or “rather”—and rejects the idea that inherited identity or religious wishful thinking can guarantee safety. The decisive issue is not what a person calls himself but what he has chosen to believe and become. Those who commit evil and are surrounded by their sin until it encompasses them are described as inhabitants of the Fire.

The Arabic wording is stronger than the occurrence of an isolated mistake. *Man kasaba sayyiʾah* can mean one who acquires evil, while *aḥāṭat bihi khaṭīʾatuhu* pictures wrongdoing enclosing a person on every side. The image is of a life in which evil is no longer an occasional lapse resisted by repentance; it has become the person’s settled direction. He protects it, repeats it, defends it, and refuses the truth that could free him from it.

Classical exegetes differed over whether “evil” here specifically refers to disbelief and association with Allah or includes every sin when it dominates a person completely. The verse must be read with the Qur’an’s other teachings. A believer who commits a major sin but dies upon faith is not automatically placed in the same category as one who rejects Allah and dies in rejection. Allah may forgive or punish the sinful believer according to His justice and mercy (4:48; 4:116). The lasting punishment described here concerns the person whose wrongdoing has encompassed him in the sense of persistent rejection and unrepentant rebellion.

### Sin Can Become an Enclosure

Sin often begins as a choice and becomes a pattern. The first lie may be defended by a second; the first unlawful gain may require concealment; the first act of injustice may create fear of accountability. With repetition, the person’s desires, friendships, habits, and public identity begin to protect the sin from correction. The sin then “encompasses” him—not because repentance is beyond Allah’s power, but because he no longer wants to leave the condition.

The Qur’an describes hearts being rusted by what people earn (83:14) and deviation increasing after people choose to deviate (61:5). These passages teach that moral states have consequences. A person who repeatedly refuses light becomes less able to benefit from it. Yet the door of repentance remains open to the living. “Except those who repent, believe, and do righteous deeds” are promised that Allah will replace their evil deeds with good (25:70). The finality of verse 81 applies to those who die in the encompassed state, not to every living sinner who has not yet returned.

### The Fire Is a Moral Consequence

The verse’s description of the Fire is not a licence for humans to declare the final fate of individuals. Allah knows what message reached each person, what he understood, what pressures surrounded him, and whether he repented at the end. Human beings judge outward conduct according to evidence; Allah alone judges the complete inward reality and final state.

The warning is nevertheless real. Faith is not a shield that allows a person to embrace evil while relying upon ancestry or religious language. The Qur’an says that it is neither the wishes of Muslims nor the wishes of the People of the Book that determine salvation; whoever does evil will be recompensed for it (4:123). This does not deny Allah’s mercy. It denies the presumption that mercy can be claimed while repentance and responsibility are rejected.

### Turning Back Before Sin Encloses the Heart

The practical lesson is urgency. A believer should not wait until a sin has become an identity before seeking help. He should interrupt the pattern early through confession to Allah, removal of the means of temptation, restitution of rights, righteous companionship, and consistent good deeds. Allah loves those who repeatedly repent (2:222), and every sincere return weakens the enclosure of sin.

The verse also warns against using theology to avoid moral reform. A person who debates the length of punishment while continuing injustice has missed the purpose of the warning. Revelation calls him to ask whether evil is surrounding his heart and whether he is still willing to let Allah’s command correct him. Safety lies in sincere faith, repentance, and obedience—not in a religious slogan.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:48, 4:116, and 4:123 on sin, disbelief, judgment, and the rejection of salvation through wishful claims.
- Qur’an 25:70 and 39:53 on repentance and Allah’s mercy.
- Qur’an 61:5 and 83:14 on deviation and the stain produced by repeated sin.
- Qur’an 2:80, 2:111–112, and 3:24 on false claims of guaranteed salvation.
- Qur’an 18:103–106 on deeds that appear good but are lost through rejection and denial.
- For *aḥāṭat bihi khaṭīʾatuhu* and the interpretations of the “evil” that encompasses a person, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:81.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:82

> And those who believe and do good will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever.

**Commentary:**

### Faith Proven by Righteous Action

After describing those whose evil has encompassed them, the Qur’an presents the other side of the contrast: those who believe and do righteous deeds are the people of Paradise. The pairing of faith and action appears throughout the Qur’an because genuine belief is not a private claim disconnected from conduct. Faith shapes worship, speech, relationships, financial dealings, and the response to hardship.

“Believe” includes faith in Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, the Last Day, and divine decree, as explained in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8). “Do good” includes the obligations Allah has revealed and every sincere act that benefits people and seeks His pleasure. A person is not required to be flawless, but he must not make sin his settled allegiance. When he falls, he returns through repentance and continues striving.

### Deeds and Allah’s Mercy

The verse should not be read as though human beings purchase Paradise from Allah through deeds independently of His grace. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that no one enters Paradise solely through his deeds, not even he, unless Allah envelops him in mercy (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Deeds are nevertheless necessary signs of faith and means through which Allah’s mercy is sought. The same Prophet said, “The most beloved deeds to Allah are those that are most consistent, even if they are few” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

This balance protects the believer from two errors. He does not become proud of worship as though he had compelled Allah to reward him, and he does not become passive by claiming that deeds do not matter. He works, prays, gives, repents, and hopes in Allah’s generosity. His good deeds are themselves enabled by Allah, and their acceptance is another gift.

### The Residents of Paradise

The expression “residents of Paradise” suggests a lasting home rather than a temporary visit. The people of Paradise will not be threatened by death, expulsion, illness, fear, or the return of grief. The Qur’an describes them as receiving peace and never hearing vain or sinful speech (56:25–26), and says that Allah will remove rancour from their hearts (15:47). Paradise is not merely a place of physical pleasure; it is a complete restoration of safety, dignity, and nearness to Allah.

The Qur’an uses many descriptions—gardens, rivers, pure companions, shade, fruits, and divine pleasure—to communicate its reality to people in worldly language. The Prophet (ﷺ) reported that Allah has prepared for His righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has imagined (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The descriptions are true, but the reality exceeds the limits of present experience.

### Permanence Contrasted with the World

“They will be there forever”—*khālidūn*. This permanence is the decisive contrast with worldly life. Every earthly home can be lost, every body ages, and every pleasure ends. In Paradise, the believer does not fear that joy will be taken away. The final home is secure because Allah’s acceptance is complete and the period of testing has ended.

The promise gives patience a destination. A person may give up unlawful wealth, endure loneliness for the sake of chastity, continue prayer through illness, or forgive an injury when forgiveness is wise. These sacrifices are not lost. The Qur’an says that Allah does not allow the reward of good-doers to be wasted (18:30), and that He multiplies good deeds far beyond their apparent size (6:160; 2:261).

### An Invitation to Hopeful Effort

The verse follows the warning of verse 81 so that the believer remains balanced. Fear prevents complacency, while hope prevents despair. The reader is not asked to decide that his final destination is already guaranteed; he is asked to walk the path of faith and good action while relying upon Allah. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that a person should work while hoping and fearing, because no one knows what his final deeds will be (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the chapters on destiny and deeds).

The promise is also a rejection of inherited privilege. Paradise belongs neither to a race nor to a label automatically. It belongs to those whom Allah accepts through sincere belief, righteous action, and mercy. The next passages will return to the covenant and conduct of the Children of Israel, showing why religious ancestry cannot replace obedience.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 18:30, 23:1–11, and 103:1–3 on faith, righteous deeds, and success.
- Qur’an 3:133–136 and 9:72 on Paradise, forgiveness, and Allah’s pleasure.
- Qur’an 6:160 and 2:261 on Allah’s multiplication of good deeds.
- Qur’an 15:47 and 56:25–26 on the purity and peace of Paradise.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8, on the foundations of faith; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on Paradise being entered through Allah’s mercy and on consistent deeds.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:83

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.” But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent.

**Commentary:**

### The Covenant Begins with Tawḥīd

This verse summarizes the covenant taken from the Children of Israel. Its first command is “worship none but Allah.” The foundation of every prophetic message is *tawḥīd*: directing ultimate worship, love, fear, hope, reliance, and obedience to Allah alone. The covenant does not begin with ethnicity, political identity, or ritual detail. It begins with the recognition that the Creator has no partner and that no created being shares His divine rights.

The Qur’an repeatedly presents this principle as the core of revelation. Allah commanded Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad (peace be upon them) to establish the same religion of worshipping Him alone (42:13). The commandments that follow—family care, truthful speech, prayer, and alms—are not detached social ethics. They are the practical consequences of worship. A person who claims to worship Allah but abuses parents, relatives, or the vulnerable has violated the covenant’s meaning.

### Kindness to Parents and the Vulnerable

The covenant next commands kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, and the needy. The ordering reflects the human network of responsibility. Parents are owed gratitude and care, relatives should not be abandoned for convenience, orphans require protection because they have lost the normal safeguard of parents, and the needy must not be ignored because poverty makes their rights less visible.

The Qur’an places kindness to parents immediately after worship in several passages (17:23; 4:36). This does not make parents divine or require obedience to them in sin. It means that gratitude for the means through which Allah brought a person into the world should be expressed through respect, patience, support, and dignified care. Relatives likewise have rights, but maintaining kinship does not require enabling abuse; justice and safety remain part of the command.

Orphans and the needy are a test of a community’s moral seriousness. They may lack social power to demand what is owed to them, so revelation protects them directly. The Qur’an warns against consuming the property of orphans unjustly (4:10), commands care for the poor, and condemns the person who repels the orphan or neglects feeding the needy (107:1–3). Charity is not merely generosity from a superior person; it is recognition of a right and a trust.

### Speak Kindly to People

The covenant includes the broad command to speak kindly to people—*qūlū li-l-nāsi ḥusnan*. Speech is a moral action. It can reconcile, teach, comfort, and defend; it can also humiliate, inflame, deceive, and destroy. The command is not restricted to speech addressed to Israelites or members of one’s own faith. It concerns people generally.

Kind speech does not mean avoiding truth or flattering wrongdoing. The Qur’an commands upright speech (33:70), justice even toward enemies (5:8), and wise correction. A truthful warning can be kind when it seeks reform and preserves dignity. A soft voice can be cruel when it conceals manipulation. The standard is not mere pleasantness but speech that is truthful, beneficial, and appropriately expressed.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should speak good or remain silent (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This principle applies to gossip, public accusation, online communication, family disputes, and religious debate. The covenant is broken when people use words to turn neighbours into enemies or make the vulnerable ashamed of their need.

### Prayer and Zakāh Complete the Social Covenant

The commands to establish prayer and pay alms-tax return to the pattern of verses 3 and 43. Prayer keeps the community connected to Allah; zakāh ensures that worship affects the circulation of wealth. The two together oppose both spiritual neglect and social indifference. Prayer without care for people can become a performance, while social service without worship can lose its ultimate purpose and accountability.

The verse’s conclusion records that most of them turned away, except for a few. This is a historical description of widespread failure, not a statement that every Israelite in every generation was corrupt. The Qur’an explicitly acknowledges upright People of the Book (3:113–115). The same qualification is necessary whenever the Qur’an criticizes a community: justice distinguishes the guilty from the innocent.

### The Covenant as a Mirror

The covenant is also addressed indirectly to Muslims. The Qur’an preserves it because the same duties govern every community that claims to follow revelation. Tawḥīd must produce family responsibility, care for the poor, good speech, prayer, and zakāh. A religious community that emphasizes ritual while neglecting vulnerable people has not fulfilled the covenant, even if it possesses a respected scripture.

The verse therefore gives a compact definition of righteousness: correct worship, humane relationships, truthful communication, disciplined prayer, and economic responsibility. The following verses will show how quickly the Children of Israel contradicted this covenant by shedding blood and expelling one another from their homes.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:40, 2:63, and 5:12 on the covenant with the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 4:36 and 17:23–26 on worshipping Allah and kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, and the needy.
- Qur’an 107:1–7 on neglecting orphans and the needy while claiming religion.
- Qur’an 33:70, 49:11–12, and 17:53 on kind, upright, and responsible speech.
- Qur’an 9:71 and 98:5 on prayer, zakāh, and communal responsibility.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on speaking good or remaining silent and on the rights of neighbours and family.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:84

> And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other’s blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness.

**Commentary:**

### The Sanctity of Life and Home

The covenant with the Children of Israel included two clear prohibitions: shedding one another’s blood and expelling one another from their homes. Human life and secure residence are not optional social benefits. They are protected rights rooted in Allah’s command. The verse speaks of a community that had pledged not to turn internal disagreement into murder, forced displacement, or the destruction of family security.

The pairing of bloodshed and expulsion is significant. Violence does not occur only when a person is killed. Driving families from homes, confiscating property, destroying livelihoods, and making a population permanently unsafe are also forms of grave harm. A home is a place of safety, memory, worship, and family life. To expel people unjustly attacks their dignity even if the aggressor does not immediately take their lives.

The covenant is addressed to a people who had received revelation. Scripture had not left them without a standard for political or communal conduct. They had pledged before Allah and bore witness to the obligation. The coming verse will show the contradiction between their formal promise and their behaviour in conflict.

### A Covenant That Applies Inside the Community

The command concerns “each other’s blood” and “each other’s homes.” It therefore applies within the community, not only to relations with outsiders. Religious identity does not permit believers to abuse members of their own group. A community can be united in public against an external enemy while privately violating the rights of its own families, minorities, neighbours, or political opponents. Allah judges the internal injustice as seriously as the external one.

The Qur’an commands believers to stand firmly for justice even when testimony is against themselves, parents, or relatives (4:135). It also warns that hatred of a people must not lead to injustice (5:8). These principles prevent the language of tribe, nation, sect, or party from becoming an excuse for murder and expulsion. The more closely related people are, the greater the betrayal when they violate one another’s rights.

### “You Gave Your Pledge and Bore Witness”

The phrase emphasizes conscious commitment. They did not merely hear a rule; they gave their word and witnessed it. A covenant creates a moral record before Allah. People may later reinterpret their promises, blame circumstances, or claim that the pledge was symbolic, but the original responsibility remains unless Allah Himself provides a lawful change.

The Qur’an commands believers to fulfil covenants and warns that promises will be questioned (17:34). A person’s word is therefore not disposable. Contracts, oaths, ceasefires, family agreements, and public commitments are all forms of trust. Breaking them for advantage damages more than the immediate victim; it makes future cooperation difficult because people no longer believe one another.

This does not mean that a promise to commit sin must be fulfilled. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that there is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator (Musnad Aḥmad and related reports), and the Qur’an does not permit a person to honour an unjust agreement that requires wrongdoing. But a lawful covenant should be honoured, and any change should be made transparently and justly rather than through betrayal.

### The Connection with the Next Verse

Verse 84 records the principle; verse 85 records their violation. This arrangement exposes the gap between moral declaration and actual policy. Communities often adopt noble constitutions, religious pledges, and public statements while allowing power, fear, or economic interest to override them in practice. The covenant is tested not when it is recited but when obedience becomes costly.

The verse therefore asks every reader to examine the agreements he has made. Does he protect the life and home of those under his authority? Does he honour the rights of family members and neighbours during conflict? Does he support the expulsion or humiliation of people when it benefits his group? A person who loudly affirms a covenant but excuses its violation has already begun to break it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:83 and 5:12 on the covenant’s wider commands.
- Qur’an 4:29, 4:93, and 5:32 on the sanctity of life.
- Qur’an 4:135 and 5:8 on justice without favouritism or group bias.
- Qur’an 17:34 and 16:91 on fulfilling pledges and covenants.
- Qur’an 59:9 and 4:66 on the dignity of homes, migration, and preferring others over oneself.
- For the historical and moral relation between the covenant in 2:84 and its violation in 2:85, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:85

> But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them—though expelling them was unlawful for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest? Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment? For Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Commentary:**

### Selective Obedience

The verse exposes the contradiction between the covenant and the community’s conduct. They had pledged not to kill one another or expel one another from their homes, yet they did both. They then aided one another in sin and aggression, and later ransomed their own captives. Their behaviour preserved one command while violating another. They valued the part of scripture that served their immediate interests and disregarded the part that condemned the political conflict producing the captives in the first place.

Classical reports connect the passage to conflicts among Jewish tribes in and around Madinah, in which some groups allied with different Arab factions. They fought one another, expelled members of their own religious community, and then paid to release captives in accordance with part of the Torah’s law. The historical setting helps explain the illustration, but the Qur’an’s principle is broader. Any community can obey scripture selectively—supporting charity while neglecting justice, defending ritual while violating life, or demanding rights for itself while denying them to others.

The act of ransoming captives was not wrong in itself. Freeing a captive can be a good and merciful deed. The contradiction lay in breaking the prohibition against expulsion and warfare, then observing the ransom command as though partial obedience could cancel the original violation. A good act does not automatically erase an unrelated injustice. Repentance requires ending the wrong and repairing its harm.

### “Do You Believe in Some of the Scripture?”

The Qur’anic question is searching: “Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?” Belief in a scripture means accepting its authority as a whole, not selecting verses according to convenience. A person cannot call a command divine when it benefits him and dismiss the same Book when it demands restraint, equality, or accountability.

The warning does not deny that scripture contains commands with different circumstances or that later revelation can supersede an earlier ruling. Allah may legislate differently for different communities and may abrogate a ruling within a revealed law according to His wisdom. The condemned behaviour is not following a valid later command; it is knowingly rejecting the authority of one’s own scripture while claiming to honour it.

Muslims must apply the warning to themselves. It is possible to love verses about mercy while ignoring justice, to recite verses about charity while exploiting the poor, or to invoke political passages while neglecting the commands against lying and oppression. The Qur’an is not a collection of quotations for winning arguments; it is a whole guidance that judges the reader.

### Aid in Sin and Aggression

The phrase “aiding one another in sin and aggression” distinguishes cooperation from solidarity. Mutual support is good when it serves justice, but people must not help one another commit wrongdoing. The Qur’an commands cooperation in righteousness and taqwā and forbids cooperation in sin and hostility (5:2). This applies to families, business partnerships, political movements, armed groups, and religious institutions.

A person may not excuse his participation by saying that he did not begin the wrongdoing. Supplying money, propaganda, weapons, legal cover, false testimony, or social approval can make him a participant. At the same time, moral responsibility is individual and proportionate. Allah knows who initiated the harm, who was coerced, who resisted, and who tried to repair it.

The warning is especially relevant to group conflict. Loyalty to one’s community is valuable, but loyalty cannot mean protecting murderers, expellers, or corrupt leaders from accountability. The Prophet (ﷺ) explained that helping an oppressor includes preventing him from oppressing (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). True solidarity restrains wrongdoing rather than giving it greater power.

### Disgrace in This World and Punishment in the Hereafter

The verse warns of disgrace in this world and the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment. Worldly disgrace can take the form of political weakness, social division, exposure, loss of trust, and the consequences of injustice. It does not mean that every hardship proves a person’s guilt; righteous people can suffer and oppressors can prosper temporarily. Here the Qur’an identifies a specific consequence of covenant-breaking and selective obedience.

Allah’s being “never unaware” closes the verse. Human beings may hide behind institutions, competing narratives, and partial acts of charity, but Allah sees the whole pattern. He knows the command they obeyed, the command they rejected, the harm they caused, and the motives behind their choices. The final judgment will not be distracted by a selective presentation of evidence.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:83–84 on the covenant to worship Allah, care for people, and avoid bloodshed and expulsion.
- Qur’an 5:2 on cooperating in righteousness rather than sin and aggression.
- Qur’an 4:135 and 5:8 on justice even against one’s own group or people one dislikes.
- Qur’an 17:34 and 16:91 on covenants and the accountability of promises.
- Qur’an 3:112 and 17:4–7 on disgrace and the consequences of transgression.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī on helping an oppressor by preventing him from committing oppression.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:86

> These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. So their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped.

**Commentary:**

### The Most Costly Exchange

The previous verse described people who selected whichever part of scripture protected their interests. This verse names the underlying transaction: they traded the Hereafter for the life of this world. The Qur’an uses commercial imagery because the exchange is deliberate. They exchanged lasting accountability and reward for temporary power, group loyalty, safety, or material advantage.

The world is not evil merely because it is temporary. Allah created lawful pleasures and commands people to seek the Hereafter without forgetting their legitimate share of worldly life (28:77). The problem is allowing the temporary world to become the final measure of truth. When a person violates revelation to preserve status, wealth, or political advantage, he has placed a limited and passing good above an eternal reality.

The exchange is irrational because the two sides are not equal. The world ends at death, while the Hereafter is lasting. A person who gives up truth for a position, bribe, or victory may gain something real for a short time, but he loses what cannot be recovered. The Qur’an asks, “What is the enjoyment of worldly life compared with the Hereafter except a little?” (9:38).

### Punishment Is Not Reduced

The verse says that their punishment will not be reduced and that they will not be helped. This is connected to persistent rejection and selective obedience, not to every believer who sins or every person who experiences moral weakness. Allah may forgive sins, accept repentance, and show mercy to those who return. The unrepentant people described here have made the exchange their settled course and meet Allah without abandoning it.

No worldly alliance can rescue them. The same group loyalty that encouraged them to violate the covenant cannot protect them before Allah. Their wealth cannot buy a ransom, their leaders cannot override judgment, and their religious label cannot replace faith and obedience. “On the Day when no soul will possess anything for another, and the command that Day will be entirely Allah’s” (82:19).

The statement about punishment also challenges false intercession and inherited privilege. Intercession exists only by Allah’s permission and for those with whom He is pleased. It is not a mechanism by which a person can knowingly persist in injustice while relying on another being to guarantee his safety.

### The World as a Test, Not an Enemy

The Qur’an does not ask believers to abandon the world or refuse lawful work, family, knowledge, beauty, or political responsibility. It asks them to put the world in its proper place. Wealth can be used for charity, authority for justice, and knowledge for service. The same things become dangerous when they are loved as ends independent of Allah.

A person trades the Hereafter for the world whenever he knows a truth but hides it to preserve a career, takes unlawful wealth because he fears poverty, supports injustice because he wants access, or neglects prayer because he treats work as more important than the Lord who gave him work. The transaction can occur in small decisions long before it appears in a public crisis.

### The Cure: Remember the Final Account

The cure is to remember the return. The Qur’an describes those who are wise as people who reflect upon creation, stand in prayer, and ask Allah to protect them from the Fire (3:190–191). Certainty in the Hereafter makes immediate advantage less intoxicating. It does not remove ambition; it purifies ambition by asking whether the means and end are pleasing to Allah.

The believer also remembers that worldly success can be a test rather than a reward. A person may prosper while moving toward loss, and a person may suffer while accumulating eternal good. The final account is the only complete measure. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that the wise person is the one who holds himself accountable and works for what comes after death (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2459).

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:177 and 14:3 on trading the Hereafter for worldly life.
- Qur’an 28:77 and 9:38 on using worldly blessings without allowing them to displace the Hereafter.
- Qur’an 82:19 and 2:48 on the limits of worldly help and intercession.
- Qur’an 3:190–191 on reflection, prayer, and fear of the Fire.
- Qur’an 103:1–3 on the universal danger of loss and the means of avoiding it.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2459, on self-accountability and preparing for the Hereafter.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:87

> Indeed, We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers. And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others?

**Commentary:**

### A Long Chain of Revelation

Allah reminds the Children of Israel that Moses was given the Book and that messengers continued to come after him. Revelation was not a single historical intervention followed by abandonment. Allah repeatedly renewed guidance when people forgot, disputed, or corrupted what they had received. The succession of prophets was a mercy, but it also created a repeated responsibility to listen and obey.

The Qur’an honours Moses and the prophets who followed him, while rejecting the idea that one community may accept some messengers and reject others. All genuine prophets brought the same foundation of worshipping Allah alone, even though their laws and circumstances differed. The Muslim confession therefore includes belief in Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets without making one prophet an enemy of another (2:136; 3:84).

### Jesus and the Holy Spirit

Jesus, son of Mary, was given *bayyināt*—clear proofs—and supported with the *Rūḥ al-Qudus*, commonly understood by the classical exegetes to be the angel Jibrīl (Gabriel). The Qur’an describes Jesus’ miracles—healing the blind and leper and bringing the dead to life by Allah’s permission—as signs of his prophethood (3:49; 5:110). The “holy spirit” is not a divine person sharing Allah’s essence. The Qur’an’s language presents support from Allah and His messenger angel, while Jesus remains a servant and messenger of Allah.

The miracles do not establish independent power in Jesus. Each is repeatedly qualified as occurring “by Allah’s permission.” This protects the doctrine of divine oneness while honouring Jesus’ extraordinary mission. The Qur’an calls him a word from Allah, a spirit created by Him, and a messenger—not Allah or the son of Allah (4:171; 19:30–36).

### Rejecting Messengers Because of Desire

The question “Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you with something you do not like, you become arrogant?” exposes a recurring pattern. The issue was not lack of guidance; it was that the guidance challenged their interests, interpretations, or political and religious authority. A messenger’s message may be true but unwelcome. Arrogance responds by rejecting the messenger rather than examining the self.

The verse mentions that some messengers were rejected and others killed. It does not say that every Israelite killed prophets, nor does it erase the upright individuals acknowledged elsewhere in the Qur’an. It describes a repeated pattern among rebellious groups and leaders. The moral responsibility belongs to those who committed, approved, defended, or continued the rejection—not to people condemned merely by ancestry.

The Qur’an describes those who killed prophets unjustly as people whose hearts had become hardened and whose worldly interests had overruled the covenant (3:21; 5:70). Violence against a messenger is the extreme form of resisting correction. A person begins by disliking the command, then attacks the messenger’s credibility, then tries to silence the message. The same pattern can occur without physical violence when communities exile, slander, or intimidate those who speak truthfully.

### Revelation Corrects Every Community

The verse is a warning to all religious communities. Muslims may honour the names of all prophets while resisting a Qur’anic command that conflicts with family custom, political loyalty, or personal desire. A community may claim to love revelation while attacking the people who remind it of its obligations. The test is not whether the message is attractive but whether the person is willing to submit when it is true.

This does not require believers to accept every person who claims to speak for Allah. Prophetic claims must be tested by revelation, truthfulness, evidence, and the established finality of Muhammad’s (ﷺ) prophethood. But once a command is authentically established, dislike is not evidence against it. The believer seeks understanding and asks Allah for the humility to obey.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:136, 3:84, and 4:136 on belief in all prophets and revelations.
- Qur’an 3:45–51 and 5:110 on Jesus, his clear signs, and miracles by Allah’s permission.
- Qur’an 4:171 and 19:30–36 on Jesus as servant, word, and messenger, not a partner with Allah.
- Qur’an 3:21 and 5:70 on rejecting and killing prophets unjustly.
- Qur’an 2:87, 5:44, and 17:55 on the succession of messengers and the responsibility of receiving scripture.
- For *Rūḥ al-Qudus* and the historical warning in this verse, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:87.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:88

> They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!” In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith.

**Commentary:**

### “Our Hearts Are Unreceptive”

The Children of Israel answer the criticism of their rejection by saying, “Our hearts are unreceptive.” The Arabic *qulūbunā ghulf* can suggest hearts enclosed, covered, or wrapped. Their statement may mean that their hearts are already full and do not need the Prophet’s message, or that they are incapable of receiving it. In either case, it functions as a defence against responsibility: they present their rejection as a fixed condition rather than a choice that can be corrected.

The Qur’an rejects the excuse. Allah says that He has condemned them for disbelief and that only a little of their faith remains. The point is not that their physical hearts were sealed before they made any decision, but that persistent rejection had produced a spiritual barrier. Their language of incapacity concealed a deeper refusal to open themselves to truth.

This resembles the earlier description of hearts being sealed (2:7), but the present verse highlights the moral process. A person repeatedly refuses guidance, then becomes accustomed to the refusal, and eventually treats the resulting hardness as proof that guidance is impossible. The Qur’an calls people to repent before the heart reaches this condition. “When they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate” (61:5): human deviation and divine judgment are both present.

### Little Faith Is Not a Compliment

“They have but little faith” may refer to their limited belief in Allah, scripture, and the truths they still acknowledged. They claimed to believe in Moses and the Torah while rejecting the consequences of that belief. Their faith was therefore selective and insufficient. It did not produce acceptance of the messenger whose message confirmed their scripture.

The phrase also exposes the contradiction of claiming that one’s heart is already complete. A heart full of true faith is not closed to truth; it is more willing to recognize and submit to it. Genuine faith is not fragile possession that needs protection from further guidance. It grows through humility, reflection, and obedience. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that faith wears out in the heart as a garment wears out, so believers should ask Allah to renew faith in their hearts (reported in al-Mustadrak and other collections with supporting narrations).

A person may possess partial truth and still reject an important truth. The Qur’an criticizes those who believe in some messengers and reject others (4:150–151). Selective faith is not the complete faith Allah requires. Belief in one prophet should lead to belief in the others when their missions are authentically established, because the source of their message is one.

### The Heart Can Be Opened

The Qur’an’s teaching is not that every closed heart is beyond hope. Allah says that He expands the breast of the person He wants to guide to Islam (6:125), and He promises to accept those who repent and reform. The condition described in verse 88 is a warning about persistent rejection, not a reason for a living person to despair.

The way to an open heart begins with honesty. A person admits that he may be wrong, asks Allah for guidance, listens without mockery, and acts upon what becomes clear. Prayer, remembrance, recitation, repentance, and keeping company with truthful people soften the heart. The Qur’an asks, “Has the time not come for those who believe that their hearts should humble themselves to the remembrance of Allah?” (57:16).

### Condemnation as Consequence

The expression that Allah condemned them for disbelief should be understood as divine judgment and abandonment, not as human emotion or arbitrary hostility. Allah’s condemnation is a just consequence of knowingly rejecting truth. The Qur’an uses similar language for those who break covenants, conceal revelation, and persist in wrongdoing. The verse should not be used to justify hatred toward a people or to pronounce the unseen state of every individual.

The text itself leaves room for moral distinction. Some of the Children of Israel believed in their prophets, some accepted Jesus, and some later accepted Muhammad (ﷺ). Others rejected. The Qur’an praises those among the People of the Book who are humble before revelation (5:83–85). Justice requires preserving these distinctions.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:7 and 61:5 on sealed hearts and deviation producing further deviation.
- Qur’an 4:150–152 on believing in some messengers while rejecting others.
- Qur’an 5:83–85 and 3:113–115 on humble and upright People of the Book.
- Qur’an 6:125 and 57:16 on Allah opening hearts and calling believers to humility.
- Qur’an 39:22 and 24:35 on the light and expansion Allah grants to receptive hearts.
- For *ghulf*, the meaning of “little faith,” and the distinction between consequence and arbitrary compulsion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:88.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:89

> Although they used to pray for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists, when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it. So may Allah’s condemnation be upon the disbelievers.

**Commentary:**

### Waiting for the Promised Messenger

The verse describes a striking contradiction. Some of the People of the Book had expected and invoked the coming of a prophet through whom they would gain victory over the polytheists. When the Book came from Allah and confirmed the scripture they possessed, however, they rejected it. Their problem was not that the message was entirely unfamiliar. They recognized features of its truth, but they refused to submit when the messenger came from a people or lineage they did not expect.

The Qur’an alludes to this recognition in several places. It says that those who were given scripture recognize Muhammad (ﷺ) as they recognize their own children, yet a group conceals the truth knowingly (2:146). It also records Jesus announcing a messenger to come after him named Aḥmad (61:6), and describes the Prophet as one whose description they find in the Torah and Gospel (7:157). These references concern the continuity of revelation and the signs of prophethood; they do not mean that every detail of every existing manuscript is preserved without dispute.

### Truth Rejected Because of Its Social Source

The rejection described here illustrates how group pride can overpower recognition. A person may hope for truth when he imagines that truth will strengthen his community, then reject the same truth when it honours another people or requires him to surrender authority. The Qur’an says that they rejected out of envy because Allah had sent His favour upon whom He willed (2:90). The disease was not lack of evidence but resentment over who received the favour.

This is a warning against making ethnicity, class, nationality, family, or institutional ownership a condition of truth. Guidance belongs to Allah, and He chooses His messengers according to His wisdom. A person who says, “I would accept truth if it came through our group,” has made group identity more important than Allah’s command.

The verse also warns Muslims. A Muslim may pray for justice and guidance, then reject a truth because it came through an unpopular scholar, another school, a poor person, a convert, or someone outside his social class. The principle is the same: the source is judged by prejudice rather than the evidence. Truth must be recognized wherever it is established, while every claim must still be tested responsibly.

### Confirmation Should Have Increased Faith

The Book was described as confirming what they had. Confirmation should have made acceptance easier because the new revelation was not asking them to abandon Abraham, Moses, or the original call to worship Allah. It was correcting distortion and completing the chain. Instead, familiarity became a basis for rejection: they treated their inherited possession as a monopoly and refused the further guidance that confirmed its source.

A community can repeat this failure by confusing loyalty to a tradition with loyalty to Allah. Tradition is valuable when it preserves truth, but it becomes an idol when it is defended against revelation. The Qur’an asks whether people will follow their forefathers even when those forefathers lacked understanding and guidance (2:170). The believer honours the past by following its truth, not by protecting every inherited claim.

### Condemnation of the Rejection

The closing prayer—“may Allah’s condemnation be upon the disbelievers”—is a divine verdict on the rejection described. It is not permission for ordinary people to become abusive or hateful. Allah’s condemnation is judgment based on complete knowledge of intention and evidence. Human beings may explain the Qur’anic criticism and defend truth, but they must not use it to justify collective injustice.

The verse’s immediate purpose is to expose the seriousness of recognizing a truth and then refusing it for pride. It asks the reader to examine his own expectations. Do I welcome guidance only when it confirms my group? Would I accept a correction from a person I regard as socially inferior? Am I more loyal to truth or to the identity from which I receive status?

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:90, 2:146, and 3:19 on envy, recognition, and rejection of the confirming revelation.
- Qur’an 7:157 and 61:6 on the Prophet’s description in previous revelation and Jesus’ announcement of Aḥmad.
- Qur’an 2:136, 3:84, and 5:48 on continuity, confirmation, and the Qur’an as a criterion.
- Qur’an 2:170 and 49:13 on inherited identity, blind following, and the measure of honour.
- Qur’an 6:108 and 5:8 on defending truth without transgressing into abuse or injustice.
- For the historical context and the meaning of recognition and rejection in this verse, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:89.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:90

> Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for—denying Allah’s revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants! They have earned wrath upon wrath. And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Selling the Soul for Envy

The previous verse described people who expected a messenger but rejected him when he came. This verse calls their choice a miserable transaction. They sold their souls by denying Allah’s revelation and allowing resentment to govern their response to divine favour. The loss is not merely the loss of an argument; it is the surrender of the soul’s capacity to recognize and submit to truth.

The cause named here is envy—resentment that Allah granted His grace to whom He willed. The Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) was not from the lineage or social group some of them expected, and the Qur’an portrays their rejection as an unwillingness to accept Allah’s choice. Envy is particularly destructive because it does not simply desire a blessing; it resents that another person has received it. The Qur’an warns that many People of the Book wished to turn believers back to disbelief out of envy after the truth had become clear (2:109).

Allah’s grace is not limited by human expectation. He gives prophethood, knowledge, provision, and guidance according to His wisdom, not according to ethnic ownership or social entitlement. The Qur’an says, “They are jealous of people for what Allah has given them of His bounty” (4:54). The proper response to another person’s blessing is gratitude to Allah, prayer for one’s own good, and recognition that divine generosity is not a finite resource.

### Revelation Rejected for a Worldly Reason

The verse does not describe a rejection caused by a lack of evidence. It identifies a moral reason: they denied what Allah revealed because the recipient of the favour did not fit their preference. This is a form of judging revelation by the ego. A person may claim to seek truth, but his real condition becomes visible when truth arrives through a source he does not admire or when it requires him to surrender authority.

The same danger appears in every community. A Muslim may reject sound advice because it comes from a woman, a convert, a poor person, an unfamiliar school, or a disliked political group. A scholar may dismiss evidence because it challenges his reputation. A leader may oppose justice because it empowers people outside his circle. The believer asks Allah to protect him from making social origin more important than truth.

### Wrath Upon Wrath

“They have earned wrath upon wrath” is a powerful expression of accumulated consequence. Classical exegetes explained the twofold wrath in different ways, including wrath for rejecting earlier revelation and another wrath for rejecting the Qur’an, or wrath for earlier sins and another for their later rejection. The exact enumeration is not essential. The phrase conveys compounded displeasure: a new rejection is added to an existing history of disobedience.

Repeated defiance can make the heart increasingly resistant. One refusal protects the next, and the person begins to defend not only the present error but the entire identity built around earlier errors. The Qur’an says that those who disbelieve and hinder others from Allah’s path have increased in disbelief (16:88). The increase is a moral accumulation, not an arbitrary punishment imposed without relation to conduct.

Allah’s wrath must be understood as divine judgment and punishment, not as an uncontrolled human emotion. It is compatible with His justice and wisdom. The verse warns that the rejection of guidance has consequences while leaving the door open to repentance for those who turn back before death.

### Humiliation and the Loss of Dignity

The punishment is described as humiliating. The people sought honour through lineage, recognition, and control over religious authority, but their pride led to the opposite of what they wanted. True dignity comes from obedience to Allah and moral integrity, not from possessing a prestigious identity while rejecting truth.

The Qur’an describes the believers as those who seek honour through Allah, His Messenger, and the believers, while honour ultimately belongs to Allah (63:8). Worldly humiliation may take many forms and is not a reliable measure of guilt in every individual case. Some righteous people are oppressed, and some wrongdoers are temporarily respected. The final humiliation described here is the exposure of the false grounds upon which the rejecters built their superiority.

### The Cure for Envy

The cure is to recognize Allah’s wisdom, ask for blessing without wishing another’s blessing removed, and practise gratitude for what one has received. The Prophet (ﷺ) warned against envy because it consumes good deeds as fire consumes wood (Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 4903). The warning does not prohibit admiring another person and asking Allah for similar good; it condemns resentful desire for the loss of another’s blessing.

A person who is grateful becomes less threatened by another’s success. He understands that Allah’s favour to one servant does not deprive another, and that the highest favour is guidance. The verse calls the reader to value truth above status and divine grace above group ownership.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:89 and 2:109 on rejection, envy, and turning people away from faith.
- Qur’an 4:54 on envying people for Allah’s bounty.
- Qur’an 16:88 on increased disbelief and the consequences of hindering others from Allah’s way.
- Qur’an 63:8 on true honour belonging to Allah, His Messenger, and the believers.
- Qur’an 3:134 and 59:9 on restraining envy, generosity, and preferring others.
- Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 4903, on envy consuming good deeds like fire consumes wood.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:91

> When it is said to them: “Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,” and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures! Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Why then did you kill Allah’s prophets before, if you are ˹truly˺ believers?”

**Commentary:**

### Selective Belief Is Not Faith in Revelation

The people addressed claim that they believe only in what was sent down to them. The Qur’an exposes the inconsistency: if they genuinely believe in the One who revealed their scripture, they should not reject a later revelation that confirms its truth and comes from the same Lord. Their position is not loyalty to revelation in general; it is loyalty to a restricted identity that refuses any guidance outside its control.

Islam affirms that Allah sent revelation to Moses, Jesus, Abraham, and the other prophets, and that the foundation of their message was one. The Qur’an commands Muslims to say, “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and what was given to Moses and Jesus” (2:136). Rejecting a genuine messenger because he comes later or from another people contradicts the claim to believe in Allah’s wisdom.

The final revelation has a distinctive status. It confirms the original truth of previous scriptures and serves as a criterion over them (5:48). Once the message of Muhammad (ﷺ) is clearly established for a person, faith in Allah includes accepting the final messenger. The verse is not saying that people who lived before him were required to believe in a revelation they had never received. It condemns those who reject the final message after its truth becomes clear while claiming to honour the God who sent it.

### The Question About the Prophets

The Prophet is told to ask why they killed Allah’s prophets if they were truly believers. The question exposes the contradiction between their claim and the history of rejecting messengers. Belief is not a declaration about a book while opposing the people who convey its commands. A genuine believer in revelation respects the messengers and accepts the moral correction they bring.

The phrase does not mean that every individual in the audience personally killed a prophet. The Qur’an sometimes attributes the action of a representative group to a community whose leaders, supporters, or later heirs approved the same pattern. It also distinguishes upright members from wrongdoers. The moral principle is that a community cannot claim loyalty to Moses while defending the rejection or killing of prophets who came with inconvenient truth.

Killing a prophet is the extreme form of refusing correction. The process often begins with resentment, mockery, accusations of ignorance, and attempts to control the message. If the messenger remains firm, those who fear the truth may seek to silence him. The Qur’an records this pattern not merely as ancient history but as a warning to every society that treats unwelcome truth as an enemy.

### Revelation Is a Coherent Whole

The verse teaches that revelation should be understood as a coherent chain. Allah is not divided against Himself, and His prophets do not compete for separate gods or contradictory foundations. Differences in law can exist because Allah legislated according to the needs of communities and periods, but the core of tawḥīd, morality, and accountability remains continuous.

A person who accepts only the scripture that supports his group is not submitting to revelation; he is using revelation to support the group. The Qur’an repeatedly criticizes people who believe in some messengers and reject others (4:150–152), while praising those who make no such division. The true believer welcomes truth from Allah even when it requires a new allegiance.

### A Test of Sincerity

The test is not whether a person can state respect for scripture. It is whether he accepts the implications of that respect. Does he allow revelation to correct ancestral customs? Does he accept the messenger when the messenger’s message challenges power? Does he honour the truth even when it transfers religious authority to another community?

The same questions apply to Muslims. A person may affirm the Qur’an but reject a command because it conflicts with culture, politics, or personal desire. He may claim to follow the Prophet while accepting only traditions that suit him. Sincere faith does not mean perfect performance, but it means that the standard remains revelation and that shortcomings are met with repentance rather than denial.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:136, 3:84, and 4:136 on believing in all prophets and revelations.
- Qur’an 4:150–152 on accepting some prophets while rejecting others.
- Qur’an 5:44–48 on the continuity of scripture and the Qur’an as a criterion.
- Qur’an 2:87 and 3:21 on the rejection and killing of prophets.
- Qur’an 61:6 on Jesus announcing the messenger who would come after him.
- For the contradiction between the claim and conduct in this verse, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:91.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:92

> Indeed, Moses came to you with clear proofs, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully.

**Commentary:**

### Clear Proofs and a Familiar Failure

The Qur’an returns to an event already mentioned: Moses came with clear proofs, yet the people worshipped the calf in his absence. The repetition is deliberate. The problem was not that the people lacked signs, instruction, or access to a messenger. They had witnessed Moses’ miracles and received his guidance, but when he was absent they returned to a visible object of worship.

The “clear proofs” include the signs given to Moses before Pharaoh and the miracles by which Allah delivered the Children of Israel. The Qur’an mentions the staff, the shining hand, the plagues, the parting of the sea, and other signs (7:103–137; 20:17–79). These proofs established Moses’ mission, but signs do not compel a person’s love and obedience. They remove excuses and test the heart’s response.

The verse’s reminder also shows that repeated mention in revelation is a form of education. Human beings forget, rationalize, and detach present choices from past evidence. A person may remember that Allah rescued him in a crisis but forget the gratitude he promised when the crisis passes. Returning to the same event helps the reader see new dimensions of the failure and examine whether he repeats the same pattern.

### Faith Attached to a Visible Leader

The calf-worship occurred in Moses’ absence. The issue was not that Moses was divine; his absence exposed whether the people were attached to Allah or merely dependent upon the visible leadership of Moses. Prophets are followed because Allah sends them, but worship belongs to Allah. When a human leader is absent, dies, or becomes unpopular, the believer’s allegiance to Allah must remain.

This lesson is essential for Muslim communities. Scholars, imams, political leaders, and teachers may guide, but none should become an object of unconditional loyalty. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that whoever worships Muhammad, Muhammad has died, while whoever worships Allah knows that Allah is alive and does not die (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). Love for a leader is good when it leads to Allah; it becomes dangerous when it replaces Allah.

The calf itself represents the attraction of visible power and identity. It could be displayed, touched, and treated as a centre of collective belonging. The unseen Creator requires trust and obedience; the idol can be manipulated by its makers. Every age has its own calves—wealth, nation, charismatic personality, ideology, or technological power—that promise security while demanding ultimate allegiance.

### Wrongdoing After Clear Evidence

The verse calls their act wrongdoing—*ẓulm*. They placed worship where it did not belong and harmed their own souls. The term does not mean that every person had the same responsibility. Aaron (ʿalayhi al-salām) warned them, and some may have resisted while others led the deviation. Allah distinguishes between the initiator, follower, resister, and repentant person.

The lesson is that knowledge increases responsibility. The same act performed by a person who never received guidance and by a person who has witnessed clear proofs cannot be evaluated in the same manner. Allah judges every person with complete knowledge of evidence, intention, opportunity, and response. The community, however, is still responsible for speaking against public idolatry and protecting vulnerable people from manipulation.

### Returning to the Story Without Despair

The repeated reminder is severe but not hopeless. The calf-worship was followed by repentance and forgiveness (2:54–52). The history shows both the danger of returning to false worship and the possibility of divine mercy. A person who has fallen after receiving guidance should not defend the fall or despair of return. He should acknowledge the wrong, abandon the substitute, and turn back to the Creator.

The verse asks the reader what happens when his most visible source of security is removed. Does he continue to worship Allah in private, hardship, and uncertainty, or does he seek a new object that promises immediate reassurance? True faith survives the absence of the leader because it is rooted in the Lord of the leader.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:51–54 and 7:148–153 on the calf, Aaron’s warning, repentance, and forgiveness.
- Qur’an 7:103–137 and 20:17–79 on Moses’ clear proofs and signs.
- Qur’an 3:144 and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī on worship belonging to Allah, not to a messenger.
- Qur’an 21:51–67 on Abraham’s rejection of visible idols.
- Qur’an 6:164 on individual responsibility and the distinction between people’s deeds.
- For the repetition and moral force of the calf narrative, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:92.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:93

> And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey,” they answered, “We hear and disobey.” The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!”

**Commentary:**

### The Covenant and the Mountain

The verse revisits the covenant at Sinai. The mountain was raised above them as a sign of the covenant’s seriousness, and they were commanded to hold firmly to the Scripture and obey. The command joins attachment to revelation with practical obedience. A community cannot claim to hold the Book while treating its instructions as optional whenever they conflict with habit or desire.

The scene is described elsewhere with the command, “Hold firmly to what We have given you and remember what is in it” (7:171). Remembering revelation means retaining its guidance in consciousness and conduct. It is not simply preserving a manuscript or repeating a creed. The Book must be allowed to judge the community’s worship, ethics, treatment of the vulnerable, and use of power.

The raised mountain symbolizes the weight of the trust. The covenant was not a light promise made for ceremonial effect. It was an agreement before Allah, and its violation would have consequences. The same principle applies to Muslims who recite the Qur’an and profess submission. Their honour lies in fulfilling its commands, not merely in identifying with its community.

### “We Hear and Disobey”

The response “We hear and disobey” is the opposite of the faithful formula “We hear and obey” (2:285). Some classical reports understand this statement as a literal declaration of defiance; others describe it as their inward attitude even if their outward words were not stated in precisely that form. Either way, the Qur’an exposes the contradiction between receiving a command and deciding in advance not to submit.

Hearing is not the same as beneficial listening. A person can receive sound through the ears and still refuse the message. The Qur’an praises those who listen and follow the best of what they hear (39:18), while it condemns those who possess ears but do not understand (7:179). Obedience is the proof that hearing has reached the heart.

The phrase also warns against the empty use of religious language. Some people say “we hear and obey” in public while privately arranging to disobey. Others obey only when the command matches their preference and call resistance “interpretation” when it does not. The believer may struggle, misunderstand, or fall, but he should not make disobedience his declared principle.

### The Calf’s Love in the Heart

The verse says that the love of the calf was made to drink into their hearts because of their disbelief. The image suggests that idolatry was not only an outward act; it had become an inward attachment. They loved the symbol of visible power and security, and that love competed with the covenant of worshipping Allah alone.

The Qur’an often speaks of love as a force that directs action. People may love wealth, status, ancestors, or desires more than they love Allah (2:165; 3:14). The object most loved becomes the object most obeyed. This is why faith requires the heart to reorder its loves, not merely the tongue to repeat correct statements.

The calf can be understood as a historical idol and as a recurring symbol. Modern people may not bow before a golden statue, yet they can love money, nation, political power, celebrity, or ideological identity in a way that makes them disobey Allah to protect it. The test is not whether a person uses the word “worship”; it is whether the thing controls his ultimate loyalty.

### Belief Tested by Conduct

The Prophet is instructed to ask how evil their supposed belief has led them to behave. The question exposes a practical contradiction: if they truly believed in the Torah and its Lord, why did that belief produce calf-worship and refusal of covenant? Genuine belief is not judged by self-description alone. It is examined through its fruits.

The verse does not require perfect conduct before a person may be called a believer. Humans fall and repent. It condemns a claim that is used to protect deliberate rebellion. A believer who sins admits the contradiction and seeks forgiveness; the hypocritical claim insists that disobedience proves nothing and that the Book must be adjusted to fit the behaviour.

The lesson is universal. Muslims should ask whether their professed faith produces prayer, honesty, justice, charity, and humility. If they love a worldly “calf” more than Allah, the remedy is not to deny the problem but to repent and redirect the heart.

- Qur’an 2:63–64 and 7:171 on the covenant, the mountain, and holding firmly to the Book.
- Qur’an 2:51–54 and 2:92 on the calf and its consequences.
- Qur’an 2:285 and 39:18 on hearing and obeying versus merely receiving words.
- Qur’an 2:165 and 3:14 on the loves that compete with love of Allah.
- Qur’an 7:179 and 16:108 on hearing without understanding and hearts becoming sealed.
- For the meaning of “we hear and disobey” and the calf’s love entering the heart, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:93.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:94

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death if what you say is true!”

**Commentary:**

### A Test of the Claim to Exclusive Salvation

The Prophet is instructed to challenge a claim of exclusive salvation. If the eternal home with Allah truly belongs only to them and no one else among humanity, then they should wish for death so that they may reach the promised reward. The argument is rhetorical. It exposes the contradiction between claiming certainty of Paradise and showing no desire to meet the One who supposedly guaranteed it.

The challenge is not a command to seek self-harm or to despise life. Islam forbids a person from wishing for death because of worldly hardship and teaches that life is a trust. The Qur’anic challenge asks what their own confidence would logically imply. If they were certain of Allah’s special favour and their own righteousness, the prospect of leaving the world for the eternal home should not terrify them.

The verse is directed at a claim made by some of them, not at every Jew or every member of the People of the Book. The Qur’an repeatedly distinguishes among them, praising upright people who believe and act righteously (3:113–115). The target is an assertion of automatic privilege, not an ethnicity.

### Paradise Is Not Inherited by Label

The claim that Paradise belongs exclusively to one community resembles other claims later mentioned in the sūrah: “None will enter Paradise unless they are Jews or Christians” (2:111). Allah rejects these declarations and asks for proof. Salvation is not secured by lineage, historical memory, or communal branding. It depends upon sincere submission, faith, righteous action, and Allah’s mercy.

The Qur’an honours the Children of Israel for the favours they received, but it also explains that the covenant does not extend to wrongdoers (2:124). Selection for a prophetic mission is not the same as a guarantee for every descendant. A community may be entrusted with revelation and lose its privilege through rejection, while an individual from outside that community may be honoured through faith.

This principle also prevents Muslim complacency. No person should imagine that belonging to a Muslim family, nation, school, or movement guarantees salvation while he neglects prayer, justice, repentance, and the rights of people. The Prophet (ﷺ) told his own daughter Fāṭimah to save herself from the Fire because lineage would not make him independently able to save her (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

### Life Is Not to Be Despised

The challenge must be read alongside the Qur’an’s respect for life. A believer does not seek death as an escape from ordinary difficulty, depression, debt, or conflict. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that none of you should wish for death because of a harm that has afflicted him; if he must make the supplication, he should ask Allah to keep him alive while life is better and to take him when death is better (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

The distinction is between a rhetorical test of a boast and a personal desire to end one’s life. The first exposes inconsistency; the second requires compassion, support, and lawful care. The verse does not make death a proof of faith. It asks whether a people’s claimed certainty about their final home is consistent with their fear of meeting Allah.

### The Meeting with Allah

A sincere believer may love meeting Allah without seeking death. The Prophet (ﷺ) explained that when a believer is given the good news of Allah’s mercy and pleasure, he loves meeting Allah, and Allah loves meeting him; when the disbeliever is given the news of punishment, he hates meeting Allah, and Allah hates meeting him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This love grows from hope, not from recklessness.

The challenge therefore tests the heart’s real priorities. A person may speak of Paradise as a possession while loving the world as though it were the only reality. He may claim that Allah’s favour is guaranteed but fear the exposure of his deeds. The next verse will explain that they will never wish for death because of what their hands have sent forward.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:111–112 and 2:124 on the rejection of inherited or exclusive claims to Paradise.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 and 5:66 on upright individuals among the People of the Book.
- Qur’an 62:6–7 on the same challenge to those who claim exclusive friendship with Allah.
- Qur’an 4:29 and 5:32 on the sanctity of life.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on not wishing for death because of worldly harm and on love of meeting Allah.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:95

> But they will never wish for that because of what their hands have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers.

**Commentary:**

### Deeds Expose the Heart

The previous verse challenged the claim that the Hereafter belonged exclusively to the Children of Israel. This verse says they would never wish for death because of what their hands had sent forward. Their actions had produced a record that made the meeting with Allah frightening. A person may publicly claim certainty of Paradise, but his private knowledge of injustice, rejection, betrayal, and concealed wrongdoing can expose the emptiness of the claim.

“Hands” is a Qur’anic expression for deeds and actions, not a limitation of wrongdoing to physical acts. What people write, speak, plan, support, conceal, and perform is all included. The phrase makes responsibility concrete. A person’s final state is not created by words alone; it is shaped by the actions he repeatedly chooses.

The verse does not mean that every sinner fears death in exactly the same way or that every person who fears death is a hypocrite. Love of life is natural, and even righteous people may fear pain, separation, and the unknown. The context concerns the contradiction between an arrogant claim of guaranteed salvation and the refusal to face the consequences of one’s deeds.

### The Record of the Hands

The Qur’an frequently describes human actions as what the hands have earned. “Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your hands have earned, yet He pardons much” (42:30). The expression joins accountability with mercy. People are responsible for their choices, but Allah often conceals, forgives, and gives time to repent. The proper response to recognizing wrongdoing is not to deny it but to seek forgiveness and repair what can be repaired.

The record of deeds includes both public and private conduct. The concealed bribe, the hidden betrayal, and the unspoken intention to harm may escape human judgment but not Allah’s. The Qur’an says that the Day when secrets are exposed will reveal the truth behind public appearances (86:9). The awareness of this record should make the believer careful without making him hopeless.

### Allah Knows the Wrongdoers

The verse closes with Allah’s perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers. He knows not only what they did but why they did it, what evidence they received, what opportunities they had to repent, and how they treated the rights of others. This complete knowledge ensures that the challenge in the previous verse is just. No one is judged by an unfair generalization or by a deed separated from its circumstances.

Allah’s knowledge also protects the innocent from the failures of human courts. A person may be falsely accused, denied compensation, or forgotten by history, but Allah knows the truth. Likewise, a wrongdoer may escape worldly consequences, but Allah’s knowledge prevents escape from the final account. The believer trusts this justice while still pursuing lawful justice in the world.

### Repentance Before the Meeting

The verse is a warning, but it also implies a way out. Fear of meeting Allah can be transformed into hope through repentance. The Qur’an says that those who repent, believe, and do righteous deeds will have their evil deeds changed into good (25:70). The person who regrets his past and repairs its harm does not need to maintain a false claim of innocence. He can meet Allah as a repentant servant relying upon mercy.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that Allah accepts repentance before the soul reaches its final moment, and that the wise person holds himself accountable before he is held accountable (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2459). The hands are still able to repair while life remains. Death closes the period of action, so the believer does not postpone reconciliation, restitution, or return to prayer.

### The Personal Mirror

The passage should not become an excuse to catalogue another community’s sins. Every reader has hands that have earned something and a heart that may be tempted to boast beyond its deeds. The honest question is not merely whether others fear judgment; it is whether one’s own deeds are being prepared for the meeting with Allah.

A sound hope is built upon faith, repentance, and action. A false hope rests on identity, wishful thinking, or the assumption that Allah’s mercy means He does not care what people do. The verse calls the reader to abandon both despair and presumption and to let Allah’s perfect knowledge produce truthful reform.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:80, 2:94, and 62:6–7 on the challenge to claims of exclusive salvation.
- Qur’an 42:30 and 86:9 on deeds, hidden secrets, and the record of what hands have earned.
- Qur’an 25:70 and 39:53 on repentance and Allah’s mercy.
- Qur’an 99:7–8 on every good and evil deed being brought to account.
- Qur’an 6:164 on individual responsibility.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2459, on self-accountability before the final reckoning.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:96

> You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.

**Commentary:**

### Attachment to Length of Life

The previous verse said that they would not wish for death because of their deeds. This verse explains the intensity of their attachment to worldly life. The Qur’an says they are eager to live—even more eager than polytheists—and describes each person wishing for a thousand years. The number is a figure of extreme length, not a prediction that they literally expected to reach a thousand years.

Love of life is not itself sinful. Life is a gift, and believers are allowed to ask Allah for health, long life in obedience, and benefit to people. The problem is clinging to life because it is the only thing valued, fearing death because it exposes an unrepentant record, and treating longevity as a substitute for righteousness. A long life can be a blessing when filled with good, but it can also increase the burden when it is spent in rebellion.

The verse speaks about a group characterized by a particular attitude; it is not a biological or ethnic claim about every Jew or every individual. The Qur’an elsewhere distinguishes upright People of the Book from wrongdoers. Its target is the person whose conduct and theology reveal an intense attachment to worldly survival without corresponding preparation for the Hereafter.

### Quantity of Years Does Not Secure the Soul

Even a thousand years would not save such a person from punishment. Time cannot erase guilt by itself. A person may postpone repentance for decades, but delay does not transform wrong into right. The Qur’an says that every soul will taste death and that the real success is to be moved away from the Fire and admitted to Paradise (3:185).

The statement also corrects the assumption that death is the greatest loss. The greatest loss is to meet Allah with a wasted life and an unrepentant heart. Conversely, a shorter life filled with faith, repentance, and good action may be more valuable than a long life spent in heedlessness. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that the best people are those whose lives are long and whose deeds are good, while the worst are those whose lives are long and whose deeds are bad (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2330).

The desire to live “a thousand years” reveals a refusal to accept human limitation. People may pursue medicine, safety, and healthy living, all of which can be lawful means, but no technology can abolish the appointed term. “Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, even if you are in fortified towers” (4:78).

### The Difference Between Love of Life and Fear of Accountability

A believer may love life because it offers opportunities to worship, repair harm, raise children, serve people, and increase in good deeds. He can also fear death naturally while trusting Allah. The blameworthy attachment described here is different: it arises from terror of what follows death and refusal to face the record of the hands.

The Qur’an teaches a balanced supplication: “Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter and protect us from the punishment of the Fire” (2:201). The believer does not ask for worldly life as an end in itself. He asks for a life that helps him reach Allah’s pleasure and for a good end when the appointed time comes.

### Allah Sees What They Do

The verse concludes that Allah is All-Seeing of what they do. Their love of life is not judged by words alone; their deeds reveal the source of their fear. Allah sees their rejection, concealment, violence, and manipulation, as well as every opportunity they had to repent. He also sees the believer who uses his remaining years for good even when no one notices.

Divine sight is therefore both warning and consolation. The person who tries to preserve life through injustice cannot escape the One who sees it. The person who sacrifices worldly advantage to preserve faith is not forgotten. The final value of life is determined by what Allah sees within it, not by its numerical length or public appearance.

### Preparing for the End

The practical lesson is to turn the love of life into purposeful gratitude. Health should be used before illness, free time before busyness, youth before old age, and life before death. The Prophet (ﷺ) advised taking advantage of five before five, including life before death and health before illness (reported by al-Ḥākim and others).

The verse does not ask believers to despise life. It asks them not to worship it. The best preparation for death is not denial but repentance, fulfilment of obligations, reconciliation, charity, and steady worship. When the heart is prepared to meet Allah, love of life becomes love of opportunity rather than terror of accountability.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:94–95 on the refusal to wish for death because of deeds.
- Qur’an 3:185 and 4:78 on the inevitability of death and the true measure of success.
- Qur’an 2:201 on seeking good in both worlds and protection from the Fire.
- Qur’an 35:11 and 63:10 on the fixed term and the regret of those who postpone charity and repentance.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2330, on long life being good when accompanied by good deeds.
- For the rhetorical meaning of “a thousand years” and the distinction between lawful love of life and blameworthy attachment, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:96.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:97

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”

**Commentary:**

### Gabriel, the Trusted Messenger

The verse addresses hostility toward Gabriel, the angel who conveyed the Qur’an to the heart of Muhammad (ﷺ). Gabriel is not the author of the Qur’an; he is the trustworthy messenger who delivered Allah’s speech by Allah’s permission. The Qur’an says that the Trustworthy Spirit brought it down upon the Prophet’s heart (26:193–194), and that it was revealed by the Lord of the worlds.

The use of “heart” is significant. Revelation was not merely placed before the Prophet as an external text; it was received, preserved, understood, and embodied within him. The heart in Qur’anic language is the centre of comprehension and moral response. The Prophet did not invent the message, nor did Gabriel independently compose it. Allah revealed, Gabriel conveyed, and Muhammad (ﷺ) recited and explained it.

The verse responds to a claim that hostility toward Gabriel could justify rejection of the Qur’an. The logic fails because the angel is carrying out Allah’s command. Resenting the messenger for conveying an unwelcome message does not alter the truth of the message. A person who rejects a letter because he dislikes the courier has not answered its contents.

### Confirming What Came Before

The Qur’an confirms the earlier revelations in their original truth. It affirms the prophethood of Moses, Jesus, Abraham, and the other messengers, and recognizes that divine guidance came before Muhammad (ﷺ). This confirmation also explains why the Qur’an should have been welcomed by those who possessed scripture. It was not asking them to worship a new god or reject the authentic message of their prophets.

At the same time, confirmation includes correction and authority. The Qur’an confirms what is true and acts as a guardian and criterion over previous scripture (5:48). It is therefore not bound to every later interpretation or textual claim. The final revelation restores the central call to tawḥīd and corrects doctrines and practices that contradict it.

The Qur’an describes itself as a guide and good news for believers. Its guidance is not limited to information about earlier history. It directs the believer’s worship, ethics, family relations, economic life, and hope for the Hereafter. Its good news is that those who respond with faith and obedience receive Allah’s forgiveness, mercy, and lasting reward.

### Why Hostility Toward an Angel Is Serious

Angels are servants of Allah who do not disobey His commands (66:6). Gabriel’s role in revelation is therefore a part of the divine order, not an independent religious authority. To show hostility toward him because he brought the Qur’an is effectively to show hostility toward the revelation he faithfully delivered. The verse does not treat the issue as a personal dispute between human beings and an angel; it concerns the acceptance or rejection of Allah’s guidance.

The passage also prevents a fragmented theology in which a person claims to believe in Allah and some of His messengers while rejecting the channels through which Allah chose to reveal. The Qur’an commands belief in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers without dividing them (2:285). Faith accepts the whole chain of revelation according to the evidence Allah has provided.

### Revelation and the Living Heart

The Qur’an being revealed to the heart also explains why it transforms people. It is not merely an academic text. It addresses fear, desire, arrogance, grief, power, and death. A sincere reader does not ask only whether the words are eloquent; he asks what they reveal about Allah and what they require of him. The heart becomes alive when it receives the Qur’an with humility and acts upon it.

The believer should ask Allah to protect him from treating revelation as a cultural object. The Qur’an was sent to guide and give good news, but guidance requires the heart’s participation. The same recitation that softens one person can anger another when it challenges his pride. The difference is not a defect in the Book; it is the response of the listener.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:98, 26:193–195, and 81:19–21 on Gabriel’s role as the trustworthy bearer of revelation.
- Qur’an 5:44–48 on the Qur’an confirming previous scripture and serving as a criterion.
- Qur’an 2:136 and 2:285 on believing in Allah, the angels, the Books, and all the messengers.
- Qur’an 16:102 and 42:52 on revelation coming by Allah’s permission and giving life and guidance.
- Qur’an 66:6 on the angels’ obedience.
- For the historical context and the relation between hostility to Gabriel and rejection of the Qur’an, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:97.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:98

> Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, and Michael, then ˹let them know that˺ Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers.

**Commentary:**

### No Selective Hostility Toward Revelation

The previous verse addressed hostility toward Gabriel. This verse broadens the principle: whoever treats Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, or Michael as enemies has placed himself in opposition to the entire divine order. The names of Gabriel and Michael are mentioned specifically because some people attempted to divide the heavenly messengers according to their preferences. The Qur’an rejects this selective approach.

Angels are not competing deities or independent powers. They are servants who carry out Allah’s commands, including revelation, provision, recording deeds, and other functions disclosed by revelation. Gabriel is associated especially with conveying revelation, while Michael is mentioned in the Qur’an by name here; further details about his functions should not be asserted beyond reliable evidence. Hostility toward either angel because of the task Allah assigned him is hostility toward Allah’s command.

The same principle applies to the messengers. A person cannot claim to believe in Allah while rejecting the prophets Allah sent because their message is inconvenient. The Qur’an says that those who say they believe in some messengers and reject others are disbelievers in truth (4:150–151). Genuine faith honours all authentic messengers while following the final messenger sent for the final community.

### “Allah Is the Enemy of the Disbelievers”

The closing expression describes divine judgment, punishment, and opposition to those who knowingly reject the truth. Allah is not “an enemy” in the human sense of emotional hostility or personal rivalry. He is the Lord who judges disbelievers justly, withdraws His approval from their rejection, and brings their rebellion to its consequence. Qur’anic expressions about Allah’s anger, hatred, or enmity are understood in a manner befitting His perfection.

The verse does not mean that Allah wrongs people or condemns them without evidence. The Qur’an says that messengers were sent so that people would have no argument against Allah after the message reached them (4:165). Divine opposition is therefore a response to deliberate rejection, not arbitrary hostility toward people whose circumstances are unknown.

The phrase also reverses the illusion of power. A person may announce enmity toward Gabriel or a messenger as though he controls the religious order. In reality, no creature can injure Allah or weaken His dominion. The Qur’an says that if all people disbelieved, Allah would be independent of them, while their disbelief would harm only themselves (14:8). The “enemy” is therefore the one who has chosen a position that places him against divine truth, not someone who can threaten Allah.

### The Unity of Faith

The verse supports the integrated nature of Islamic belief. Faith includes Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers (2:285). It cannot be divided according to personal preference. A person who accepts Allah but rejects revelation, accepts Moses but rejects Jesus, or accepts the Qur’an but rejects the messenger who conveyed it has not embraced the whole structure of faith.

This unity also protects the dignity of prophets and angels from rivalrous religious systems. Muslims do not worship Gabriel, Michael, Jesus, Moses, or Muhammad (peace be upon them). They honour them as servants and messengers of Allah. Worship belongs to Allah alone, while love and respect for His messengers arise from obedience to Him.

### The Ethical Meaning of the Warning

Hostility to revelation is often expressed through mockery, distortion, or selective obedience rather than open declarations of hatred. A person may claim respect for Allah while attacking the command He revealed, or praise one prophet while rejecting another because of social prejudice. The believer looks beyond slogans to the actual response to truth.

The warning also applies to those who use the names of angels or messengers to support superstition, political manipulation, or fabricated religious claims. Honour for revelation requires evidence. The Qur’an should not be made to serve a story that Allah and His messengers did not disclose.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:97 and 2:285 on Gabriel, the angels, Books, and messengers within one faith.
- Qur’an 4:136 and 4:150–152 on believing in all elements of revelation without selective rejection.
- Qur’an 4:165 on the establishment of proof through messengers.
- Qur’an 14:8 and 35:15 on Allah’s independence from creation and the harm of disbelief returning to the disbelievers.
- Qur’an 66:6 on angels carrying out Allah’s commands without disobedience.
- For the historical context of hostility toward Gabriel and Michael and the meaning of divine “enmity,” see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:98.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:99

> Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious.

**Commentary:**

### Clear Revelations

Allah tells the Prophet (ﷺ) that He has sent down clear revelations—*āyāt bayyināt*. The Qur’an is not presented as an intentionally obscure text whose central message is inaccessible. Its core call to worship Allah alone, believe in revelation, practise justice, repent, and prepare for the Hereafter is clear. Its language, signs, arguments, narratives, and commands provide sufficient proof for a sincere seeker.

Clarity does not mean that every verse has only one possible interpretation or that no scholarly study is needed. The Qur’an contains verses requiring language, context, prophetic explanation, and specialized knowledge. It also distinguishes clear foundational verses from matters that may be less immediately apparent (3:7). The clarity affirmed here concerns the reality and authority of the revelation, not the removal of every interpretive question.

Allah’s signs include the Qur’anic text, the Prophet’s character, its coherence, its moral and spiritual effect, its signs in creation and history, and the fulfilment of its guidance. The Qur’an says that it is a guidance and healing for believers (17:82), and that Allah will show people His signs in the horizons and within themselves until the truth becomes clear (41:53). The evidence is abundant, though people differ in whether they respond with humility.

### Rejection as Rebellion

The verse says that only the rebellious—*fāsiqūn*—deny the clear revelations. *Fisq* means departing from the proper boundary. The description emphasizes that denial is often moral before it is intellectual. A person may have enough evidence to recognize a claim but reject it because it threatens desire, identity, power, or inherited loyalty. Rebellion uses doubt as a shield rather than as a sincere path toward knowledge.

This does not mean that every person who has a question or is not yet convinced is a rebellious disbeliever. The Qur’an encourages honest reflection, consultation, and seeking evidence. A sincere seeker may hesitate while investigating. The condemnation concerns deliberate denial after clarity—treating truth as an enemy because obedience is unwelcome.

The same distinction protects the integrity of religious discussion. Believers should not label every critic or questioner as rebellious, nor should they pretend that rejection after clear evidence is morally neutral. They must explain with wisdom, provide evidence, and leave the unseen judgment of hearts to Allah.

### Clear Signs and Human Choice

The Qur’an’s clarity does not compel every person mechanically. Signs can be accepted, ignored, distorted, or mocked. The opening of the sūrah already showed that the same revelation becomes guidance for the mindful and a source of argument for the disbelieving. The difference lies in the response of the heart.

The Qur’an gives the example of the people of Makkah, who knew the Prophet’s trustworthiness yet accused him of fabrication when his message challenged their authority. It says that Allah knows they do not truly call him a liar; rather, the wrongdoers reject Allah’s signs (6:33). The issue was not simply uncertainty about his character. It was unwillingness to accept the consequences of the truth.

A person should therefore ask Allah for a heart that responds to clear evidence. Knowledge can be blocked by pride, but humility makes understanding fruitful. The believer recites the prayer, “Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us” (3:8), recognizing that steadfastness is a gift.

### The Prophet’s Responsibility

The verse also consoles the Prophet. His duty was to convey clear revelation, not to force every person to accept it. Rejection by the rebellious does not make the message unclear or the messenger unsuccessful. The Qur’an says, “Your duty is only to convey clearly” (16:82), and “you are not over them a controller” (88:22).

This is important for anyone who teaches truth. A parent, scholar, or caller may explain with patience and kindness, but he cannot control another person’s heart. He should not distort the message to make it more popular, nor should he despair when some people reject it. Clarity, sincerity, and good character are his responsibility; guidance belongs to Allah.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:7 on clear foundational verses and matters requiring deeper interpretation.
- Qur’an 6:33 and 17:101 on rejecting signs despite recognition and evidence.
- Qur’an 16:82 and 88:21–22 on the messenger’s duty to convey rather than control hearts.
- Qur’an 17:82 and 41:53 on the Qur’an’s healing and the signs in the horizons and within people.
- Qur’an 3:8 on praying for steadfastness after guidance.
- For *āyāt bayyināt*, *fisq*, and the relation between clarity and moral rejection, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:99.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:100

> Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? In fact, most of them do not believe.

**Commentary:**

### Covenant After Covenant

The verse asks why a group of them repeatedly casts aside its covenant. The question gathers together the earlier examples: the covenant of worship, the command to hold firmly to scripture, the prohibition against bloodshed and expulsion, the responsibility to preserve truth, and the obligation to recognize the messengers Allah sent. The problem is not the absence of agreements. It is the habit of treating agreements as temporary instruments that can be abandoned when they become inconvenient.

A covenant is more than a verbal promise. It creates a relationship of trust and imposes duties upon the parties. Allah took covenants from the Children of Israel through their prophets and scripture, and they publicly witnessed those commitments. The Qur’an says that covenants will be questioned (17:34), while it commands believers to fulfil what they have pledged (16:91). Repeatedly breaking a covenant damages the moral fabric of a community.

The verse refers to a group, not every Israelite. The Qur’an’s careful language is important: it describes widespread failure without erasing the upright individuals among the People of the Book (3:113–115). The final statement that “most of them do not believe” is a judgment about the dominant response being addressed, not a licence to assign disbelief to every person of a named community.

### Rejection of Truth After Recognition

Casting aside a covenant often begins with rejecting its implications. A person may accept a scripture as a respected object but reject its command when it challenges his wealth or authority. He may honour a prophet in theory but oppose the next messenger because the messenger comes from an unexpected people. He may promise justice in public and then support oppression when his group benefits. The Qur’an treats these contradictions as evidence of deficient faith.

Faith in the Qur’an is not only verbal assent. It includes trust in Allah, acceptance of His messengers, and willingness to obey. The Qur’an says that the believers respond, “We hear and obey” (2:285), while those who repeatedly break covenants show that their claims have not reached sincere submission. A believer can fail and repent, but he does not make covenant-breaking a settled policy.

The phrase “every time” emphasizes repetition. A single lapse may arise from weakness or misunderstanding; repeated rejection after reminders reveals a pattern. Each broken promise makes the next one easier and teaches the community that words have no binding force. This is why the Qur’an condemns treachery even when the other side is disliked and commands that agreements be honoured or ended openly rather than betrayed (8:58).

### The Social Cost of Broken Covenants

When communities cannot trust promises, peace becomes fragile. Contracts are doubted, ceasefires become traps, families fear one another, and public institutions lose legitimacy. The Qur’an’s covenantal language therefore has social as well as spiritual significance. Worshipping Allah requires honesty in agreements, protection of life, fulfilment of trusts, and fair treatment of opponents.

The verse is especially relevant to political and religious leadership. Leaders may invoke scripture to secure loyalty and then abandon its restrictions once they possess power. Institutions may publish ethical codes while rewarding the opposite behaviour. A community may demand that others keep agreements while excusing its own violations. The Qur’an rejects this double standard. Covenants are obligations before Allah, not tools for managing public relations.

The command to fulfil agreements does not require obedience to a promise to commit sin or oppression. A covenant that violates Allah’s commands cannot make wrongdoing lawful. But even then, the correct response is transparent withdrawal, repentance, and repair—not secret treachery. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that signs of hypocrisy include breaking promises and betraying trusts (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

### Most of Them Do Not Believe

The final assessment returns to belief. The repeated covenant-breaking, rejection of prophets, concealment of scripture, and selective obedience reveal that most of the group being addressed lacks genuine faith. This does not mean that faith requires flawless performance. It means that a person who persistently rejects the foundation of the covenant cannot claim that religious identity alone proves belief.

The statement also encourages believers to examine their own covenants. Muslims make promises to Allah in the testimony of faith, in prayer, in contracts, in marriage, in public office, and in private repentance. A person should not repeat the words of commitment while deliberately planning to violate them. If he fails, he should return quickly, fulfil what can be fulfilled, restore rights, and ask Allah for help.

The chapter’s message is therefore universal. Revelation brings guidance, but guidance creates a covenant. Favour brings honour, but honour creates responsibility. A community is not protected by possessing a scripture if it repeatedly casts aside what the scripture commands. True belief is shown by truthful adherence when obedience is difficult.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:40, 2:63–64, 2:83–85, and 5:12 on the covenants taken from the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book and the importance of avoiding blanket judgments.
- Qur’an 8:56–58 on repeated treachery, clear warning, and ending agreements openly rather than betraying them.
- Qur’an 16:91 and 17:34 on fulfilling covenants and being accountable for promises.
- Qur’an 2:285 on the believing response: “We hear and obey.”
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on breaking promises and betraying trusts as signs of hypocrisy.
