## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:181

> Whoever changes a bequest after hearing it, the sin is only upon those who change it. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### Protecting the Last Will

The previous āyah prescribed a fair bequest when death approaches. This verse protects that bequest from later manipulation. Whoever hears a valid will and changes it unjustly carries the sin of the alteration. The responsibility may fall upon a witness, executor, relative, lawyer, or any other person who knowingly replaces the deceased person’s instruction with something that serves his own interest.

The verse does not forbid correcting a mistake, clarifying an ambiguity, or refusing an instruction that violates Allah’s inheritance law. It condemns deliberate alteration of what was valid and known. A will is a trust, and the people who handle it must remember that the person who wrote it may no longer be present to defend his rights.

### The Conditions of a Valid Bequest

A bequest must be lawful in its subject, fair in its amount, and consistent with the fixed shares Allah later detailed in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ (4:11–12, 4:176). A person cannot use a will to deprive an heir of his or her divinely assigned share, nor can an executor honour a forbidden instruction simply because it appears in writing. The Sunnah states that Allah has given each heir his right and that there is no bequest for an heir except with the consent of the other heirs.

Legal systems and juristic schools differ over documentation, witnesses, and the treatment of disputed instructions. The basic moral rule is constant: preserve the truth of the deceased person’s lawful intention, and do not use technical knowledge to steal from the vulnerable.

### Sin Belongs to the Alterer

The statement “the sin is only upon those who change it” distinguishes the deceased person from the one who commits the fraud. If the original bequest was honest and lawful, the person who wrote it is not blamed for another’s betrayal. This is part of Allah’s justice: each person bears his own deed, and no one is punished for a wrong he did not commit.

The principle does not excuse the testator from writing carefully. Ambiguous wording can produce conflict, and negligence may contribute to harm. A responsible person records debts, identifies assets, names trustworthy executors, and seeks qualified advice. Yet deliberate falsification remains the sin of the falsifier.

### The Trust of Witnesses and Executors

Witnessing a will is not a ceremonial favour. A witness may be called to testify about what was said, the condition of the testator, and the authenticity of the document. He must not conceal relevant information, accept a bribe, or change his testimony to please a powerful heir. The Qur’an commands that testimony be upheld for Allah even against oneself and relatives (4:135).

An executor, *waṣī*, must administer the estate with *amānah*. He should settle debts, protect minors, distribute lawful shares, and keep transparent records. Taking a hidden commission, delaying distribution to pressure heirs, or favouring one branch of the family turns guardianship into betrayal.

### Allah Hears and Knows

Allah is *Samīʿ* and *ʿAlīm*. He hears the spoken will, the secret agreement to change it, the false oath, and the silent plea of the heir whose share is being stolen. He knows the intention behind every amendment and every claim that appears legally polished. Human courts may miss deception, but no action escapes divine knowledge.

This ending is a protection for the powerless. An orphan or widow may not be able to challenge a sophisticated fraud, but Allah knows what happened. It is also a warning to those who think family pressure or social respectability can hide injustice.

### Repair after Alteration

If a person has changed a bequest unjustly, repentance requires restoring the lawful instruction or compensating those harmed. Private regret is not enough when property has been taken from identifiable people. The wrongdoer should disclose the truth, return assets, and seek forgiveness without placing pressure on the victims.

The community can prevent such harm through clear written wills, independent witnesses, accessible legal advice, and education about Islamic inheritance. These practical measures are part of protecting the trust Allah has placed in families.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:180–182 on bequests, alteration, reconciliation, and Allah’s mercy.
- Qur’an 4:11–14 and 4:176 on fixed inheritance shares and the limits set by Allah.
- Qur’an 4:58 and 4:135 on returning trusts and truthful testimony.
- Qur’an 5:106–108 on witnesses in bequests and the danger of concealment.
- Sunan Abī Dāwūd and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, the hadith “There is no bequest for an heir,” and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the duty to write a will.
- For the meaning of altering a bequest, the scope of the sin, and the responsibility of witnesses and executors, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:181.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:182

> But whoever fears from a testator an error or injustice, then brings about a reconciliation between them, there is no sin upon him. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Reforming a Harmful Bequest

This verse makes an important exception to the prohibition against changing a bequest. If someone fears that the testator has made an error or acted unjustly, and he brings reconciliation between the concerned parties, he is not sinful for correcting the matter. The goal is not to honour a document mechanically when doing so would violate Allah’s law or cause clear harm. The goal is to preserve justice and the real purpose of the bequest.

The person who intervenes must be motivated by *iṣlāḥ*, repair, not by desire to divert the estate toward himself. He should establish what the testator intended, identify the rights of heirs and creditors, and seek a lawful solution. Reconciliation does not mean secretly replacing a will; it means correcting injustice transparently and with proper evidence.

### Error, Injustice, and Ambiguity

An error may be a mistaken name, an impossible condition, a misunderstanding of the law, or an instruction that becomes impossible to carry out. Injustice may include depriving an heir, favouring one child because of anger, assigning more than the lawful charitable limit, or directing property toward something forbidden. A will can be validly written yet contain an element that must be corrected.

The phrase *janaf* often refers to unintentional deviation or error, while *ithm* refers to deliberate sin. The verse covers both. People may make mistakes through illness, pressure, ignorance, or confused speech, and others may need to protect the estate from the effects. Allah knows the difference between a genuine mistake and calculated fraud.

### Reconciliation Requires Knowledge

Not every person who suspects unfairness is qualified to alter a bequest. The mediator should consult the Qur’an, authentic Sunnah, competent scholars, legal experts, and all affected parties. He should hear both sides and avoid relying on gossip. A sincere intention does not make an uninformed intervention just.

The process should be as transparent as possible. The family can document the disagreement, preserve evidence, protect minors, and seek a trustworthy adjudicator. If a dispute concerns a fixed inheritance share, no private preference may cancel what Allah has prescribed. If it concerns a voluntary bequest to a non-heir, the parties can negotiate within the lawful limits.

### Reconciliation Is Not Self-Serving

A person who changes the will to obtain a larger share cannot claim this verse as cover. The correction must serve justice between the parties, not the mediator’s appetite. If the mediator is himself an heir, an independent adviser or court is especially important. Hidden alteration creates the very sin condemned in the previous āyah.

The Qur’an praises reconciliation in many contexts, but it never calls injustice reconciliation. A settlement that forces the weak to surrender their rights, protects an abuser, or hides a crime may produce outward peace while deepening the wrong. True *iṣlāḥ* restores rights and reduces harm through truth.

### Forgiveness and Mercy

Allah closes with His forgiveness and mercy. This gives hope to the testator who made an error and to the person who intervenes imperfectly while sincerely seeking repair. It also calls families to show mercy. Not every disagreement requires litigation; relatives may voluntarily accommodate one another after each person’s right is known and protected.

Mercy does not erase the rules of inheritance. It makes people willing to implement them without spite and to give additional gifts or relinquish a claim voluntarily after receiving it. A person cannot call coercion generosity. Consent must be real, informed, and free from threat.

### A Broader Principle of Reform

The verse teaches a general principle: preserving the purpose of a command can require correcting a harmful application of its outward form. Documents, institutions, and customs are means; justice and obedience to Allah are the ends. At the same time, people must not invoke “higher purpose” whenever a clear rule frustrates their preference. Reform is guided by revelation, evidence, and trustworthiness.

The Muslim community should train mediators who combine legal knowledge with compassion. They can prevent family disputes from becoming permanent hostility and protect the rights of children, widows, the elderly, and relatives who lack social power.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:180–182 on bequests, alteration, correction, and reconciliation.
- Qur’an 4:35 and 49:9–10 on mediation, reconciliation, and restoring justice.
- Qur’an 4:11–14 and 5:8 on fixed rights, Allah’s limits, and justice.
- Qur’an 8:1 and 16:90 on restoring disputes to Allah and acting with justice and excellence.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on reconciliation and the prohibition of taking another person’s wealth without consent.
- For *janaf*, *ithm*, *iṣlāḥ*, and the conditions for correcting an unjust bequest, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:182.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:183

> O believers, fasting has been prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may become mindful of Allah.

**Commentary:**

### The Purpose of Fasting

Allah announces the obligation of fasting with the call “O believers.” This address honours faith while also preparing the community for a demanding act of worship. Fasting—*ṣawm* or *ṣiyām*—is abstaining from food, drink, and the other things that invalidate the fast from dawn until sunset, with an intention for Allah. Its purpose is not hunger for its own sake, but *taqwā*: conscious obedience, self-restraint, and awareness of Allah.

The verse does not say simply that fasting makes a person hungry. It says “so that you may become mindful.” Fasting trains the servant to leave a lawful desire at a lawful time because Allah commanded it. That training can then restrain him from unlawful desires when no human observer is present. The hidden nature of fasting makes it an especially strong school of sincerity.

### Continuity with Earlier Revelation

Fasting was prescribed to communities before the Muslims. The details may have differed, but the spiritual principle is ancient: human beings need disciplined abstention, remembrance, and gratitude. Islam therefore does not present fasting as an alien invention. It restores and completes a practice found in the prophetic heritage.

This continuity honours earlier messengers while establishing the distinct legislation given to Muhammad ﷺ. Muslims do not assume that every religious practice currently attributed to previous communities remains unchanged, but they recognize that revelation has always cultivated self-control and devotion.

### Taqwā and the Inner Self

*Taqwā* comes from the idea of protecting oneself. The fasting person builds a protective barrier between his soul and disobedience. He experiences thirst but does not drink, hunger but does not eat, and desire but waits for Allah’s permission. The heart learns that not every urge must be obeyed immediately.

This restraint is not intended to make the body an enemy. Islam recognizes bodily needs and permits nourishment after sunset and throughout the allowed hours. Fasting teaches the body its place under worship rather than declaring that physical life is evil. The best fasting produces a calmer appetite, a more grateful heart, and greater compassion for those who face hunger without choice.

### The Moral Fast

The Prophet ﷺ warned that whoever does not abandon false speech and acting upon it, Allah has no need for him to abandon food and drink, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. This does not remove the legal fast, but it warns that physical abstention without moral restraint may leave the spiritual purpose unrealized.

The tongue must fast from lying, insults, backbiting, and quarrelling; the eyes from what is forbidden; the hands from harm; and the mind from nursing envy. If anger is provoked, the fasting person says, “I am fasting,” and refuses to answer evil with evil. Fasting is therefore a whole-person discipline.

### Fasting and Compassion

A person who experiences hunger voluntarily can better understand the vulnerable, though he must not assume that one month of fasting equals the suffering of those facing chronic poverty. The ethical response is charity, feeding people, fair trade, and reform of conditions that create hunger. The Prophet ﷺ was especially generous in Ramadan, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī.

Fasting also reduces the heart’s dependence on consumption. A culture that measures happiness by endless acquisition needs this discipline. The believer learns that contentment, prayer, family, knowledge, and service can nourish the soul in ways that material excess cannot.

### The Community and the Individual

The plural wording addresses the community because fasting has a communal rhythm. Families share the pre-dawn meal, break the fast together, pray at night, and help those in need. Yet the intention and accountability remain personal. No one can fast on behalf of another or borrow another’s sincerity.

The communal practice should not create hardship for the poor, the ill, or those with legitimate exemptions. The next verses explain concessions, showing that Allah’s command is paired with mercy and knowledge of human capacity.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:183–187 on the obligation, purpose, concessions, timing, and spiritual limits of fasting.
- Qur’an 19:26 on Maryam’s abstention from speech and the wider prophetic history of disciplined restraint.
- Qur’an 33:35 on fasting men and women and the rewards of devotion.
- Qur’an 7:31 and 17:27 on moderation and avoiding waste in consumption.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith qudsī “Fasting is for Me and I shall reward for it,” and the hadith warning against false speech while fasting.
- For *ṣawm*, *ṣiyām*, *taqwā*, and the continuity of fasting in earlier communities, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:183.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:184

> Fasting is for a prescribed number of days. But whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then let him fast an equal number of days later. For those who can only fast with great difficulty, there is a ransom: feeding a needy person. Whoever does extra good, it is better for him, and fasting is better for you, if only you knew.

**Commentary:**

### A Measured Obligation

Fasting is prescribed for a limited number of days, not as a permanent burden. The wording prepares the believer for a duty with a clear beginning and end. Islam does not ask the body to abandon nourishment indefinitely. It disciplines appetite within a defined period so that the servant can worship with steadiness rather than destructive excess.

The verse also reveals the law’s attention to capacity. The sick and the traveller are permitted to delay the fast and make up the same number later. Illness is assessed according to whether fasting worsens the condition, delays recovery, or causes serious hardship, and a trustworthy medical opinion can help. Travel has legal details in the Sunnah and fiqh; the concession is not a licence for casual inconvenience, but neither should a traveller be shamed for using the mercy Allah granted.

### Making Up Missed Days

The phrase *faʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhar* requires an equal number of days to be fasted later. The obligation is not cancelled; it is transferred to a time when the person can fulfil it. A person should make up the fasts before the next Ramadan when reasonably able, while someone whose illness is permanent has a different ruling explained in the juristic tradition.

The requirement of equal days reflects justice and mercy. The person does not owe more because he was ill, and he does not lose the worship because his circumstances temporarily changed. Allah values the intention to obey and provides a lawful route to completion.

### The Ransom for Inability

The verse mentions a ransom—*fid-yah*—for those who can only fast with great difficulty. Classical scholars discussed the scope of this category, especially in relation to the early stages of the fasting legislation and to people with permanent inability. The established legal application includes an elderly person or one with a chronic condition who cannot safely fast and cannot reasonably expect recovery; such a person feeds a needy person for each day, according to the relevant school and local guidance.

A temporary illness is generally treated by making up the fast later, while a permanent inability calls for feeding rather than a physically harmful fast. People should not impose a universal ruling on medical situations without considering the person’s condition. Allah does not require worship that causes serious harm.

### “Whoever Does Extra Good”

The phrase *fa-man taṭawwaʿa khayran* has been interpreted in several related ways: whoever voluntarily feeds more than the minimum, gives additional charity, or undertakes the fast despite being capable only with difficulty in the earlier legal context. The general principle is that voluntary generosity is rewarded, but it must not contradict the concessions Allah has provided.

Extra good includes feeding more than one needy person, providing nutritious meals, supporting a fasting family, or helping someone make up missed obligations. Charity should preserve dignity. It is better to offer good food without reminding the recipient of the favour or making the poor perform gratitude before people.

### Fasting Is Better for Those Who Can

“Fasting is better for you, if only you knew” encourages those who are able to fulfil the ordinary obligation rather than searching for excuses. It does not condemn the sick or traveller who takes the concession. The verse places encouragement alongside mercy: the healthy resident fasts; the person with a legitimate excuse delays or feeds according to his condition.

The believer should avoid both extremes. One extreme treats every discomfort as permission to abandon the fast. The other treats illness, pregnancy, old age, or serious medical danger as a test of religious toughness. The Prophet ﷺ sometimes fasted while travelling and sometimes broke his fast, teaching that the lawful concession is part of obedience.

### Faith and Bodily Care

Fasting requires care for health. The pre-dawn meal, hydration during the permitted hours, moderation at the evening meal, and attention to medical advice help the servant worship responsibly. The Qur’an does not praise self-harm. The Prophet ﷺ said that the body has a right over the person, and this right remains during Ramadan.

Care is especially important for diabetes, pregnancy, breastfeeding, chronic illness, physically demanding work, and mental health conditions. A person should ask a reliable scholar and qualified clinician. Religious commitment is not measured by ignoring genuine danger.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:183–185 on fasting, making up missed days, fidyah, and Allah’s ease.
- Qur’an 4:29 and 2:195 on not harming oneself and not casting oneself into destruction.
- Qur’an 22:78 and 64:16 on capacity and the absence of undue hardship.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports that the Prophet ﷺ sometimes fasted and sometimes broke the fast while travelling, and the hadith that Allah loves His concessions to be taken as He dislikes disobedience.
- Sunan Abī Dāwūd and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, reports on the elderly and the traveller, considered with the juristic rulings on fidyah and qaḍāʾ.
- For *fid-yah*, *qaḍāʾ*, illness, travel, and the interpretations of “whoever does extra good,” see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:184.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:185

> Ramadan is the month in which the Qur’an was revealed as guidance for humanity, with clear proofs of guidance and a criterion. So whoever witnesses the month must fast it; but whoever is ill or travelling must fast an equal number of days later. Allah intends ease for you, not hardship, so that you may complete the prescribed period, glorify Allah for guiding you, and be grateful.

**Commentary:**

### Ramadan and the Revelation

Ramadan is honoured because it is the month in which the Qur’an was revealed. The revelation is described as guidance—*hudā*—clear proofs that illuminate the path, and *furqān*, a criterion that distinguishes truth from falsehood. The month is therefore not merely a season of hunger, family gatherings, or cultural celebration. Its centre is the Book that teaches humanity how to know Allah and live rightly.

The Qur’an was sent down in Ramadan, and the first revelation came in the Night of Decree (97:1). The revelation’s descent and the believer’s fasting belong together: the body is restrained, the heart is opened, and the community returns to the message. Ramadan should increase recitation, reflection, charity, prayer, and moral repair rather than only changing meal times.

### The Obligation to Fast the Month

“Whoever witnesses the month must fast it.” A person who is present and able when Ramadan begins undertakes the fast from dawn to sunset. The beginning and end of the month are established through the recognized methods of sighting and reliable calendrical calculation discussed by scholars. The practical details should be handled by trustworthy communal authorities so that worship does not become a source of constant suspicion and division.

The obligation concerns the days of the month, while the person’s capacity remains relevant. Illness and travel are explicitly repeated from the preceding verse. A sick person delays the fast when fasting causes serious harm or prevents recovery; a traveller may take the concession according to the legal conditions. Both the fast and the lawful postponement are acts of obedience.

### Allah Intends Ease

“Allah intends ease for you, not hardship”—*yurīdu Allāhu bikum al-yusr wa-lā yurīdu bikum al-ʿusr*. This is one of the great legal principles of Islam. Ease does not mean that every obligation should be redesigned around comfort, nor does it permit people to dismiss a clear duty because it is inconvenient. It means that Allah has built concessions into the law and does not desire unbearable or destructive hardship.

The principle appears in the allowances for illness, travel, old age, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and other genuine conditions, with their detailed rulings. It also guides scholars when applying general commands to new circumstances. The believer does not make the religion needlessly severe, and he does not make ease an excuse for desire.

### Completing the Period

The command to complete the prescribed period shows that concessions are not abandonment. A person who misses days because of illness or travel makes up an equal number later when able. The period is completed according to the person’s circumstances, and Allah knows the sincerity of the servant who longs to fulfil the duty.

Completion also includes completing Ramadan’s ethical work. A person who fasts physically but persists in lies, oppression, broken promises, and contempt has missed the purpose of the month. The Prophet ﷺ warned that hunger alone is not the goal when the tongue and conduct remain corrupt. The month is completed through repentance and a change that continues after Eid.

### Glorifying Allah for Guidance

At the end of the fasting regulations, Allah commands believers to glorify Him—*li-tukabbirū Allāha*—for guiding them. The takbīr of Eid expresses that Allah is greater than appetite, wealth, fear, and every rival. It is a public declaration that the month’s success came from Allah and that the community’s joy is an act of worship.

Glorification is not limited to words. To magnify Allah is to give His command greater weight than social pressure and personal desire. A person who has learned patience, generosity, and Qur’anic discipline in Ramadan carries that magnification into the rest of the year.

### Gratitude as the Fruit

The verse ends with gratitude—*laʿallakum tashkurūn*. The believer thanks Allah for the Qur’an, for the ability to fast, for the mercy of the concessions, for family and community, and for the opportunity to repent. Gratitude is expressed in worship and in service to people who lack food, safety, or support.

Ramadan’s charity is not a substitute for justice throughout the year. Its spiritual generosity should inspire ongoing care for the poor, honest economic practice, and concern for the oppressed. The one who thanks Allah for guidance helps others find relief and clarity.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:183–185 on Ramadan, fasting, guidance, ease, and gratitude.
- Qur’an 97:1 and 44:3 on the Qur’an’s revelation in Ramadan and the Night of Decree.
- Qur’an 17:9 and 25:1 on the Qur’an as guidance and criterion.
- Qur’an 2:286, 22:78, and 4:28 on capacity and Allah’s intention of ease.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on fasting Ramadan with faith and hope of reward, and the hadith on sighting the new moon and completing the month when it is not seen.
- For *Ramadan*, *hudā*, *furqān*, *yusr*, and the relation between fasting and Qur’an, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:185.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:186

> When My servants ask you about Me, surely I am near. I respond to the call of the supplicant when he calls upon Me. So let them respond to Me and believe in Me, so that they may be rightly guided.

**Commentary:**

### Allah’s Nearness

This āyah appears in the middle of the fasting verses, but its message is universal. When people ask about Allah, He does not command the Messenger ﷺ to answer with a distant title or an elaborate intermediary. Allah speaks directly: “I am near.” His nearness is a nearness of knowledge, hearing, mercy, and response, not physical containment or likeness to creation. He knows the whispered prayer before the tongue completes it.

The verse gives special comfort during Ramadan, when hunger and worship expose the heart’s need. It also answers the anxious person who wonders whether his prayer travels through layers before reaching Allah. Allah hears directly. No priesthood or sacred human gatekeeper stands between the servant and his Lord.

### The Supplicant’s Call

“I respond to the call of the supplicant when he calls upon Me.” *Duʿāʾ* is both invocation and worship. It includes asking for needs, praising Allah, seeking forgiveness, confessing dependence, and turning to Him in every condition. The response may take the form requested, a protection from equivalent harm, or a reward stored for the Hereafter, according to the authentic hadith reported in Musnad Aḥmad and other collections.

A delayed or differently shaped response is not proof that Allah did not hear. The servant sees one moment; Allah sees the whole life and the consequences beyond death. The Prophet ﷺ warned against saying, “I supplicated but was not answered,” because impatience can cause a person to abandon the means of acceptance.

### Responding to Allah

The verse joins Allah’s response to the servants’ response: “Let them respond to Me and believe in Me.” The relationship is not a demand that Allah grant every wish while the servant ignores every command. Responding to Allah means obeying His revelation, accepting His limits, repenting after disobedience, and trusting His wisdom. The supplicant asks for guidance while walking in the direction Allah has revealed.

This does not mean that sinful or struggling people may not pray. They should pray especially because they need Allah. But they should not deliberately persist in injustice while treating supplication as a technique for obtaining worldly success. Repentance and lawful effort are part of answering Allah.

### Faith and Nearness

Belief in Allah—*yuʾminū bī*—includes trusting His promises, affirming His names, and knowing that He is capable of every response. Faith protects the supplication from becoming superstition. The believer does not worship a formula or assume that a particular human being controls divine acceptance. He calls upon Allah alone while using lawful means.

The verse’s closeness also corrects despair. A servant may feel distant because of sin, grief, or distraction, but Allah invites him to call. The first step back can be a simple confession: “My Lord, I need You.” Allah’s mercy is not earned by perfect wording.

### Supplication in Fasting

The fasting person experiences a direct relationship with Allah. He leaves food and drink privately for Allah, even when no human being could detect the violation. At the moment of breaking the fast, he has a special opportunity for supplication. The Prophet ﷺ taught words of praise at ifṭār and encouraged sincere invocation.

Ramadan’s nights also include prayer, Qur’an, and seeking forgiveness. These practices do not replace ordinary supplication during work, illness, family difficulty, or injustice. The believer carries the awareness of nearness beyond the month.

### Etiquette and Reasons for Response

The Sunnah teaches beginning with praise of Allah, sending blessings upon the Prophet ﷺ, asking with humility, seeking lawful provision, choosing appropriate times, and avoiding supplication for sin or the severing of kinship. The servant should persist without demanding a particular timetable. He should also make the effort to remove barriers such as unlawful income and deliberate wrongdoing.

These are not magical conditions that limit Allah. They are disciplines that align the heart with what it is asking. A person who asks for mercy should become merciful; one who asks for forgiveness should forgive and repair; one who asks for guidance should be willing to follow it.

### Guidance as the Result

The purpose clause—“so that they may be rightly guided”—shows that the highest answer to prayer is guidance itself. A person may ask for wealth and receive contentment, ask for escape and receive courage, or ask for a specific outcome and receive clarity about what Allah wants instead. Guidance enables him to use every outcome well.

The community should teach children and vulnerable people that they can call upon Allah directly. It should not exploit their needs through paid promises of guaranteed miracles. The one who knows Allah is near treats supplication as worship, not as a commercial product.

- Qur’an 50:16, 57:4, and 58:7 on Allah’s knowledge and nearness without likening Him to creation.
- Qur’an 40:60 and 27:62 on calling upon Allah and His promise to respond.
- Qur’an 17:110 and 7:55–56 on the etiquette of supplication.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith qudsī “If he remembers Me in himself, I remember him,” and the hadith warning against impatience in supplication; Musnad Aḥmad records the teaching that a supplication is answered in one of several beneficial ways.
- For *duʿāʾ*, *qarīb*, response, and the relation between supplication and obedience, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:186.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:187

> It has been made lawful for you, on the nights of the fast, to be intimate with your wives. They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them. Allah knew that you were betraying yourselves, so He turned to you in mercy and pardoned you. So now be intimate with them and seek what Allah has decreed for you. Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread of night. Then complete the fast until sunset. Do not be intimate with them while you are staying for worship in the mosques. These are the limits of Allah, so do not approach them. Thus Allah makes His revelations clear to people so that they may become mindful.

**Commentary:**

### Mercy in the Night of Ramadan

This āyah gives the detailed boundaries of the fast and begins with a permission: marital intimacy is lawful during the nights of Ramadan. Early Muslims understood an earlier restriction more narrowly and found it difficult; some nevertheless approached their spouses after sleeping or violated the intended limit. Allah knew their weakness, accepted their repentance, and revealed an easier rule. The verse therefore combines legal clarity with a beautiful picture of divine mercy.

Fasting is abstention during the day, not a rejection of marriage or bodily affection. Islam does not treat lawful intimacy as dirty. It places it within marriage, dignity, mutual rights, and the times Allah has permitted. The night becomes a time for nourishment, affection, rest, and family life, while the daylight fast remains a serious act of worship.

### Spouses as Garments

“They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them.” A garment covers, protects, warms, beautifies, and remains close to the body. The metaphor describes mutual intimacy, privacy, emotional shelter, and protection from temptation. Marriage in the Qur’an is not merely a legal arrangement for reproduction; it is a relationship of tranquillity, affection, and mercy (30:21).

The mutual wording rejects one-sided entitlement. Each spouse has a right to kindness, privacy, emotional consideration, and physical dignity. Intimacy must not be used as punishment, coercion, humiliation, or a weapon in an argument. The fasting regulations protect the relationship by placing it within lawful time and mutual responsibility.

### “Betraying Yourselves” and Divine Pardon

Allah knew that the believers were betraying themselves—*takhtānūna anfusakum*. The wording is compassionate but serious. By violating or struggling under a previous restriction, they had harmed themselves rather than harmed Allah. Allah knows the weakness of His servants before they confess it, and He opened a lawful route instead of leaving them trapped in guilt.

This is a model for repentance. A servant should not hide wrongdoing from Allah or defend it with excuses. He acknowledges the failure, stops it, and accepts the mercy of the revealed correction. Divine pardon does not mean the earlier command was meaningless; it means Allah legislated with knowledge of human capacity and guided the community to a more suitable rule.

### Seeking What Allah Has Decreed

“Seek what Allah has decreed for you” has been understood by the exegetes to include seeking children and lawful marital benefit, while some also emphasize seeking the reward Allah has written through lawful intimacy. The phrase directs desire toward what is wholesome and permitted. It does not support exploitation, adultery, or treating a spouse as an object.

The command also gives a broader principle: desire is not automatically sinful when directed through a lawful channel. Eating, drinking, sleep, affection, and sexual intimacy can all be acts of gratitude when the servant follows Allah’s boundaries and intends to preserve himself and fulfil legitimate rights.

### The Beginning of the Fast

“Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread of night.” The image refers to the first horizontal light of true dawn, *ṣubḥ ṣādiq*, not to literal threads placed beside one another. The Prophet ﷺ explained the verse to ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, clarifying that the white and black refer to the light of day and darkness of night, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

The fast begins at true dawn and continues until sunset. Local timetables should be based on reliable calculation and observation. When a person is genuinely uncertain, he follows the established timetable and the guidance of trustworthy local scholars rather than turning ordinary uncertainty into obsessive hardship.

### Completing the Fast until Sunset

The command “then complete the fast until sunset” establishes the end of the daily abstention. As soon as the sun has set, eating and drinking become lawful. The Sunnah encourages hastening the breaking of the fast once sunset is confirmed and delaying the pre-dawn meal until near dawn without entering uncertainty.

Completion means avoiding food, drink, and sexual intimacy during the fasting hours, along with guarding the moral fast. The Prophet ﷺ warned that lying and harmful conduct undermine the spiritual benefit even when the legal fast remains technically valid. The fast ends at sunset, but its lesson in self-control should continue throughout the night and the rest of the year.

### Iʿtikāf and Its Boundary

The verse prohibits marital intimacy while a person is engaged in *iʿtikāf*, a dedicated stay in the mosque for worship. Iʿtikāf is an intensified retreat for prayer, Qur’an, remembrance, and supplication. Its purpose is not to despise family life, but to give the servant a temporary period of concentrated devotion. The prohibition preserves the distinctive boundary of that retreat.

The Sunnah records that the Prophet ﷺ observed iʿtikāf during the last ten nights of Ramadan and that his wives visited him. Their visits demonstrate affection and lawful interaction, while the intimacy restriction remained. The person in iʿtikāf should follow the legal conditions concerning the mosque, duration, necessary needs, and invalidating actions according to qualified guidance.

### Do Not Approach the Limits

“These are the limits of Allah”—*ḥudūd Allāh*. The verse says not to approach them, not merely not to cross them. This teaches caution around situations that predictably lead to violation. A person who wants to protect the fast avoids private circumstances, media, conversations, and habits that make prohibited intimacy, food, drink, or anger likely.

The principle extends beyond Ramadan. The Qur’an tells believers not to approach adultery (17:32), because prevention includes closing the doors that lead toward it. Legal boundaries are not arbitrary obstacles; they protect worship, family, dignity, and the heart.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:188

> Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly, nor use it to bribe judges so that you may knowingly consume a portion of other people’s wealth sinfully.

**Commentary:**

### The Sanctity of Wealth

After discussing the fairness of bequests and the discipline of fasting, the Qur’an sets a broad rule for economic life: do not consume one another’s wealth through false or unlawful means. “Consuming” is wider than eating. It includes taking, using, controlling, or benefiting from property without a legitimate right. Wealth belongs to Allah ultimately, and people hold it as a trust governed by law and ethics.

The prohibition covers theft, fraud, embezzlement, deception in trade, exploitation of the vulnerable, breach of trust, false claims, unlawful gambling, bribery, interest in its prohibited forms, and any contract that is not based on genuine consent or a lawful subject. The Qur’an elsewhere permits trade by mutual agreement (4:29), showing that Islam does not condemn wealth or commerce; it condemns injustice in obtaining them.

### Bribing Authorities

The verse specifically mentions presenting wealth to authorities—*tudlū bihā ilā al-ḥukkām*—so that a person may consume another’s property. This includes bribing judges, officials, police, regulators, mediators, or anyone with power to alter a decision. A bribe can make falsehood appear lawful in court, but a judgement does not transform stolen wealth into a rightful possession before Allah.

The Prophet ﷺ cursed the briber and the one who takes the bribe, as reported in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and Sunan Abī Dāwūd. The condemnation protects the integrity of justice. If legal outcomes can be purchased, the poor lose access to their rights, the powerful become untouchable, and society’s trust collapses.

### “A Portion” Is Still Sinful

The verse says they knowingly consume a portion of another’s wealth. A person may not have taken the entire property; a single dishonest share is enough to be a grave sin. Someone who wins a case through a false witness, hidden evidence, or a paid official may receive only a fraction, but the fraction remains unlawful.

The word “knowingly” emphasizes moral responsibility. A person who genuinely makes an innocent mistake should correct it when it becomes clear. The one who knows the claim is false yet proceeds cannot hide behind a favourable legal ruling. The Prophet ﷺ warned that disputants may present arguments more persuasively than the other, and that a judgement in their favour does not make what they take lawful.

### Law and Morality Are Connected

Courts determine rights through human evidence and procedure, but Allah knows the underlying reality. A successful lawsuit is not a licence to take what a person knows belongs to another. Likewise, losing a case does not always mean that one’s moral claim was false; a person may need to seek appeal or another lawful remedy. The believer respects judicial process while remembering that divine judgement is more complete.

This principle applies to modern contracts and institutions. A company may comply with a technical rule while hiding a material fact from customers. An employer may use unequal bargaining power to take advantage of workers. A public official may direct contracts to friends. If the wealth is acquired through deception or abuse of trust, its outward paperwork does not make it *ḥalāl*.

### Honest Trade and Mutual Consent

The Qur’an permits trade by mutual consent, but consent must be informed and free from coercion and fraud. A seller discloses defects, uses accurate weights, honours warranties, and does not manipulate a desperate buyer. A buyer pays what was agreed and does not exploit a seller’s mistake. Both parties remain accountable to Allah after the document is signed.

The Prophet ﷺ passed by a pile of food and put his hand into it, finding hidden wet grain underneath. He said, “Whoever deceives us is not one of us,” as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith makes transparency a religious obligation, not merely a commercial preference.

### Bribery and the Public Good

Bribery is especially destructive because it converts public authority into private property. A person may pay to obtain a licence, avoid a penalty, win public land, secure a job, or defeat a rightful claimant. Even when the payment is described as a “gift,” its timing and purpose may reveal that it is a bribe.

There is a legal distinction between a person who pays under coercion to recover a right or avoid oppression and the person who pays to obtain what he has no right to receive. The exact judgement requires qualified advice and evidence. The general rule remains: no one may purchase injustice, and officials may not demand payment for duties they are already obliged to perform.

### Repentance and Restitution

A person who has consumed unlawful wealth must repent and return what can be returned. If the owner is known, restitution is required. If the money cannot be returned directly, the person should seek qualified guidance about disposing of it in a way that removes the unlawful benefit, while not treating charity as a shortcut that excuses deliberate theft. Public institutions should investigate corruption and recover stolen funds.

The fasting passage reminds us that worship and wealth are connected. A person cannot use prayer, fasting, or pilgrimage to cleanse a property right that he refuses to restore. The rights of people require repair.

- Qur’an 4:29, 4:58, and 5:8 on trade by consent, returning trusts, and justice.
- Qur’an 83:1–3 and 55:7–9 on honest measures, balance, and economic fairness.
- Qur’an 5:90 and 24:4 on gambling, false accusation, and the social harms of unlawful gain.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “Whoever deceives us is not one of us,” and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and Sunan Abī Dāwūd, the hadith condemning the briber and the one who accepts the bribe.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:189

> They ask you about the new moons. Say, “They are measurements of time for people and for Hajj.” Righteousness is not entering houses from the back; rather, righteousness is the way of one who is mindful of Allah. Enter houses through their doors, and be mindful of Allah so that you may succeed.

**Commentary:**

### The Moon as a Measure of Time

The people asked the Prophet ﷺ about the changing shapes of the moon. The answer directs attention to its practical purpose: the lunar phases are measures of time—*mawāqīt*—for people and for Hajj. The new moon marks the beginning of a lunar month, and its phases provide a visible calendar for fasting, pilgrimage, waiting periods, contracts, and other acts of worship.

The verse does not reject scientific curiosity about the moon’s physical causes. It answers the question according to the guidance people most need: how the signs of creation regulate human life and worship. The Qur’an frequently invites observation, but it also reminds people that knowledge should serve gratitude and obedience rather than vanity.

### A Lunar Calendar and Shared Worship

The Islamic calendar is tied to lunar observation. Ramadan, Hajj, ʿId, and the waiting period of a divorced or widowed woman are connected to months and phases. This allows people in different climates and nations to share a ritual calendar without depending upon one political empire or a single agricultural season.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the month begins with sighting the crescent and that if it is obscured, the preceding month is completed as thirty days, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Scholars have discussed the role of calculation, regional sightings, and communal authority. The verse’s purpose is unity and reliable timekeeping, not endless conflict over methods.

### A Strange Custom Corrected

The verse then mentions a custom of entering houses from their backs during pilgrimage or after entering consecration. Reports from early Muslims explain that some people regarded this as a sign of piety. Allah corrects the superstition: righteousness is not found in an invented ritual. “Enter houses through their doors” restores ordinary good sense and lawful practice.

The lesson is broader than the physical doorway. People often attach holiness to unnecessary difficulty, unusual clothing, secret formulas, or inherited gestures while neglecting justice and obedience. Revelation distinguishes genuine worship from religious performance that has no evidence.

### The Door as a Metaphor

The command to use doors also carries an ethical meaning. A person should approach matters through their proper means: knowledge through learning, reconciliation through honest conversation, wealth through lawful work, and private spaces through permission. Trying to enter through the back can symbolize evasion, intrusion, manipulation, or seeking a result without accepting the lawful process.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that asking permission is required before entering homes and that a person should not look inside when permission is refused, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Physical privacy and moral transparency are both protected by the principle.

### Taqwā Is the Substance of Righteousness

“Righteousness is the way of one who is mindful of Allah.” The verse echoes 2:177, where *birr* was defined through faith, charity, prayer, promises, and patience. Here, the same term corrects an outward custom. Taqwā is not a theatrical display of hardship; it is protecting oneself from Allah’s displeasure through truthful obedience.

A Muslim should therefore ask whether a practice has evidence and whether it produces justice, humility, and mercy. If a person enters through the door but lies, steals, or violates privacy, he has observed the outer instruction while missing its moral purpose. The physical and spiritual meanings should support each other.

### Asking the Right Question

The question about the moon may also teach that people’s questions are sometimes redirected toward their deeper need. Human beings ask about appearance, measurement, and unusual phenomena; revelation answers with worship, time, and accountability. This does not belittle the questioner. It teaches him to connect information with purpose.

Muslim educators can follow this method by answering clearly and then showing how knowledge affects faith and action. The Qur’an is not merely a storehouse of facts. It is guidance for the person who wants to live well.

- Qur’an 24:27–29 on seeking permission and respecting household privacy.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:190

> Fight in Allah’s cause those who fight you, but do not transgress. Surely Allah does not love the transgressors.

**Commentary:**

### The Permission Is Bounded

This verse addresses the Muslim community in a context of persecution, expulsion, and armed conflict. It permits fighting those who fight them in Allah’s cause, but immediately places a boundary: do not transgress. The permission is not a general command to attack every non-Muslim, nor is it a licence for private violence. It concerns a legitimate community defending itself under lawful authority and within moral limits.

The phrase “in Allah’s cause”—*fī sabīl Allāh*—does not sanctify every battle claimed in Allah’s name. A conflict motivated by conquest, wealth, ethnic hatred, revenge, or personal power is not made righteous by a religious slogan. The cause, intention, authority, conduct, and treatment of non-combatants all matter.

### “Those Who Fight You”

The immediate wording identifies fighting opponents who are actively waging war. Other Qur’anic passages confirm that Muslims are to act kindly and justly toward those who do not fight them or expel them because of religion (60:8), and that if an enemy inclines to peace, Muslims should incline to it (8:61). The command therefore cannot be separated from the surrounding legal and historical context.

This does not mean that a community must wait passively until every attack reaches its homes. Defence can include protecting borders, preventing imminent aggression, and assisting people under attack, according to lawful authority and just cause. But claims of “pre-emptive defence” cannot become a cover for domination or aggression. The principle is protection of life and freedom from oppression, not the pursuit of empire.

### Do Not Transgress

The prohibition of transgression—*lā taʿtadū*—is the controlling limit. It includes initiating unjust aggression, killing children, women, elderly people, religious recluses, and other non-combatants, mutilating bodies, destroying crops and homes without military necessity, torturing prisoners, betraying agreements, and exceeding proportionality. The Prophet ﷺ prohibited the killing of women and children and forbade treachery, as reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

The legal details of combat are handled by qualified authorities and jurists, but the moral core is clear. The enemy’s wrongdoing does not make every act against him lawful. Muslims remain servants of Allah in war; they do not suspend prayer, truthfulness, mercy, or accountability when violence begins.

### War and the Sanctity of Life

The Qur’an normally treats life as sacred and killing as a grave matter (5:32; 17:33). Fighting is permitted under exceptional circumstances because refusing to restrain aggression can expose more people to death and oppression. The permission must therefore be directed toward ending aggression and restoring safety, not feeding anger.

The Prophet ﷺ showed this balance in his conduct. He prepared for defence, accepted treaties, forgave when victory made revenge possible, protected those who sought safety, and prohibited the abuse of captives. His military practice cannot be reduced either to pacifism or to unrestricted violence.

### Legitimate Authority and Accountability

Individual Muslims do not decide on their own that a neighbour, government, religious group, or stranger is an enemy who may be attacked. Declaring war and applying criminal penalties require legitimate authority, evidence, consultation, and accountability. The chaos produced by private militias and terrorist groups is itself a form of transgression.

Leaders are not exempt from the verse. They must state the cause honestly, protect civilians, care for prisoners and wounded people, and stop hostilities when the lawful objective is achieved. Soldiers remain accountable for unlawful orders; “I was following orders” does not make deliberate cruelty permissible.

### Justice under Pressure

The command tests the believer’s character precisely when anger is strongest. The Qur’an later says that hatred of a people must not cause believers to abandon justice (5:8). Even an enemy retains human rights, and the possibility of repentance, surrender, or peace must be respected. Justice is not a favour granted only to friends.

This principle also applies outside war. A person who is insulted cannot answer with slander, assault, or harm to innocent relatives. A community that suffers injustice may defend itself, but its defence must remain proportionate and directed at the wrongdoer.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:191

> Kill them wherever you encounter them, and expel them from the places from which they expelled you. Persecution is worse than killing. Do not fight them at al-Masjid al-Ḥarām unless they fight you there. If they fight you, then kill them. Such is the recompense of the disbelievers.

**Commentary:**

### Read the Verse with Its Context

This āyah must be read together with 2:190 and the verses that follow it. The pronouns refer to the hostile forces who fought the Muslims, persecuted them, and expelled them from Makkah—not to every non-Muslim person in every place and time. Verse 2:190 begins with “those who fight you” and prohibits transgression; verse 2:192 commands cessation if they stop. Removing those limits turns a passage about an armed conflict into a meaning the surrounding text explicitly rejects.

The command belongs to a community in war, under lawful authority, against those responsible for aggression and expulsion. It is not permission for individuals to attack civilians, neighbours, visitors, or people of another faith. The Prophet ﷺ protected non-combatants, honoured treaties, granted safety, and forbade killing women and children.

### Expulsion from the Homeland

The believers had been driven from their homes because they worshipped Allah. Their property was seized, families were divided, and the persecution continued. The command to expel the aggressors from the places from which they expelled the Muslims describes a military response to an established campaign of expulsion. It is not a general doctrine of ethnic replacement or forced removal.

The Qur’an’s language of justice matters: the response is tied to the crime. Muslims do not gain a right to harm a population merely because another generation once committed an offence. Contemporary conflicts must be judged by their present facts, lawful authority, and the protection of civilians, not by a verse detached from its occasion.

### Fitnah as Persecution and Religious Coercion

“Persecution”—*fitnah*—has been explained by the exegetes as persecution, coercion, and the attempt to force people away from their faith through torture and violence. It can also include the disorder created when people are prevented from worshipping Allah and are driven from their homes. The statement that fitnah is worse than killing does not make killing trivial. It describes the grave, sustained harm of a system that attacks people’s lives, faith, safety, and families.

The Qur’an uses *fitnah* in more than one sense, including trial, temptation, and civil strife. Its meaning here is determined by the context of persecution and armed aggression. Muslims must not use the word as a vague label for anyone who disagrees with them.

### The Sanctity of the Sacred Mosque

The verse gives a special restriction: do not fight at al-Masjid al-Ḥarām unless the aggressors fight there. The sanctuary is a place of safety and worship, and violence within it is not the normal rule. The exception addresses an enemy who violates that sanctity and attacks first; even then, the command is framed as a response to combat, not a licence to initiate bloodshed in the sanctuary.

The broader Qur’anic principle is that the Sacred House and the sacred months are protected (5:97; 9:36). Muslims must respect sacred spaces and avoid turning them into military arenas. If an armed force attacks from such a place, the matter falls under the rules of defence and legitimate authority; individual people may not manufacture a crisis to justify violence there.

### Cessation Remains the Goal

The next verse says that if the opponents cease, Allah is forgiving and merciful, and 2:193 says that if they cease, there is no hostility except against oppressors. Thus the command is not a demand to continue killing after the aggression ends. The lawful objective is to stop persecution and restore security, not to destroy people because they once fought.

The Prophet ﷺ accepted surrender and peace when enemies stopped fighting. He also granted safety to those who sought it, as required by 9:6. These practices show that the command is bounded by conduct and can end when the cause of fighting ends.

### “The Recompense of the Disbelievers”

The closing statement describes the recompense of those who rejected truth and committed aggression. It is not a formula that makes disbelief alone a capital crime. The immediate subject is the hostile disbelieving force engaged in persecution and war. The Qur’an repeatedly distinguishes peaceful non-Muslims from those who fight and expel believers (60:8–9).

The distinction is essential for Islamic ethics. Muslims may disagree with a creed while still protecting its adherents from unlawful harm. Religious difference is addressed through invitation and evidence; armed aggression is addressed through lawful defence and justice.

### The Moral Discipline of War

Even in the harshest conflict, the believer remains answerable to Allah. He must verify targets, avoid revenge against relatives or civilians, respect safe conduct, release or exchange prisoners according to lawful arrangements, and stop when peace becomes possible. The grief of persecution does not permit transgression.

A person who quotes this verse to justify terrorism, murder in public places, or attacks on houses of worship is not applying the Qur’an; he is violating 2:190, 2:192, 5:8, and the Prophet’s explicit teachings. The text’s first guardrail is context, and its second is justice.

- Qur’an 8:61 and 9:6 on accepting peace and granting protection to one who seeks safety.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:192

> If they cease, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Stopping Hostilities

This brief verse places a decisive limit on the preceding permission to fight. If the hostile party ceases its aggression, the reason for fighting ends. Muslims are not commanded to continue violence merely because the enemy once attacked, because hatred has grown, or because victory is now possible. The door to cessation is open, and Allah’s forgiveness and mercy are named at the moment when fighting stops.

“Cease” includes stopping attacks, ending persecution, accepting a truce, withdrawing from aggression, or moving toward a reliable peace according to the circumstances. The exact legal arrangement may require negotiation and guarantees, but the moral principle is plain: war is not an end in itself.

### Allah’s Forgiveness and Human Conduct

Allah’s forgiveness is mentioned even in relation to former aggressors. This does not erase the rights of victims or prevent lawful accountability for crimes. It shows that repentance, restraint, and the end of injustice can change the relationship. If Allah opens the way to forgiveness for those who stop and return, believers should not treat every opponent as permanently beyond mercy.

The Prophet ﷺ demonstrated this principle by accepting the repentance and allegiance of former enemies, granting amnesty when circumstances allowed, and refusing to turn every conflict into hereditary vengeance. Justice may require compensation or prosecution, but revenge for its own sake is not the Qur’anic goal.

### Peace Is Not Weakness

Some people imagine that stopping when the enemy stops is a sign of weakness. The verse teaches the opposite. Restraint requires confidence in Allah and discipline over anger. A leader who continues a war solely to preserve prestige or gain territory has moved beyond the cause permitted by revelation.

Peace must be genuine and safe, not a pause used to prepare an attack. The Qur’an allows caution and verification. But suspicion cannot become an excuse to reject every offer, break every agreement, or keep a population in permanent fear. Allah’s mercy is reflected in reducing bloodshed when the conditions for safety exist.

### Forgiveness with Justice

Forgiveness in this context does not mean pretending that persecution never happened. The injured may need safety, property restored, missing people returned, and crimes investigated. A ceasefire protects life immediately; reconciliation may require a longer process of truth, reparations, and trust-building. The verse gives the direction—stop aggression and open mercy—while justice determines how rights are restored.

The Qur’an praises pardon when it leads to reform, but it does not demand that victims expose themselves to renewed harm. A community can forgive emotionally while maintaining lawful safeguards. Mercy includes preventing future victims.

### The Inner Application

The verse also applies to personal disputes. When an opponent stops insulting, stealing, or attacking, the believer should not keep escalating merely to win. If a family member apologizes, a neighbour ends a feud, or a colleague withdraws a false accusation, accepting peace may be more beloved to Allah than rehearsing the old injury.

This does not require trusting a person blindly. Boundaries, evidence, and accountability can remain. The moral change is that the believer no longer seeks harm as an independent goal.

### The Names of Mercy

*Al-Ghafūr* and *al-Raḥīm* teach that Allah loves the path of return. Forgiveness covers the past when repentance is sincere, while mercy guides the servant into a better future. A believer who has been protected from aggression thanks Allah by refusing to become an aggressor himself.

The verse also comforts those who fear that ending conflict will surrender their rights. Allah knows the difference between strategic retreat, lawful truce, and cowardly abandonment of the vulnerable. The community takes necessary means while trusting that Allah’s mercy is greater than the apparent advantage of continuing violence.

- Qur’an 42:40 and 24:22 on forgiveness when it serves reform and mercy.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:193

> Fight them until there is no more persecution and worship is devoted to Allah. If they cease, then there is to be no hostility except against the oppressors.

**Commentary:**

### The Objective Is the End of Persecution

This verse continues the rules of the conflict described in 2:190–192. The objective is that *fitnah*—persecution, coercion, and violent obstruction of faith—should end, and that religion should be for Allah. It is not a command to force every person to become Muslim. The Qur’an explicitly says there is no compulsion in religion (2:256), and it commands protection for a non-Muslim who seeks safety (9:6).

“Religion is for Allah” means that people are free from being forced to worship rulers, idols, or a persecuting power, and that worship belongs to Allah alone. The immediate conflict was with those who prevented the believers from worshipping and expelled them. The verse protects freedom of sincere worship by removing the armed oppression that denied it.

### No Coercion in Faith

The distinction between ending persecution and forcing belief is crucial. Faith is an act of the heart and cannot be produced by a sword. Islam may require a state to defend its people and stop an armed force from destroying religious freedom, but it does not make conversion the condition for every person’s safety. The Prophet ﷺ entered treaties with non-Muslim communities and recognized their rights under lawful agreements.

The Qur’an’s use of “until” does not mean that fighting continues forever against anyone who holds another creed. It identifies the condition under which the conflict ends: when aggression and persecution end. Once that condition is met, the next sentence removes hostility except toward those who continue to oppress.

### If They Cease, Hostility Ends

The repetition of “if they cease” makes the rule unmistakable. When the enemy stops fighting and persecution, Muslims may not continue aggression as punishment for a former identity. The remaining exception is directed at *ẓālimūn*, those who persist in wrongdoing. Even then, hostility must be lawful, proportionate, and administered by legitimate authority; it does not authorize private revenge.

A ceasefire may require monitoring, exchange of prisoners, return of property, and guarantees of safety. These practical measures serve the moral end of protecting life. The Prophet ﷺ honoured treaties and condemned treachery. A community cannot invoke divine authority while breaking agreements whenever it becomes advantageous.

### Religion Belongs to Allah

The phrase *yakūna al-dīn lillāh* also has an inward meaning. The believer should not make wealth, tribe, nation, leader, or desire the object of ultimate worship. A society can be free from external religious persecution while individuals remain enslaved to created powers. Tawḥīd liberates the heart from these rivals.

Publicly, the phrase requires that no human authority claim the right to determine worship by force. Privately, it requires sincerity. Muslims defend the freedom to worship Allah while not treating their own power as divine or using religion to justify oppression.

### The Limits of the Exception

“Except against the oppressors” does not turn every disagreement into a state of war. *Ẓulm* means wrongdoing and injustice, but legal action requires evidence, authority, and a specific offence. A person may be morally wrong without being a lawful military target. The Qur’an distinguishes theological disagreement, political opposition, crime, and armed aggression.

This precision is essential today. A group cannot label a civilian population “oppressors” and attack them indiscriminately. A leader cannot call critics enemies of Allah to silence them. The verse’s own limits—those who fight, the prohibition of transgression, and cessation when aggression stops—must remain in view.

### Justice after Victory

If persecution ends through Muslim victory, the victors remain responsible for justice. They must protect non-combatants, secure places of worship, return rights, and prevent revenge. Victory is not proof that every act of the victorious side was righteous. Allah judges means as well as ends.

The Prophet ﷺ showed mercy at moments when his enemies were powerless. He protected those who sought refuge and did not require every defeated person to adopt the faith. This is a practical interpretation of the verse: end aggression, establish safety, and leave hearts to Allah.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:194

> The sacred month is for the sacred month, and violations are subject to equal retribution. So whoever attacks you, respond in proportion to the attack. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those who are mindful.

**Commentary:**

### Sacred Time and Conflict

The Arabs recognized four sacred months in which fighting was restricted, but some used the calendar to manipulate the restriction or to attack while claiming that others had violated it. This verse establishes a principle of justice: a violation of sacred time does not give an aggressor immunity from defence, and the response must remain equivalent to the offence.

“The sacred month is for the sacred month” was also connected by the exegetes to the events surrounding the expedition to ʿUmrah and the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah, when the Muslims were prevented from entering Makkah in a sacred period. The exact historical applications are discussed in the sīrah and tafsīr literature, but the legal principle is broader: sacred status cannot be used as a weapon to make one side untouchable while it attacks the other.

### Equal Retribution Is Not Revenge

“Violations are subject to equal retribution”—*al-ḥurumātu qiṣāṣ*—means that the response must match the violation without exceeding it. If an enemy attacks, the believers may defend themselves; they may not use the attack as an excuse for unlimited vengeance. This echoes 2:178–179, where *qiṣāṣ* is measured legal accountability rather than tribal rage.

Proportionality includes the target, means, scale, duration, and likely harm. A limited attack cannot justify killing uninvolved people or destroying an entire city. The stronger party has no right to call excess “victory.” Allah’s command remains binding even when retaliation would be emotionally satisfying.

### Responding to Aggression

The language “whoever attacks you, attack him in the same way” is addressed to a community in an active conflict and presupposes lawful authority. It does not authorize individuals to assault someone who insulted them or to punish a whole group for the act of one person. The response must be directed at the aggressor, supported by evidence, and constrained by the goal of restoring safety.

The Qur’an repeatedly joins permission to defend with a prohibition of transgression (2:190) and a command to accept peace when it is genuinely offered (8:61). The verses must be read as a complete legal and moral framework, not isolated as slogans.

### Sacred Months and Sacred Places

Sacred time teaches restraint. The lunar calendar includes Dhū al-Qaʿdah, Dhū al-Ḥijjah, Muḥarram, and Rajab as sacred months, according to the authentic hadith. Their sanctity is part of Allah’s order and is not to be manipulated for convenience. Likewise, Makkah has a sacred sanctuary in which bloodshed, hunting, and other violations have special gravity.

The principle has a modern analogue in respect for truces, humanitarian pauses, places of worship, hospitals, and protected persons. Declaring a space or time protected and then exploiting that protection to attack others is a betrayal of trust. Defence may respond to an attack, but it should seek to restore the protection rather than erase all limits.

### Taqwā in Retaliation

The verse inserts “Be mindful of Allah” between permission to respond and the promise of divine companionship. This placement shows how easily retaliation can become sin. *Taqwā* asks the believer to monitor intention, restrain anger, verify facts, and stop when the lawful response is complete.

A person may feel that an enemy deserves more than the law permits. Taqwā means refusing to make emotion the judge. It also means caring for one’s own fighters, civilians, and future peace. Every act is recorded, and victory cannot excuse a violation of Allah’s boundary.

### Allah Is with the Mindful

“Allah is with those who are mindful” is a promise of guidance, support, and protection. It does not mean that the mindful will never suffer military loss. It means that they are not abandoned when they preserve justice under pressure. Allah gives them clarity, steadiness, and reward even when the worldly result is delayed.

This promise encourages restraint as well as courage. The community can defend itself without becoming what it opposes because its real strength is obedience to Allah rather than hatred of an enemy.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:195

> Spend in Allah’s cause and do not throw yourselves into destruction with your own hands. Do good; surely Allah loves those who do good.

**Commentary:**

### Spending for the Common Good

The command to spend follows the rules of conflict and sacred limits. A community cannot protect life, support the vulnerable, or resist aggression without material resources. Spending in Allah’s cause—*al-infāq fī sabīl Allāh*—includes charitable support, care for the poor, preparation for legitimate defence, relief for displaced people, education, and other lawful efforts that serve Allah’s purposes.

The verse does not restrict spending to military activity. In the Qur’an, “Allah’s cause” includes the broad work of establishing worship, justice, mercy, and protection. A person may spend on a hungry family, a hospital, a school, a refuge, or the defence of a community, provided the cause and method are lawful.

### Do Not Destroy Yourselves

“Do not throw yourselves into destruction with your own hands” has been interpreted by the early scholars in more than one connected way. It includes abandoning necessary spending until the community becomes helpless, deliberately exposing oneself to avoidable danger, wasting resources, and engaging in reckless conduct that predictably destroys life or capacity. The verse is not a command to refuse every risk; it is a prohibition of self-inflicted ruin.

A report from Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī explains the verse as warning against abandoning the struggle and support of the community, thereby handing oneself over to destruction. Other reports and jurists apply it broadly to self-harm, excessive risk, and neglect. The meanings complement one another: a community must not destroy itself through cowardly abandonment, and individuals must not glorify reckless danger.

### Spending and Trust in Allah

The believer spends while knowing that provision belongs to Allah. This does not mean giving away every resource without planning or leaving dependants destitute. The Prophet ﷺ advised Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ that leaving heirs financially secure is better than leaving them dependent upon people, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

Balanced spending distinguishes obligation, need, and preference. Zakah must be paid, dependants supported, debts honoured, and urgent suffering addressed. Voluntary charity is then offered according to ability. Wasteful display and donations to harmful causes are not piety, even when they are advertised as sacrifice.

### Self-Harm and Reckless Ideology

The verse is sometimes misused to encourage dangerous behaviour or, in the opposite direction, to prohibit every form of sacrifice. Its actual balance is clear. A person may accept danger while protecting others in a legitimate cause, but he may not intentionally kill himself or treat innocent lives as expendable. Courage requires a reasonable hope of benefit and obedience to lawful authority; recklessness turns the self and others into instruments of emotion.

The Prophet ﷺ prohibited suicide and condemned killing non-combatants. He also praised those who protected people and stood firm against aggression. The moral difference lies in intention, lawful cause, reasonable means, and the prohibition of deliberately targeting one’s own life or the lives of innocents.

### Goodness Is the Final Principle

The verse ends with *iḥsān*: “Do good.” Spending, defence, and restraint are not isolated legal actions. They should be performed with excellence, sincerity, and care. *Iḥsān* means doing what is beautiful and correct, exceeding mere minimum compliance when generosity is possible.

Allah loves the *muḥsinūn*, those who bring benefit and avoid harm. The person who gives money but humiliates the recipient has missed part of iḥsān. The authority that defends a community but abuses prisoners has not acted beautifully. Good ends do not justify corrupt means.

### Prevention and Public Responsibility

The community has a duty to prevent the conditions that lead to destruction. It should support people before hunger becomes famine, treat illness before it becomes fatal, address addiction, protect children, and provide safe roads and workplaces. “Do not throw yourselves” includes institutional negligence. Leaders who knowingly expose people to preventable harm are accountable before Allah.

Individuals also take practical means: they seek medical care, use safety equipment, avoid intoxicants, protect mental health, and consult experts. Reliance on Allah is not a substitute for responsible action. The Prophet ﷺ taught, “Tie it and rely upon Allah,” in the well-known report of tawakkul.

- Qur’an 2:215 and 59:9 on spending for relatives, the needy, and those who give others priority.
- Qur’an 8:60 on preparing strength without transgression, and 9:34–35 on corrupt hoarding of wealth.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the advice to Saʿd about dependants and the prohibition of suicide and unlawful killing; reports from Abū Ayyūb concerning the interpretation of “destruction.”

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:196

> Complete Hajj and ʿUmrah for Allah. If you are prevented, then offer whatever sacrificial animal is available, and do not shave your heads until the sacrifice reaches its place. Whoever among you is ill or has an ailment of the head and must shave, then he must offer a ransom of fasting, charity, or sacrifice. When you are secure, then whoever performs ʿUmrah followed by Hajj must offer whatever sacrifice is available. Whoever cannot find one must fast three days during Hajj and seven when you return—ten complete. This is for those whose families do not reside near al-Masjid al-Ḥarām. Be mindful of Allah and know that Allah is severe in punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Completing the Pilgrimage for Allah

The command “complete Hajj and ʿUmrah for Allah” establishes sincerity and fulfilment. Hajj is not a tourism exercise, a social title, or a display of wealth. It is an act of *ʿibādah* performed for Allah alone, following the rites taught by the Messenger ﷺ. Completion includes entering the consecration of *iḥrām*, observing the prohibitions, performing the required rites, and completing the pilgrimage without deliberately abandoning its obligations.

The wording also teaches that worship is not offered to impress people. The pilgrim should renew his intention when fatigue, crowding, expense, or praise arise. Allah sees whether he sought His pleasure or a reputation as someone who has performed Hajj.

### Prevention and the Sacrifice

The verse addresses a person who is prevented—*uḥṣirtum*—from completing the rites by an enemy, siege, serious obstruction, or another circumstance recognized by the law. He offers a *hady*, a sacrificial animal, at the place and in the manner explained by the Sunnah and qualified scholars, then exits iḥrām according to the applicable ruling. The event of Ḥudaybiyyah is the clearest prophetic example: the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions were prevented from entering Makkah and sacrificed before returning.

The sacrifice is not a payment to purchase Allah’s favour. It is an act of obedience and a dignified conclusion to a pilgrimage interrupted by circumstances outside the pilgrim’s control. The details vary according to whether a person is genuinely prevented, placed under a binding condition, or merely inconvenienced.

### Do Not Shave before the Sacrifice

Shaving or shortening the hair—*ḥalq* or *taqṣīr*—marks release from iḥrām after the appropriate stage. The pilgrim who is prevented waits until the sacrifice reaches its lawful place before shaving, unless the relevant legal condition changes. The physical act symbolizes the completion of the interrupted rite and obedience to Allah’s sequence.

The Prophet ﷺ shaved his head at Ḥudaybiyyah after the Companions sacrificed. His practice teaches that a command may be fulfilled even when the hoped-for destination is not reached. Allah rewards the sincere effort and does not require what circumstances have made impossible.

### Ailment and the Fidyah

A pilgrim who has an illness or painful condition of the head and must shave is given a ransom—*fidyah*: fasting, charity, or sacrifice. The Sunnah explains this through the case of Kaʿb ibn ʿUjrah, who had lice and was permitted to shave, then was instructed to fast three days, feed six needy people, or sacrifice a sheep, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

The concession shows that iḥrām does not require a person to tolerate serious harm. The normal restriction remains, but necessity opens a lawful alternative. The options are not an invitation to choose whichever is most convenient without considering the legal conditions; the pilgrim asks qualified guidance and fulfils the appropriate expiation.

### Tamattuʿ and the Hady

“When you are secure, whoever performs ʿUmrah followed by Hajj” describes *tamattuʿ*: entering iḥrām for ʿUmrah during the months of Hajj, completing ʿUmrah, exiting iḥrām, and then entering iḥrām again for Hajj in the same season. The pilgrim who chooses this form offers a *hady* if he is not a resident of the Sacred Mosque. The sacrifice expresses gratitude and helps feed the poor of the sanctuary.

Other forms include *ifrād*, Hajj alone, and *qirān*, combining Hajj and ʿUmrah in one iḥrām. The Prophet ﷺ taught the rites and allowed the Companions to choose among forms according to their circumstances. The pilgrim should follow an established legal guide rather than arguing over which valid form is most virtuous in every situation.

### The Ten Days of Fasting

Whoever cannot find the required sacrifice fasts three days during Hajj and seven after returning—ten complete. The three days may be observed within the pilgrimage period according to the juristic details, and the seven are completed after returning home. The number “ten” is stated explicitly to prevent the mistaken idea that the first three and later seven are interchangeable or incomplete.

The alternative protects pilgrims of limited means. Hajj should not become a privilege in which only the wealthy can fulfil every form of worship. At the same time, a person should not claim inability while possessing the means to offer the sacrifice. Allah knows the real condition of the servant.

### Residents of the Sacred Area

The rule is for those whose families do not reside near al-Masjid al-Ḥarām. The resident of Makkah and the surrounding sanctuary has a different legal status in the matter of tamattuʿ sacrifice, because the specific travel and return circumstances underlying the rule do not apply in the same way. The boundaries of the *ḥaram* and the detailed conditions are matters for qualified fiqh.

This distinction illustrates a general legal method: rulings can depend upon location, intention, ability, and the form of worship. One should not transfer a concession or obligation from one category to another without understanding its cause.

### Fear Allah in Pilgrimage

The verse ends with taqwā and a warning of punishment. Pilgrimage strips away many social distinctions, but it also exposes character. The pilgrim must not cheat in sacrifice, harm others in crowds, violate iḥrām, or use wealth to purchase special treatment that injures the weak. Taqwā makes the outward rites an act of inward submission.

The warning is not intended to destroy hope. Allah’s law contains concessions throughout the verse. Fear protects the pilgrim from carelessness, while hope in Allah’s mercy helps him complete the journey with humility.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:197

> Hajj is during well-known months. Whoever undertakes Hajj in them must abstain from sexual intimacy, sin, and quarrelling during Hajj. Whatever good you do, Allah knows it. Take provisions, but surely the best provision is taqwā. Be mindful of Me, people of understanding.

**Commentary:**

### Hajj Has an Appointed Season

Hajj is not an improvised journey performed whenever a person chooses. It has known months and appointed rites. The classical scholars discuss the precise beginning and ending of the Hajj months, commonly identifying them as Shawwāl, Dhū al-Qaʿdah, and the first days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah. The season allows pilgrims to prepare, enter iḥrām, travel, and perform the rites at their prescribed times.

The fixed season teaches that worship is received through obedience, not through private invention. A person may perform ʿUmrah at other times, but Hajj has its own communal calendar and legal structure. The pilgrim submits his schedule to revelation and accepts that sacred time belongs to Allah.

### The Three Prohibitions

Once a person undertakes Hajj, he must avoid *rafath*, *fusūq*, and *jidāl*. *Rafath* includes sexual intimacy during iḥrām and obscene or sexually explicit speech. Marriage remains honoured, but the pilgrim temporarily suspends intimacy while in the relevant state of consecration. *Fusūq* includes disobedience, foul conduct, and violation of Allah’s commands. *Jidāl* is quarrelling, hostile argument, and disputation that destroys the spirit of the pilgrimage.

The list covers desire, moral rebellion, and anger. Hajj is physically crowded and emotionally demanding; people of different languages, schools, and cultures share narrow spaces. The pilgrim must restrain the tongue, hands, gaze, and temper. A person who performs the outer rites while abusing others has not understood the purpose of iḥrām.

### Not Every Discussion Is Forbidden

The prohibition of *jidāl* does not ban necessary communication, legal questions, correcting a mistake, or calm discussion of a genuine difference. Pilgrims must ask for directions, resolve disputes, and protect people from harm. What is forbidden is quarrelling driven by ego, insult, factional pride, or the desire to dominate.

The same distinction applies to religious scholarship. A person may learn the rites and follow a qualified teacher without turning every valid difference into a fight. The variety of recognized legal opinions should produce humility and cooperation, not abuse in the sacred precinct.

### Hajj as a School of Character

Hajj removes markers of status through simple clothing, common movement, and shared rites. The wealthy and poor stand together at ʿArafah; the ruler cannot purchase a separate Hereafter; the scholar must still wait in a crowd. These conditions reveal the heart. Patience in a queue, sharing water, protecting another pilgrim from heat, and apologizing after a mistake can be greater signs of accepted Hajj than public emotion.

The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever performs Hajj without sexual misconduct or sin returns like the day his mother gave birth, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith highlights moral purification, not only the completion of a travel itinerary.

### Provision for the Journey

“Take provisions” is a literal instruction. Pilgrims should prepare food, water, money, clothing, medicine, documents, and knowledge of the rites. Tawakkul does not mean travelling unprepared or expecting other people to support an avoidably careless person. The Qur’an corrects those who treated lack of provisions as a sign of piety and then depended upon people for food.

The instruction also becomes a metaphor: the journey to Allah requires provision of faith, repentance, patience, lawful wealth, and good character. Material supplies sustain the body for days; taqwā sustains the soul through life and the Hereafter.

### Taqwā Is the Best Provision

*Taqwā* is awareness of Allah that protects the servant from disobedience. It is the best provision because it remains useful when money, luggage, status, and companions cannot follow a person beyond death. A pilgrim who takes taqwā seriously prepares by settling debts, seeking forgiveness, reconciling with relatives, and ensuring that his travel wealth is lawful.

Taqwā also protects the rights of other pilgrims. A person does not reserve excessive space, steal belongings, push the weak, or use ritual urgency to justify abuse. The sacred journey is a test of how one treats servants of the same Lord.

### “People of Understanding”

The closing address to *ūlī al-albāb* invites reflection. People of sound understanding see the relation between external rites and inner character. They do not confuse a costly journey with automatic salvation, nor do they dismiss the rites as empty symbolism. They perform the outward obligations while pursuing the inward transformation they signify.

The wise pilgrim returns home with a more disciplined prayer, a more truthful tongue, a more generous hand, and greater care for people. If the journey produces pride and contempt, its lessons have been resisted.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:198

> There is no blame upon you for seeking bounty from your Lord. When you depart from ʿArafāt, remember Allah at al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām. Remember Him because He has guided you, for before this you were surely among those who were astray.

**Commentary:**

### Lawful Trade during Hajj

Some Muslims feared that earning lawful income while travelling for Hajj might diminish the purity of the pilgrimage. This verse removes that unnecessary anxiety: there is no blame in seeking Allah’s bounty through lawful trade. Worship and work are not enemies when intention, conduct, and priorities remain sound.

“Allah’s bounty” can include commerce, wages, transport, and other permissible means of provision. The permission does not make business the purpose of Hajj or allow the pilgrim to neglect the rites. It teaches that a person may support himself and his family while worshipping, provided he avoids fraud, exploitation, excessive distraction, and prohibited trade.

The verse corrects two extremes. One treats earning money as inherently impure and abandons legitimate responsibility; the other turns a sacred journey into a commercial opportunity and neglects Allah. The balance is lawful livelihood joined to remembrance.

### Departing from ʿArafāt

The standing at ʿArafāt is the central rite of Hajj. Pilgrims gather there in humility, supplication, and remembrance, then depart after sunset toward Muzdalifah. The movement is not a private spiritual retreat. It is a shared act in which people of every background stand before one Lord, recalling that all humanity will be gathered for judgement.

The Prophet ﷺ said, “Hajj is ʿArafah,” in the authentic reports of Sunan Abī Dāwūd, Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, and Sunan al-Nasāʾī. This highlights the importance of reaching the place and time of the standing according to the established rite. Pilgrims should learn the schedule and follow qualified organisers rather than rely upon guesswork.

### Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām and Remembrance

Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām refers to the sacred place at Muzdalifah, where pilgrims remember Allah after leaving ʿArafāt. The night, prayer, supplication, and collection of pebbles in the following rite are governed by the Sunnah and detailed fiqh. The place is not worshipped. Its sanctity comes from Allah’s designation and its role in the pilgrimage.

The command is repeated: “Remember Him.” Hajj is a movement of the body that must be animated by *dhikr*. Without remembrance, the pilgrim may experience only heat, crowds, and logistics. With remembrance, every step recalls Ibrāhīm, the unity of believers, death, resurrection, and Allah’s mercy.

### Remembering Guidance

The believers are told to remember Allah because He guided them. Guidance—*hidāyah*—is not a personal achievement that makes a person superior to others. The pilgrim may have wealth, health, and opportunity, but the ability to know the rites and reach the sanctuary is a gift. Gratitude protects him from pride.

“Before this you were astray” refers to the condition of humanity before prophetic guidance and to the specific ignorance from which the Arabs and other peoples were rescued. It does not require a Muslim to despise every aspect of his past or to deny the good traits that existed before Islam. It means that without revelation, people cannot reliably know how to worship Allah and organize sacred rites.

### Work and Worship as One Life

The verse’s permission for trade also establishes a wider Islamic vision. A person can earn lawfully, care for family, travel, study, and worship without dividing life into a sacred part and a godless part. The intention and method can make ordinary work an act of obedience. A trader who is honest during Hajj is displaying the worshipful meaning of seeking bounty.

At the same time, commercial success must not become the measure of the pilgrimage. The pilgrim should avoid exploiting the demand for accommodation, food, transport, or religious services. Sacred need makes profiteering especially ugly. Fair prices and good service are part of *iḥsān*.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:199

> Then depart from where the people depart, and seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Leaving the Old Pride

The verse instructs the pilgrims to depart from where the people depart. Classical exegetes connect this to the Quraysh and allied groups who, before Islam, distinguished themselves from the rest of the pilgrims by refusing to stand at ʿArafāt, stopping instead at Muzdalifah because of their claimed special status as people of the sanctuary. Allah removed this aristocratic boundary and commanded them to join the wider human assembly.

The lesson is larger than that particular custom. Sacred worship is not a stage for inherited privilege. The wealthy, powerful, local, foreign, learned, and ordinary all stand as servants before Allah. A tribe’s closeness to the Kaʿbah, a family’s religious reputation, or a person’s wealth does not give him permission to invent a superior route through the rites.

### Following the Prophetic Rite

“Where the people depart” also teaches conformity to the established communal rite. Hajj is not redesigned around personal symbolism. The Prophet ﷺ said, “Take your rites from me,” and demonstrated when to stand, depart, pray, stay, and move. The pilgrim learns from the Sunnah and qualified scholarship rather than making unusual practice a badge of spiritual distinction.

Following the people here does not mean following a crowd into error. It means joining the rightly established rite instead of preserving an arrogant pre-Islamic exception. A Muslim avoids both blind conformity and self-important innovation.

### Seeking Forgiveness at the End of the Rite

After the standing at ʿArafāt and the departure, the pilgrims are told to seek Allah’s forgiveness—*istaghfirū Allāh*. Even after a major act of worship, the servant does not assume that his performance is perfect or that he is entitled to reward. He recognizes shortcomings in intention, attention, patience, and treatment of others.

Istighfār is not an admission that Hajj was worthless. It is an expression of humility and a request that Allah cover faults, accept the worship, and allow its lessons to continue. The Prophet ﷺ would seek forgiveness after prayer, and the Qur’an instructs him to glorify and seek forgiveness after the completion of his mission (110:3).

### Humility and Equality

The command to join the people and seek forgiveness links social humility with spiritual humility. A person cannot claim closeness to Allah while looking down on the crowd. Hajj exposes the illusion of status: all pilgrims need water, shade, guidance, and mercy. The one who pushes the weak or treats service workers with contempt has contradicted the communal lesson of the rite.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah does not look at bodies or wealth but at hearts and deeds, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The pilgrimage garment and gathering remind people that their true rank is determined by taqwā, not by passport, family, or income.

### Mercy after Imperfection

Allah is *Ghafūr* and *Raḥīm*. The pilgrim may have made mistakes, lost concentration, argued, or struggled with the logistics of the journey. He repents, repairs harm, and hopes in Allah’s forgiveness. Mercy is not a reason to repeat avoidable wrongdoing, but it prevents one error from turning into despair.

A community should help pilgrims learn rather than shame them for honest mistakes. If a serious legal violation occurs, the person seeks qualified advice about its remedy. He does not conceal harm behind the claim that Hajj is already complete.

### The End of Hajj and the Beginning of Change

The departure from the sacred places is also a test. A pilgrim may feel spiritual emotion in Makkah and then return to the old habits that harmed his family, employees, or neighbours. The command to seek forgiveness invites a continuing relationship with Allah after the journey. The accepted Hajj should leave a person more humble, truthful, and just.

The real proof is not the souvenir or title but the changed conduct. A person who leaves the sanctuary with less pride and more mercy has understood the verse’s direction.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:197–199 on Hajj conduct, ʿArafāt, departure, equality, and forgiveness.
- Qur’an 49:13 and 53:32 on honour through taqwā and avoiding self-praise.
- Qur’an 110:3 on glorifying Allah and seeking forgiveness after completing a great task.
- Qur’an 3:96–97 on the House as a sanctuary for humanity, not a private possession of an elite.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that Allah looks at hearts and deeds rather than bodies and wealth; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the Prophet’s teaching to take the rites of Hajj from him.
- For the pre-Islamic privilege of Quraysh, the meaning of “where the people depart,” and the link between Hajj and humility, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:199.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:200

> When you have completed your rites, remember Allah as you used to remember your forefathers, or with even stronger remembrance. Among the people are those who say, “Our Lord, give us in this world,” and they will have no share in the Hereafter.

**Commentary:**

### Remembrance after the Rites

The pilgrimage does not end spiritually when the physical rites are completed. Allah commands the pilgrims to remember Him intensely after Hajj. Before Islam, some Arabs would remain at the sacred gathering boasting about their ancestors, recounting tribal victories and praising lineage. The Qur’an redirects that energy: remember Allah with greater seriousness than you remembered your fathers.

The command does not forbid honouring parents or preserving family history. Islam values gratitude to ancestors and maintaining kinship. It forbids turning ancestry into the centre of pride and identity while forgetting the One who created every ancestor. The best legacy of a family is obedience to Allah, not boasting about names.

### Dhikr as the Heart of Hajj

*Dhikr* includes takbīr, tahlīl, praise, supplication, Qur’an recitation, and the remembrance of Allah’s gifts and promises. The pilgrim has witnessed the Kaʿbah, stood at ʿArafāt, spent the night at Muzdalifah, and performed the rites of Mina. These experiences should deepen awareness of Allah rather than become stories about personal achievement.

“Or with even stronger remembrance” indicates intensity of attention and love. The heart should be more occupied with Allah than with tribal reputation, social media documentation, business conversations, or comparisons about who performed Hajj best. The greatest journey becomes a means of humility only when Allah remains its centre.

### Two Kinds of Supplication

The verse contrasts two groups. Some people ask only for worldly benefit: wealth, health, success, status, or immediate security. These requests are not inherently forbidden. The problem is reducing life to the present world and having no concern for forgiveness, guidance, righteous character, or the Hereafter. Such a person may complete the physical rites while the horizon of his prayer remains worldly.

The next verse gives the balanced supplication: “Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.” Islam does not require abandoning lawful worldly needs. It teaches believers to ask for them within a larger desire for eternal success.

### The Danger of a Worldly Hajj

A pilgrim may spend money, endure travel, and seek public honour while neglecting the purpose of worship. He may want the title of ḥājj, a business advantage, political influence, or social admiration. The verse asks whether his remembrance after the rites is directed to Allah or to the status he gained from being seen at the sanctuary.

The remedy is renewing intention, hiding voluntary worship where possible, limiting unnecessary display, and remembering death and resurrection. Hajj is a rehearsal for the final gathering, where lineage and wealth will not save a person without faith and righteous action.

### Family and the Hereafter

The reference to fathers also acknowledges that human beings naturally organize memory around family and belonging. Islam does not erase family identity; it places it under tawḥīd. A person can love his parents, serve his relatives, and honour his ancestors while refusing to follow them in error. The question is whether family pride leads to gratitude or to superiority.

The Prophet ﷺ warned that boasting about ancestors is a remnant of jāhiliyyah and taught that people are all from Ādam, while true honour comes through taqwā. This protects communities from racism, tribal contempt, and inherited claims to religious privilege.

### The Hereafter Must Remain in View

The phrase “they will have no share in the Hereafter” is a warning about a person whose entire aim is the world and who meets Allah without faith or righteous concern. It is not a declaration that a believer who asks for a job, healing, marriage, or safety has no Hereafter. The distinction is between a lawful worldly request joined to faith and a worldview that excludes the Hereafter.

Worldly blessings can become provision for the Hereafter when used in obedience. Wealth supports family and charity; health supports prayer and service; knowledge benefits people. The believer asks Allah to bless the world without allowing it to become his ultimate destination.

### Completion and Continuity

The verse’s placement after the main Hajj rites teaches that completion is a beginning. The pilgrim returns to ordinary life carrying the habits of remembrance, prayer, patience, generosity, and equality. If the journey was accepted, its effects should appear in how he treats relatives, workers, neighbours, and the poor.

The community can preserve this transformation by continuing gatherings of Qur’an and dhikr, supporting pilgrims in making changes, and discouraging competition over prestige. Remembrance should be stronger than the memory of the journey itself.
