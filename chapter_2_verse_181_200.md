## Verse 181

Verse 181 says: *"But whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those who made the change. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse protects the will: whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those who made the change. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

The verse follows the command of the will (2:180): the will is prescribed, and the will must be respected. The one who is told the will — the witness, the executor, the beneficiary, the relative — and then changes it — adds, deletes, turns the bequest of the fair into the bequest of the unjust, takes from one heir and gives to another — bears the blame. The verse's "the blame will only be on those who made the change" — the particular blame, the one whose change alters the instruction of the dying person.

The commentators connect the two verses: 2:180 prescribed the will, 2:181 protects it from the alteration. The will is the last act of the person's authority over the world; the change of the will after the death (or the hearing) is the violation of the last act. The verse's precise wording — "after hearing it" — identifies the moment: the change is made by those who heard the will, who know the intention of the bequeather, and who then alter it. The one who changes what he has heard is the one who changes what he knows.

"Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — the verse's close is the ground of the blame: the One who is All-Hearing has heard the will and the change; the One who is All-Knowing knows the motive and the alteration. The testimony of the witnesses will be weighed by the One who heard the original instruction and the alteration, and who knows the intention of both. The verse's emphasis: the change is not a private matter among the heirs; it is the matter known to the One who heard and knows.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the executor and the witness. The will of the dying person is a trust: the executor, the witness, the lawyer, the relative — the one who hears the will is the one who is trusted with it. The verse's warning: the trust is betrayed by the change — the alteration that serves the interests of the changer at the expense of the bequeather's intention. The verse's teaching: the one who changes the will bears the blame alone; the One who is All-Hearing and All-Knowing is the witness of both the original and the change.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 182

Verse 182 says: *"Whoever suspects an error or an injustice in the will and brings about a ˹fair˺ settlement among the parties will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse provides the remedy for the flawed will: whoever suspects an error or an injustice in the will and brings about a fair settlement among the parties will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse follows the protection (2:181): the will is protected, but the will itself may be flawed — the bequeather may have erred, may have committed an injustice, may have been pressured, may have forgotten someone. The verse creates the exception: the one who *khafa* — fears, suspects — an error or an injustice in the will, and then acts as the peacemaker — *aslaha* — brings about the settlement among the parties — the correction, the reconciliation, the fair adjustment — will not be sinful.

The verse's two conditions are the boundary of the exception. The first: the suspicion — the one who suspects the error or the injustice, not the one who seeks the benefit. The second: the settlement — the one who brings about the fair settlement among the parties, not the one who imposes his own preference. The verse's remedy is the reconciliation: the flawed will is corrected, not by the litigant who gains but by the peacemaker who repairs; the correction is the *islah* — the making right — and the making right is among the parties, through the just agreement.

The commentators note the relation between the two verses: 181 warns the changer, 182 excuses the corrector. The distinction is the intention: the changer takes from the will for himself; the corrector adjusts the will for the justice. The one who changes for the gain bears the blame; the one who corrects for the fairness will not be sinful. The verse's close: "Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — the mercy covers the one who acts with the sincere intention of the repair, because the reconciliation of the parties and the correction of the injustice, even in the last affairs of the dead, is the mercy of the Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the mediation of the estate. The disputes over the wills and the estates are among the most painful of the family conflicts; the verse speaks to the person who sees the injustice — the will that omits the child, the bequest that favors one at the cost of another, the instruction that was made under the pressure — and who acts: not to grab for himself, but to bring about the fair settlement. The verse's message: the repair of the injustice is not a sin; the sincere reconciliation is the work of the mercy; and the One who is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful covers the one who repairs.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 183

Verse 183 says: *"O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you—as it was for those before you—so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺."*

In simple terms, the verse inaugurates the command of the fasting: O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you — as it was for those before you — so perhaps you will become mindful of Allah.

The verse's command: the fasting — *al-siyam* — the abstinence from food, drink and sexual activity, with the sincere intention for Allah alone (Ibn Kathir). The fasting is *kutiba* — prescribed — the same verb used for the qisas (2:178) and the will (2:180): the obligatory command, the divine prescription. The verse's address — "O believers!" — is the address of the community: the fasting is the command of the believers.

"As it was for those before you" — the verse links the fast to the previous communities: the fasting was prescribed for the People of the Book and the nations before; the Muslims' fast is the continuation of the practice of the age of the prophets. Ibn Kathir: Allah mentioned that He has ordained fasting for Muslims just as He ordained it for those before them, they being an example for them in that, so they should vigorously perform this obligation more obediently than the previous nations. The verse's link is also the honor: the fast is not a new burden imposed on the Muslims; it is the command of the ages, the practice of the people of the faith before them. The commentators note (Ibn Abbas tradition): "those before you" are the People of the Book.

"So perhaps you will become mindful of Allah" — the verse's purpose: the *taqwa* — the mindfulness. The fasting is the means of the taqwa: Ibn Kathir: the fast cleanses the body and narrows the paths of Shaitan; Al-Jalalayn: it curbs the desires that prompt the acts of disobedience. The fast is not the mere abstinence; the fast is the training of the taqwa: the hunger teaches the dependence, the thirst teaches the need, the abstinence teaches the restraint, and the restraint of the permitted teaches the restraint of the forbidden. The verse's purpose: so that you will become mindful — the fast is the school of the mindfulness.

The verse's context: it follows the verses of the rights of the people — the qisas, the will, the estate. The verse turns to the rights of Allah: after the justice among the people, the worship of the Lord; after the laws of the society, the fast of the self. The verse is also the moment of the medicine of the ummah: the fasting was revealed in the second year of the hijrah, the time of the establishment of the community.

In modern life, the verse is the invitation to the discipline that produces the awareness. The modern self is the self of the impulse — the hunger, the thirst, the desire, the gratification. The fast is the training of the self against the impulse: the command says no to the appetite, no to the desire, no to the gratification — and the "no" is for the purpose: so that the person becomes mindful. The fast of the month is not the end; the month is the school; the mindfulness is the graduate. The verse's message: the discipline of the appetite is the path of the awareness of Allah.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 184

Verse 184 says: *"˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew."*

In simple terms, the verse specifies the fast and its concessions: fast a prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then let them fast an equal number of days after Ramadan. For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person for every day not fasted. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew.

The verse's first clause: "a prescribed number of days" — *ayyaman ma'dudat* — days few in number, limited, numbered: the fast is not the perpetual abstinence; it is the limited period. The verse's mercy is in the limitation: the fast of the year is a small number of days, and the obligation is not the burden but the limited, the endurable, the counted.

The illness and the journey: the verse gives the concession — the ill and the traveler do not fast the month; they fast an equal number of days later. The concession is the command of the ease: the obligation is transferred, not cancelled; the fast is made up when the person is able.

"For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty" — the verse's phrase *alladhina yutiqunahu* — the ones who can fast with difficulty — is the key of the understanding. Ibn Kathir records the reading: the ayah refers to those who find it difficult to fast — the old man, the old woman, the chronically ill — the ones who are not able to fast and are not likely to improve; for them, the fidyah: feeding a needy person for every day not fasted. The history of the verse: in the beginning of Islam, when a person wished to fast he fasted, and when he wished he did not fast and fed a poor person instead (the reports of Salamah ibn al-Akwa', Mu'adh, Ibn 'Umar); later the verse 2:185 was revealed and abrogated the prior concession for the healthy person, and the fidyah remained for the old and the ailing. Ibn Abbas, however, held that the ayah was NOT abrogated — it is for the old man and the old woman who are able to fast with difficulty but choose instead to feed a poor person for every day.

"But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them" — the verse's *tatawwa'* — the voluntary: the one who feeds an extra poor person, who gives more than the minimum, does the better; the verse's encouragement of the generosity. "And to fast is better for you, if only you knew" — the verse's final clause: the fasting itself is the better — the mercy of the concession should not become the preference; the fast is the better of the two.

In modern life, the verse is the structure of the mercy in the obligation. The religion's commands are not the crushing burdens; they are the limited, the endurable, with the concessions built into the law: the ill and the traveler postpone; the old and the severely ailing give the food; the volunteer gives more; and the fast itself is the better. The verse's message: the obligation is the prescribed and the limited; the mercy is the concession; and the one who knows chooses the better — the fast.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 185

Verse 185 says: *"Ramaḍân is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. So whoever is present this month, let them fast. But whoever is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. Allah intends ease for you, not hardship, so that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you, and perhaps you will be grateful."*

In simple terms, the verse identifies the month of the fast: Ramadan is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard to distinguish between right and wrong. So whoever is present this month, let them fast. But whoever is ill or on a journey, then let them fast an equal number of days after Ramadan. Allah intends ease for you, not hardship, so that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you, and perhaps you will be grateful.

The verse opens with the honor of the month: the month of the revelation of the Qur'an. The Qur'an was revealed — Ibn Kathir records the tradition (from Wathilah ibn al-Asqa', Musnad of Ahmad, and others) that the Scriptures of Abraham were revealed on the first of Ramadan, the Torah on the sixth, the Evangel on the thirteenth, and the Qur'an on the twenty-fourth; the Ma'arif tradition (Jabir) places the Psalms on the twelfth and the Evangel on the eighteenth. The Qur'an's own revelation: it was sent down from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven in the Night of Decree, all of it, and revealed to the Prophet gradually during twenty-three years. The verse's description of the book: the guidance for humanity; the clear proofs of the guidance; the Furqan — the criterion, the standard that distinguishes between the right and the wrong. The month's honor is the book's honor: the month of the revelation of the criterion.

"So whoever is present this month, let them fast" — the verse's command: the one who witnesses the month — is present, able, resident — fasts the month. The verse completes the sequence of the fast: 2:183 commanded the fast; 2:184 gave the limited days and the concessions; 2:185 identifies the month and the fuller obligation. The commentators note the abrogation: the previous verse's concession (the choice of feeding instead of fasting) was abrogated for the healthy resident; the fidyah remained for the old and the chronically ailing.

"But whoever is ill or on a journey, then let them fast an equal number of days after Ramadan" — the repetition of the concession: the ill and the traveler postpone; the number is made up later. "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship" — the verse's principle of the law: the divine intention is the ease — *al-yusr* — and the verse's statement is the principle of the religion: the command of Allah is the ease, not the hardship; the concession is not the exception to the principle — the concession is the expression of the principle.

"So that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you" — the verse's two purposes: the completion of the number — the fast is completed, not abandoned, in the month or after; and the takbir — the magnifying, the proclamation of the greatness of Allah — at the completion of the month, the takbir of the Eid: Allahu Akbar for having guided the believers to the fast, the Ramadan, the Qur'an, the worship. The verse's thanksgiving: "and perhaps you will be grateful" — the gratitude is the purpose of the worship and the fruit of the month of the fast.

In modern life, the verse is the charter of the month. The verse names the month and gives its identity: the month of the Qur'an — the guide, the clear proofs, the criterion. The fasting of the month is the response to the month's gift: the fast of the month of the revelation, the discipline of the body joined to the book of the guidance. The verse is also the principle of the religious ease: the religion is not the hardship; the ease is the divine intention; and the month's completion and the takbir, the thanksgiving, is the believer's response to the guidance.

## Verse 186

Verse 186 says: *"When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me. So let them respond ˹with obedience˺ to Me and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided ˹to the Right Way˺."*

In simple terms, the verse breaks into the fasting passage with the direct speech of Allah: When My servants ask you, O Prophet, about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me. So let them respond with obedience to Me and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided to the Right Way.

The verse's place is one of the most striking in the surah: it sits in the middle of the commands of the fasting, between the verses of the month (185) and the night (187). The commentators note the occasion: the Companions asked the Prophet, "Where is our Lord? Is He near so we should whisper, or far so we should call out?" — and the verse answers. The verse is Allah's own speech, answering directly, without the mediation of "say": the reply does not begin "Say, O Prophet"; it begins with the divine "I" — I am near, I respond. The point of the verse is the directness: the believer's prayer is not routed through a gatekeeper; the servant calls and the Lord answers.

"I am truly near" — the verse's *qarib* — the nearness. The commentators explain: near by His knowledge, His hearing, His response; not the nearness of the physical location — the Lord is above the heavens and the nearness is the nearness of the response. The verse is the answer to the separation: the believer may feel far from Allah, and the verse says: near. The nearness is the nearness of the one who hears the whisper, who knows the intention, who responds to the call.

"I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me" — the verse's *ujibu da'wata al-da'i* — I answer the call of the caller when he calls Me. The verse is the promise of the response: the call is answered. The commentators qualify carefully: the response is the divine answer — the granting, or the alternative, or the deferral for the better (the Qur'an and the Sunnah state the believer's beautiful patience with the unanswered-seeming prayer); but the verse's statement is the promise: the caller is not ignored. The verse's placement in the fast's passage is significant: the fasting person, hungry and thirsty, calls in the night of the Ramadan; the verse says: I am near, and I respond.

"So let them respond with obedience to Me and believe in Me" — the verse's second command: the response is mutual. The servant calls and the Lord answers; the servant responds and the Lord is obeyed. The verse's *liyastajibu li* — "let them respond to Me" — the obedience, the answering of the call of Allah; and "believe in Me" — the faith, the trust. The verse's structure: the call and the answer; the answer and the obedience; the obedience and the guidance: "perhaps they will be guided to the Right Way."

In modern life, the verse is the believer's standing before Allah. The modern person has lost the nearness: the distance, the abstraction, the mediation, the machinery. The verse says: I am near — the nearness that does not require the intermediary, the appointment, the ritual gate; I respond — the call that is answered; and the response — the obedience and the faith. The verse's message: the prayer is the direct line; the Lord is not far; and the one who calls and obeys is guided.

## Verse 187

Verse 187 says: *"It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them. Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you. So now you may be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you. ˹You may˺ eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night, then complete the fast until nightfall. Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not exceed them. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to people, so they may become mindful ˹of Him˺."*

In simple terms, the verse sets the limits of the night of the fast: it has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them. Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you. So now you may be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you. You may eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night, then complete the fast until nightfall. Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not exceed them.

The verse's history: in the beginning of Islam, the Muslims were permitted to eat, drink and have marital relations only until the Isha' prayer — unless one slept before the Isha'; the one who slept after the Isha' could not eat, drink or have relations until the next night. The Companions found this difficult; some, like the man who slept before breaking the fast, continued fasting and fainted by the afternoon (recorded in the tradition). The verse was revealed to end the practice: the night of the fast — the whole night — is permitted for the eating, the drinking, and the intimacy. Ibn Kathir records the earliest phase: the Muslims were allowed to eat, drink and have sexual intercourse only until the Isha' prayer, and the ayah brought the relief.

"Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them" — the verse's image: the *libas* — the garment, the covering, the screen, the repose. Ibn Abbas: the spouses are a repose for each other — the covering: the one covers the other's nakedness, the one is the protection of the other, the one is the intimacy of the other. The verse's image is the Qur'an's description of the marriage: the garment is the closest of the coverings — the one worn by the other, the one that protects and conceals and comforts — the marriage is the garment.

"Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you" — the verse's *tahtanu* — the self-deception: the Companions had been deceiving themselves — the ones who slept and then ate, or had relations after the sleep, the private violations of the earlier rule. Allah knows — He who is All-Knowing knows the self-deception — and the verse: He accepted the repentance and pardoned them. The verse's mercy: the earlier rule is not confirmed with the punishment; it is replaced with the permission and the pardon.

"So now you may be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you" — the verse's *fa al-ana bashiru hunna* — now be intimate with them — the permission; *wa ibtaghu ma kataba Allahu lakum* — and seek what Allah has prescribed for you — the offspring, the good, the mercy of the marriage (Ibn Abbas: the offspring). "You may eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night" — the verse's night limit: the eater and drinker continue until the dawn; the white thread of the dawn becomes distinct from the black thread of the night (the verse's famous image; the tradition of Bilal's adhan and the ascending whiteness, Muslim; the admonition of the samarah tradition: do not be stopped by Bilal's adhan or the ascending whiteness until it spreads). "Then complete the fast until nightfall" — the day: the fast from the dawn to the nightfall.

"Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques" — the verse's i'tikaf: the retreat in the mosque is the exception: the eating and drinking continue in the i'tikaf (the night permission), but the intimacy with the spouses is not permissible in the i'tikaf — "not even in the night" (Ma'arif). "These are the limits set by Allah" — the verse's *hudud Allah* — the limits: the fast, the night, the dawn, the nightfall, the i'tikaf — the limits of the religion; "so do not exceed them" — the verse's prohibition against the transgression; "This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to people, so they may become mindful of Him."

In modern life, the verse is the balance of the religion and the human. The verse does not make the religion the enemy of the human nature: the night is the night — the eating, the drinking, the marriage are the permitted; the day is the day — the fast. The verse's teaching: the religion is the limits, set by Allah, clear, and the mindfulness is the purpose of the clarity. The verse's marital image — the garment — is the teaching of the marriage: the covering, the protection, the intimacy, the repose. And the verse's balance: the command of the fast and the permission of the night; the worship and the humanity; the limits and the life within them.

## Verse 188

Verse 188 says: *"Do not consume one another's wealth unjustly, nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others' property, knowing that it is a sin."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the sanctity of the property: do not consume one another's wealth unjustly, nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others' property, knowing that it is a sin.

The verse's first prohibition: the consumption of the wealth unjustly — *la ta'kulu amwalakum baynakum bi al-batil* — do not eat your wealth among yourselves in falsehood. The "eating" is the consumption, the use, the devouring: the wealth of the others is consumed by the theft, the robbery, the deception, the fraud, the interest, the gambling. Ibn Kathir: in any illegal way — stealing, robbing, deceiving. Ma'arif: the verse covers the usurpation, the theft, the robbery, interest, gambling, bribery, defective sales and transactions, money acquired through lying or false oath — all haram and false; and "eating" means "using in the absolute sense" — by eating, drinking, wearing, or any other mode of use.

The verse's second prohibition: the bribery — *wa tudlu biha ila al-hukkam* — do not present it to the authorities, the judges, the rulers, in order to consume a portion of others' wealth sinfully while you know. Ibn Kathir: the verse is about the person who knows the claim is false and the property belongs to another, but presents the case to the authorities, hoping the judge will rule for him by the formal strength of the claim — while he knows the falsehood. The verse's *wa antum ta'lamun* — while you know it is unlawful: the sin is compounded by the knowledge; the one who knows the falsehood and proceeds is the one who consumes with the knowledge.

The commentators draw the important principle: the judgment of the authorities does not change the reality of the truth. Ibn Kathir: the ruling of the judge does not make the prohibited lawful or the lawful prohibited; if the ruling agrees with the truth there is no harm; otherwise the judge acquires his reward and the cheater acquires the evil burden. The verse's point: the one who wins the case by the bribe or the deception has not gained the property; he has consumed the sin. Maududi records the two meanings: the bribery of the magistrates, and the filing of the false claim against the other party's lack of evidence.

In modern life, the verse is the law of the property in the modern economy. The verse's command: the wealth is not to be consumed unjustly — the fraud, the theft, the corruption, the exploitation, the unfair transaction, the exploitation of the legal loophole. And the bribery: the modern world's case — the payment to the official, the judge, the regulator, the authority — the verse says: the one who bribes to win the property, the case, the contract, has consumed the sin. The verse's "while you know" is the modern conscience: the one who knows the transaction is unjust and proceeds has eaten the sin, whatever the court, the official, the paper says. The verse's principle: the legality is not the morality; the court's ruling is not the divine judgment.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 189

Verse 189 says: *"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, "They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage." Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺. So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors, and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful."*

In simple terms, the verse answers the two questions: they ask you about the phases of the moon. Say, they are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage. Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful of Allah. So enter your homes through their proper doors, and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful.

The first question: the phases of the moon. The verse's answer: *mawaqit* — they are means of time, the appointed times, the measures of the periods: the months, the dates, the pilgrimage. The verse's answer is the plain, functional answer: the moon's phases are not the pagan mystery; they are the clock and the calendar — the marks of the time for the people and the hajj. The commentators note the occasion: the questioners asked about the moon — why it appears thin like a thread, grows until full, then decreases — as if it never stays in one form; the verse answers the practical purpose.

The second matter: the entering of the houses from the back. The verse's context: in the Jahiliyyah, the people who assumed the ihram entered their houses from the back, believing it was the righteousness (al-Bara' in al-Bukhari: during the time of Jahiliyyah, they used to enter the house from the back upon assuming the ihram); some also did this upon returning from a journey (the Ansar tradition). The verse's answer: it is not the righteousness — the *birr* is not the entering from the back; the *birr* is the taqwa — the mindfulness. The verse's command: enter the houses through their doors — the proper, the normal, the command; and be mindful of Allah.

The verse's principle: the religion is not the invented piety — the custom that was not commanded, the ritual that was invented and called devout. The righteousness is the obedience; the entering from the back was the invented piety; the entering through the doors is the command. The verse's "enter the houses through their doors" is also the Qur'an's principle of the straightforwardness: the religion is the direct, the commanded, the proper — not the contrived, the reverse, the invented.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to the two modern questions: the question of the timekeeping (the moon — the verse says: the means of the time, the calendar, the appointed — the functional, not the superstitious); and the question of the invented piety. The modern person has his own "entering from the back": the invented rituals, the religious novelties, the customs dressed as the commands, the demonstrations of the piety that were never commanded. The verse says: the righteousness is the mindfulness; the entering through the doors is the obedience; the religion is not the invention but the command.

## Verse 190

Verse 190 says: *"Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the command of the defensive fighting: fight in the cause of Allah only against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors.

The verse's command: fight — *qatilu* — in the cause of Allah — *fi sabil Allah* — against those who fight you. The verse's three restrictions are the key: the cause (the cause of Allah — not the material interest, the tribal revenge, the plunder); the target (those who fight you — the combatants, the aggressors, not the whole of the people of the other faith); and the limit (do not exceed — the transgression is forbidden). Ibn Kathir: "Fight for the sake of Allah and do not be transgressors" — such as by committing prohibitions; and the Prophet's command recorded by Muslim (from Buraydah): "Fight for the sake of Allah, fight those who disbelieve in Allah... do not be treacherous, do not mutilate, do not kill children or the people of the monasteries."

The verse's historical position: the verses of the fighting were revealed in the time when the believers were permitted to fight in self-defense — after the years of the persecution and the expulsion from Mecca. The commentators note: earlier, when the Muslims were weak and scattered, they were asked merely to preach and be patient with the wrongful repression; the verse now grants the permission of the defensive fighting — the fighting against those who fight, in the cause of Allah, without the transgression. The verse's "in the cause of Allah" is the fundamental: the motivation of the fighting is the cause of Allah, not the revenge, not the territory, not the loot; the fighters of the Jahiliyyah fought for the plunder and the revenge; the believers fight for the cause.

"But do not exceed the limits" — the verse's *la ta'tadu* — the transgression forbidden: the killing of the non-combatants, the mutilation, the treachery, the destruction beyond the need, the continuation of the fighting after the cessation. The verse's "Allah does not like the transgressors" — the divine dislike of the transgression: the fighters are commanded the justice even in the war.

In modern life, the verse is the Qur'an's standard of the just war: the cause (the cause of Allah — the justice, the protection, the defense of the faith and the vulnerable); the target (those who fight — the combatants, not the civilians, not the children, not the people of the monasteries); and the limit (do not exceed — the proportionality, the restraint, the prohibition of the atrocities and the excess). The verse's message: the fighting is the permitted and the limited; the transgression is the forbidden; the cause is the justice and the fight is the defense. And the verse's warning: those who fight beyond the limits are the ones Allah does not like — whatever their cause.

## Verse 191

Verse 191 says: *"Kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. For persecution is far worse than killing. And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they attack you there. If they do so, then fight them—that is the reward of the disbelievers."*

In simple terms, the verse expands the command of the fighting: kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. For persecution is far worse than killing. And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they attack you there. If they do so, then fight them — that is the reward of the disbelievers.

The verse's first command: "kill them wherever you come upon them" — the command against the enemy who fights: the believers are not to stop at the borders; the war against the aggressors continues wherever they are encountered. And "drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out" — the expulsion of the aggressors from the places they took: the believers had been expelled from Mecca; the command: drive them out of the places they drove you from. Ibn Kathir: "your energy should be spent on fighting them, just as their energy is spent on fighting you, and on expelling them from the areas from which they have expelled you, as a law of equality in punishment."

"For persecution is far worse than killing" — the verse's famous principle: the *fitnah* is worse than the killing. The commentators explain the *fitnah* here as the persecution — the oppression that turns the believers from their religion, the harassment, the intimidation — and the verse: the persecution is worse than the killing. Ibn Kathir: these men are committing disbelief in Allah, associating with Him in the worship and hindering from His path, and this is a much greater evil and more disastrous than killing; "shirk (polytheism) is worse than killing." The verse's principle: the persecution of the faith is the greater evil, and the removal of the persecution is the justification of the fighting.

"And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they attack you there" — the verse's sanctuary exception: the Sacred Mosque, the Haram, is the place of the sanctity; the believers do not fight there — unless the enemy attacks them there. Ibn Kathir: "Do not fight them in the area of the Sacred Mosque unless they start fighting you in it. In this case, you are allowed to fight them and kill them to stop their aggression." The verse's balance: the sanctity of the place is honored; the aggression in the place is answered. "If they do so, then fight them — that is the reward of the disbelievers" — the battle at the Mosque, when the aggressors initiate, is the recompense of the disbelievers: the fighters of the aggression receive the consequence of the aggression.

In modern life, the verse is the Qur'an's standard of the war against the persecution. The verse's principle: the persecution of the faith — the oppression of the believers, the forced conversion, the harassment, the denial of the worship — is worse than the killing, and the believers are permitted the fight against the persecutors. And the verse's limits: the sanctuary is not fought except against the aggression; the sacred places are honored; the aggression is answered by the fight, and the fight is the recompense of the aggression. The verse's message: the faith is not to be persecuted; the persecution is the worse evil; and the fight against it is the commanded, limited, and just.

## Verse 192

Verse 192 says: *"But if they cease, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the condition of the cessation: but if they cease, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse's "if they cease" — *in intahaw* — if they stop, if they desist — from the fighting, from the persecution, from the hostility — the believers are not to continue. The verse's short length is the verse's force: after the command of the fighting (190–191), the verse states the end: the cessation of the enemy is the end of the fighting. Ibn Kathir: "If they (the polytheists) cease fighting you in the Sacred Area, and come to Islam and repent, then Allah will forgive them their sins, even if they had before killed Muslims in Allah's Sacred Area. Indeed, Allah's forgiveness encompasses every sin, whatever its enormity, when the sinner repents it."

The verse's second half: "then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — the divine forgiveness for the cease: the cessation is met by the forgiveness. The verse's point: the war is not the perpetual enmity; the war ends with the cessation, and the end is the mercy. The commentators read the forgiveness in the two ways: the forgiveness of the enemies who cease and repent — the sins of the past, even the killing in the sacred area, are forgiven by the repentance; and the forgiveness of the believers — the ones who fought justly are not held sinful for the fight that was the command.

The verse's structure: the fight is the command against the aggressors (190–191); the forgiveness is the command at the cessation (192). The verse's message: the enmity is not the religion's end; the religion's end is the reconciliation. The enemy who stops the persecution is met by the divine All-Forgiving, Most Merciful; the door of the forgiveness is open at the cessation.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the end of the conflict. The modern conflicts know the difficulty of the end: the wars continue beyond the cessation, the hostilities persist beyond the peace, the fighting is prolonged by the momentum of the revenge. The verse says: if they cease — the cessation is the end; the forgiveness is the divine response. The verse's message: the conflict of the believers ends with the cessation; the continuation after the cessation is the transgression; and the end of the conflict is the moment of the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful.

## Verse 193

Verse 193 says: *"Fight against them ˹if they persecute you˺ until there is no more persecution, and ˹your˺ devotion will be to Allah ˹alone˺. If they stop ˹persecuting you˺, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors."*

In simple terms, the verse states the purpose of the fighting: fight against them if they persecute you until there is no more persecution, and your devotion will be to Allah alone. If they stop persecuting you, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors.

"Until there is no more persecution" — the verse's purpose: the *fitnah* — the persecution — is removed. The verse's *hatta la takuna fitnah* — until there is no fitnah — is the objective of the fighting: not the conversion of the people, not the conquest, not the plunder — the removal of the persecution; the believers' fighting ends when the persecution ends. The commentators explain the fitnah in this verse as the state in which the object of the obedience is someone other than God: the persecution of the faith, the obstruction of the worship, the hindrance of the religion.

"And your devotion will be to Allah alone" — the verse's second clause: *wa yakuna al-din lillah* — and the religion will be for Allah. The verse's purpose: the worship is free; the religion is Allah's; the devotion is not forced by the persecution but is the free devotion to Allah. The verse's point: the fighting is not for the religion's sake as the powerful's religion; it is for the freedom of the religion — the worship of Allah, freely, without the persecution.

"If they stop persecuting you, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors" — the verse's third clause: the cessation is the end. Ibn Kathir: "If they stop their Shirk and fighting the believers, then cease warfare against them. Whoever fights them afterwards will be committing an injustice. Verily aggression can only be started against the unjust." The verse's *fa la 'udwana illa 'ala al-zalimin* — no hostility except against the wrongdoers: the enmity is not the religion's permanent state; the hostility is against the aggressors; the one who stops the aggression is no longer the aggressor and is no longer fought.

In modern life, the verse is the Qur'an's law of the just war in its fullest: the purpose (the removal of the persecution); the end (the religion free for Allah); the cessation (the end of the hostility when the persecution stops). The verse's message: the believers fight the persecution, not the people; the believers remove the oppression, not the believers' opponents; and the end of the persecution is the end of the fighting. The verse's modern application: the wars against the oppressive regimes, the persecution of the religion and the people — the objective is the persecution's removal; the hostility ends with the aggression's end; and the hostility is only against the aggressors.

## Verse 194

Verse 194 says: *"˹There will be retaliation in˺ a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation. So, if anyone attacks you, retaliate in the same manner. ˹But˺ be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those mindful ˹of Him˺."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the law of the retaliation in the sacred months: there will be retaliation in a sacred month for an offence in a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation. So, if anyone attacks you, retaliate in the same manner. But be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those mindful of Him.

The verse's context: the sacred months — the four months of the sanctity — in the Jahiliyyah and the Islam: the war was forbidden in the sacred months; the enemy had fought in the sacred month; the believers hesitated to retaliate in the same month, fearing the violation of the sanctity. The verse answers: there will be retaliation in the sacred month for the offense in the sacred month — the aggression in the sacred month is answered in the sacred month; the sanctity is not the enemy's shield. The commentators note: the way the state of defense grants an exception to observing the sanctity of the Haram of Mecca, it also allows an exception to observing the sanctity of the sacred months.

"And all violations will bring about retaliation" — the verse's *wa al-hurumatu qisas* — and the sacred things are [subject to] retaliation: the general principle of the violations; every violation of the sanctity brings the equivalent. The verse's principle: the sanctity is not the one-way: the one who violates the sanctity is subject to the retaliation of the sanctity; the violator cannot claim the sanctity against the retaliation.

"So, if anyone attacks you, retaliate in the same manner" — the verse's command: *fa man i'tada 'alaykum fa i'tadu 'alayhi bi-mithli ma i'tada 'alaykum* — whoever transgresses against you, you transgress likewise against him — the principle of the proportionality: the retaliation is the same manner, the equivalent, not the excess. Ibn Kathir (in the commentary on 2:193): "just as Allah said: whoever transgresses against you, you transgress likewise against him" — the equal return.

"But be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those mindful of Him" — the verse's command: the taqwa in the retaliation; the retaliation is not the raw revenge; the retaliation is the command of the mindfulness, and the mindfulness is the presence of Allah with the mindful. The verse's structure: the retaliation (the like for like); the mindfulness (the guard against the excess); the presence (Allah is with the mindful). The verse's message: the justice is the like-for-like, and the justice is guarded by the mindfulness; the one who retaliates the equal and keeps the mindfulness is the one with Allah.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the proportionate response. The modern world's wars and conflicts know the escalation: the one offense answered by the ten, the one attack answered by the total. The verse says: the retaliation is in the same manner; the offense in the sacred month is answered in the sacred month; the violation is answered by the equivalent — not by the excess. The verse's message: the justice is the like-for-like; the excess is the transgression; the mindfulness is the guard; and Allah is with the mindful.

## Verse 195

Verse 195 says: *"Spend in the cause of Allah and do not let your own hands throw you into destruction ˹by withholding˺. And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the spending: spend in the cause of Allah and do not let your own hands throw you into destruction by withholding. And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers.

The verse's command: the spending — *anfiqu* — in the cause of Allah — *fi sabil Allah*: the spending in the jihad, in the obedience, in the ways of the drawing closer to Allah. Ibn Kathir: "The Ayah includes the order to spend in Allah's cause, in the various areas and ways that involve obedience and drawing closer to Allah. It especially applies to spending in fighting the enemies and on what strengthens the Muslims against the enemy." The verse follows the command of the fighting: the fighting requires the spending; the cause of Allah is carried by the wealth as well as the life.

"And do not let your own hands throw you into destruction by withholding" — the verse's phrase: *wa la tulqu bi-aydikum ila al-tahlukah* — do not throw yourselves with your own hands into destruction. The commentators read the "destruction" in several ways. The most common (Hudhayfah, al-Bukhari; Abu Ayyub): the destruction is the withholding of the spending — the one who refrains from spending in the cause of Allah, especially in the fighting, throws himself into the destruction (the enemy gains the advantage, the cause is weakened, the self is ruined). Abu Ayyub's narration: the verse was revealed about the companions who said when Islam became strong, "warfare has ceased, let us go back to our families and children" — the verse commanded the continuation of the spending and the jihad; the "destruction" refers to staying with the families and estates and abandoning the jihad. Other readings (Ibn Abbas): the destruction is the sin and the refusal to repent; or the despair of Allah's mercy. The verse's core: the withholding from the cause of Allah is the self-destruction; the spending is the self-preservation.

"And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers" — the verse's command: the ihsan — the doing good, the excellence — and the love of Allah for the muhsinin. The verse's message: the spending is the good; the good is the excellence; the excellence is loved by Allah: "and do good. Truly, Allah loves al-Muhsinin (those who do good)" (Ibn Kathir: the ihsan is the highest part of the acts of obedience).

In modern life, the verse is the command of the generosity and the cause. The modern person's wealth: the spending in the cause of the justice, the relief, the faith, the good. The verse says: spend — the wealth is the instrument of the cause; and the withholding is the destruction — the person who withholds from the cause of the good has thrown himself into the destruction: the cause is weakened, the good is lost, the self is ruined. The verse's message: the spending is not the loss; the spending is the preservation; the good is loved by Allah — and the one who does the good is the one who is loved.

## Verse 196

Verse 196 says: *"Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺, then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford. And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination. But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment ˹requiring shaving˺, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering. In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford. Whoever cannot afford that ˹offering˺, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning ˹home˺—completing ten. These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House. And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment."*

In simple terms, the verse prescribes the pilgrimage and its rules: complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. But if prevented from proceeding, then offer whatever sacrificial animals you can afford. And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination. But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment requiring shaving, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering. In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage, then make the sacrificial offering you can afford. Whoever cannot afford that offering, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning home, completing ten. These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House.

The verse's first command: the completion of the hajj and the umrah — for Allah. The pilgrimage is the completion — *atammu* — the perfect, the full, the complete performance of the rites. The verse's "for Allah" is the dedication: the pilgrimage is not the travel, the tourism, the show; the pilgrimage is for Allah — complete for Allah.

"But if prevented from proceeding" — the verse's ihsar: the one who is prevented from completing the pilgrimage — by the illness, the enemy, the barrier — offers whatever sacrificial animal he can afford, and the restriction is released. Ibn Kathir: if one is prevented while on route, he slaughters the sacrifice, shaves his head and ends the ihram. The hadith (Hafsah, in the Sahihayn): the Prophet said, "I matted my hair and I have garlanded my Hady, so I will not finish my ihram till I offer the sacrifice." The verse's mercy: the prevented one is not bound by the impossible; he offers the sacrifice at the point of the prevention.

"And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination" — the verse's rule: the shaving (the end of the ihram) is after the sacrifice reaches its place; the ihram is not ended until the offering is made. "But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment requiring shaving, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering" — the verse's fidyah: the Ka'b ibn 'Ujrah case — the one sick or with the ailment of the head (the lice, the pain) who shaves — the compensation: the fast three days, or the feed of six poor persons (the charity), or the sacrifice. Ibn Kathir: the Prophet said to Ka'b: shave it, then fast three days, or feed six poor people, or sacrifice an animal.

"In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford" — the verse's tamattu': the one who performs the umrah in the months of the hajj and then the hajj — the combined pilgrimage — offers the hadi (the sacrificial animal). "Whoever cannot afford that offering, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning home, completing ten" — the alternative: the three days during the hajj (before the day of Arafah, per the tradition of Ibn Abbas) and the seven after the return — the ten complete. "These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House" — the verse's condition: the tamattu' ruling is for the non-residents of Mecca; the residents of the Sacred House are not bound by the offering. "And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment" — the verse's close.

In modern life, the verse is the charter of the pilgrimage: the completion — the full, the dedicated; the prevention — the mercy of the alternative; the sacrifice — the offering of the means; the compensation — the mercy of the ailment; the tamattu' — the combination and the offering; and the mindfulness — the whole of the pilgrimage under the mindfulness of Allah. The pilgrimage is the journey, and the verse's laws are the mercy that makes the journey possible for the ill, the prevented, the poor — and the mindfulness that makes it the worship.

Whoever commits to ˹performing˺ pilgrimage, let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, Allah ˹fully˺ knows of it. Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness. And be mindful of Me, O people of reason!"*

In simple terms, the verse sets the months and the discipline of the pilgrimage: commitment to pilgrimage is made in appointed months. Whoever commits to performing pilgrimage, let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, Allah fully knows of it. Take necessary provisions for the journey — surely the best provision is righteousness. And be mindful of Me, O people of reason!

The verse's first clause: the pilgrimage is in the well-known months. The commentators: Shawwal, Dhul-Qa'dah, and the first ten days of Dhul-Hijjah (Ibn Abbas, Jabir, 'Ata', Tawus, Mujahid); some say the whole of Dhul-Hijjah. The verse's point: the hajj is in the appointed months, as the prayer has its fixed time; the pilgrimage is not a matter of the any-time convenience — it is the appointed, the fixed, the well-known.

"Whoever commits to performing pilgrimage" — the verse's *farada* — the one who makes the hajj obligatory upon himself by the ihram — "let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage" — the verse's three prohibitions of the pilgrimage: the *rafath* (the intimate relations), the *fusuq* (the wickedness, the foul language, the sin), the *jidal* (the arguments, the disputes). The verse's discipline: the pilgrimage is the worship; the worship is the leaving of the intimacy, the foulness, and the disputation. The verse's *rafath* is the marital/intimate relations during the ihram; the *fusuq* is the sins in the journey; the *jidal* is the quarreling, the contention — the pilgrimage is the season of the purity, the virtue, and the peace.

"Whatever good you do, Allah fully knows of it" — the verse's *ma taf'alu min khayr* — the command of the good in the pilgrimage: the good deeds, the charity, the remembrance — Allah knows them. "Take necessary provisions for the journey — surely the best provision is righteousness" — the verse's *zad al-taqwa* — the best provision is the taqwa. The verse's context: the people used to come to the hajj without the provisions, considering it the righteousness (the takaful); the verse says: take the provisions, and the best provision is the righteousness. The verse's wisdom: the journey's provision (the food, the water, the means) and the journey's better provision (the taqwa); the pilgrimage's need and the pilgrimage's purpose. "And be mindful of Me, O people of reason!" — the verse's address: *ya uli al-albab* — the people of the reason — the ones who understand: the pilgrimage's wisdom is the mindfulness.

In modern life, the verse is the pilgrimage's discipline and the verse's provision. The pilgrimage's months — the appointed; the pilgrimage's discipline — the purity, the virtue, the peace: the leaving of the intimacy, the foulness, and the arguments; the pilgrimage's provision — the necessary means and the better means: the taqwa. The verse's message: the journey of the worship is the journey of the purity; the one who prepares the journey's means and the journey's mindfulness is the one who understands: the best provision is the righteousness — the protection of the journey and the worship.

## Verse 198

Verse 198 says: *"There is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord ˹during this journey˺. When you return from 'Arafât, praise Allah near the sacred place and praise Him for having guided you, for surely before this ˹guidance˺ you were astray."*

In simple terms, the verse permits the trade in the pilgrimage and commands the remembrance at the return from Arafat: there is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord during this journey. When you return from Arafat, praise Allah near the sacred place and praise Him for having guided you, for surely before this guidance you were astray.

The verse's first clause: "there is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord" — the verse's *fadl* — the bounty: the trade, the commerce, the earning during the pilgrimage. The verse's context: the Jahiliyyah people believed the trade in the seasons of the pilgrimage was forbidden; the believers hesitated. The verse says: there is no blame — the trade is permitted; the bounty of the Lord is sought; the commerce in the pilgrimage is not the violation of the pilgrimage's worship. Umar's reply to the question: "Indeed! People had no other way of earning a living!" (recorded in the tradition) — and he recited the verse. The commentators note the verse's "no blame" — the permission, and the hint that the avoidance, if possible, is still better because it affects the ideal of the perfect sincerity in the hajj.

"When you return from Arafat" — the verse's *afada* — the departure, the return from Arafat after the standing of the day — "praise Allah near the sacred place" — the verse's *al-mash'ar al-haram* — the sacred monument: the mountain in Muzdalifah; the verse's command: the dhikr of Allah at Muzdalifah — the praise, the takbir, the remembrance — after the departure from Arafat.

"And praise Him for having guided you, for surely before this guidance you were astray" — the verse's remembrance: the praise of the guidance; the acknowledgment of the being astray. The verse's frame: the trade is permitted (the world's need), the worship is the pilgrimage (the remembrance), and the remembrance is the gratitude for the guidance (the being astray before).

In modern life, the verse is the balance of the worship and the livelihood. The pilgrimage is the worship, and the worship is not the renunciation of the livelihood: the trade is permitted; the earning is the bounty of the Lord; the journey is the gathering of the worship and the work. And the verse's central: the remembrance — the praise of the guidance; the believer's journey — from the astray to the guided — and the praise at the sacred place: the gratitude for the guidance that came, the acknowledgment of the astray that was. The verse's message: the believer's life is the journey from the astray to the guided, and the praise of the Lord at the journey's sacred places is the gratitude of the journey.

## Verse 199

Verse 199 says: *"Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. And seek Allah's forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the departure with all the pilgrims: then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. And seek Allah's forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse's command: "go forth with the rest of the pilgrims" — the verse's *afidu min haythu afada al-nas* — depart from where the people depart: the departure from Muzdalifah with all the people. The verse's context: the Quraysh and the Hums — the ones who called themselves the people of Allah — used to stand at Muzdalifah and depart from there, while the rest of the Arabs stood at Arafat; the Hums considered the standing at Arafat beneath them. The verse's message: the pilgrimage is the equal pilgrimage: the Hums do not depart from the better place; the people of Allah are not the ones who depart from the special place — the believers depart from where the people depart; the worship is the equal, the community, the common.

The verse's "the rest of the pilgrims" — the equality: the believers are not the separate ones, the special ones, the ones who depart from the sacred place first; they go forth with the people. The verse's correction of the elitism of the Hums: the "we are the people of Allah" of the Quraysh is corrected by the command: go forth with the people — the ummah is the people, and the worship is the common.

"And seek Allah's forgiveness" — the verse's command: the istighfar — the seeking of the forgiveness — at the end of the pilgrimage. The verse's place: after the standing at Arafat, after the remembrance at Muzdalifah, after the departure with the people — the seek of the forgiveness. The pilgrimage's course: the standing, the remembrance, the departure, the forgiveness. "Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — the verse's close: the divine names of the forgiveness — the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful — the ones who are asked.

In modern life, the verse is the correction of the spiritual elitism and the command of the humility. The modern religious person has the temptation of the elitism: the special practice, the better place, the higher rank, the "we depart from where the others depart" — and the verse says: go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. The worship is the common; the community is the most of the people; the believer is not the one above the people but the one with the people. And the verse's second: the seeking of the forgiveness — the pilgrimage's end is the humility before Allah: the standing, the departure, the forgiveness. The verse's message: the worship's finish is the istighfar — the believers leave the sacred places with the acknowledgment of the sin and the seeking of the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful.

## Verse 200

Verse 200 says: *"When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately. There are some who say, "Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world," but they will have no share in the Hereafter."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the remembrance after the rites: when you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers before Islam, or even more passionately. There are some who say, "Our Lord! Grant us Your bounties in this world," but they will have no share in the Hereafter.

The verse's first command: the remembrance after the completion of the rites. The verse's context: after the hajj, the Arabs used to hold rallies at Mina — remembering their forefathers: the stories of the achievements, the genealogies, the extravagant self-praise (Maududi's note). The verse says: praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers — or even more passionately — the remembrance that was given to the fathers is now given to Allah: the dhikr of Allah replaces the dhikr of the fathers; the gatherings of the tribal glory become the gatherings of the divine remembrance. The verse's command: the memory of the fathers (the pride, the genealogy, the achievements) is replaced by the remembrance of Allah (the praise, the mercy, the greatest); the "or even more passionately" — the remembrance of Allah is the more passionate, the deeper, the greater.

"There are some who say, 'Our Lord! Grant us Your bounties in this world,' but they will have no share in the Hereafter" — the verse's contrast: the supplication of the worldly. The verse's *rabbana atina fi al-dunya* — "Our Lord, give us in this world" — the prayer of the ones who ask only the world: the wealth, the livestock, the slaves, the goods (Ibn Abbas's enumeration); and the verse: they will have no share in the Hereafter. Ibn Kathir: this criticism serves to discourage people from imitating those mentioned — the ones who ask only the world. The verse's frame: the remembrance is the dhikr of Allah; the supplication is the prayer of the world (the one who asks only the world) or the prayer of the both worlds (the one who asks the hasanah of the world and the hasanah of the Hereafter, 201 — the next verse); and the verse's contrast: the world-only request has no share in the Hereafter.

In modern life, the verse is the examination of the remembrance and the request. The modern person remembers: the ancestors, the tribe, the nation, the glory, the achievements — and the verse says: praise Allah as you praise your forefathers, or more: the remembrance of the Lord replaces the remembrance of the self. And the request: the modern person prays — and the verse's question: what is asked? The verse's *rabbana atina fi al-dunya* — the request of the world only — the wealth, the success, the goods — has no share in the Hereafter; and the prayer of the two worlds (201) is the comprehensive. The verse's message: the believer's rites end with the remembrance of Allah, and the believer's prayer asks the good of both the worlds — the world's good (the wealth, the health, the guidance) and the Hereafter's good (the mercy, the paradise, the salvation).
