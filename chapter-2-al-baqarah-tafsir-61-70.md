# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 61–70

The catalogue of ten favours closed at verse 60 with water from a rock and one condition: do not spread corruption. What follows now is the verdict, and it opens with an act of pure ingratitude. They had food that arrived daily without labour, and they complained about the menu (61). Then, in the middle of that indictment, comes one of the most generous and most misread verses in the Qur'an — an open door extended to every believing community (62). The covenant is recalled, and the mountain raised above them to receive it, and the turning away that followed, and the grace that alone kept them from ruin (63–64). Then the Sabbath-breakers and the transformation that has been used as a slur for fourteen centuries, and the lesson the God-conscious alone can read in it (65–66). And finally the story that gives this surah its name — a command to slaughter any cow at all, which three rounds of questions turned into an almost impossible task (67–70). The arc is deliberate: the surah has finished proving that the failure was never on the supply side, and it now turns to show exactly what the failure was made of.

## Verse 61

**"And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for.” They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah’s signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations."**

In simple terms: the Children of Israel complained that they were tired of the manna and quails, and asked Moses to pray for ordinary vegetables instead. Moses rebuked them for wanting to trade something better for something worse, and told them that if they wanted the produce of the earth, they could go down to a city and get it — and it would cost them their standing. The verse closes by naming the real cause of their disgrace.

*Lan nasbira 'ala ta'amin wahidin* — "we will never endure one kind of food." Note the verb: *lan nasbira*, we will never be patient. This is the same construction used in verse 55, where they said *lan nu'mina* — we will never believe. The surah is tracking a single disposition expressed in different keys: an inability to bear anything. It is not the food that is the problem. It is the incapacity to receive.

*Fa-ud'u lana rabbaka* — "so call upon your Lord for us." Watch the pronoun. *Rabbaka* — your Lord, not ours. They want the benefit of the connection without the relationship. They acknowledge that Moses has access and they want him to use it on their behalf, while they themselves hold back. Almost every age has produced this posture: "you pray, I'll take the result."

*Min baqliha wa qiththa'iha wa fumiha wa 'adasiha wa basaliha* — "of its herbs, its cucumbers, its garlic, its lentils and its onions." There is a genuine and well-known disagreement over one word in this list. *Fum* is explained by most commentators as garlic, which fits the context — they wanted flavour, piquancy, something to break the monotony — and a variant reading with *tha* in place of *fa* is transmitted, which is the usual word for garlic. A second body of opinion explained *fum* as wheat or grain. The majority lean is towards garlic, and the difference does not affect the meaning of the verse: they wanted the ordinary produce of the earth instead of what came to them from the heaven.

*Wa qiththa'iha* — "its cucumbers." The Arabic is قِثَّائِهَا, qiththā': a long, ridged cucumber/gherkin.

*A-tastabdiluna alladhi huwa adna bi-alladhi huwa khayrun* — "would you exchange what is lower for what is better?" This is the crux of the verse, and the word to notice is *adna* — lower, baser, nearer to the ground. What made the manna * khayr*, better? Not its flavour. It was better because it came directly from Allah with no labour, because it was a standing miracle renewed every morning, and because receiving it was itself an act of worship. The lentils and onions were "lower" not because vegetables are bad but because they were the ordinary produce of the earth, obtainable anywhere, requiring only farming — a step down from being fed by the hand of God. The commentators put it simply: they traded the food of honour for the food of effort, the miraculous for the ordinary, the direct gift for the cultivated crop.

That structure — trading what is better for what is worse — is the anatomy of every bad bargain a human being makes, and the Qur'an names it repeatedly. "Those are the ones who have purchased error at the price of guidance" (2:16). "They preferred the life of this world over the Hereafter" (14:3). The trade is always the same in shape: something certain, present and small, exchanged for something greater that requires trust.

*Ihbitu misran fa-inna lakum ma sa-altum* — "go down to any city, and you will have what you asked for." *Misr* means either Egypt specifically or a great city generally; the commentators held both, and the second is the more common. Note the verb: *ihbitu*, go down. It is the same verb used for the descent of Adam in verse 38, and it carries the same downward sense here. If they wanted the food of the earth, they had to leave the station in which they were being fed from the heaven and descend to the ordinary cities where food is farmed. They chose the produce and lost the privilege — and the choice was theirs.

Now the hardest clause in the verse, and the one that requires the most care.

*Wa duribat 'alayhim al-dhillatu wa al-maskanatu wa ba'u bi-ghadabin mina Allahi* — "and humiliation and wretchedness were struck over them, and they brought upon themselves wrath from Allah." The verb *duribat* means literally "was struck" — the image is of a dome stamped down over them, or a seal struck onto a coin. *Dhillah* is abasement, *maskanah* is wretchedness and low estate. And *ba'u bi-ghadabin* means they came back bearing wrath — they incurred it, carried it home.

This clause has been used for centuries as a weapon against Jewish people, and it must therefore be read with precision on four points.

First, the verse itself gives the cause, and the cause is conduct, not descent: *dhalika bi-annahum* — that is *because* they rejected the signs of Allah and killed the prophets unjustly, *because* they disobeyed and transgressed. A decree about an ethnic essence would not be phrased as a consequence of acts.

Second, this surah itself abolishes inherited guilt in so many words: "That was a nation which has passed on. It will have what it earned, and you will have what you have earned, and you will not be asked about what they used to do" (2:134; repeated at 2:141). A reader who takes verse 61 as a verdict on a people for all time has contradicted a verse in the same surah.

Third, the Qur'an immediately qualifies. The very next verse extends the promise of reward to every believing community. And 3:113 says of the People of the Scripture: "They are not all alike. Among them is an upright community who recite the verses of Allah through the night, prostrating; they believe in Allah and the Last Day, enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong, and hasten to good deeds — and those are among the righteous."

Fourth, the principle is universal, not particular. Read 16:112: "Allah presents the example of a city that was safe and secure, its provision coming to it in abundance from every place, but it denied the favours of Allah, so Allah made it taste the envelopment of hunger and fear for what they had been doing." Nothing about that is specific to one people. The same law operates on every community, including the Muslim one — which is precisely why this surah rebukes the believers themselves at verse 44 and why 61:2–3 addresses the identical rebuke to them.

And the record of the Prophet ﷺ settles the tone. When a funeral procession passed him and he stood, and it was pointed out that the deceased was a Jew, he said: "Was he not a soul?" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). That is the Islamic posture, and it is not a modern accommodation; it is the earliest example we have.

One further clarification the verse invites. The charge of killing prophets is repeated in the Qur'an (3:21, 3:112, 3:181, 4:155, 5:70), and it is serious. But it is not unrestricted: the Qur'an explicitly denies that they killed Jesus — "they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them" (4:157). The charge is real, specific and bounded, and it cannot be inflated into a general accusation.

For practice, the first half of this verse is more immediately useful to most readers than the second. The disease it names is *lan nasbira 'ala ta'amin wahidin* — I cannot bear to eat the same thing again. It is the restlessness that cannot receive a blessing twice without tiring of it. It is the marriage that has become monotonous, the job that has lost its shine, the provision that has become invisible through repetition. Gratitude is not a feeling you wait for; it is a discipline exercised precisely on the things that have stopped feeling special. The manna arrived every morning for years and it never stopped being a miracle. What changed was the eyes.

And the second application is the trade. Every person is making *istibdal* — exchange — all the time: comfort for obedience, the immediate for the lasting, the approval of people for the pleasure of Allah. Moses' question is the one to ask at the point of decision, and it takes four words: *a-tastabdiluna alladhi huwa adna bi-alladhi huwa khayr*? Are you really trading the better for the worse?

## Verse 62

**"Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."**

In simple terms: whoever — from among any of these named communities — truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does righteous deeds, will be rewarded by his Lord and will be safe on the Day of Resurrection.

The four groups. *Alladhina amanu*, "those who believe." *Alladhina hadu*, "those who became Jews" — from a root meaning to turn or to return, and the commentators explained it either as turning in repentance to the religion of Moses or as a name derived from Judah. *Al-Nasara*, the Christians — most commonly explained as named after Nazareth, or after the disciples' self-description as the "helpers" (*ansar*) of Allah in 3:52 and 61:14. And *al-Sabi'un*, the Sabians, who are the genuinely uncertain group. The classical commentators described them variously as a people who revered the stars, or as a community between the Jews and the Christians, or as monotheists without a revealed law. Some modern scholars identify them with the Mandaeans of southern Iraq, who call themselves Sabians; but the classical descriptions do not match Mandaean belief closely, so the identification remains uncertain. The jurists differed over whether the Sabians count as People of the Scripture — Abu Hanifah's position, on the strength of this very verse grouping them with the Jews and Christians, was that they do; other schools expressed caution because their actual beliefs were unclear. A small grammatical note: the word appears in the accusative form *al-sabi'ina*, coordinating onto the underlying grammatical position of the preceding relative clause, which the grammarians discuss.

Now the crux, which is the most consequential question in this verse and one of the most consequential in the Qur'an.

Does this mean Jews, Christians and Sabians are saved as they are? The answer turns entirely on the conditional clause. The verse does not say "each of these groups is acceptable as it stands." It says: from among all of them, *man amana* — whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good. The conditional governs every group named. Membership is not the criterion; faith and deed are.

So the question becomes: what counts as believing in Allah and the Last Day *after* the coming of Muhammad ﷺ? Here the commentators speak with one dominant voice, and it must be stated plainly because this verse is quoted constantly in interfaith conversation and quoted wrongly in both directions.

The majority classical position is that this verse concerns the sincere believers of the earlier communities in their own times — those who truly followed Moses in his era, those who truly followed Jesus in his era, and those seekers who recognised the Prophet when he came or died around the time of his mission without having encountered it. Their faith was valid in its time. After the mission, the criterion includes him. The textual basis for this is explicit: "And whoever desires other than Islam as religion, never will it be accepted from him, and he in the Hereafter will be among the losers" (3:85); and the hadith recorded by Muslim from Abu Hurayrah, in which the Prophet ﷺ swore: "By Him in Whose hand is the soul of Muhammad, no one of this community hears of me — whether Jew or Christian — and then dies without believing in that with which I was sent, except that he will be among the companions of the Fire."

A second reading, held by some commentators, restricts the verse to those among the People of the Scripture who did accept Muhammad ﷺ. A third reading treats the verse as specified rather than abrogated by 3:85 — noting that the conditional clause already does the restricting work, so no abrogation is needed. Some early authorities did claim abrogation; the stronger reading is specification. And a minority of contemporary readers take the verse as a statement of salvific pluralism. That reading exists and should be acknowledged, but it does not represent the classical consensus, and it sits uneasily with both 3:85 and the Muslim hadith.

Now the boundary that must be stated alongside all of this, because it is where the verse is most often mishandled in practice.

**No human being may take this verse and use it to declare any individual person destined for the Fire.** The judgment belongs to Allah alone, and He alone knows who received the message, in what form, with what clarity, and with what sincerity. The Qur'an says so directly about exactly this question: "Indeed, those who have believed and those who were Jews and the Sabians and the Christians and the Magians and those who associated others with Allah — Allah will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection. Indeed Allah is, over all things, Witness" (22:17). And again: "The Jews say the Christians stand on nothing, and the Christians say the Jews stand on nothing, though they both recite the Scripture... So Allah will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they used to differ" (2:113).

This is why the classical tradition also developed the question of *ahl al-fatra* — those who lived in an interval between messengers and never received the message in a recognisable form — over which the scholars held three positions, as noted at verse 39 of this batch's predecessor. A whole category of people is held back from human verdict because their circumstances are known only to Allah.

*Wa la khawfun 'alayhim wa la hum yahzanan* — "and no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve." The same double negation that closed verse 38: *khawf* is dread of what lies ahead, *huzn* is sorrow over what lies behind. The promise covers both directions of the heart. And note the near-identical verse later in this surah: "Yes — whoever submits his whole self to Allah and is a doer of good will have his reward with his Lord, and no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve" (2:112). The parallel is close enough that some commentators read the two together as one statement.

Two practical consequences follow, and both matter for how a Muslim lives.

The first is a discipline of hope and humility. The verse names four communities and extends the promise to whoever among them meets the stated conditions. It is a remarkably generous statement to place in the middle of a passage that has been indicting the Children of Israel for six verses. It teaches the reader to state the criteria clearly and then leave the verdict to Allah — which is the only posture available to someone who does not know hearts.

The second is about the conditions themselves. Note that the verse conjoins belief *and* deed: "believes in Allah and the Last Day **and does good**." Faith in the Qur'an is never a bare interior state that leaves conduct untouched. Whoever wants to claim the promise of this verse must claim it on the verse's own terms, and the terms include a life that shows it.

## Verse 63

**"And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.”"**

In simple terms: Allah took a solemn covenant from them, raised the mountain over their heads as they received it, and commanded them to take the scripture with firm resolve and to keep its contents in mind and in action, so that they might attain *taqwa*.

*Wa idh akhadhna mithaqakum* — "and when We took your covenant." The *mithaq* has appeared already at verse 27 (the covenant that is broken) and verse 40 (the covenant owed to the Children of Israel). Here it is taken formally, at Sinai, and the circumstances are extraordinary.

*Wa rafa'na fawqakum al-tura* — "and We raised the mount above you." The fullest account is at 7:171: "And when We raised the mountain above them as if it were a canopy, and they were certain it was going to fall upon them: 'Take what We have given you with determination and remember what is in it, that you may be mindful of Allah.'" The majority of commentators understood this literally — the mountain was uprooted and held over them as a sign and a warning, and they accepted the covenant under it. Some read the raising as figurative, meaning that the command came to them from the mount with overwhelming majesty rather than that the mountain physically moved. The literal reading is the dominant one, and the transmitted reports describe them prostrating and glancing upward in fear that it would fall. Because those descriptions come from reports rather than from the Qur'anic text, they are cited as reports; the Qur'an itself establishes only that the mountain was raised above them.

The image is worth sitting with for a moment, because it tells us something about human nature. Here were a people who had watched the sea split and an army drown, who were being fed from the sky, and who still needed a mountain suspended over their heads before they would take a covenant seriously. The external sign had to become a physical threat before the internal response arrived. That is the diagnosis the surah has been building since verse 6.

*Khudhu ma ataynakum bi-quwwatin* — "take what We have given you with strength." *Quwwah* here is not physical force. The commentators explained it as resolve, seriousness and effort: memorise it, understand it, act on it, and do not treat it lightly. Note the shape of the command. Revelation is not offered as a suggestion to be sampled; it is to be seized with both hands. The opposite of *quwwah* in this verse is the casual, part-time, take-it-or-leave-it engagement with religion that is the norm in every comfortable age, including ours.

*Wa-dhkuru ma fihi* — "and remember what is in it." The Arabic says literally "remember what is in it," and the translation you are reading renders it "observe its teachings." That is not a mistranslation but an interpretation, and it is the right one: *dhikr* in the Qur'an is rarely bare recollection, and the commentators understood it here to mean holding the contents in mind in a way that shows in conduct — studying it, reflecting on it, and acting by it. To "remember" the scripture in the Qur'anic sense is to let it govern. Forgetting it, correspondingly, is not losing information but neglecting to live by it — which is why the Qur'an says of the hypocrites "they forgot Allah, so He forgot them" (9:67).

*La'allakum tattaqun* — "so that you may be mindful." *Taqwa* is the goal, and it is the goal the surah named at the very beginning as the purpose of the whole Book: "a guidance for those who are mindful of Allah" (2:2). Note the chain the verse constructs: covenant → scripture → taken with resolve → remembered and acted upon → *taqwa*. Every link is necessary. A person can possess the scripture, study it, even memorise it completely, and still miss the stated end of the whole exercise. The end is not information, not scholarship, not ritual competence. It is *taqwa* — the lived awareness of Allah that changes what you do when nobody is watching.

The same command with the same phrasing appears at 2:93 and 7:171, and the parallel account at 4:154 adds the Sabbath and the gate. What the repetition tells us is that this is the standing terms of the relationship in every revelation: take it seriously, live by it, and let it produce God-consciousness.

For practice, this verse gives a three-part self-examination that is unusually direct. First: am I taking the scripture with *quwwah* — with effort and resolve, or as a background presence in my life? Second: am I remembering what is *in* it, meaning am I letting its contents actually determine decisions, or do I know it exists and consult it rarely? Third, and this is the one that settles the other two: is the result *taqwa*? If the answer to the third is no, the first two have not happened in the sense this verse means, whatever the outward appearance.

## Verse 64

**"Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers."**

In simple terms: despite the covenant and the mountain, they turned away — and nothing but Allah's unearned favour and His mercy stood between them and utter loss.

*Thumma tawallaytum min ba'di dhalika* — "then you turned away after that." *Tawalla* means to turn one's back, to withdraw oneself and go. It belongs to the same root as *wali*, the guardian or protecting friend, so the word carries the sense of withdrawing allegiance rather than merely walking away. And the phrase *min ba'di dhalika* — "after that" — is doing heavy lifting. It is the third time in this passage the surah has used it: after the calf (verse 52), after the demand to see Allah (verse 56), and now after the covenant taken under a suspended mountain. The pattern is the argument. Turning away is one thing; turning away *after that* is another, and it is the thing the surah keeps returning to.

*Fa-lawla fadlu Allahi 'alaykum wa rahmatuhu* — "and were it not for the bounty of Allah upon you and His mercy." Two nouns, and the commentators were careful to distinguish them. *Fadl* is bounty — what He gives that was never owed. *Rahmah* is mercy — His withholding of what was owed. The first is the gift; the second is the non-punishment. Both are named because both are needed, and the distinction is worth holding: a person may be grateful for what Allah has given and forget that the greater favour is often what He has not taken away.

*La-kuntum min al-khasirin* — "you would certainly have been among the losers." Note the word. *Al-khasirun* is exactly the word used at verse 27 for those who break the covenant after confirming it: "those are the losers." The surah has brought its own opening argument full circle. What verse 27 described in principle, verse 64 describes in the actual history of an actual people. They were the covenant-breakers of verse 27, and they were the losers — except that grace intervened.

That is the theological heart of this verse, and it deserves to be stated without hedging. **No one is saved by his deeds.** The people who received a covenant under a mountain and then broke it had no balance in their favour; only *fadl* and *rahmah* stood between them and loss. The Prophet ﷺ said the same about himself, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah: "None of you will be saved by his deeds." They asked, "Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?" He said, "Not even me — unless Allah envelops me in His mercy and bounty."

This is not a licence to neglect deeds. The tradition is consistent that deeds are the necessary evidence and the appointed means, and that the one who has no deeds has no ground for hope. But the price of Paradise is not paid by the creature; it is covered by grace. The distinction matters pastorally. The person who believes he is earning his way to Paradise becomes either proud or exhausted, and often both in sequence. The person who knows he is entering by grace becomes grateful — and gratitude, as verse 52 told us, is the actual purpose of being forgiven.

The practical reach of this verse has two directions.

The first is humility. If the generation that was fed from the sky, shaded by cloud, given water from a rock, and handed a covenant beneath a suspended mountain needed unearned mercy to avoid being losers, then no reader of this surah is in a position to be confident on his own record. The appropriate response to the favours in one's own life is not satisfaction but the quiet recognition that the only thing standing between anyone and ruin is the same two nouns.

The second is a warning about the sequence. These people were not ruined by ignorance or by a hard beginning. They were ruined by turning away *after that* — after clarity, after favours, after the covenant. The Qur'an consistently treats the post-clarity failure as more serious than the pre-clarity one, which is why this surah keeps repeating *min ba'di dhalika*. The practical question it puts to a person who has known the religion for years is not "do you know more?" but "have you turned?"

## Verse 65

**"You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, “Be disgraced apes!”"**

In simple terms: they knew what happened to those among them who broke the Sabbath, and Allah commanded them to become apes, driven away and humiliated.

The story is told at length in Surah al-A'raf, and it should be read alongside this verse. There was a town by the sea — the commentators identified it variously as Ayla, or Tabuk, or another coastal settlement, and the identification is not settled. Its people were tested in a precise way: fish would come to them in abundance on the Sabbath, when fishing was forbidden, and would not come on other days (7:163). It was a test engineered so that obedience would cost them something visible, which is the shape of every real test.

*Alladhina i'tadaw minkum fi al-sabt* — "those among you who transgressed concerning the Sabbath." *I'tada* means to cross a limit. According to the transmitted reports, some of them devised a way around the prohibition: they dug trenches and pools on the Sabbath so the fish would swim in, and then netted them on Sunday, reasoning that they had not fished on the Sabbath. This is the part of the story that matters most for later readers, because the device was legally ingenious and morally empty. They did not break the letter of the prohibition; they defeated its purpose while observing its form.

That is the disease Islamic law calls *hiyal* — legal stratagems that keep the wording of a ruling while nullifying its intent. The schools of law were severe about it, and the literature against it is substantial. Its modern forms are easy to recognise: the financial product engineered to be a sale on paper and a loan in substance; the marriage contracted with the intention of undoing it; the technicality that satisfies a contract and defeats its purpose. The lesson of this verse is that a device designed to let you do what Allah prohibited does not make the act lawful. It makes it worse, because it adds the claim that you were obeying.

Now the difficult clause.

*Fa-qulna lahum kunu qiradatan khasi'in* — "We said to them: be apes, despised." The question is whether the transformation was physical or moral, and the commentators genuinely differed.

The majority read it as literal. The imperative *kunu*, "be," is the creative command that in the Qur'an accomplishes what it states — "When He decrees a matter, He only says to it 'Be,' and it is" (2:117). The transmitted reports describe them becoming apes and dying after a short interval, and those details are reports, not text. The transformation is mentioned three times in the Qur'an: here, at 4:47 and 5:60, and at 7:166.

A significant minority, including a report from Mujahid, read it as moral and not physical: what was transformed was their hearts and characters, so that they became like apes in conduct rather than in form. Al-Tabari records this reading and discusses it.

Both readings exist in the tradition and neither can simply be dismissed. What must be said about the literal reading is that it is a *maskh*, a miraculous suspension of the ordinary order — not an evolutionary event, and no one in the tradition has ever suggested otherwise. What must be said about the whole question is that nothing in the lesson depends on settling it. Whether the change was in their bodies or in their characters, the crime and the consequence are identical: people who were given a clear prohibition, defeated it with a clever device, and lost their humanity in the process.

And now the guardrail, which is not optional. **This verse has been used for centuries as an ethnic slur, and there is no warrant in the Qur'an or the Sunnah for that use whatever.** Four things should be said plainly.

The text makes the punishment particular to specific actors for a specific act: *alladhina i'tadaw minkum* — those **among you** who transgressed. It does not say all of them, and it does not say their descendants. The surah's own rule on inherited guilt is stated twice in its own pages: "That was a nation which has passed on. It will have what it earned, and you will have what you have earned, and you will not be asked about what they used to do" (2:134 and 2:141).

The Qur'an forbids the manner of speech itself: "And do not insult those they invoke other than Allah, lest they insult Allah in enmity without knowledge" (6:108). A Muslim has no licence to revile anyone's religion or ancestry, whatever this verse says about a punished town.

And the immediate context refutes the generalisation. Verse 65 is followed by verse 66, which makes the event a lesson for the God-fearing. And 3:113 says of the same people: "They are not all alike. Among them is an upright community..." A reader who takes this verse as a description of a people has contradicted a verse that explicitly denies it.

One clarification about the Sabbath itself, since it often confuses readers. The Sabbath was prescribed for them, not for the Muslims: "The sabbath was only appointed for those who differed over it" (16:124). For the Muslim community the day of gathering is Friday, and the Prophet ﷺ said: "We are the last but we will be the foremost on the Day of Resurrection... this is the day which Allah has prescribed for us; the Jews have Saturday and the Christians have Sunday" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). The difference between Friday and Saturday is a difference in revealed law between communities — "For each of you We prescribed a law and a method" (5:48) — and not a ranking of communities.

For practice, the most useful thing in this verse is the three groups of Surah al-A'raf, which this verse's parallel passage spells out. There were those who transgressed. There were those who forbade the evil. And there were those who did neither — who simply kept silent and took no part. "And when they forgot that by which they had been reminded, We saved those who had forbidden evil and seized those who wronged with a wretched punishment" (7:165). Notice what the verse does and does not say. It names the saved explicitly. It names the destroyed explicitly. And the silent ones are not named at all — which the commentators read as the most sobering silence in the passage. The fate of the bystander who saw the wrong and said nothing is passed over in a verse that accounts for everyone else. That should worry every reader of it.

## Verse 66

**"So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing."**

In simple terms: that punishment was made a deterrent warning for everyone who witnessed it and everyone who would hear of it afterwards, and a moral lesson for those who are conscious of Allah.

*Fa-ja'alnaha nakalan* — "so We made it a deterrent." *Nakal* comes from a root that gives both the sense of an exemplary punishment that restrains others and the word for a shackle or fetter. The two senses converge: the punishment is a fetter that holds the onlooker back from the same act. That is precisely what a deterrent is, and the commentators drew from this verse that divinely decreed punishments in this world function as restraint for the community and not only as retribution on the offender.

It should be noted carefully that this verse concerns a specific historical punishment, not the code of prescribed legal penalties (*hudud*). Jurists have long discussed the purposes of that code — whether deterrence, expiation, or both — and this verse is cited in support of the deterrent function of punishment generally. But no specific legal ruling for the Muslim community is derived from it directly, and it should not be quoted as though it were a proof text for the *hudud* as such.

*Lima bayna yadayha wa ma khalfaha* — "for what was before it and what was after it." There are several readings of this phrase, and they are worth distinguishing. The dominant one, and the one the translation you are reading follows, is temporal: for the generation that witnessed it and the generations that came after — meaning for everyone who would ever hear the story. Other commentators took the phrase as "the sins committed before it and the sins that would follow," reading the punishment as covering both. Others read it geographically. The phrase family recurs in the Qur'an with different referents — at 5:48, 10:37, 12:111, 34:31 and 46:30 — so the meaning has to be settled from context each time. Here the temporal reading is the strongest and the most natural.

*Wa maw'izatan lil-muttaqin* — "and a lesson for those who are mindful of Allah." This is the clause that carries the verse, and the distinction it draws is both subtle and profound. The punishment was made two things at once: a *nakal*, a deterrent, for everyone — and a *maw'izah*, an admonition that softens the heart, for the *muttaqun* specifically.

Why the specification? Because the same event does two different things to two kinds of person. To the heedless, it is a threat — a reason not to transgress, held over the head. To the God-conscious, it is a lesson — something they take into the heart, learn from, and are changed by, without needing to be threatened. The *muttaqi* is precisely the person who can watch someone else's ruin and be improved by it. The heedless person can only be restrained by it, and often not even then.

This is the third time *taqwa* has appeared in four verses — "so that you may be mindful" at verse 63, and here twice over in *lil-muttaqin*. The passage is saturated with the word, and the saturation is the point. Everything in these verses — the covenant, the mountain, the scripture, the punishment, the deterrent — exists to produce one quality in the human being.

The practical consequence of that distinction is one of the most useful things in the batch. There is a hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim in which the Prophet ﷺ said: "A believer is not stung twice from the same hole." That is the definition of the *muttaqi* in operational terms — the person who learns from the first catastrophe rather than requiring a personal one. It is also a sharp test to apply to oneself. When you read about a marriage that collapsed, a business that failed through dishonesty, a community that tore itself apart, a public figure whose private corruption was exposed — do you read it as news, or as a *maw'izah*? The difference between those two readings is, in this verse's terms, exactly the difference between the attentive and the heedless.

And there is a gentler corollary. The God-conscious person does not need to make every mistake personally. Much of the Qur'an's narrative is given to us precisely so that we can be instructed by histories we did not live — which is why the Qur'an says of these stories, "There was certainly in their stories a lesson for those of understanding" (12:111). Reading the Qur'an's accounts of the ruined as a spectator at a disaster is a failure of reading. They were made a *maw'izah* for you specifically.

## Verse 67

**"And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow.” They replied, “Are you mocking us?” Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!”"**

In simple terms: Moses conveyed a divine command to slaughter a cow. They accused him of mocking them, and he sought refuge with Allah from being among those who behave ignorantly.

First, the setting, because it explains the whole story. The cow was commanded in order to resolve an unsolved murder. That much is stated in the Qur'an itself, two verses on: "And when you slew a man and disputed over it, but Allah was to bring out that which you were concealing — so We said, 'Strike him with part of it.' Thus does Allah bring the dead to life, and He shows you His signs so that you might reason" (2:72–73). The transmitted reports fill in the narrative — a man killed for his inheritance, a body found, a dispute, the corpse struck with a piece of the cow, the dead man reviving and naming his killer. Those details come from the commentaries and earlier tradition and should be cited as such; the Qur'anic core is the murder, the dispute, the striking and the revival.

It is also worth stating a common misunderstanding at the outset, since it is genuinely widespread: **this surah is not named after a cow that Muslims venerate.** Muslims do not worship or revere a cow. The surah is called al-Baqarah because this story appears in it and nowhere else in the Qur'an, and the name functions as a label and a reminder — a reminder, as we will see, of an instructive failure more than of a miracle.

*Inna Allaha ya'murukum an tadhbahu baqaratan* — "Allah commands you to slaughter a cow." Notice how the command is phrased in its first form. *Baqaratan* is indefinite: **a** cow. Any cow. There is no age, no colour, no description, no price, no condition. The command as it was first delivered was as easy as a command can be.

That is the single most important fact in the entire cow narrative, because everything that follows is a consequence of rejecting it. Had they gone out and taken the first cow they found, it would have sufficed, and the matter would have ended there. Instead they asked, and each question narrowed the command, and by verse 71 the cow they needed was so specific that finding it was nearly impossible. The commentators are unanimous on this point and draw from it a principle of practice: unnecessary questioning of a clear command tends to make the obligation heavier, not lighter.

The hadith that states the principle explicitly is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim. The Prophet ﷺ was asked, "O Messenger of Allah, is the pilgrimage due every year?" and he remained silent until the man asked three times; then he said: "Had I said yes, it would have become due, and you would not have been able to do it. So leave me as I have left you, for those before you were destroyed only by their excessive questioning and their disagreeing with their prophets."

*A-tattakhidhuna huzuwan* — "do you take us in ridicule?" Note why they said this. The command bore no visible relation to the problem they had brought him. They came about a murder; he told them to slaughter a cow. The wisdom of the command was not apparent to them, and their conclusion was that it must be a joke.

That is the precise shape of a test that every believer meets. When a ruling's wisdom is not apparent — why this prohibition, why this ritual, why this particular obligation — the response of faith is to comply and then understand, and the response of suspicion is "is this serious?" The Qur'an is full of commands whose full wisdom is not visible from the inside: the number of rak'ahs, the prohibition of a few specific foods, the rules of inheritance, the timings. The person who demands to see the reason before he obeys will find that the reason usually arrives after the obedience, and never before it.

*Qala a'udhu billahi an akuna min al-jahilin* — "he said: I seek refuge with Allah lest I be among the ignorant." Two things deserve attention.

The first is *jahl*. In the Qur'an, *jahl* is not ignorance in the sense of lacking information; it is the opposite of *hilm* — forbearance, restraint, dignity, self-command. Moses is not saying "I am not stupid." He is saying: I take refuge with Allah from being among those who treat serious things lightly, who jest about the command of Allah, who answer an insult with an insult. He is refusing a whole manner of being.

The second is what he did not do. He did not counter-mock. He did not defend himself, argue his sincerity, or explain the wisdom he had not been given. He turned to Allah. That is the prophetic response to being ridiculed for the religion, and it is the model for anyone mocked for their faith today — at school, at work, in a family, online. The Qur'an instructs the same elsewhere: "And when you see those who engage in offensive discourse concerning Our verses, then turn away from them until they enter into another conversation" (6:68), and "do not sit with them until they enter into another conversation, for indeed you would then be like them" (4:140).

There is a further detail worth noticing: Moses took their accusation seriously enough to seek refuge from it, rather than laughing it off. A person who is comfortable around mockery of the religion has already drifted. The reflex of *isti'adha* — turning to Allah for shelter the moment the sacred is treated lightly — is not a formality in this verse. It is the whole answer.

## Verse 68

**"They said, “Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!” He replied, “Allah says, ‘The cow should neither be old nor young but in between. So do as you are commanded!’”"**

In simple terms: they asked Moses to have his Lord specify what kind of cow, and the answer came back: middle-aged, neither old nor young — and then a rebuke in the form of an instruction: just do what you have been told.

*Ud'u lana rabbaka yubayyin lana ma hiya* — "call upon your Lord to make clear to us what it is." The phrase *ud'u lana rabbaka* is repeated from verse 61 and will recur at verses 69 and 70 — four times in this passage, always in their mouths, always *your* Lord. The repetition is itself part of the characterisation.

Now, a necessary fairness. Asking for clarification is not inherently blameworthy. The Qur'an commands it: "Ask the people of the Reminder if you do not know" (16:43), and the Prophet ﷺ said, "The cure for ignorance is to ask." So the fault here cannot be the asking as such. The commentators located it elsewhere: the command was already clear enough to act on, since any cow would do, and so the question was not a request for knowledge needed for compliance but a device for delay.

That distinction is the whole practical lesson, and it is a fine one. The same question — "but what exactly does this mean?" — is either the question of a person preparing to obey or the question of a person preparing to postpone. Outwardly identical; inwardly opposite. The reliable test is what happens after the answer arrives. The person who wanted to understand obeys. The person who wanted to delay asks again — and indeed they do ask again, twice more, at verses 69 and 70.

*Innahu yaqulu innaha baqaratun la-faridun wa la-bikrun 'iwanun bayna dhalika* — "He says: it is a cow, neither *farid* nor *bikr*, but *'iwan*, between that." The vocabulary here is precise and worth learning, because the specification carries the meaning.

*Farid* is an aged animal, past bearing and past its prime. *Bikr* is a heifer that has never calved — from the root for "first," the same word used for a virgin and for a firstborn. *'Iwan* is the middle: an animal that has borne once or twice and is in the fullness of its strength. The commentators explained the wisdom in obvious terms — the old has declined, the young has not yet arrived, and the middle is the fittest and most valuable.

And that specification opens a genuine application that has nothing to do with cattle. The Qur'an says of this community: "And thus We have made you a middle community" (2:143) — *ummatan wasatan*. And elsewhere in this surah: "O you who believe, spend from the good things which you have earned... and do not aim toward the defective from it, spending while you would not take it yourselves except with closed eyes" (2:267). The cow demanded was the one in its prime, not the one that was surplus to requirements. That is a standing principle for what a person gives to Allah: the middle of your strength, the best of your wealth, the prime of your time — not the leftovers after everything else has taken its share. This is offered as a legitimate reflection on the specification; it is not the primary meaning of the verse, but it follows naturally from it.

*Fa-af'alu ma tu'marun* — "so do what you are commanded." That is the refrain of the passage and its moral centre. Three times in four verses the answer to their questions ends with a form of this instruction. The specification was given, and the specification was not the point; the doing was the point.

For practice, this verse addresses one of the most common failure modes in religious life — the person who wants the complete map before taking the first step. He wants to know the full ruling, the complete ruling, the ruling for his exact situation including the edge cases, before he does anything at all. And meanwhile the first thing is already clear. The Qur'an's method throughout is progressive: do what is clear to you now, and the rest unfolds in the doing. The person who waits for total clarity before acting generally waits forever, and the waiting itself becomes the disobedience.

## Verse 69

**"They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.’”"**

In simple terms: they asked a second question, about colour, and the answer specified a vividly yellow cow, beautiful to look at.

*Qalu ud'u lana rabbaka yubayyin lana ma lawnuha* — "they said: call upon your Lord to make clear to us what its colour is." This is the second question. Note the escalation: at verse 68 they asked what it was; here they ask its colour; at verse 70 they will ask again. Each answer was met with another question, and each question narrowed the command. The surah is showing us a mechanism in operation, and it is worth naming plainly: **the questions did not clarify the command for them; the questions made the command harder, and the questions were chosen, not necessitated.**

*Baqaratun safra'u faqi'un lawnuha* — "a yellow cow, intense is its colour." *Safra'* is the ordinary word for yellow; *faqi'* is the intensifier, used for a deep, vivid, saturated yellow rather than a pale one. Note the Arabic construction: not "a cow with an intense colour" but *faqi'un lawnuha* — "intense is its colour," with the colour as the subject. The description is intensified by the grammar as well as the vocabulary, and the effect is that the cow is now unmistakable.

*Tasurru al-nazirin* — "delighting the observers." The cow was to be beautiful: its colour gladdens those who look at it. The commentators observed the consequence, which is the sting in the verse. A cow that is middle-aged, vividly yellow, and pleasing to the eye is not an ordinary animal; it is rare, and rarity is expensive. By asking for more specification they had priced themselves out of an easy purchase. The transmitted reports put the eventual price of the cow at an enormous figure — its weight in gold, in one version. Those numbers are transmitted reports and not Qur'anic data, and nothing in the lesson depends on them; the Qur'anic point is simply that the specification made the task heavier, and that they brought the heaviness on themselves.

There is one further observation that some commentators have made, and it is striking enough to mention with appropriate caution. The calf they had worshipped was made from their ornaments — gold — and described in the Qur'an as a body with a lowing sound (7:148, 20:88). Being commanded to take a vividly golden-yellow animal and slaughter it has the shape of a deliberate undoing: the thing they venerated in gold was to be destroyed by an act performed on gold-coloured flesh. Read that way, the command was not arbitrary but therapeutic — a cure administered in the exact shape of the disease. This is offered as a commentarial observation and a reflection rather than as an established textual reading; the text does not state it, and it should not be pressed beyond that.

The practical reach of this verse is considerable, and it splits into two.

The first is the pattern it exposes. Notice what they asked about: colour. The externals. They had been told to slaughter a cow and they wanted to know what shade it should be. This is the perennial temptation in religious life — to invest enormous energy in the visible and the superficial while the substance goes untouched. The colour of the garment, the precise wording of a formula, the outward form of an observance — pursued with real diligence, while the command itself, which was simple, remains undone. The cow, in this reading, is whatever you are supposed to be doing; the colour is what you have substituted for it.

The second is the cost of the questions. Every specification they requested was granted, and every specification made obedience harder. There is a direct modern equivalent in the person who will not begin praying until he has resolved every disputed detail of the prayer, who will not give charity until he has settled the entire law of zakah in his own mind, who will not take the first step of repentance until he understands everything about his own psychology. Allah answered every question in this story — that is grace, and it should be noticed. But the answers were not improvements. They were burdens, and they were self-inflicted.

## Verse 70

**"Again they said, “Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow, for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.”"**

In simple terms: they asked a third time, using the identical words of their earlier question, claiming that the cows all looked alike to them — and they added, this time, "if Allah wills, we will be guided."

*Qalu ud'u lana rabbaka yubayyin lana ma hiya* — "they said: call upon your Lord to make clear to us what it is." Note that this is word for word the question of verse 68. The repetition is not carelessness on the surah's part; it is the point. They are going in circles. The question has already been answered, and they are asking it again as though it had not been — which tells the reader that the difficulty was never informational. They had been told the animal's age. They had been told its colour. There was nothing left to be confused about.

*Inna al-baqara tashabaha 'alayna* — "indeed the cows are alike to us." The verb *tashabaha* means to be similar, ambiguous, hard to distinguish — and it is the very root from which the *mutashabihat*, the ambiguous verses, are named in verse 7 of this surah. The connection is worth making carefully but it is worth making: in verse 7, those in whose hearts is deviation pursue what is ambiguous. Here, the cows become "mutashabih" to people who do not want to find one.

That is a precise description of how excuse-making works. When a person does not want to do something, everything about it becomes suddenly unclear. The requirements that looked plain last week become ambiguous today. The options "all look alike." Confusion arrives conveniently at the moment when clarity would have obliged him to act. Most readers will recognise this in themselves immediately — the sudden fog that descends on a decision the moment it starts to cost something.

*Wa inna in sha'a Allahu la-muhtadun* — "and indeed we, if Allah wills, will be guided." And here is the turn. This is the first time in the entire exchange that they attach the outcome to the will of Allah. Three times they had said *ud'u lana rabbaka* — call upon *your* Lord — holding the matter at arm's length. Now, for the first time, they say *in sha'a Allah*.

The commentators observed that this was the turning point, and there is real substance in the observation whether or not one accepts the specific narrations attached to it. Up to this moment the whole exchange had been conducted on their own terms: their questions, their doubts, their specifications, their calculations of difficulty. The obstruction was never a shortage of information. It was that the matter was being handled entirely in their own hands. The moment the outcome was referred to Allah's will, the obstruction dissolved — and the very next verse tells us that they found the cow and slaughtered it, though they came close to not doing so.

The Qur'anic instruction behind this is general and binding: "And never say of anything, 'Indeed, I will do that tomorrow,' except [when adding], 'If Allah wills'" (18:23). And the Prophet ﷺ illustrated what is lost when the exception is omitted, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah: Solomon son of David said, "Tonight I will go to seventy women, each of whom will bring forth a boy who will fight in the cause of Allah." His companion said to him, "Say: if Allah wills." But he did not say it, and he forgot. He went to them, and none brought forth anything except one woman who brought forth a half-formed child. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Had he said 'if Allah wills,' he would not have broken his oath, and it would have been a means of fulfilling his need."

For practice, this verse gives the diagnosis and the cure in a single sentence, and they are the same sentence.

The diagnosis: *inna al-baqara tashabaha 'alayna*. When you find that a clear obligation has suddenly become ambiguous, suspect the will before you blame the text. The cows did not become alike. The wanting changed.

And the cure: *in sha'a Allah*. Not as a verbal habit — the phrase is not an incantation, and saying it mechanically while still handling everything on your own terms changes nothing. It is a genuine transfer: I intend this, I will strive in it, and the outcome is Yours. Every person who has ever struggled to begin something difficult and found the difficulty evaporate at the moment of surrender knows this from experience. The cow was findable all along. What was needed was to stop calculating whether it was findable.

And note the surah's patience, which is the last thing to see here. Three times they questioned, and three times Allah answered. Every evasion was met with a real response, and every response narrowed the command further — not out of vindictiveness but because that is what a specification does. The way back was always open; it only required the sentence they finally said.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses turn the surah from a catalogue of favours into a verdict, and the verdict has four parts.

There is the ingratitude itself: food arriving daily without labour, and a complaint about the menu, and a trade of the better for the worse that cost them their station (61). There is the door that remains open regardless: whoever, from any named community, truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have his reward and will not fear or grieve — with the strict and necessary boundary that no human being may convert that into a verdict on any individual (62). There is the covenant taken beneath a mountain, the turning away, and the plain statement that nothing but unearned favour and mercy stood between a covenant-breaking people and ruin (63–64). There is the Sabbath-breakers and the clever device that kept the letter of the law and destroyed its purpose, and the transformation made a deterrent for all and a lesson for the God-conscious alone (65–66). And then there is the story that names the surah: a command as easy as a command can be — slaughter any cow — converted by three rounds of questioning into a near-impossibility, and resolved only when they said, at last, "if Allah wills" (67–70).

Three things stand out across the whole passage. First, the failure is never informational. Not one of these failures came from a shortage of evidence or clarity; every one came from a will that would not receive what it had already been given. Second, the mechanism of evasion is remarkably consistent: ask for more detail, discover that everything has become ambiguous, and defer — which is why the passage's cure is equally consistent and is stated in its last three words. And third, mercy is the constant. Every one of these indictments is either preceded or followed by a door: a promise to every believing community, a grace that prevents loss, an answer given to yet another question. The surah is not building a case for despair. It is building a case for the only response that has ever worked.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators genuinely differ — whether *fum* means garlic or grain, whether the mountain was raised literally, whether the transformation was physical or moral, and whether the Sabians are People of the Scripture — the disagreement has been presented rather than asserted. Five cautions have been observed. The background narrative of the murder behind the cow is flagged as transmitted report rather than Qur'anic text, with the Qur'anic core identified at 2:72–73. The reported price of the cow and the reported interval before the transformed died are flagged as reports, not text. The identification of the town by the sea and of the Sabians is flagged as unsettled. The claim that verse 62 is abrogated is noted as a minority claim, with specification as the stronger reading. And the connection between the golden calf and the yellow cow is offered explicitly as a commentarial reflection, not as the meaning of the text.

Two guardrails have been stated at length because these verses are the ones most often weaponised. On verse 61: the humiliation described there is a consequence of named conduct, not of descent; this surah abolishes inherited guilt in its own words at 2:134 and 2:141; the principle is universal and is stated universally at 16:112; 3:113 denies that the People of the Scripture are all alike; and the Prophet ﷺ's own conduct is the earliest evidence of the Islamic posture. On verse 65: the punishment was particular to named transgressors for a named act, the Qur'an forbids reviling what others hold sacred at 6:108, and there is no warrant in the Qur'an or Sunnah — none — for using "apes" as a description of any group of people, then or now. And on verse 62: the majority classical position on the fate of other communities after the coming of Muhammad ﷺ has been stated plainly rather than softened, and it has been stated alongside the boundary that no human being may apply it to any individual person, whose circumstances are known only to Allah, Who will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection (22:17).
