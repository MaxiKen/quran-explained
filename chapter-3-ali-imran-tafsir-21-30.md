# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 21–30

The passage we are entering is the hardest stretch so far. Verses 11–20 ended on a note of extraordinary restraint: *your duty is only to deliver*. Verses 21–25 then turn and deliver something severe. And verses 26–30 do something unexpected with it — they stop arguing with the people being addressed and turn to God instead.

That movement is itself the lesson, and it is worth naming before we start, because it is easy to read these ten verses as a polemic and miss what they are doing. The surah has been in dispute with the People of the Book since verse 19. It has invited them to be judged by their own scripture (verse 23) and they have refused. At the point where the argument closes, God does not tell the Prophet ﷺ to argue harder. He tells him to pray — and the prayer He gives him is the greatest statement of divine sovereignty in the Qur'an (verses 26–27). Then, having established who actually holds power, He draws the boundary of loyalty (28), the knowledge of the hidden (29), and the Day when every deed is made present (30).

So the shape is: **the crime (21), the verdict (22), the exposure (23), the false assurance behind it all (24), the reckoning (25), the prayer (26–27), the boundary (28), the secret (29), the presentation (30).** And the whole thing is bracketed by an ironic "good news" at the start and an unconditional "God is Ever Gracious to His servants" at the end.

One boundary needs stating at the front, because verses 21–25 are among the verses most often used to indict whole groups of people. The Qur'an here describes **a pattern of conduct** — denying signs, killing messengers, silencing those who call for justice — and it attributes that pattern to those who did it. It does not license any reader to declare a living person or a present community guilty of it. Judging individuals remains God's, and the same surah will say explicitly, at verse 113, that "they are not all alike," and at verse 199 that some among the People of the Book truly believe. Those verses are not a retraction of these; they are the guardrail that keeps these from being misused.

## Verse 21

**"Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment."**

In simple terms: those who reject God's signs, and kill the prophets without any right, and kill the people who command justice among mankind — give them the news of a painful punishment.

**The three crimes, and the order they come in.** This verse is built as an escalation, and the escalation is deliberate.

The first is *yakfurūna bi-āyāti Allāh* — they disbelieve in, they cover over, God's signs. This is the root.

The second is *yaqtulūna al-nabīyīna bi-ghayri ḥaqqin* — they kill the prophets **without right**. The qualifier matters. The Qur'an is not issuing a blanket condemnation of killing here; there is killing that is lawful in the Qur'an's own legal vocabulary (capital punishment, legitimate warfare, retribution). What is being said is that killing a prophet has no warrant under any law — including, pointedly, under the law of the people being addressed, whose own scripture commanded them to obey the messengers God sent them. They killed the very people their religion told them to follow.

The third is *yaqtulūna alladhīna ya'murūna bi-al-qisṭi mina al-nāsi* — they kill **those who command justice from among the people**. Note the description carefully. These are not prophets; the phrase *mina al-nāsi* marks them as ordinary human beings. And the thing they are killed for is not having an opinion but *commanding* — *ya'murūna* — actively calling for *qisṭ*, justice.

So the escalation runs: reject the message → kill the messenger → kill the ordinary person who says the message out loud. That is the full life-cycle of a society that has decided to stop listening. And it is the last stage that is the true diagnosis, because a community that kills its prophets has not yet finished collapsing; a community that kills the man next door for saying "this is unjust" has.

Read that third clause against verse 18 of this surah, where we were told that God, the angels, and the people of knowledge testify *qā'iman bi-al-qisṭ* — upholding justice. And against verse 20, where the Prophet's followers were described as those who *follow him*. The surah is building toward a definition of the believing community as the people who command what is just — which will be stated outright at 3:104 and 3:110. This verse is the negative image of that: the community that kills the people who command justice.

**An internal cross-reference worth holding.** The same phrase — "killing the prophets unjustly" — reappears at 3:181: "God has certainly heard the statement of those who said, 'God is poor while we are rich.' We will record what they said and their killing of the prophets unjustly." The two verses bookend the surah's argument with the People of the Book, and the later one names the charge as a *recorded* fact with a consequence. The charge is not rhetorical.

In al-Baqarah the same pattern was named: "Is it that whenever a messenger came to you with what your souls did not desire, you became arrogant — a party you denied and a party you killed?" (2:87; see also 2:61 and 2:91). The Qur'an returns to this because it is, for it, the paradigmatic crime: the rejection of truth does not stay theoretical. It moves from denial to violence, and the violence is directed first at those who carry the message and then at anyone who repeats it.

**"Give them good news of a painful punishment."** *Fa-bashshirhum bi-'adhābin alīm.* And here is the rhetorical device that sets the verse's tone.

*Bashshara* is the verb of **glad tidings**. It is the word used for the angels' announcement to Mary, for the announcement of a son to Abraham, for "give good news to those who believe and do good" (2:25). And here it is attached to a painful punishment.

This is a known figure in Arabic rhetoric — the ironic or biting use of a word of good news for its opposite — and the Qur'an uses it several times: at 4:138 ("give the hypocrites good news of a painful punishment"), at 9:34, at 45:8, and at 84:24. The effect is not comedy. It is the coldest possible form of address: the thing you were expecting to be told is the opposite of what you are told, and the word you associate with relief is the word used to announce your ruin. It also, and this is the sharper edge, frames the punishment as **news** — as information delivered, not as vengeance extracted. They are being told, so that they might turn. The irony is aimed at their confidence, and the confidence is the thing the surah wants broken.

## Verse 22

**"They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers."**

In simple terms: those are the ones whose works have come to nothing, in this world and the next. And they will have no one to help them.

**"Whose deeds are wasted."** *Ḥabiṭat a'māluhum.* The verb *ḥabaṭa* has a vivid origin in the language: it is used of a camel that grazes on something poisonous and swells up and dies — the animal that looked fullest is the one that is finished. From that comes the sense of a deed that swells with apparent substance and then comes to nothing.

The word is the technical term for one of the largest disputes in Islamic theology, and I want to give you the dispute rather than smooth it away, because it determines how you read a great many verses.

The question is: **do sins nullify good deeds?**

The Mu'tazila held that they do. On their scheme, a person who commits a major sin and dies without repenting occupies "an intermediate position" (*al-manzilah bayna al-manzilatayn*) — neither believer nor disbeliever in this life — and his obedience is nullified by his sin (*iḥbāṭ*), so that his good works earn him nothing and he abides in the Fire forever. Their concern was moral: they wanted the gravity of sin to be real, and they reasoned from the principle that reward and punishment are deserved (*istiḥqāq*) and cannot coexist.

The Ahl al-Sunnah held that they do not. On the position that became established, a believer who commits a major sin remains a believer, his good deeds are not nullified, and he is not eternally in the Fire — he may be punished, or God may forgive him, and he comes out eventually. What nullifies deeds is not sin but *kufr* and *shirk* and apostasy. The proof-texts for that are explicit: "Whoever of you turns back from his religion and dies a disbeliever — those are the ones whose deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter" (2:217); "If you associate [anything with God], your work will surely become worthless" (39:65); and 5:5 and 47:33 on the same pattern.

Now: **which reading applies to this verse?** On the Sunni reading, the answer is straightforward and it is why the verse does not decide the wider dispute. The people described here are the people of verse 21, whose defining and first-named act is *kufr* — denial of God's signs. Their deeds are wasted **because of their denial**, not because of some mechanism by which a sin cancels a prayer. The verse is about disbelievers, and both schools agree that a disbeliever's deeds avail nothing in the Hereafter. So the verse is not, on this reading, evidence for *iḥbāṭ* in the Mu'tazilī sense at all.

The Mu'tazila, for their part, read it as supporting their position, since the verbal form (*ḥabiṭat*) is the same word used in the *iḥbāṭ* debate. The disagreement is real and I am not going to resolve it here. But I will say where the weight lies: the immediate referent of "they" is the group defined in verse 21 by *kufr*, and that is the more natural reading of the passage.

There is one further distinction worth knowing, because it appears in the literature and it prevents a crude reading. Some scholars discussed a limited form of *iḥbāṭ* — the idea that a specific good deed can be offset by a specific bad one in the scales, rather than all good being wiped out by any sin. That is a much narrower claim than the Mu'tazilī one, and it is a different question. I note it so that you recognise it if you meet the term; I am not advancing it.

**"In this world and the Hereafter."** This is the phrase to sit with. We expect deeds to be wasted in the Hereafter if a person rejected God. But *in this world* too?

The commentators' answer is that the nullification reaches into this life as well: the good they did — their charity, their kinship obligations, whatever of worth they performed — brings them no blessing, no lasting benefit, no good name that outlives them. There is a harder reading too, which is that everything they *schemed* comes to nothing: the plans laid against the message and its bearers did not work. The messengers died and the message outlived the killers. On either reading, the point is that futility is not postponed. It starts now.

**"And they will have no helpers."** *Wa-mā lahum min nāṣirīn.* A *nāṣir* is one who gives *nuṣrah* — help, victory, backing. The denial is total: *mā lahum min*, "there is not for them any." On the Day, no partisan, no intercessor with a claim, no power to fall back on. The people who killed the ones commanding justice in order to keep their standing will find there is no standing left to keep.

## Verse 23

**"Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures? Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly."**

In simple terms: have you not considered those who were given a share of the Book? When they are called to the Book of God, so that it may judge between them, a party of them turns away in refusal.

**"Have you not seen?"** *Alam tara.* This is the Qur'an's standard opening for "have you not considered / have you not reflected." It does not mean physical sight. It means: look at this case and draw the conclusion. The addressee is the Prophet ﷺ, and through him every reader.

**"Those who were given a portion of the Scriptures."** Note the word: *naṣīban mina al-kitāb* — a **portion**, a share, not the whole. The Qur'an's phrasing here is pointed. They were given a share of revelation, and the charge is that they mishandled even that. The diminishment is part of the rebuke — and it stands in deliberate contrast to 3:20, which called them *alladhīna ūtū al-kitāb*, those who were given the Book.

**"When they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes."** *Idhā du'ū ilā kitābi Allāhi li-yaḥkuma baynahum.* Note what is made the arbiter: **the Book**, not a person, not a tribe, not a council. The invitation is to let revelation itself decide. That is the whole principle the surah has been pressing since verse 3 — that there is a Criterion by which disputes are settled.

**"Some of them turn away heedlessly."** *Thumma yatawallā farīqun minhum wa-hum mu'riḍūn.* And note the qualification, which is easy to skip and which the Qur'an never skips: **a party** (*farīq*) of them. Not all. The surah will say so explicitly later — "they are not all alike; among the People of the Scripture is an upright community" (3:113) — and it will close, at 3:199, by naming those among them who truly believe. The Qur'an's condemnations are never wholesale, and a reader who turns them into wholesale condemnations is contradicting the text he is quoting.

*Mu'riḍūn* — averse, turning aside, deliberately disengaged. Not confused, not unaware: averse.

**The reported occasion.** The commentators differ here, and the sources record more than one account. Al-Wāḥidī, in his *Asbāb al-Nuzūl*, gathers several; the scholar Mahmoud Ayoub notes that he reports three. The one most often cited runs like this: a Jewish scholar said to the Prophet ﷺ, "What is your religion, Muḥammad?" He replied, "The religion of Abraham." The man said, "But Abraham was a Jew." The Prophet ﷺ said, "**Then let the Torah judge between us.**" And they refused. On that occasion this verse came down. Related versions, transmitted by Sa'īd ibn Jubayr and 'Ikrimah from Ibn 'Abbās, place the exchange in the *bayt al-midrās* — the Jewish house of study — where a group of Jews were assembled and the Prophet ﷺ called them to God.

These are **reported** accounts with variant details; they are not ṣaḥīḥ hadith with an uninterrupted chain, and the tafsir literature itself records the variation. What they agree on is the substance, and the substance is what makes the verse bite: the Prophet ﷺ did not ask them to accept a foreign standard. He asked them to be judged by **their own Book** — and they refused.

That is the exposure. It is not that they lacked revelation. It is that when revelation was about to rule against them, they walked away from it. Anyone who has ever invoked scripture to win an argument and then set it down the moment it turned against them knows exactly what this verse describes.

**And note the connection to verse 24.** The very next verse explains *why* they could afford to walk away from their own Book. They had a doctrine that made them safe regardless. The refusal and the false assurance are one disease.

## Verse 24

**"This is because they say, “The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.” They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying."**

In simple terms: that is because they say: the Fire will not touch us except for a few numbered days. And what they used to fabricate has deluded them in their religion.

**The parallel that unlocks this verse.** We have met this claim before, almost word for word, in al-Baqarah: *"˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, 'The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.' Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Have you taken a pledge from Allah — for Allah never breaks His word — or are you just saying about Allah what you do not know?'"* (2:80).

The two verses are a matched pair, and reading them together shows the division of labour between the two surahs. Al-Baqarah **challenges** the claim — where did you get this? Did God promise you? Or are you inventing? Āl 'Imrān **explains** it — here is what produced the claim: they fabricated it, and then they believed their own fabrication. Al-Baqarah demands the warrant; Āl 'Imrān supplies the psychology.

**"This is because they say."** *Dhālika bi-annahum qālū.* The demonstrative *dhālika* points back to the turning away in verse 23. So the causal chain the surah is laying out is precise: **they refused to be judged by the Book because they believed they were already safe.** A false assurance about the end produces corruption in the middle. If you are convinced the Fire can only touch you briefly, scripture stops being a standard you are accountable to and becomes a possession you display.

**"The Fire will not touch us except for a few days."** *Lan tamassanā al-nāru illā ayyāman ma'dūdāt.* *Ma'dūdāt* — numbered, counted, finite. The claim is not that they are sinless; it is that the punishment is on a timer.

Reports in the tafsir literature explain the number: that they claimed they would be punished only for the number of days they worshipped the calf. I give that as a **reported explanation of the number**, transmitted in the commentaries, rather than as established fact — which is all it needs to be, since the verse's point does not depend on how they computed the days. The point is that they computed them.

**"They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying."** *Wa-gharrahum fī dīnihim mā kānū yaftarūn.* And this is the verse's heart.

*Gharrahum* — it deluded them, it took them in. And what deluded them? *Mā kānū yaftarūn* — **what they used to fabricate.** The agent of the deception is their own invention.

Look at the circularity. They made up a doctrine that suited them. The doctrine was not grounded in revelation — al-Baqarah had already asked them for the warrant and they had none. But once invented and repeated, it functioned as religion: *fī dīnihim*, **in their religion**. The lie became the thing they trusted. That is why the verse says they were deceived *in their religion* rather than merely *about* something. The corruption is not a mistake they hold; it is the ground they stand on.

This is one of the most penetrating diagnostics in the Qur'an, and it is worth translating into our own idiom, because the specific doctrine changes and the mechanism never does. The modern versions are easy to name: "I'm a good person, so I'll be fine." "My family has been religious for generations." "Our group is the saved one." "God is merciful, so it doesn't matter." "I was born into it." None of these are claims about God derived from what God said. They are claims about ourselves, retro-fitted with a religious vocabulary, and then believed. And their practical effect is identical to the effect described here: **they remove the fear that would otherwise make a person submit to being judged.**

That is the connection back to verse 23, and it is why the two verses are inseparable. A person who believes he is safe does not need an arbiter. He can afford to walk away from the Book when it rules against him.

## Verse 25

**"But how ˹horrible˺ will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt—when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged!"**

In simple terms: so how will it be, when We gather them for a Day about which there is no doubt, and every soul is paid in full for what it earned, and they are not wronged?

**"But how will it be when..."** *Fa-kayfa idhā jama'nāhum.* The Arabic opens with *kayfa* — **how**? — and then stops.

The supplied translation inserts "˹horrible˺" in half-brackets to make the sentence readable in English, and it is a fair gloss. But the Arabic leaves the predicate unspoken, and that omission is the rhetoric. The grammarians note that the predicate is suppressed for effect: the sentence asks *how* and then refuses to supply the adjective, leaving the reader to supply a worse one than the text would. Whatever you are imagining, it is that.

This is a feature of Qur'anic style whenever the subject is the Day: the description stops short, because a completed description would be smaller than the thing. Compare 6:40, 22:1–2, 82:17–19 — "And what will make you know what the Day of Judgement is? Then, what will make you know what the Day of Judgement is?"

**"When We gather them together."** Note who does the gathering: *jama'nāhum* — **We**. The same verb of muster that appeared in verse 12 (*tuḥsharūna*, you will be gathered to Hell). The people who refused to be summoned to the Book will be summoned to the reckoning, and that summons cannot be declined.

**"On the Day about which there is no doubt."** *Li-yawmin lā rayba fīh.* And this is the third time we have met this exact formula in this project, and the repetition is a signature. It was said of the **Book** — *dhālika al-kitābu lā rayba fīh* (2:2). It was said of the Day in the prayer of the firmly-rooted — *li-yawmin lā rayba fīhi* (3:9). And here it is again.

The two certainties of the faith are introduced with the same four words: **the Book has no doubt in it, and the Day has no doubt in it.** Everything in the religion rests on those two, and the Qur'an states them identically so that you cannot hold one without the other. To doubt the Meeting is, in the Qur'an's logic, already to have set down the Book.

**"When every soul will be paid in full for what it has done."** *Wa-wuffiyat kullu nafsin mā kasabat.* The verb is from *waffā* — to pay in full, to complete, to settle a debt to the last measure. The register is mercantile and exact: no rounding, no partial settlement, no balance written off. *Mā kasabat* — what it **earned**. The word keeps the moral weight on the doer: this is not a random allotment but a return on what was done.

**"And none will be wronged."** *Wa-hum lā yuẓlamūn.* And this clause is the one to hold onto, because without it the verse could be read as vengeance.

*Ẓulm* is to put a thing in the wrong place, to give less or more than is due. The denial is absolute: **they will not be wronged.** Not one of them receives a punishment beyond what their own hands produced. The severity of the verse and the justice of the verse are the same fact seen from two sides. A God who punishes exactly is a God who cannot be accused, and the Qur'an never allows the description of the Fire to float free of this clause. Wherever punishment is described, a qualifier of this kind is attached — because the reader's reflex is to assume disproportion, and the text will not let that reflex stand.

The pairing is also the answer to a question that arises naturally after verse 24. They invented a doctrine that shortened the punishment. The correction is not "actually the punishment is longer than you think." The correction is: **the punishment is exactly as long as your deeds were.** They were wrong about the duration, but the deeper error was imagining duration as a thing to be negotiated in the first place.

## Verse 26

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please; You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please—all good is in Your Hands. Surely You ˹alone˺ are Most Capable of everything."**

In simple terms: Say: O God, Owner of all dominion. You give dominion to whom You will and take it away from whom You will; You honour whom You will and humble whom You will. All good is in Your hand. You have power over everything.

This is one of the great prayers of the Qur'an, and it is a prayer — note the opening *qul*, "say." The Prophet ﷺ is being told to say it. It is revelation given in the form of supplication, which means it is also instruction: this is how a person should address God when the argument with the world has run out.

**The context we have just come from is what makes it land.** Verses 21–25 ended with the People of the Book refusing to be judged, protected by a doctrine they invented. And the response is not another argument. It is: *O God, Owner of all dominion.* When people will not submit to the truth, the cure is not better debating. It is to re-establish in your own heart who actually holds the world.

**"O Allah."** *Allāhumma.* This is the intensive form of the divine name used in address. The vocative particle is dropped and the emphatic *mīm* takes its place — a compression that signals urgency and intimacy at once. It occurs five times in the Qur'an: here, at 5:114 (the disciples of Jesus), at 8:32 (the challenge of the disbelievers at Badr), at 10:10 (the greeting of the people of Paradise), and at 39:46 (the opening of a supplication). Every occurrence is at a moment of maximum intensity.

**"Lord over all authorities."** And here is a reading note worth having. The printed text reads *mālika al-mulki* — **Owner** of dominion — and that is the reading in the standard copies. But the verse is also transmitted in the canonical readings as *malika al-mulki* — **Sovereign** of dominion. Both are established, and the difference is the whole point of the phrase.

*Mālik* is the owner — the one to whom the thing belongs. *Malik* is the king — the one who rules it. A king can be a figurehead; an owner can be absent. The verse says both, and by saying both it closes both gaps: He owns it and He rules it. And note that the same root appears twice in three words — *mālika al-mulki*. Not merely "the king," but the one who owns kingship itself. Even the thing by which kings rule belongs to Him. No human authority is ultimate because authority as such is His property.

**The fourfold repetition.** *Tu'tī al-mulka man tashā'u* … *wa-tanzi'u al-mulka mimman tashā'u* … *wa-tu'izzu man tashā'u* … *wa-tudhillu man tashā'u.* Four clauses, each ending in *tashā'u* — "You will." The effect is a drumbeat. God gives, God takes, God honours, God humbles, and in every case the agent is His will.

Note also the preposition in the second clause: *mimman* — "**from** whom You will." The Arabic repeats the preposition rather than eliding it, which is a small emphasis: the taking away is as deliberate as the giving.

**The crux this verse forces: God gives authority to tyrants.** This is where honest readers get stuck, and the verse should not be softened.

If God gives dominion to whom He wills, then every ruler who has ever held power held it by His giving — including Pharaoh, including every oppressor in history. Does that make God responsible for their oppression?

The distinction the tradition draws here is essential and I want to state it plainly. The scholars distinguished between God's **creative, decretive will** (*al-irāda al-kawniyya*) — what He wills to exist, which encompasses everything that exists, good and evil, obedience and disobedience — and His **legislative will of approval** (*al-irāda al-shar'iyya*) — what He commands and loves and is pleased with. Everything that happens falls under the first; only what He has commanded falls under the second.

So: God willed Pharaoh's reign into existence. He did not will it in the sense of approving it. He gave it, and He held Pharaoh accountable for what he did with it. The two wills are not in conflict, and collapsing them produces either the conclusion that God approves of tyranny or the conclusion that tyrants hold power independently of God — and the Qur'an denies both.

What the verse *is* for, then, is not a political theory. It is a comfort and a warning, delivered in the form of worship. To the powerless: the tyrant did not seize this; it was given, and it can be taken, and the One who takes it is being asked. To the powerful: you did not earn this, it was lent, and the lender is the one being addressed in this prayer.

**The reported occasion.** Al-Wāḥidī, citing Ibn 'Abbās and Anas ibn Mālik, records: when the Prophet ﷺ conquered Makkah and promised his community the dominions of Persia and Byzantium, the hypocrites and the Jews said, "How far-fetched! How could Muḥammad conquer Persia and Byzantium? Are Makkah and Madinah not enough for him?" And this verse came down. Al-Jalālayn gives the same occasion. A related account attributes it to 'Abdullāh ibn Ubayy's remark after the Conquest, and another to a Quraysh taunt: "The king of Persia sleeps on silk brocade; if you are a prophet, show us your dominion." *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* maps the clauses onto that occasion concretely — "You give dominion to whom You will," meaning Muḥammad; "and take it from whom You will," meaning Persia and Byzantium.

These are **reported** occasions in the tafsir literature. The strongest thing to say about them is that they fit: the verse answers mockery of the community's weakness by relocating power entirely in God. And that is the verse's permanent use.

**"All good is in Your Hands."** *Bi-yadika al-khayr.* Two things here, and the first is a matter of creed.

God's **hand** is among the attributes the Qur'an affirms of Him. The established Sunni position is to affirm it as God has affirmed it, **without asking how** (*bi-lā kayf*), without likening it to a created hand (*tashbīh*), and without interpreting it away (*ta'wīl* that empties the attribute of meaning). This is the same discipline applied to God's *nafs* in verse 28 and to His other attributes throughout. The verse says *yad*; we say *yad*; and we do not speculate about its modality. I state the rule briefly and leave it there, because the history of what happened when people stopped leaving it there is not edifying.

The second thing is the word **good**. The verse does not say "all things are in Your hand." It says **all good** is in Your hand. And the Sunnah makes the complement explicit. In the Prophet's ﷺ supplication — reported by Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, Ibn Mājah and Aḥmad — the words are: *wal-khayru kulluhu fī yadayka, wa-al-sharru laysa ilayka* — "**all good is in Your hands, and evil is not attributable to You.**"

How to understand that, without either emptying it or creating a second creator? The formulation the scholars settled on runs like this: God's decree, considered in itself, proceeding as it does from His mercy and wisdom, has no pure evil in it. Evil enters by relation — what is evil with respect to one thing is good with respect to another, and what is evil in one respect is good in another. Drought is evil for the crop and mercy for the soil; punishment is evil for the punished and justice for the wronged. So evil is real, and it is created, and it is never attributed to God as *evil* — because in relation to Him it is always, on some axis, good and wise.

That is a careful and demanding position, and it is worth knowing that it is a position rather than a slogan. It is also why the verse's own phrasing matters so much: the Qur'an itself, at the very moment of asserting total divine control over who rules and who falls, says *bi-yadika al-khayr*. Control without the attribution of evil.

**"Surely You alone are Most Capable of everything."** *Innaka 'alā kulli shay'in qadīr.* The prayer closes by grounding everything just said in power. He can give, and He can take, and He can honour, and He can humble — because nothing is beyond Him.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 27

**"You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit.”"**

In simple terms: You make the night enter into the day and the day into the night. You bring out the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whom You will without measure.

The prayer continues and descends. Verse 26 was about kings and kingdoms — the largest scale. Verse 27 moves to the alternation of night and day, to life and death in the smallest things, and finally to your daily bread. From the throne to the table, in three clauses.

**"You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night."** *Tūliju al-layla fī al-nahāri wa-tūliju al-nahāra fī al-layl.*

And the word to notice is *tūliju* — not "You alternate," not "You replace." *Īlāj* is to insert one thing **into** another, to make it enter. The night does not simply follow the day; it is made to pass into it, and the day into it. The image is of interpenetration and gradual change, which is exactly what anyone who has watched a season turn knows: the daylight does not switch, it creeps, gaining minutes and then losing them.

Note also that all four verbs in this verse are in the imperfect — **ongoing**. This is not a description of an original act of creation after which the machinery runs itself. God is making the night enter the day now, and now, and now. That is the Qur'an's characteristic insistence: creation is a continuous act, not a completed event.

**"You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living."** *Tukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti wa-tukhriju al-mayyita mina al-ḥayy.* And here the commentators have offered a range of readings, and it is worth having them all rather than picking one.

The most common classical reading is biological and observational: the living bird from the lifeless egg, the living shoot from the seemingly inert seed, the living human from the drop that is not, in the classical understanding, itself alive. The symmetry is exact and it was visible to anyone without instruments.

A second reading, reported from Ibn 'Abbās and others, is spiritual rather than biological: God brings forth the **believer from the disbeliever** and the disbeliever from the believer. On this reading the verse is about guidance and misguidance, about the child of a righteous household who goes astray and the child of a corrupt one who is guided. This reading is transmitted in the tafsir literature and it is a legitimate extension of the words, though it is an interpretation rather than the plain sense.

A third reading, common in modern writing, maps the phrase onto what contemporary biology says about living matter: organisms composed of non-living molecules, life arising from what is not alive. I want to be careful here, and the caution is the same one I have applied throughout this commentary. The verse is a statement about **who acts**, not a claim about mechanism. Using it as a scientific prediction creates a falsifiable reading out of a verse that does not need one — and the classical readings, note, are genuinely different from the modern one, not primitive versions of it. Where modern knowledge is useful is in deepening wonder: to know what actually happens inside a seed is to be more astonished by the One who brings the living out of it, not less. That is the right use, and it is enough.

The same phrase recurs at 6:95, 10:31 and 30:19, always as a proof of the Resurrection, which is its function here too: the One who can bring living out of dead and dead out of living, continuously, in front of you, can raise the dead. That is why it sits in a prayer immediately before a provision about sustenance.

**"And You provide for whoever You will without limit."** *Wa-tarzuqu man tashā'u bi-ghayri ḥisāb.*

And here is a connection I would not want you to miss, because the reader who has come through al-Baqarah with me will recognise it instantly. **This is the closing clause of 2:212** — "And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit" — the verse that also spoke about the life of this world being made appealing to those who disbelieve and about the mindful ranking above them on the Day of Judgement. The identical phrase, closing the identical argument. And it will recur once more in this surah, at 3:37, about Mary's provision in the sanctuary.

*Bi-ghayri ḥisāb* carries two senses, and the commentators give both. It can mean **without measure** — beyond counting, abundant. And it can mean **without reckoning** — not computed by desert, not settled as a wage. Both are true and both are needed, and the second is the one that explains a fact everyone notices: the provision of this world is distributed in ways that have no visible correlation with piety. The verse says so, in advance, and explains it: provision is not a wage. It is a gift, given on a basis the Giver has not disclosed.

**Why does this prayer stand where it does?** I said at the start of the batch that the movement here is the lesson, and it is worth drawing out at the end of verse 27.

The Prophet ﷺ has been told to argue (verse 20), and the argument failed — they refused their own Book (verse 23) on the strength of an invention (verse 24). And the Qur'an's instruction at that point is not to refine the case. It is to stop talking to them and start talking to God, and the very first thing to say is: **You own it all.** Dominions, honour, humiliation, the alternation of day and night, life and death, and bread.

That is the pastoral sequence for anyone whose arguments with the world have run out. Do not escalate. Relocate the sovereignty, and then notice that the One who owns the kingdoms is the One who is already feeding you.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 28

**"Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah— unless it is a precaution against their tyranny. And Allah warns you about Himself. And to Allah is the final return."**

In simple terms: let not the believers take the disbelievers as allies instead of the believers. Whoever does that has nothing from God at all — unless it is a guarding against them, as a safeguard. And God warns you about Himself, and to God is the journey's end.

This is one of the most consequential and most misused verses in the Qur'an, and I am going to spend real time on it, because the difference between the correct reading and the common one is the difference between a verse that protects the community and a verse that is used to do a great deal of harm.

**"Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians."** The word is *awliyā'*, the plural of *walī*. And *walī* is one of the widest words in Arabic. It covers: a protector, a guardian, an intimate friend, an ally, a patron, a person to whom you entrust your affairs, one whose side you are on. The whole difficulty of the verse is deciding which of those is meant and how far it goes.

Then comes the qualifier that does the real work: *min dūni al-mu'minīna* — **instead of** the believers, to the exclusion of the believers. The prohibition is not on contact, or on knowledge of them, or on dealings. It is on a **transfer of allegiance**: taking them as your *awliyā'* in the place where the believers should be.

**"And whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah."** *Wa-man yaf'al dhālika fa-laysa mina Allāhi fī shay'in.* And here the commentators divide, and the division has consequences.

The severe reading is al-Jalālayn's: "whoever does that — that is, takes them as patrons — **does not belong to the religion of God in any way**." On this reading, the act takes a person out of the religion entirely.

The milder reading is the one in *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*: "has no honour, mercy or protection from Allah." On this reading, the person forfeits God's protection and support — a grave sin, but not expulsion from the faith.

How are these held together? The distinction the tradition drew, and which I think is right, is between **the allegiance of the heart** and **outward dealing**. *Muwālāh* of the heart — loving their religion, approving it, preferring it, giving one's loyalty to it — is what the severe reading describes, and it is unbelief. Outward dealing, kindness, commerce, protection agreements, neighbourliness, and even affection in the ordinary human sense — none of that is what the verse prohibits, and the Prophet ﷺ himself did all of it continuously. He traded with the People of the Book, accepted their counsel, married into them, was owed money by a Jew, and stood for the funeral of one.

I want to be blunt about why this matters, because the stakes are not academic. This verse is regularly quoted to forbid Muslims from friendship, cooperation, employment, or ordinary civic life with non-Muslims, and to declare those who do so outside the religion. That reading cannot survive contact with the Prophet's own practice, and it cannot survive contact with the rest of the Qur'an.

The decisive qualifier is 60:8–9, which states the boundary explicitly: God does not forbid you from dealing kindly and justly with those who have not fought you because of religion and have not driven you from your homes — He loves those who act justly. What He forbids is taking as allies those who **fight you because of your religion and drive you out and help in driving you out**. The qualifier is hostility, not difference of religion. A verse that is quoted without 60:8–9 is being quoted in a way that the Qur'an itself prevents.

**"Unless it is a precaution against their tyranny."** *Illā an tattaqū minhum tuqātan.* And this exception is one of the most interesting small pieces of Arabic in the surah.

**A reading note.** The 'Uthmānic script writes the word in a form that can be read two ways, and both readings are transmitted. Most of the canonical readers read it *tuqātan* (تُقَاةً). One of the ten — Ya'qūb al-Yamānī — reads it *taqiyyatan* (تَقِيَّةً). Al-Jalālayn notes the relationship directly: "*tuqātan*, 'as a safeguard', is the verbal noun of *taqiyyatan*." The meaning is identical in both.

**And the root is the point.** *Tuqāh* is from *w-q-y* — the same root as ***taqwā***. The exception is not called "deceit" or "cunning" or "concealment." It is called **guarding**. The word for the exception and the word for the whole inner life of the religion are the same word. That single fact reframes the exception completely: it is not a licence to be duplicitous. It is the same protective posture before harm that *taqwā* is before God.

**What the exception permits.** The classical position among the Ahl al-Sunnah is narrow and specific: concealment is permitted — and in some cases required — where there is genuine compulsion or well-founded fear for one's life, and it is confined to **outward speech and conduct**, while the heart remains at rest in faith. The proof-text is 16:106: "Whoever disbelieves in God after having believed — except one who is forced while his heart is at rest in faith." That verse was revealed about 'Ammār ibn Yāsir, who was tortured until he spoke words he did not mean, and the Prophet ﷺ told him that if they returned to it, he should say it again.

The Shī'ī tradition developed *taqiyyah* into a broader and more central doctrine of prudent concealment extending well beyond cases of coercion. I am noting that difference factually: it is a real difference in scope between the traditions, and it is not resolved by this verse alone, since the verse's own phrasing is restrictive — *tuqātan minhum*, a guarding **against them** — which ties the permission to an actual threat from them.

Maududi's note here is a good statement of the Sunni limit: concealment under extraordinary constraint, to save one's life, and it "should remain within reasonable limits." *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* describes the permitted form precisely: "saving yourselves from them by speaking in a friendly way towards them, **while your hearts dislike this**." The heart's refusal is the condition. The moment the heart agrees, it is no longer *tuqāh*; it is the thing the verse prohibits.

**The reported occasion.** Two accounts are transmitted, and together they are unusually instructive.

Al-Kalbī's account: the verse was revealed about the hypocrites — 'Abdullāh ibn Ubayy and his companions — who allied themselves with the Jews and the idolaters, **passing them information** about the Muslims and hoping that they would prevail over the Messenger of God. So God revealed this, warning the believers against acting like them.

The second, transmitted by Juwaybir from al-Ḍaḥḥāk from Ibn 'Abbās, is about 'Ubādah ibn al-Ṣāmit — one of the veterans of Badr, a trusted man — who had alliances among the Jews. When the Prophet ﷺ marched out to the Battle of the Confederates, 'Ubādah stood and dissolved them, declaring himself free of their alliance and turning to God and His messenger.

Set those two side by side. Same city, same moment, same question: which side are you on when it costs you something? One group kept the alliance and fed information to the enemy. One man walked away from allies he had held for years. The verse is not about a theoretical loyalty. It is about what you do with your alliances on the day they are tested.

**"And Allah warns you about Himself."** *Wa-yuḥadhdhirukumu Allāhu nafsah.*

This is one of the most famous half-verses in the Qur'an, and it deserves the attention it gets.

On the level of creed, the rule is the same as for the hand in verse 26: God's **self** (*nafs*) is affirmed as He affirmed it, without asking how, without likening it to a created self, without explaining it away.

On the level of rhetoric, notice what is happening. Normally a warning has three parts: the one who warns, the one warned, and the danger. Here the one who warns and the danger are **the same**. God warns you *about God*.

That is not a threat and it is not sadism, and it is worth being clear about why. A warning is given by someone who wants you to escape the thing he is warning about. The reason this phrase lands with the weight it does is precisely that it is said by the One who has been, since verse 1, establishing His mercy: *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm*, the One who provides without reckoning, the One whose servants He calls servants. To be warned *about* the One who loves you is the most serious and also the most intimate form of address available. It is the fear that belongs to love, not the fear that belongs to danger.

The scholars of the heart sometimes put it like this: the person who fears God alone has no one else left to fear, and the person who does not fear God ends up fearing everything.

**"And to Allah is the final return."** *Wa-ilā Allāhi al-maṣīr.* *Maṣīr* is the end of a journey, the place you arrive at. Everything returns there, including every alliance you made and every one you declined.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 29

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”"**

In simple terms: Say: whether you hide what is in your breasts or make it known, God knows it. And He knows everything in the heavens and the earth. And God has power over all things.

**The placement.** This verse follows directly on verse 28, and it is aimed straight at the case that verse raises. Verse 28 forbade an allegiance of the heart taken *instead of* the believers. But an allegiance of the heart is invisible — which invites the thought that the prohibition can be evaded by concealment. Verse 29 closes that: **whether you hide it or show it, God knows it.**

This is why the verse uses *ṣudūr* — **breasts** — rather than *qulūb*, hearts. The heart is the seat of what you feel; the chest is the container of what you keep. "What is in your breasts" is the thing you are holding in, and the verse says the holding in accomplishes nothing.

**The parallel in al-Baqarah.** The reader will recognise 2:284 almost word for word: "Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it." That verse is one of the most discussed in the whole Qur'an, because of the narration in which the Companions were distressed by it and came to the Prophet ﷺ saying they had been given a responsibility they could not bear, and 2:285–286 came down in response — the verse ending "Our Lord, do not burden us with what we cannot bear."

Āl 'Imrān handles the same material differently. Here, the statement of God's knowledge is not followed by a verse of relief addressed to the Prophet ﷺ; it is followed by the reckoning (verse 30) and then by **"And Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants."** The relief is stated by God directly rather than requested. The two surahs are running the same argument in two keys.

**The crux: are we accountable for what we conceal?** This matters enormously, and the answer requires two texts.

The verse establishes that God **knows** the hidden. The hadith establishes what we are **accountable** for. In the ṣaḥīḥ narration from Abū Hurayrah, the Prophet ﷺ said: "**God has pardoned for my community what their souls whisper to them, as long as they do not act on it or speak of it**" (reported by al-Bukhārī and Muslim).

Those two are not in tension. The distinction the scholars drew is between a *khaṭrah* — a passing thought, a whisper, something that crosses the mind without settling — and an *'azm* — a settled resolve, an intention the heart has taken hold of. The first is forgiven by that hadith and is not a sin. The second is what this verse is about, and it is written as a deed.

So the correct reading of verse 29 is not "every intrusive thought is held against you," which would be unbearable and is explicitly denied by the Sunnah. It is: **the settled state of your heart is known and counts.** Which is exactly what verse 28 requires and exactly what makes the religion interior rather than performative.

**"For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth."** The verse generalises from your breast to everything. The God who knows your hidden allegiance is the God who knows everything there is — so there is no question of His knowledge being partial, or of something escaping it because it is large or small or far away.

**"And Allah is Most Capable of everything."** And the verse closes on power rather than on knowledge alone. He knows, and having known, He is able to act. That is the completion of the warning: not only is nothing hidden, nothing is beyond reach.

**The spiritual use.** This verse is the ground of *iḥsān*, which we met at 3:5 and which the Prophet ﷺ defined in the hadith of Jibrīl: "that you worship God as though you see Him — for if you do not see Him, He sees you" (reported by Muslim from 'Umar). Verse 29 is that definition in Qur'anic form. And it is worth noticing that it is placed immediately after a verse about political and social allegiance: the Qur'an treats the inside and the outside as one continuous thing. You cannot sort your loyalties in public and have a separate arrangement in private, because the private one is the real one.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 30

**"˹Watch for˺ the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done. And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off. And Allah warns you about Himself. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants."**

In simple terms: the Day when every soul finds present whatever good it did, and whatever evil it did — it will wish that there were a great distance between itself and that evil. And God warns you about Himself. And God is Ever Gracious to His servants.

**"The Day when every soul will be presented with."** The supplied translation adds "˹Watch for˺" at the start, which is a fair way of handling the grammar: the Arabic opens with *yawma*, an accusative of time, "the Day when…", and it hangs on the warning in verse 29. Read it as one sentence: He knows what you conceal, so watch for the Day when…

**"Every soul will find present whatever good it has done."** *Tajidu kullu nafsin mā 'amilat min khayrin muḥḍaran.*

Two words carry the weight. *Tajidu* — it **finds**. Not "is told about," not "remembers." It finds it there, the way you find something waiting in a room. And *muḥḍaran* — **made present**, produced, brought forth.

So the deed is not a record consulted. It is an object encountered. The good you did has become a thing that is in the room with you. That is one of the strangest and most repeated claims in the Qur'an's eschatology: acts have a kind of endurance, an objectivity, and on that Day they are brought into the presence of the person who did them. See 18:49 ("and they will find present whatever they did"), 99:6–8 ("that Day people will emerge separately to be shown their deeds — so whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it"), and 100:10.

**"And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off."** *Wa-mā 'amilat min sū'in tawaddu law anna baynahā wa-baynahu amadan ba'īdan.*

Now the second half, and it is the one I would want a reader to sit with.

*Tawaddu* — it will wish, it will long. *Law anna baynahā wa-baynahu amadan ba'īdan* — that between it and that evil there were an **amad ba'īd**. *Amad* is a term, a stretch, a reach of distance or of time — the word carries both. So the soul wants an unbridgeable stretch between **itself** and **its own deed**.

Notice what has happened. The evil is described as though it were a separate entity with a location, something a person could in principle be far away from. And the wish is for distance from something that is, in the most literal sense, part of you. That is the horror of the image and its precision: there is no distance to be had, because the thing you want to be far from came out of you and now stands in front of you. You can want the gap. You cannot have it.

And notice the asymmetry with the first clause. The good is **present** (*muḥḍaran*). The evil is **wished distant**. Both are there; the difference is entirely in the soul's posture toward them. On that Day, being near your own good is joy and being near your own evil is agony — and the nearness is not chosen by anyone but you, in advance.

**"And Allah warns you about Himself."** *Wa-yuḥadhdhirukumu Allāhu nafsah.*

And the phrase returns, **three verses after it appeared at the end of verse 28**. In a book as economical as the Qur'an, repetition of this kind is never filler. And the thing to notice is what each occurrence is paired with:

- Verse 28: "And Allah warns you about Himself. **And to Allah is the final return.**"
- Verse 30: "And Allah warns you about Himself. **And Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants.**"

Identical warning, two different closings. The first ends on the return — you are going back to Him. The second ends on His gentleness — and He is *ra'ūf* to you. Those two sentences, taken together, are the whole architecture of Islamic hope and fear in a single line: **you are returning to Him, and He is gentle with servants.** Any spirituality that keeps only the first becomes despair; any that keeps only the second becomes presumption. The Qur'an will not let you separate them, and it says so by repeating the same warning with a different ending three verses apart.

**"And Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants."** *Wa-Allāhu ra'ūfun bi-al-'ibād.*

*Ra'ūf* — from *ra'fah*, a tender compassion, the kind of mercy that **spares**. It is a gentler, more intimate word than *raḥmah*, and it is used sparingly in the Qur'an, often precisely where the context is severe.

And note who it is directed at: *bi-al-**'ibād*** — to **servants**. Not "to the believers," not "to the obedient." The gentleness named at the close of the harshest passage in the surah so far is addressed to servants as such. The word itself carries the relationship: a servant is someone the Master is responsible for.

**The bracket on the whole passage.** Look back at where this stretch began. Verse 21 used the verb of glad tidings to announce a painful punishment, addressing the killers of prophets with the coldest irony in the Qur'an. Ten verses later, the passage closes with "And Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants."

Those are not two moods that the compilers failed to reconcile. They are the two ends of a single argument, and the Qur'an holds both deliberately. The severity is real, and it is aimed at a specific pattern of conduct — denial that escalates to violence, protected by an invented assurance. The gentleness is real, and it is aimed at servants, which is to say at you. A reader who takes only the severity has misread the passage. A reader who takes only the gentleness has not understood what the severity is for.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses form a single movement from indictment to prayer to accountability, and the turning point is verse 26.

**Verses 21–25 are the indictment**, and they are built as a chain of cause and effect. The crime is named in three escalating stages: denial of the signs, killing the messengers, killing the ordinary people who command justice (21). The verdict is that such works come to nothing here and hereafter, with no one to help (22). Then the surah exposes the mechanism: these are people who were given a portion of scripture, who were invited to let scripture judge between them, and who walked away (23). And the reason they could afford to walk away is that they had invented a doctrine of immunity — the Fire will only touch us for a few days — and had then been deluded **by their own fabrication** (24). The reckoning is exact, and no one is wronged (25).

**Verse 26–27 are the turning.** At the point where argument has failed, the Prophet ﷺ is told to stop addressing the people and address God instead, and the prayer he is given is the most complete statement of sovereignty in the Qur'an: dominion given and withdrawn, honour and humiliation, the hand in which all good lies, the night entering the day, the living from the dead, provision without reckoning. The descent is deliberate — from thrones to bread — and the whole thing is said in the second person, to the One who holds it.

**Verses 28–30 are what follows once sovereignty is rightly located.** The boundary of allegiance is drawn (28), with its single narrow exception of *tuqāh* — a word from the same root as *taqwā*, guarding rather than deceiving. The hidden is declared known, and the settled intention counted while the passing whisper is pardoned (29). And the Day is described in the verse that gives this batch its most haunting image: the soul finding its own deeds present and wishing for an unbridgeable distance from them (30).

**Four threads run through the whole.**

The first is **"no doubt in it."** *Lā rayba fīh* was said of the Book at 2:2 and of the Day at 3:9 and again at 3:25. The two certainties of the faith are stated in the identical words, and this passage shows what happens when the second one is quietly replaced with an invented assurance.

The second is **"Allah warns you about Himself,"** said at verse 28 and again at verse 30 — the first time closed by "to Allah is the final return," the second by "Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants." Return and gentleness, said about the same God, three verses apart.

The third is **"without limit."** *Bi-ghayri ḥisāb* closed 2:212, closes 3:27, and will close 3:37 about Mary. The God who owns the kingdoms is the God who feeds the individual, and neither is a wage.

The fourth is the **'aqīdah pair** this passage hands us: God's *hand* (26) and God's *self* (28, 30). Both affirmed as stated, both without asking how, both without likening or emptying. The rule is one rule, and it is applied here to two attributes in five verses.

And the hinge on which all of it turns is the movement I named at the start. When the argument with the People of the Book closed and they refused to be judged by their own scripture, the Qur'an did not tell the Prophet ﷺ to press harder. It told him to pray — and the first words of the prayer were *O God, Owner of all dominion*.

- Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb* — the discussion of *iḥbāṭ* and of the attributes in 3:26 and 3:28

- al-Jalālayn — the severe reading of *laysa mina Allāhi fī shay'in*, the note that *tuqātan* is the verbal noun of *taqiyyatan*, and the occasion for 3:26
- *Tanwīr al-Miqbās min Tafsīr Ibn ʿAbbās* (a late compilation attributed to Ibn 'Abbās; read with the caution its chain warrants) — the mapping of 3:26 onto Muḥammad, Persia and Byzantium; the milder reading of 3:28; the description of permissible *tuqāh*

- Muftī Muḥammad Shafīʿ, *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* — on 3:26 as an encouragement to gratitude, and on the warning in 3:28

**Other works**
- Works on *ʿaqīdah* — the affirmation of *yad* and *nafs* without *tashbīh* or *taʾtīl*; the distinction between the decretive will (*irāda kawniyya*) and the will of approval (*irāda shar'iyya*); the formulation that evil is not attributed to God in itself
- Works on *kalām* and *uṣūl al-dīn* — the *iḥbāṭ* dispute and the Mu'tazilī doctrine of *al-manzilah bayna al-manzilatayn*, alongside the position of the Ahl al-Sunnah on the nullification of deeds
- Works on *uṣūl al-fiqh* and *fiqh* — the distinction between the allegiance of the heart and outward dealing, and the limits of *tuqāh*
- Works on *qirā'āt* — the two readings of *mālika al-mulki* / *malika al-mulki* in 3:26, and of *tuqātan* / *taqiyyatan* in 3:28
- Mahmoud M. Ayoub, on the variant accounts for the occasion of 3:23
- al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, *al-Mufradāt*; Ibn Manẓūr, *Lisān al-ʿArab* — lexicography, particularly *daʾb*, *ḥabaṭa*, *naṣīb*, *gharra*, *iftarā*, *waffā*, *īlāj*, *amad*, *raʾfah*, and the root *w-q-y*

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes, or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where a report is transmitted with a variable chain — the occasions for 3:23, 3:26 and 3:28, the number of days in 3:24, the spiritual reading of "the living from the dead" in 3:27 — I have flagged it as reported rather than established. Where the disagreement is real — the severity of *laysa mina Allāhi fī shay'in* in 3:28, the scope of *tuqāh*, the mechanism of *iḥbāṭ* in 3:22, the two readings of the divine attributes — I have given the arguments on each side. On 3:22 I have indicated where I think the weight lies without presenting it as settled, and on 3:28 I have stated plainly that the verse cannot be read without 60:8–9.
