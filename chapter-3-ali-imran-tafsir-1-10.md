# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 1–10

We come now to Āl 'Imrān, the third surah and — by the agreement of the scholars — a Madani one. It sits immediately after al-Baqarah, and the two are so closely joined that the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have named them together: "Recite the Qur'an, for it will come on the Day of Resurrection as an intercessor for those who recite it. Recite the two bright ones — al-Baqarah and Sūrat Āl 'Imrān — for they will come on the Day of Resurrection like two clouds, or two shades, or two flocks of birds in ranks, pleading for their companions" (reported by Muslim from Abū Umāmah al-Bāhilī). The name *al-Zahrāwān*, "the two luminous ones," is explained by al-Nawawi as referring to their light, their guidance, and the greatness of their reward.

The pairing is visible on the page before a single line is read. Al-Baqarah opens *Alif-Lām-Mīm. That is the Book!*; Āl 'Imrān opens *Alif-Lām-Mīm. Allah! There is no god except Him.* The same three letters, the same declaration about the Book, and then the same material treated from a new angle: where al-Baqarah was addressed at length to the Children of Israel and laid down the law of the new community, Āl 'Imrān turns outward to the other claimants to revelation and inward to the community's own wounds. It holds the long argument with the People of the Book, the story of Mary and Jesus, the challenge of *mubāhalah*, and then the autopsy of Uḥud — the day the Muslims lost a battle they thought they had won.

That last point matters for how we read these first ten verses. Āl 'Imrān was not revealed in one sitting. The standard view, reported by the sīrah-based commentators from Ibn Isḥāq and others, is that the opening passage — roughly the first eighty verses — belongs to the visit of the Christian delegation from Najrān, which is placed in the ninth year after the migration, the famous "Year of Delegations," while the Uḥud material in the middle of the surah (verses 121 onward) belongs to the third year. If that is right, the surah was arranged by command rather than by chronology: the earliest material is not at the front. This is a reminder we will need again and again — the Qur'an's order is not the order of events, and reading it as if it were flattens the argument.

The reported occasion for the opening is worth stating plainly, with its qualifications. Ibn Isḥāq's *Sīrah* records that a delegation of Christians from Najrān came to the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah — sixty riders, including fourteen of their leading men — and disputed with him about Jesus, claiming divinity for him. Ibn Kathīr and others state that God sent down the beginning of this surah to answer their claims. This report is the most commonly cited context for the passage, and it fits the content well: the surah's opening insists that God is one, that He has no son, that the Torah and the Gospel were genuinely revealed, and that Jesus is God's word and a spirit from Him but not God. But it comes from the sīrah literature, transmitted by Ibn Isḥāq, and hadith critics have long treated Ibn Isḥāq's reports that lack a full chain back to a Companion as needing corroboration. It is **reported**, and it is the majority's frame for the passage; it is not a ṣaḥīḥ hadith with an unbroken chain. A second report, which we will meet in the commentary on verse 7, connects that verse instead to Jewish scholars of Madinah computing the lifespan of the community from the letter-values of *Alif-Lām-Mīm*. The honest position is that both are transmitted reports of varying strength, and that the safe reading takes the verses as addressing a general and recurring phenomenon rather than tying them to one afternoon.

With that in hand, these first ten verses form a single, deliberate movement. Verses 1–2 name God. Verses 3–4 establish the revelation and what it does. Verses 5–6 establish what God knows and what God does. Verse 7 delivers the surah's most consequential single verse, on how human beings mishandle the Qur'an itself. Verses 8–9 give us the prayer of those who handle it correctly. Verse 10 closes with what no wealth and no child can buy off. Read as one arc: God, revelation, knowledge, creation, scripture, prayer, accountability.

## Verse 1

**"Alif-Lãm-Mĩm."**

In simple terms: the surah opens with three Arabic letters, recited one by one by their names — *Alif*, *Lām*, *Mīm* — not joined into a word and not forming a sentence. They are part of the revealed text. They are recited exactly as they appear, and they are written in every copy of the Qur'an. What they *mean* is one of the genuinely unsettled questions of Qur'anic study, and the most honest answer is the one the earliest authorities gave: God knows best.

The translators call them the "disjoined letters" or "the opening letters" — *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭa'āt*. Twenty-nine surahs begin with some combination of them. *Alif-Lām-Mīm* itself opens six: al-Baqarah (2), Āl 'Imrān (3), al-'Ankabūt (29), al-Rūm (30), Luqmān (31), and al-Sajdah (32). So when you reach this verse having just finished al-Baqarah, the effect is deliberate: you are being told that this surah belongs with the one before it. It is the same door, opened again.

Now, the interpretations. I will give you the main ones and tell you which are strong and which are weak, because this is a place where a great deal of confident-sounding material circulates.

The position of most of the early authorities — reported from Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, 'Alī ibn Abī Ṭālib, 'Ā'ishah, and Ibn Mas'ūd, and it is the position that later scholars such as al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr incline to — is that these letters are among the things whose knowledge God kept to Himself. They are recited for their blessing and their reward; their meaning is not sought. On this view, this verse is the first instance of what verse 7 will call *mutashābih*: the ambiguous, whose full meaning belongs to God. That is a coherent reading of the surah, and it may well be why God placed these letters at the head of a surah whose seventh verse is precisely about the ambiguous.

A second position, reported from Ibn 'Abbās and others with some variation, is that the letters are an oath by God, or that each stands for one of His names or attributes — *Alif* for *Allāh*, *Lām* for *Laṭīf*, *Mīm* for *Majīd*, for instance. These reports exist in the literature, and al-Suyūṭī collects a number of them in *al-Itqān*. But hadith critics have generally found their chains weak, and the attributions are inconsistent with one another — one report will give a different name for the same letter than another. They are **transmitted but weak**, and no sound hadith establishes any particular expansion of the letters.

A third position — associated with al-Zamakhsharī and developed at length by Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, and favoured by many modern commentators — is rhetorical rather than lexical. On this view the letters are a challenge. The Arabs who were told the Qur'an was inimitable spoke these very letters; the Qur'an is built out of the same twenty-eight sounds they used every day, and yet they cannot produce anything like it. Opening a surah with the bare alphabet says, in effect: here are the bricks; build. This reading has the virtue of explaining why the letters appear at the head of surahs that immediately go on to speak about the Book, and it explains why the letters differ from surah to surah rather than being one fixed formula. It is **a strong and well-argued interpretation**, though it is an interpretation and not the plain sense of the words.

A fourth position — that the letters encode dates, chronologies, or the lifespan of the community through their numerical values (the *abjad* or *ḥisāb al-jummal* system) — is **rejected by mainstream scholarship**, and it should be, for reasons I will spell out in verse 7. It is worth knowing that this is where that report about the Jews of Madinah belongs, and worth knowing that it has no standing as a method.

What is *not* disputed is the practice. Every canonical reader recites these letters, spelled out, in every prayer. Whatever they mean, they are Qur'an, and reciting them is worship.

One more thing worth noticing, and it is the one I would want you to carry. The letters come *before* the statement about the Book. Al-Baqarah said the letters, then "That is the Book!" Āl 'Imrān says the letters, then "Allah!" — a different second move. The point in both cases is the same: the human alphabet is being put in its place. God is not made of letters; the Book that is made of letters is His, and the letters themselves confess it by standing there meaningless until He gives them meaning.

## Verse 2

**"Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him —the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining."**

In simple terms: God — there is no god but Him, the Living, the Self-Sustaining by Whom all things stand.

This is a verse that will feel familiar, and that is the point. The sentence *Allāhu lā ilāha illā Huwa al-Ḥayyu al-Qayyūmu* appears almost word for word as the opening of Āyat al-Kursī in al-Baqarah (2:255). There it arrived as the crest of the longest surah, at the summit of the greatest verse in the Qur'an. Here it arrives in the second verse of a new surah, as a foundation rather than a summit. Al-Baqarah climbed for two hundred and fifty-four verses to reach this sentence; Āl 'Imrān starts with it and then spends two hundred verses showing what follows from it.

The English rendering needs two notes. First, the half-brackets around "worthy of worship" are the translator's, and they are doing real work. The Arabic is *lā ilāha illā Huwa* — "there is no god except Him." Taken with brutal literalism, that would deny the existence of everything anyone has ever called a god, which is not the Qur'an's argument; the Qur'an repeatedly acknowledges that people worship things other than God (see 25:43, "Have you seen the one who has taken his own desires as his god?"). What is being denied is that anything deserves worship, or is a god *in fact*. The supplied translation's gloss is good, and it prevents exactly the misunderstanding that a bare "there is no god but Allah" invites in English.

Second, note the phrasing "*the* Ever-Living, All-Sustaining" rather than simply "Living and Self-Sustaining." *Al-Ḥayy* and *al-Qayyūm* are two of God's names, and they are a pair that does a great deal of theological work. *Al-Ḥayy* is the One who has life — not life as a borrowed quality but life as His own, unacquired, undiminished, without beginning or end. *Al-Qayyūm* is harder to render. It carries the sense of the One who stands by Himself and by Whom everything else stands; the One who needs nothing and upon whom everything depends at every instant. "All-Sustaining" catches part of it. "Self-Subsistent," used in several older translations, catches the other part. Neither English word carries both.

Why these two names here, and why now? Because the argument that is coming is about Jesus. In the ancient Near East, the debate about a divine son was at bottom a debate about life and dependence. The claim being made for Jesus by the Najrān delegation was, in the Qur'an's own summary, that he was God or God's son. The answer is not a polemic; it is a definition. God is the Living and the Self-Sustaining. A son, in the sense the Christians of Najrān meant, is one who shares his father's nature, who is begotten, who derives. But the Living One does not derive life from another, and the Self-Sustaining One does not need a partner to hold things up. The verse does not say "God has no son." It says something more foundational, from which "God has no son" follows necessarily: God is *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm*, and anything that is begotten is neither.

There is a spiritual register here too, and it is why these two names are so often on the tongue in prayer. *Al-Ḥayy* means that you are addressing someone who hears you, not an absent principle. *Al-Qayyūm* means that the reason your heart is beating this second is not the momentum of yesterday but His holding it now. A great deal of the anxiety people carry comes from a practical belief that things hold themselves up and that the burden is therefore on them. This verse is the correction, said in four words of Arabic.

Notice, finally, the structure. Verse 1 gave the letters. Verse 2 gives the Name. The surah will spend its length unpacking what that Name entails, and it will end — as we shall see when we get there — with patience, perseverance, standing firm, and mindfulness of God (3:200). Those are not two different gods. The Self-Sustaining One who opens the surah is the One who is sufficient at the end of it.

## Verse 3

**"He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel"**

In simple terms: God has sent the Book down to you, Muhammad, in truth — confirming the scriptures that came before it. He sent down the Torah and the Gospel.

Before anything else, a mechanical note that will save you real confusion. Read verses 3 and 4 as one sentence. The supplied English ends verse 3 with "the Gospel" and no full stop, and begins verse 4 with "previously, as a guide for people." That is not a stylistic choice. The verse-numbering was fixed long after the revelation, and here it happens to cut in the middle of a clause: the Arabic of 3:3 ends with *wa-anzala al-Tawrāta wa-al-Injīl* ("and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel"), and 3:4 opens with *min qablu hudan li-al-nāsi wa-anzala al-Furqān* ("before, as guidance for people, and He sent down the Criterion"). So "previously, as a guide for people" modifies what came before, not what follows. Read it as: He revealed the Torah and the Gospel **previously, as a guide for people**, and He revealed the Criterion. If you read verse 4 as beginning a new thought you will mis-handle the grammar and lose a claim that matters.

Now to the substance, and there is a great deal of it.

**"He has revealed to you the Book in truth."** The verb here is *nazzala* — the intensive form, which carries repetition: He sent it down *in stages*, again and again. The commentators point out that this is deliberate, and that the contrast with the next clause is precise. Al-Jalālayn notes it directly: God uses *nazzala* for the Qur'an and *anzala* for the Torah and the Gospel, because the Qur'an came down piecemeal over twenty-three years while those two were sent down at once. There is a theological claim inside that grammar, and it is one Muslim apologists have often under-used. A book that arrives in fragments across two decades, answering arguments as they arise and legislating for circumstances as they change, is a book in conversation with a live community. The gradualness is not a weakness to be explained away; it is the mode of a revelation that intends to form a people rather than merely inform them.

**"In truth"** — *bi-al-ḥaqq*. This attaches to the revelation itself: the Book is not a plausible fabrication that happens to be useful; it is true, and it came with the truth, and it establishes the truth. The phrase recurs at the seams of the Qur'an's argument, and it usually appears where a claim to revelation is being contested.

**"Confirming what came before it."** The word is *muṣaddiqan* — the active participle of "to declare true." The Qur'an's relationship to the earlier scriptures is one of confirmation, and this is a point on which Muslims are frequently unclear, sometimes in both directions at once. Some speak as if the Qur'an simply replaced everything before it; others as if the Bible in its present form is the confirmed scripture. The classical position is more careful than either. The Qur'an affirms that the Torah was truly revealed to Moses and the Gospel truly revealed to Jesus. What it denies is that the texts now in Jewish and Christian hands are those revelations intact. The Qur'an's own vocabulary for what happened to them is *taḥrīf* — a word that covers displacement and distortion of meaning as well as alteration of wording, and the commentators have long differed on how far each goes. Maududi's position, stated in his notes here, is representative: the Torah is not identical with the first five books of the Old Testament even though those books contain part of it, and likewise the Gospel is not identical with the four Gospels; scattered fragments of the original remain within them, and it is those that the Qur'an confirms.

That may sound like a convenient dodge. It is not, and it is worth seeing why. It is the only position consistent with the Qur'an's own statements taken together. If the present Biblical text were wholly the revealed Torah, then the Qur'an's corrections of it (on the crucifixion, on the sinlessness of the prophets, on the law) would be contradictions of a confirmed revelation — which is impossible for a book that says it confirms. If it were wholly corrupted with nothing of the original left, then the Qur'an's repeated appeals to "the People of the Book, you have a scripture; judge by it" would be incoherent. What remains is the middle: genuine revelation, genuinely transmitted, genuinely overlaid. The practical consequence is that a Muslim reading the Bible reads it with respect and without surrender — looking for what rings true to the confirmed core, and judging by the Criterion.

**"As He revealed the Torah and the Gospel."** Note that the Qur'an here, in a surah largely addressed *against* Christian claims about Jesus, goes out of its way to affirm that the Gospel was revealed. The argument is not "your book is fake." It is "your book is Mine, and you have misread it." That is a far more demanding thing to say, because it requires the addressee to take their own scripture seriously enough to be corrected by it.

One small linguistic note that matters for English readers. *Al-Injīl* corresponds to the Greek *euangelion*, "good news," and it refers to what was revealed to Jesus — a single revelation, in the Qur'an's framing, not four biographies written decades later in a different language by authors who never met him. When the Qur'an says "the Gospel," it does not mean the Gospel of Matthew. Keeping that distinction in mind prevents a great deal of muddle in interfaith conversation.

## Verse 4

**"previously, as a guide for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment. For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment."**

In simple terms: He sent those down before, as guidance for people, and He sent down the Criterion. Those who reject God's signs will have a severe punishment. God is Mighty, Master of Retribution.

**"Previously, as a guide for people."** As noted above, *min qablu hudan li-al-nāsi* attaches to the Torah and the Gospel: they were sent down before the Qur'an, and they were *hudā* — guidance. Not merely information, and not merely law. Guidance is the Qur'an's own preferred word for what revelation is *for*, and it is used of the earlier scriptures as freely as of the later one. There is a single divine curriculum here, and the same teacher.

**"And He revealed the Criterion"** — *wa-anzala al-Furqān*. *Al-Furqān* is from the root *f-r-q*, to split or separate, and the form carries intensity: that which separates decisively. It is the instrument that divides truth from falsehood, guidance from misguidance, lawful from unlawful. The supplied translation's "Standard," glossed as "to distinguish between right and wrong," is a legitimate rendering — though "Criterion" is more standard in English and is the word most readers will meet elsewhere. (The translation elsewhere renders *al-Furqān* as "the Standard," so I am keeping that, but you should know that when you see "Criterion" in another translation it is the same word.)

Now — what is *al-Furqān* here? The commentators differ, and the difference is worth having because it changes the shape of the claim.

The first view, reported from a number of early authorities and favoured by al-Jalālayn, is that *al-Furqān* here means **the Qur'an itself**. On this reading the verse says: He sent down the Book to you, confirming what preceded it, and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel earlier as guidance — and He sent down the Criterion, meaning this Qur'an, which discriminates between truth and falsehood. The repetition is emphatic rather than redundant: the Book is named once as *confirming* and once as *discriminating*, because it does both.

The second view, which al-Jalālayn also records, is that *al-Furqān* is a **general term covering all revealed scripture** — the discriminating quality that belongs to every genuine revelation. On this reading God mentions the Criterion after naming the three scriptures so that the term gathers up every revelation beyond those three: the Psalms, the scrolls of Abraham, whatever was sent to messengers not named here. This has the advantage of resolving a puzzle: the Qur'an speaks of many messengers (see 40:78, "We have already sent messengers before you; of them are those We have related to you and those We have not"), and a term restricted only to the Qur'an would leave them unaccounted for.

The third view, found in some of the older sources including *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, identifies it simply with the Qur'an as that which distinguishes the lawful from the unlawful — a legal rather than a creedal emphasis.

These are not mutually exclusive, and it is legitimate to hold that the word picks out the Qur'an *as* the supreme instance of the criterion that every revelation possessed. The strongest reading, I think, is the second, because it fits the verse's evident scope: this is a statement about God's whole dealing with humanity, not a statement about one book among others. But the disagreement is real, and no one is obliged to pick.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, in his lexicon of Qur'anic vocabulary, is useful here. He points out that *furqān* is used in the Qur'an for the Qur'an, for the Torah (2:53, "when We gave Moses the Scripture and the Criterion"), and for the deliverance at Badr (8:29 calls it *yawm al-Furqān*, the day the two hosts were separated). The common thread is the act of separating — and that includes God's separating a community out of danger. Which gives us the fourth layer, and the one to carry into the rest of the surah: *al-Furqān* is not only a book. It is what God gives a person so that they can tell the difference. In 8:29, "O you who believe, if you are mindful of God, He will grant you a *furqān*" — a criterion, a discernment, a way out. The external standard and the internal faculty bear the same name, and the one leads to the other. You read the Criterion so that you become capable of criterion.

**"Surely those who reject Allah's revelations will suffer a severe torment."** Having named the three scriptures and the Criterion, the verse immediately names the alternative. *Āyāt* here means revelations — the signs God has sent — though the word covers both the textual signs and the signs in the world, and the classical commentators read it as including both. The rejection is *kufr*, which in Qur'anic usage is not primarily intellectual disbelief but the act of covering over what one knows.

**"For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment."** *ʿAzīz* and *dhū intiqām*. The second phrase is often softened in translation; the supplied "capable of punishment" is one such softening, and other translations give "Lord of Retribution" or "Owner of Vengeance." The Arabic is stronger than "capable." *Intiqām* is retribution exacted — and the point of the phrase is not that God is vengeful in a human sense, but that the One who has the right to punish and the power to punish is the same One who has been sending guidance for millennia. The warning is not cruelty; it is the seriousness of a long patience that has been refused.

There is a deliberate balance to hold here, and it runs through the whole surah. God is *ʿAzīz* — mighty, unopposable — and *dhū intiqām*, master of retribution. In the very next breath the surah will call Him *al-Wahhāb*, the constant Giver (verse 8). Neither name is the whole picture. A faith built only on the first produces terror; a faith built only on the second produces presumption. The Qur'an's habit is to set them side by side and let the reader hold both.

## Verse 5

**"Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah."**

In simple terms: nothing at all — nothing on the earth and nothing in the skies — is hidden from God.

The Arabic is *inna Allāha lā yakhfā ʿalayhi shayʾun fī al-arḍi wa-lā fī al-samāʾ*. There is a striking detail here for anyone who reads the Qur'an attentively across surahs: the usual order is "the heavens and the earth" — *al-samāwāt wa-al-arḍ* — and it appears that way hundreds of times. Here the earth comes first. The commentators note the inversion and offer reasons: that the earth is what is nearest to the addressee and most immediate to their experience; or that the context is about human action, and human action happens on the earth; or, as some of the rhetoricians argue, that placing the nearer and humbler term first and then ascending is a way of saying "and how much more the heavens." I would not build doctrine on it, but it is a live observation in the tafsir literature and a good example of how closely these books read.

What the verse *does* establish is beyond dispute: God's knowledge is exhaustive. Nothing is concealed from Him. Not the large matters only, but every particular.

That last clause — "every particular" — is the one that theologians argued over for centuries, and it is worth knowing why, because it is not an abstract quibble. Among the Muslim philosophers working in the Aristotelian and Neoplatonic tradition, most notably Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna), a position developed according to which God knows universals but not changing particulars as particulars — because particulars are known through senses and through time, and God has neither. On that scheme, God knows that a human being will be born, and knows the universal laws governing conception, but does not know *this* embryo being formed in *this* womb at *this* moment.

Al-Ghazālī attacked this in the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers") as one of three positions that take a person outside Islam, the other two being the eternity of the world and the denial of bodily resurrection. His proof-texts were exactly this kind of verse. If nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from God, then *kull* — every particular, down to the individual — is known to Him.

I am telling you this not to drag you into a medieval dispute but because the stakes are personal. The difference between "God knows the general run of things" and "God knows you" is the difference between a deistic first cause and a Lord you pray to. The Qur'an's God is the second. This verse, standing where it does — immediately before a verse about God shaping each individual in the womb — is making precisely that point, and the two verses are one argument.

There is also a plain moral application, and it is the one the preachers of every generation have drawn. If nothing on earth is hidden from God, then the gap between what people see and what is real is closed. The Qur'an calls this quality of living *iḥsān*, and the Prophet ﷺ defined it in the well-known hadith of Gabriel: "that you worship God as though you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He sees you" (reported by Muslim from 'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb). That is verse 5 turned into a way of being.

## Verse 6

**"He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise."**

In simple terms: He is the One who forms you in your mothers' wombs however He wills. There is no god but Him, the Mighty, the Wise.

This verse is a hinge, and it rewards slow reading.

**"He is the One Who shapes you."** The verb is *yuṣawwirukum*, from the root *ṣ-w-r* — to form, to fashion, to give shape. It is the verb from which *ṣūrah* (form, image) comes, and it is the same root that appears in the hadiths warning against image-making, where the makers of images are called *al-muṣawwirūn*. That is not incidental. One of the deep claims of the Qur'an's theology of creation is that *giving form* is God's act, and that the creature's attempt to rival it is a kind of theft. The classical discussions of representational art and, later, of photography, all turn on how far a human act of forming encroaches on a divine one.

Note also the tense. *Yuṣawwirukum* is present continuous: He *is shaping* you. The commentators point out that on one reading this refers back to the original formation in the womb, and it certainly does. But the present-tense form is not a mistranslation of a past event. God's shaping is ongoing: the same verb that formed you at the beginning holds your form now. The womb is named because it is where the forming is most obviously not yours.

**"In the wombs of your mothers."** *Fī al-arḥām.* The word *raḥm* for womb is from the same root as *raḥmah*, mercy — a connection the commentators never tire of noting, and one that the Prophet ﷺ made explicit in the narration in which God says: "I am the Most Merciful (*al-Raḥmān*), and I created the womb (*al-raḥim*) and derived a name for it from My name; whoever maintains it I will maintain, and whoever severs it I will sever" (reported by al-Tirmidhī from Abū Hurayrah, and graded ṣaḥīḥ). The place where you were formed is named after the attribute by which you were formed.

**"As He wills."** *Kayfa yashāʾ.* This is the phrase that carries the verse's weight, and the supplied translation renders it well. It is not only that God forms; it is that He forms *however He wills* — which is to say, freely, and with difference. No two faces are alike. Every human being who has existed is distinguishable from every other, and this is not a manufacturing variable; it is the will of the One who is shaping. In a surah that will argue about God's power to create Jesus without a father, this is the setup: the One who varies the formation of every child in every womb at will is not constrained to a single mode of creating. That is the argument the surah will make explicitly at 3:47 and 3:59, and its root is here.

The classical commentators also read *kayfa yashāʾ* as covering the sex, the appearance, the completeness, the term, and the destiny of the child — everything about the forming is by will, not by the physics of conception alone.

**Now to the hadith material**, because this is where readers usually want specifics, and because there is a genuine scholarly disagreement here that is routinely flattened.

The most famous narration is that of 'Abdullāh ibn Mas'ūd, reported by both al-Bukhārī and Muslim: "Each of you is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop (*nuṭfah*), then he is a clinging thing (*ʿalaqah*) for a similar period, then he is a chewed lump (*muḍghah*) for a similar period. Then an angel is sent and commanded with four matters: he writes his provision, his lifespan, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or blessed. Then the soul is breathed into him" (al-Bukhārī, in the Book of the Beginning of Creation, and Muslim). This is among the most securely transmitted hadiths in the corpus, and it is the natural companion to this verse.

The complication is that another narration — that of Ḥudhayfah ibn Asīd, reported by Muslim — places the sending of the angel and the giving of shape at **forty-two nights**, not at a hundred and twenty days. The two narrations sit awkwardly together, and the scholars have handled it in several ways. Some reconcile by saying that the angel named in Ḥudhayfah's narration is a different angel performing a different task from the one in Ibn Mas'ūd's. Some hold, as the Ḥanafī scholar Waheed al-Zaman argued in his notes on al-Bukhārī, that the apparent meaning of the sound narrations places the breathing of the soul early and that the contrary claims of physicians are what must be set aside. Others, including Ibn al-Qayyim, reject the reconciliation outright as doing violence to the plain sense of the texts. A number of contemporary hadith scholars, having gathered all the narrations, have concluded that no sound or fair narration clearly states that the soul is breathed at four months, and that the stronger position is forty to forty-three days after conception.

I am laying this out at length because it has consequences. The majority of jurists historically took the hundred-and-twenty-day figure from Ibn Mas'ūd's narration and built rulings on it — most consequentially in the law of abortion, where the four-month line marks a sharp change in the ruling for most schools. If the ensoulment point is in fact earlier, then the fiqh built on it needs revisiting. That is a live debate, not a settled matter, and it is **disputed**. I am not giving a ruling here; the point is that people who cite "120 days" as though it were a settled certainty are overstating the case, and people who dismiss the classical position out of hand are understating it.

**On modern science.** The standing rule in this commentary has been to treat scientific material as illustrative, never as proof — and this verse is the clearest test of why. The vocabulary of *nuṭfah*, *ʿalaqah*, and *muḍghah* describes what the unassisted eye can see: a drop of fluid, something that clings, a small mass like something chewed. That is honest and accurate as description. The trouble begins when a verse about *who is acting* is converted into a claim about *what the embryo looks like at week six*, and then defended as a miracle. Two generations of apologists have done exactly that, mapping the Qur'anic terms onto Carnegie stages of embryology, and the result has been a literature that is falsifiable in a way the Qur'an is not — because when the embryology textbooks revise a stage description, the "miracle" has to be re-derived. That is a bad trade, and it is unnecessary. The verse is not a textbook. It says: the One who is forming you in there is God, and He forms as He wills. That is a claim about agency and about meaning, and no microscope will confirm or refute it. Where modern knowledge is genuinely useful is in the *wonder* — in knowing enough about what actually happens in those weeks to be more astonished, not less. That is the right use of it.

**"There is no god except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise."** The verse closes where verse 2 closed, with the same negation, but with different names. Verse 2 ended *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — the Living, the Self-Sustaining. This verse ends *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — the Mighty, the Wise. The movement is exact. Having established that God knows everything (verse 5) and forms every person (verse 6), the conclusion is that the One who does this is unopposable in power and perfect in wisdom. Power alone would be frightening; wisdom alone would be inert. Their pairing is the answer to the question every reader of the previous sentence wants to ask: if He shapes as He wills, what happens to the child formed with a defect, or the pregnancy that ends? The verse does not answer that. It names the One who does, as Mighty and as Wise, and leaves the rest with Him.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 7

**"He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason."**

This is the most consequential verse in the passage, and one of the most consequential in the Qur'an. It is the verse that governs how the Qur'an is to be read — and, therefore, how everything else is to be read. I am going to spend real time on it, because almost every error people make with the Qur'an is an error this verse already diagnosed.

**In simple terms:** God sent down this Book. Some of its verses are clear and decisive — those are the foundation, the mother of the Book. Others are ambiguous, open, resistant to a single settled reading. Now here is what happens. People in whose hearts there is a crookedness go after the ambiguous ones, chasing discord and chasing an interpretation they can impose. But the full meaning of those verses is known to God alone — and the ones who are firmly rooted in knowledge say: we believe in it; all of it is from our Lord. And only people of deep understanding take the point.

**The Arabic, word by word.**

*Muḥkamāt* — from *ḥ-k-m*, the root of "to judge," "to make firm," "to restrain." A *muḥkam* verse is one that has been made firm: precise, decisive, unambiguous, standing on its own and able to be the reference point for others. The supplied translation's "precise" is an excellent choice, and I want to say why, because the older English tradition used "fundamental" or "basic" and the looser tradition used "clear," and both miss something. *Muḥkam* is not "simple"; a verse can be profound and perfectly precise. It means that its meaning is *settled* — it is not open to being pulled another way.

*Mutashābihāt* — from *sh-b-h*, "to resemble." The literal sense is "those that resemble one another," or "those that resemble something other than themselves." The supplied translation's "elusive" is, I think, the best single English word anyone has produced for it, and it is a real improvement on the "allegorical" that appears in older translations. "Allegorical" imports a Western literary category — a story whose surface meaning is not its real meaning — and the Qur'an is not claiming that. What is being said is that these verses are *resistant*: they admit of more than one reading, and no reader can settle which is intended by the text alone.

And here is the first thing most people get wrong, and it is worth pausing on. *Mutashābih* is **not a pejorative word** in the Qur'an. In 39:23 the entire Qur'an is praised as *kitāban mutashābihan mathāniyā* — "a Book, consistent with itself, in paired repetitions." The whole revelation is *mutashābih* there, in the sense of being internally consonant and mutually resembling. And in 2:25 the fruits of Paradise are described as *mutashābihan* — resembling one another, which the supplied translation rendered as "looks similar." So the word, on its own, means "resembling," and it is only in *this* verse that it carries the sense of resistant-to-interpretation. The ambiguity is in the thing, not a defect in it.

*Umm al-kitāb* — "the mother of the Book," which the supplied translation renders "the foundation of the Book." Both are right. *Umm* is mother; the phrase means the source, the reference, the place to which everything else returns. The *muḥkamāt* are the mother of the Book in the sense that when you do not know how to read a *mutashābih* verse, you take it back to a *muḥkam* one and let the clear verse interpret the less clear one. This is the single most important methodological rule in all of Qur'anic interpretation, and it is the verse's own instruction. It is also, incidentally, why the "proof-text" method — finding one verse that seems to support a position and stopping there — is not merely lazy but contrary to the Qur'an's stated procedure.

*Alladhīna fī qulūbihim zayghun* — "those in whose hearts is *zaygh*." *Zaygh* is deviation, a leaning away from the straight, a crookedness. Now look very carefully at the grammar. The verse does **not** say "those who follow the ambiguous verses develop crooked hearts." It says "those **in whose hearts is** crookedness follow the ambiguous verses." The deviation is already there. It is the premise, not the consequence.

This is the point the verse is built on and the point that is most consistently missed, including by Muslims defending the Qur'an. The question people ask is: why would a wise God put unclear verses in His Book at all? Doesn't that invite exactly the misreading that follows? The verse's answer is that the ambiguity is not the cause. The same text — the same ambiguity — is met by two kinds of people and produces two opposite results. One group goes hunting for trouble; the other says "all of it is from our Lord." The variable is not the text. The variable is the heart. The old analogy, which I have never found bettered: the same sun melts the wax and hardens the clay.

*Ibtighāʾa al-fitnah* — "seeking *fitnah*." *Fitnah* is trial, temptation, sedition, the shaking of a community's cohesion. So what they seek is not understanding; it is division. And *ibtighāʾa taʾwīlihi* — "seeking its *taʾwīl*." This is the crucial and most mistranslated phrase.

*Taʾwīl* comes from *ʾ-w-l*, "to return to the origin," and in the Qur'an's own usage its basic meaning is *the end to which a thing returns*. In Sūrat Yūsuf, *taʾwīl al-aḥādīth* is the interpretation of dreams (12:6) — and what is being interpreted is the *future event* to which the dream returns. Later, in the technical vocabulary of the scholars, *taʾwīl* came to mean: diverting a word from its apparent meaning to a less apparent one on the basis of evidence. Both senses are live here, and I think both are intended.

So "seeking its *taʾwīl*" means: wanting to pin down the thing the ambiguity points to. Not wanting to understand the verse — wanting to *crack* it. To compute the Hour from the letters at the head of a surah. To determine the date of the end. To produce a number. That is why the verse pairs it with "*and none knows its interpretation except God*": the thing they are trying to force out is precisely the thing reserved.

*Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi* — "those firmly rooted in knowledge." *Rasikh* is something driven in deep and holding: a mountain rooted, a tree with deep roots. Not "those who know a lot," but those whose knowledge has taken hold of them. And note what they are described as *doing*: they say. Their defining act is a sentence, not a discovery.

*Wa-mā yadhdhakkaru illā ulū al-albāb* — "and none takes heed except those possessed of *albāb*." *Lubb* is the kernel, the innermost part of a nut or a seed — the opposite of the shell. We met it in al-Baqarah at 2:179 ("so that you may use your reason" — *ulū al-albāb*) and again at 2:197. A person of *lubb* is one who gets past the husk. The surah is telling you at verse 7 that what follows requires getting past the husk.

**Now the crux, and I must flag it directly.**

The supplied translation reads: "...but none grasps their full meaning except Allah. **As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say**..." That is an interpretation, and it is a consequential one. The supplied translation has chosen to place a sentence-break after "except Allah" and to begin a new sentence with "As for those well-grounded in knowledge."

Why does this matter? Because the printed text of the Qur'an does not tell you where to pause. The Arabic runs: *wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā Allāhu wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi yaqūlūna āmannā bihi*. There is no punctuation in the consonantal script, and both punctuations are grammatically defensible:

**Reading 1 — pause at "Allah":** "None knows its interpretation except God. And those firmly rooted in knowledge say: we believe in it; all of it is from our Lord."

**Reading 2 — run it on:** "None knows its interpretation except God **and those firmly rooted in knowledge**, who say: we believe in it; all of it is from our Lord."

The difference is not academic. On Reading 1, the full meaning of the ambiguous verses belongs to God alone, and the mark of the learned is that they *stop* — they believe without claiming to have mastered. On Reading 2, the firmly-rooted are granted knowledge of the interpretation, and the verse distributes a share of the unseen to them. Reading 2 is the proof-text for much of the tradition of *taʾwīl* of the divine attributes, and for the Shīʿī doctrine that the Imāms are the *rāsikhūn* who know the interpretation. Reading 1 is the proof-text for *tafwīḍ* — consigning the knowledge to God.

**Who held which?** Honestly: the weight of the early authorities is on Reading 1. It is the reading attributed to Ibn 'Abbās in the narration most commonly cited, to 'Ā'ishah, to 'Urwah ibn al-Zubayr, to 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Azīz, and it is the reading that al-Shāfiʿī's school and the majority of the commentators followed. 'Abdullāh Yūsuf 'Alī, whose translation takes Reading 2, adds a footnote saying so plainly: the connected reading "is rejected by the majority of commentators, but accepted by Mujāhid and others." Reading 2 is attributed to Mujāhid and to others among the Successors, and it was taken up by a good number of later scholars.

**What are the arguments?** The strongest argument for Reading 1 is the one Ṭabāṭabāʾī develops in *al-Mīzān*, and it is genuinely forceful. If the word "and" were conjunctive — if the *rāsikhūn* were being joined to God as co-knowers — then the Prophet ﷺ would certainly be first among them. It is unthinkable that the Qur'an was revealed to him and he did not know what it meant. So God would have had to say "none knows its interpretation except God, and His Messenger, and those firmly rooted in knowledge." He did not say that. Further: the sentence that follows, "they say: we believe in it," is *itself* the mark of someone who does not claim exhaustive knowledge. A person who knows the interpretation does not say "we believe in it"; he says what it is. And structurally, the verse is built on a contrast between two groups — the crooked-hearted and the firmly-rooted — and what is being contrasted is their *response* to the same ambiguity, not their respective information budgets. On this reading the *rāsikhūn* are praised for their *adab*, their comportment before what they do not know, and not for omniscience.

The argument for Reading 2 is that the phrase "those firmly rooted in knowledge" is otherwise awkwardly placed, and that it is strange to praise a group by mention and then give them nothing. Supporters also cite reports in which Ibn 'Abbās is said to have claimed: "I am of those firmly rooted in knowledge who know its interpretation." This report is transmitted — al-Suyūṭī cites it in *al-Itqān* — but its chain is discussed, and even if it is accepted it proves only that *some* knowledge of the ambiguous is granted to some people, which no one denies. It does not prove that the *rāsikhūn* as such know the full *taʾwīl*.

There are also variant recitations reported that would settle the question by inserting the equivalent of "who say" in a way that forces Reading 2. These are reported from some of the Companions, but they are **not part of the canonical readings** — not among the seven, and not among the ten — and the scholars of *qirāʾāt* do not accept a reading as Qur'an on the basis of such reports. Ṭabāṭabāʾī dismisses them outright as being of no probative value. I mention them only so that you are not blindsided if you meet them; they do not change the text.

**Where I think the stronger reading lies**, stated as a judgment and not as a certainty: Reading 1 is stronger on the grammar, stronger on the transmitted attribution, and — most tellingly — stronger on the *rhetoric*. The whole verse is about the discipline of stopping. If you turn it into a verse about who has the most information, you have inverted its point. But I want to name the thing that matters more than the verdict: **the two readings converge on the ethics**. Even on Reading 2, the *rāsikhūn*'s response to what they know is "we believe in it; all of it is from our Lord." They do not say "we have solved it." They do not publish a date. The practical distance between the two positions is much narrower than the centuries of argument suggest, and a person who holds Reading 2 while behaving like the *rāsikhūn* is in a far better place than a person who holds Reading 1 while behaving like the people with crooked hearts.

**What the verse is *not* saying.** Three common misreadings, all of them serious.

One: it is not saying that most of the Qur'an is unclear. The *muḥkamāt* are *umm al-kitāb* — the foundation, the majority, the reference. The ambiguous are the exception, and the rule for handling them is to refer them back to the clear. Anyone who tells you that the Qur'an cannot be understood without a special key has inverted the verse.

Two: it is not authorising you to declare any verse you find difficult to be *mutashābih* and therefore exempt from obedience. That is the sleight of hand by which inconvenient rulings get set aside. The category is real, but it is defined by the text's own resistance — established by the consensus of qualified readers, or by the inability of a verse to be settled by reference to the clear ones — not by personal discomfort.

Three: it is not condemning *tafsīr*. What is condemned is *ibtighāʾ* — the seeking after, with an agenda. The verse's own words are "seeking discord" and "seeking its interpretation." The object of the criticism is the motive as much as the method. Exerting oneself to understand the Qur'an is commanded (38:29, "a Book We have sent down to you, blessed, that they may ponder its verses"). What is forbidden is using the Qur'an's ambiguity as a weapon.

**The reported occasions, and how to hold them.** Two are commonly given.

The first is the Najrān delegation, already mentioned. If the opening of the surah came down in response to Christian claims about Jesus, then verse 7 is describing a live phenomenon: people coming to the text with a settled doctrine and pressing the ambiguous verses into its service. That fits *ibtighāʾ al-fitnah* well.

The second is the report involving Jewish scholars of Madinah and the disjoined letters — the *abjad* computation. In the version transmitted in the sīrah literature, Ḥuyayy ibn Akhṭab and others came to the Prophet ﷺ and said that they had computed the lifespan of his community from the numerical values of the opening letters: *Alif* is 1, *Lām* is 30, *Mīm* is 40 — seventy-one years. When told of *Alif-Lām-Mīm-Ṣād*, they computed a hundred and sixty-one years; and so on through *Alif-Lām-Rā* and *Alif-Lām-Mīm-Rā*, concluding that the matter was obscure to them. If this report is sound, it is a perfect illustration of the verse, because it is exactly *ibtighāʾ taʾwīlihi* — the attempt to extract a concealed date from the deliberately opaque.

But it is a **sīrah report, transmitted through Ibn Isḥāq, and it is not a ṣaḥīḥ hadith**. It is cited widely in the tafsir literature and it is the standard illustration given for this verse in popular teaching. It should be presented as an illustration, not as established history. Which, happily, is all it needs to be: whether or not that conversation happened, the behaviour it describes is real and recurring.

**And it is recurring, right now.** If you want to see *ibtighāʾ al-fitnah* in its modern dress, look at the numerology industry — the "code 19" literature, the abjad calculations that produce dates for the end of the world, the YouTube exegesis that finds current events encoded in chapter-and-verse numbers. Look at the opposite error too: the person who treats every verse about the divine attributes as radioactive and refuses to affirm anything, and the person who treats them as physical descriptions and refuses to negate anything. Both are chasing an interpretation rather than submitting to a text. And look at the most common version in ordinary religious life: the verse screenshotted to win an argument, torn out of the *muḥkam* verses that would qualify it, and presented as though the whole Book said only that.

The verse's cure is a single sentence, and it is the sentence the *rāsikhūn* actually say: *āmannā bihi, kullun min ʿindi rabbinā* — "we believe in it; all of it is from our Lord." Not "we believe in the parts we have solved." *All of it.* The clear and the resistant, the comfortable and the uncomfortable, the verses that settle my argument and the verses that unsettle it. All of it, from one Lord, who does not contradict Himself.

**And the closing clause.** "But none will be mindful except people of reason." After everything, the verse ends by telling you that the ability to receive this is itself a condition of the heart. Which is why the very next thing in the surah is a prayer.

## Verse 8

**"˹They say,˺ “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺."**

In simple terms: they say: Our Lord, do not let our hearts swerve after You have guided us. Grant us mercy from Yourself. You are the constant Giver.

Two mechanical notes before the substance, because both will otherwise confuse you.

First, the supplied translation opens this verse with "˹They say,˺" and an opening quotation mark, and it does not close the quotation until the end of verse 9. So verses 8 and 9 are one quoted prayer, split across two numbered verses. The "they" are the *rāsikhūn* of verse 7. This is the point I flagged there: verse 7 says the firmly-rooted "say: we believe in it," and verses 8–9 are what they say. If you ever want a one-verse summary of what sound scholarship sounds like when it prays, it is these two verses.

Second, a small inconsistency in the supplied translation worth naming. Verse 8 has "after **you** have guided us" with a lowercase *you*, while verse 9 has "**You** will certainly gather" with a capital. The addressee in both is God. Elsewhere the translation capitalises divine pronouns — "Your mercy," "You are indeed the Giver" — so the lowercase in this one clause is an inconsistency in the supplied English rather than a feature of the Arabic. The Arabic is *baʿda idh hadaytanā*: "after **You** guided us." Read it as divine. It is worth noticing because the sense of the petition depends on it: this is not a request that God refrain from undoing someone else's guidance. It is a request to the One who did the guiding, to continue.

**Now the thing to see, and it is the single best observation available in this batch.** The verb in this verse is *lā tuzigh* — "do not cause to deviate." The noun in verse 7 was *zaygh* — deviation. *Same root.* Verse 7 diagnoses the disease: there are people *in whose hearts is zaygh*. Verse 8 prays against it: *lā tuzigh qulūbanā*. The cure is named in the same breath as the pathology, using the same three letters.

The Qur'an does this constantly, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it. The surah will later say of the hypocrites that God "left them" in their transgression, and the prayer of the believers is that He not leave them to it. Verse 7 describes the condition; verse 8 hands you what to say about it. The gap between diagnosis and remedy is one verse long.

This is also, I think, the answer to a question that troubles a lot of readers. If deviation is a matter of the heart, and if hearts are as God made them, then what is the point of praying about it? The answer is that the prayer *is* the point. The Qur'an does not present guidance and misguidance as a mechanical process to which supplication is an irrelevant addendum; it presents the asking as one of the causes. And it is emphatic, in the Sunnah, that this asking is the practice of the one who had the most reason to feel secure.

The narrations here are well known and firmly transmitted. In Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 'Abdullāh ibn 'Amr ibn al-'Āṣ reports that the Prophet ﷺ said: "The hearts of all the children of Adam are between two of the fingers of the Most Merciful, like one heart; He turns it however He wills." Then he said: "O God, Turner of the hearts, turn our hearts to Your obedience" (Muslim, in the Book of Divine Decree). And Anas ibn Mālik reports that the Prophet ﷺ used to say frequently: "O Turner of the hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion" — whereupon it was said to him, "O Messenger of God, we believe in you and in what you have brought; do you fear for us?" and he said: "Yes. The hearts are between two of the fingers of God; He turns them however He wills" (reported by al-Tirmidhī, who graded it sound). 'Ā'ishah is reported to have said that one of the supplications he ﷺ made most often was this one, and that when she asked him about it he replied: "The heart of a person is between two of the fingers of God; if He wills He causes it to go astray, and if He wills He makes it steadfast" (reported by Aḥmad; graded sound on the basis of its supporting narrations by later hadith scholars).

If the one who was promised Paradise feared for his heart, the rest of us can dispense with confidence about ours.

**"Grant us Your mercy."** The Arabic is *hab lanā min ladunka raḥmah* — "give us, from Your own presence, mercy." Two details. *Hab* is a verb of pure gift: it carries no sense of exchange, no hint that the asker has earned it or will repay it. And *min ladunka* — "from Yourself," "from Your very presence" — is emphatic. It is not "send us mercy" or "grant us mercy," as though through a channel. It asks for mercy that comes directly from Him, unmediated, uncaused by anything in the recipient. That is the right posture before a gift one has no claim on.

Notice too that *raḥmah* is left indefinite and unrestricted. Not "mercy in this matter," not "forgiveness for this sin." Just mercy — which means everything that mercy contains: guidance, steadiness, provision, pardon, and the good end. The petitioners are not negotiating. They are asking for the one thing that covers all the others.

**"You are indeed the Giver."** *Innaka anta al-Wahhāb.* *Al-Wahhāb* is one of God's names, and the form is intensive — *faʿʿāl* — which in Arabic denotes constancy and repetition. Not one who gave once, but the constant Giver, the One whose giving is His habit. It is the perfect name to close this petition with, because the logic of the prayer runs: we have nothing; You give; You give always; give.

And notice what the prayer does *not* do. It does not say "we believed, so keep us firm." It does not say "we were obedient, so do not turn us away." There is no merit cited, no bargain offered, no record of service produced. The supplicant's entire case is the character of the One being asked. That is the *adab* of du'ā, and it is why this verse is on the tongue of scholars more than of anyone else: the more a person knows, the more clearly they see that they have no claim.

One boundary I want to state explicitly, because verses like this are sometimes used to frighten people. This prayer is not evidence that God capriciously turns hearts away from people who are trying to reach Him. The Qur'an's own account is uniformly that deviation follows from a person's own choices — from rejecting what they knew (2:26, "He leaves none to stray except the rebellious"), from breaking a covenant after affirming it (2:27), from preferring this life. The prayer is the believer's acknowledgement of dependence, not a description of divine arbitrariness. No one who asks to be kept firm is turned away for asking.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 9

**"Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.”**

In simple terms: Our Lord, You will gather all people together for a Day about which there is no doubt. God does not fail in His promise.

**"Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity."** The Arabic is *rabbanā innaka jāmiʿu al-nāsi li-yawmin* — and here is a feature a careful reader will want to stop on. Grammatically, this is a **statement**, not a request. "You will gather." It is not "gather us" or "forgive us" or "keep us firm" — all of which are imperatives, and all of which is what we have just had in verse 8. So why does a petition open with an assertion?

The commentators give several answers, and they complement each other. One: this is the *reason* for what was asked in verse 8. We ask not to be left to deviate *because* we are going to be gathered and questioned; the certainty of the meeting is what makes steadiness urgent. On that reading the sentence is doing the work of a "since." Two: it is praise offered before the request — the supplicant begins by affirming what he knows of God, and the affirmation *is* the worship. Three: the declaration is itself the petition in the mode of confident hope, as when someone says to a generous man, "you are the one people turn to" — the statement carries the ask within it.

I think the first is the primary sense, and it matters practically. The order of the two verses is: keep us firm (8), *because* You are going to gather us (9). Devotion and eschatology are not separate chapters in the Qur'an; the second is the engine of the first.

**"For the Day about which there is no doubt."** *Li-yawmin lā rayba fīhi.* And here is the echo I would not want you to miss. Al-Baqarah opened: *dhālika al-kitābu lā rayba fīhi* — "That is the Book, about which there is no doubt" (2:2). Āl 'Imrān now says of the Day: *lā rayba fīhi*. The two certainties of the faith are introduced with the identical formula, in the opening pages of the two surahs that are called the two luminous ones. **The Book has no doubt in it, and the Day has no doubt in it.** Everything else in the religion is built on those two certainties, and the Qur'an states them in the same words.

It is also worth saying plainly what "no doubt" means here, because it is sometimes over-claimed. The Qur'an is not saying that no one has ever doubted the Resurrection — the whole Qur'an is a record of people doubting it, arguing about it, and asking for it to be hastened. It is saying that there is no *ground* for doubt: the evidence is sufficient, the promise is true, and the One promising is able. Doubt remains psychologically possible; it is not rationally warranted. That is the correct scope of the phrase.

**"Surely Allah does not break His promise."** *Inna Allāha lā yukhlifu al-mīʿād.* *Mīʿād* is the appointed time — the tryst. So the closing claim is twofold: that God will keep His word, and that He will keep His appointment. The Day is coming on schedule.

And with that, the quotation closes. The prayer of the firmly-rooted, which began in verse 8, ends here. Look at what it consists of: a request for steadiness, a request for mercy, an affirmation of the Gathering, an affirmation of God's faithfulness. Four lines. No request for wealth, no request for victory, no request for the defeat of an enemy. The people described in verse 7 as "firmly rooted in knowledge" turn out to be praying about their hearts and their ending. That is what deep knowledge produces. Everything else is a detail.

There is one more structural point, and it connects this verse back to the whole passage. The prayer is in the plural throughout — *rabbanā*, "**our** Lord"; *hadaytanā*, "You guided **us**"; *hab lanā*, "give **us**." Not once "my Lord." Even in the most intimate moment of the passage, the firmly-rooted do not pray as isolated individuals. That is characteristic of the Qur'an's supplications generally — *rabbanā* is the standard opening, not *rabbī* — and it is worth carrying into one's own du'ā. The habit of praying in the plural is a small, daily cure for a self-absorbed faith.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 10

**"Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire."**

In simple terms: as for those who disbelieve, their wealth and their children will not help them at all against God. They are the fuel of the Fire.

The drop is deliberate. Verses 8 and 9 put a prayer on our lips; verse 10 puts a warning in front of our faces. The surah will do this repeatedly — the people of faith and the people of refusal, set side by side without transition, so that the reader cannot settle into either one.

**A translation note first, and it is not pedantic.** The supplied translation has "the disbelievers," with a definite article. The Arabic is *inna alladhīna kafarū* — "**those who** disbelieve," a relative clause describing people by their act, not a noun naming a fixed class. The difference matters. "The disbelievers" in English can be heard as a named group with membership criteria, which invites the reader to start checking people off a list. "Those who disbelieve" describes a posture. It is also a description that remains open: a person is in it by the act of rejection and leaves it by the act of faith. Given how often this category is used to write people off permanently, the Arabic's phrasing is worth preserving, and it is worth saying here — as it has been said throughout this commentary and will be again — that **no one is entitled to declare a particular living person an inhabitant of the Fire.** That knowledge belongs to God, as verse 7 reminded us about the unseen generally.

**"Neither the wealth nor children."** *Amwāluhum wa-lā awlāduhum* — their wealth and their children. Why these two? Because they are the two things human beings instinctively rely on as insurance. Wealth is what protects you now; children are what continues you after. Together they are the answer to both halves of mortality — the fear of not having enough and the fear of being forgotten. The Qur'an names this pairing over and over: "our wealth and our children are more" (34:35); "your wealth and your children are only a trial" (64:15); "wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world" (18:46); and in this very surah, at verse 14, the love of gold and silver and children is described as made to seem beautiful to people.

And the verse says they will not avail *mina Allāhi* — "against God," or "from God." The preposition is doing something specific: these things cannot buy off, cannot substitute, cannot stand in the place of what is owed. Not because wealth and children are worthless in themselves, but because the currency is wrong. You cannot settle a debt to the Creator with created things.

**"And they will be the fuel for the Fire."** *Wa-ulāʾika hum waqūdu al-nār.* *Waqūd* is fuel — that which is burned. The image reaches back to al-Baqarah: "then fear the Fire, whose fuel is people and stones" (2:24). There the fuel was named generically. Here it is named personally: *they* are it. The shift is the whole warning.

There is a terrible precision in the choice of image. Fuel is consumed; it does not negotiate with the fire, does not endure it, does not survive being put to it. A thing that was meant to be used becomes the thing that is used up. The person who spent their life converting everything — money, children, status — into protection discovers that the protection was the payment.

**Now the misunderstanding that this verse almost always attracts,** and it is worth stating at length because it does real damage. This is **not** a condemnation of having wealth or having children. Both are repeatedly called gifts and blessings in the Qur'an. What is being said is that they cannot function as a substitute for faith — and that for the person whose reliance is *only* on them, they will not even function as protection.

The positive counterpart is clear in the Sunnah, and it is one of the most consoling narrations in the corpus. Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet ﷺ said: "When a person dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: ongoing charity, knowledge that is benefited from, and a righteous child who prays for him" (reported by Muslim). A righteous child — the very thing named in this verse as useless — is here named as one of three things that outlast death. The two statements are not in tension. They are the same point from both sides: children do not help by *existing*, and they are not a policy against judgment. They help when they are righteous and when the relationship with them was built on something other than using them as insurance. And wealth does not help by being accumulated; it helps when it is given, which is why the Qur'an pairs *amwāl* with *infaq* almost every time it mentions it.

So the honest application is not "wealth and children are bad." It is: **what are you relying on them for?** If they are a trust you will be asked about, they are a blessing. If they are a hedge against having to answer to God, this verse is addressed to you.

**Why here?** Because of what is coming. This surah will narrate Uḥud, where the Muslims were told to hold and did not, and where the argument among them was over spoils and over position. It will narrate the defeat, and the whisper that the Prophet ﷺ had died, and the question of how a community that had been promised victory could lose. And threaded through all of it is the question of what people trust in: numbers, equipment, alliances, the market. Āl 'Imrān opens by settling that question before a single battle is mentioned. Wealth and children will not avail. Only what is with God will.

That is the frame for the whole surah, and it is stated in the first ten verses. God is one and self-sustaining (1–2). The revelation is His and it confirms what came before (3–4). He knows everything and forms everyone (5–6). The Book is to be received with discipline, not raided (7). The response of those who understand is to pray for steadiness and to remember the Gathering (8–9). And nothing you own or produce can stand in for any of it (10). Everything that follows is an outworking of those six claims.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses are a compressed statement of the entire religion, and they repay being read as one argument rather than ten.

Verses 1–2 establish **who God is**. The letters stand meaningless until He speaks; then the Name comes, and the Name is glossed with *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — living, self-sustaining. That is the attribute against which every claim of a son, a partner, or a rival will be measured in this surah.

Verses 3–4 establish **what He has sent**. The Qur'an came down in stages, confirming the Torah and the Gospel, which came down before as guidance, and He sent the Criterion. The grammar itself carries the theology: *nazzala* for the gradual Book, *anzala* for the ones sent at once. Then the warning, in the same breath — those who reject the signs have a severe punishment, because the Mighty One is master of retribution.

Verses 5–6 establish **what He knows and does**. Nothing is hidden from Him — not universals only but every particular, which is why al-Ghazālī could rest a charge of unbelief on this verse. And He forms every human being in the womb as He wills, which is why the argument about Jesus' fatherless creation is already won by verse 6.

Verse 7 establishes **how the Book is to be handled**, and it is the pivot. Some verses are decisive and are the reference; some are resistant and are to be referred. People with crooked hearts chase the resistant ones to spread discord and to force an interpretation. The firmly-rooted say: we believe in it; all of it is from our Lord. The great crux — whether the knowledge of the *ta'wīl* belongs to God alone or is shared with the firmly-rooted — is genuinely disputed, with the majority of early authorities on the side of stopping at "except God." But both readings converge on the same comportment, which is the point.

Verses 8–9 establish **what the proper handler prays for**: steadiness, mercy, and remembrance of the Gathering. And the prayer is built on the same root as the disease it treats — *zaygh* in verse 7, *lā tuzigh* in verse 8.

Verse 10 establishes **what cannot be substituted**: wealth and children, the two great human insurances, will not avail against God.

Three threads run through the whole and are worth carrying forward. The first is **the pairing with al-Baqarah** — the shared opening letters, the shared *lā rayba fīhi* formula, the shared closing on God's self-sufficiency. The second is **the pedagogy of pairing opposites**: the crooked-hearted and the firmly-rooted, the prayer and the warning, the gift and the retribution. Āl 'Imrān will keep doing this for two hundred verses. The third is **the discipline of the text itself** — verse 7's instruction to let the decisive verses interpret the resistant ones, which is the method this commentary tries to follow and the thing most often abandoned when a verse is inconvenient.

The surah that opened with the alphabet and the Name now turns, in verse 11, to the fate of Pharaoh's people — and from there to the two armies that met at Badr, which is where the real argument of Āl 'Imrān begins.
