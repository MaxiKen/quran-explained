# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 101–110

This batch closes the long polemical passage and turns to matters of law, method and community conduct. The People of the Book threw the Book of Allah behind their backs (101) and followed sorcery instead — which brings one of the hardest verses in the Qur'an: the exoneration of Solomon, the two figures in Babylon, and the nature of magic itself (102). Had they believed and been mindful, the reward would have been better (103). Then a small, piercing matter of etiquette: a word the believers had picked up that carried a double meaning (104). They do not wish you good, but Allah chooses whom He wills for His grace (105). Then the great verse on abrogation, and the sovereignty that makes it coherent (106–107). Then a warning to the believers themselves: do not badger your messenger as Moses was badgered (108). The instruction to pardon and overlook until Allah brings His command (109). And finally the surah turns back to the standing obligations — the prayer, the zakah, and whatever you send ahead for yourselves (110). Two of these verses, 102 and 106, are among the most consequential and most mishandled in the entire Book, and both are treated at length below.

---

## Verse 101

**"Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them— confirming their own Scriptures—some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs as if they did not know."**

In simple terms: when a messenger came to them confirming the scriptures they already held, a party of those who had been given revelation threw the Book of Allah behind their backs, behaving as though they knew nothing.

*Wa lamma ja'ahum rasulun min 'indi Allahi musaddiqun lima ma'ahum* — "and when there came to them a messenger from Allah, confirming what was with them." Note the small variation from verse 89, which described the arrival of "a **Book** from Allah... confirming what was with them." Here it is "a **messenger**." The two verses state the same arrival from its two sides: the revelation and the man who carried it. Both were confirming, and both were rejected.

*Nabadha fariqun min alladhina utu al-kitaba* — "a party of those who had been given the Book cast aside." *Nabadha* is the same verb used two verses earlier for the covenant they flung away; now the object is the Book itself. To cast something behind the back (*wara'a zuhurihim*) is not to lose it accidentally or to set it down. It is to put it where you will not have to look at it.

And note how the subject is described: *alladhina utu al-kitab*, those who **had been given** the Book. The description is an aggravation, not a mitigation. It was not the ignorant who did this. It was the people who had been entrusted with revelation. The surah has been making this point since verse 75: possession of scripture confers no immunity, and in the people who treat it as property it produces the opposite.

Two things should be noted about the qualifier *fariqun*, "a party." This is the seventh time in this stretch of the surah that a condemnation has been restricted to a subset — verses 59, 65, 75, 83, 85, 100, and now here. The consistency is not incidental. The surah is modelling a discipline in the very act of delivering an indictment, and no reader may generalise where the text particularises seven times running.

*Ka-annahum la ya'lamun* — "as though they do not know." The particle *ka-anna* introduces a comparison that is not literally true, and that is exactly the point. They knew. The whole passage has established it: they distorted the word *after understanding it* (2:75), they denied *what they recognised* (2:89), and 2:146 will say they knew him as they know their own sons. This clause describes a performance, not a state. They behaved like people with no information, and the behaviour was a strategy.

For practice, the verse describes something so common that most people will recognise it in themselves within a few seconds.

It is the strategic use of ignorance. The Book is not lost; it has been placed behind the back, where it cannot be seen and need not be consulted. In practice this looks like: knowing exactly what the ruling is and not checking; remembering the verse that supports your position and not the one that qualifies it; having read the passage and never having thought about it since; arranging your life so that the relevant text is never in front of you.

The second application is the one that makes the verse uncomfortable. Those who had been *given* the Book were the ones who discarded it. Religious knowledge does not protect against this. It makes the failure worse, because it gives you more to avoid and a more sophisticated vocabulary for the avoidance. The person with a shelf of unread religious books, the graduate of a religious programme whose life shows no trace of it, the community with the most scripture and the least obedience — all of them are described here, and the description is precise: they knew, and they put it behind them.

And the third is the remedy, which the verse implies by its own image. Something placed behind the back can be brought back to the front. That is all that is required, and it is available at any moment: take the Book from behind you and put it in front of you, and read it as though it applied to you — because it does.

## Verse 102

**"They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, “We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.” Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!"**

This is one of the most difficult verses in the Qur'an, and it should be approached slowly. It contains at least five separate problems, and the tradition has been honest about every one of them. What follows takes them in the order the text presents them.

**1. The charge against Solomon, and his exoneration.**

*Wa-ttaba'u ma tatlu al-shayatinu 'ala mulki Sulayman* — "and they followed what the devils used to recite concerning the kingdom of Solomon." The background, transmitted in the commentaries, is that some among the People of the Book claimed that Solomon's dominion was achieved through sorcery rather than granted by Allah as a miracle — that he was a magician who ruled by magic. The devils (*shayatin*) fabricated and circulated these stories.

*Wa ma kafara Sulaymanu wa lakinna al-shayatina kafaru* — "and Solomon did not disbelieve, but the devils disbelieved." The exoneration is flat and unqualified. Solomon is a prophet in Islam (4:163, 6:84, 21:78–82, 27:15–44, 34:12–14, 38:30–40), and the Qur'an rejects the claim that he practised magic.

The distinction the verse depends on is one worth stating clearly, because it is the difference between two things that look identical from outside. Solomon's command over the jinn was a **miracle** granted by Allah: "And [We subjected] to Solomon the wind — its morning [journey was that of] a month and its afternoon [journey was that of] a month... and [also] the devils, every builder and diver, and others bound in chains" (38:36–38). A miracle is a suspension of the ordinary order granted by Allah to a prophet as a proof. Sorcery is an attempt to obtain effects through means Allah has forbidden. The difference between them is not in the visible result — which can look the same — but in the source and the permission. The Qur'an's own narratives make the point dramatically: when Pharaoh's magicians saw Moses's staff swallow their ropes, they did not say "he is a better magician." They said, "We have believed in the Lord of the worlds" (7:121–122), because they recognised the difference between what they did and what he did.

**2. The grammatical crux: was something sent down to the two angels?**

*Wa ma unzila 'ala al-malakayni bi-Babila Haruta wa Maruta* — "and what was sent down to the two angels in Babylon, Harut and Marut." The question is whether this clause is coordinated onto "magic" — i.e., the devils taught people magic **and** taught them what had been sent down to the two angels — or whether the *ma* is a negative, giving "and **nothing** was sent down to the two angels."

The majority reading, followed by every standard translation, is the first: Allah sent down something to Harut and Marut as a *fitna*, a trial, and they conveyed it with a warning. A minority reading, argued for by some grammarians and discussed at length by al-Razi, takes *ma* as negative, largely in order to avoid the theological difficulty of attributing the sending-down of magic to Allah. Both readings exist in the tradition. The first is the standard; the second should be known because it is cited and because it tells you how seriously the difficulty was felt.

A variant vocalisation is also transmitted in which the word is read as *al-malikayn*, "the two kings," rather than *al-malakayn*, "the two angels" — a reading that would dissolve the problem entirely by making the two figures human. The standard reading is "the two angels."

**3. The theological problem: angels do not disobey.**

If they are angels, how can they teach magic? The Qur'an is explicit that angels do not disobey: "Over it are angels, stern and severe; they do not disobey Allah in what He commands them, but do what they are commanded" (66:6). And 16:50: "They fear their Lord above them, and they do what they are commanded."

The majority answer is the one the text itself supplies: they were **commanded** to teach it, and they did not teach it as a recommendation. *Wa ma yu'allimani min ahadin hatta yaqula innama nahnu fitnatun fa-la takfur* — "and they did not teach anyone without first saying: we are only a trial, so do not disbelieve." That is, the teaching was obedience to a command; the warning preceded every lesson; and the content of the warning — "do not disbelieve" — is the opposite of teaching disbelief. On this reading the angels are entirely exonerated, and the verse becomes a description of a test rather than of a corruption.

Two further readings should be noted. Some commentators held that what was sent down to them was not magic itself but the **means of recognising magic and undoing it** — a mercy granted so that people could distinguish a prophet's miracle from a sorcerer's trick and protect themselves from harm. That reading is transmitted and it resolves the difficulty elegantly. And some held that Harut and Marut were men rather than angels, which is what the variant vocalisation reflects.

**4. The story in the commentaries.**

This is where the greatest caution is required, and it cannot be overstated.

The elaborated narrative — that Harut and Marut were angels sent down to earth, that they were tempted by a woman, that they committed grave sins, and that they now hang by their feet in a well in Babylon where people come to learn sorcery from them — is transmitted in the commentaries on the authority of various early figures. It is also widely regarded by hadith critics and by a substantial body of mufassirun as **Isra'iliyyat**: narrative material derived from earlier scriptural and Near Eastern tradition rather than from the Prophet ﷺ. Ibn Kathir and others explicitly cautioned against it. Some modern scholars have noted that the story resembles extra-Qur'anic traditions about angelic "watchers" who descend and teach humanity forbidden knowledge and are punished for it; that observation is a scholarly comparison and should be cited as such, not as a finding.

The point for a reader is simple and should be held firmly: **the Qur'an's text here is extremely sparing.** It names two figures, says that something was sent down to them in Babylon, says that they taught it with a warning attached, and says that people learned from them what harmed them. It tells no story. The story is transmitted material of doubtful authenticity, and no part of the verse's lesson depends on accepting any of it.

**5. Is magic real?**

Yes, on the position of Ahl al-Sunnah — and this verse is one of the proof texts. *Wa ma hum bi-darrina bihi min ahadin illa bi-idhni Allah* — "but they do not harm anyone by it except by the permission of Allah." The verse presupposes that harm can occur and locates its occurrence in Allah's permission. The Mu'tazilah denied that magic has real effects, holding it to be illusion and deception only, and they interpreted this clause accordingly. The dispute is real and the Sunni position is the majority one: magic has real effects, created by Allah, as a trial.

The Prophet ﷺ was himself afflicted by it. In the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'A'ishah, a man of the Jews named Labid ibn al-A'sam worked magic on the Prophet ﷺ, with the result that he would imagine he had done something when he had not done it. Jibril came to him and told him where the object was, and the Prophet ﷺ had it retrieved and untied, and he recovered. The scholars discussed at length how this is consistent with prophetic protection, and the settled answer is that what touched him was a physical and imaginative effect of the kind any human being can suffer, and that it did not touch his conveyance of the revelation, his reasoning, or his message — which are what prophethood protects.

The classical remedy for magic is the Qur'an and supplication. The last three surahs are called *al-mu'awwidhat*, the seeking-of-refuge, and 113 and 114 were revealed in connection with exactly this — Surah al-Falaq explicitly mentions "the evil of those who blow on knots" (113:4), which is a description of sorcery.

**The legal ruling on magic, and a necessary guardrail.**

Learning, teaching and practising sorcery is prohibited in Islam, and it is listed in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim as the second of the seven destructive sins, immediately after associating partners with Allah. The jurists differed on whether practising it constitutes disbelief in itself or a major sin short of it, with the difference turning on whether the particular act involves worship of other than Allah. On the punishment in this world, the schools differed, and the discussion belongs to works of law rather than to a verse commentary. What must be said about it, plainly and without qualification:

**Nothing in this verse, and nothing in the hadith, authorises any private individual to accuse, harm, or punish anyone for practising magic.** Accusations of sorcery have killed enormous numbers of people across history — overwhelmingly women, the poor, the elderly and minorities — and that is a grave abuse with no sanction in the properly applied law, which requires proof, a court, a qualified judge and the strictest evidentiary standards. And it should be noted that slandering a chaste believing woman is *itself* one of the seven destructive sins listed in the very hadith that names magic. Accusations of witchcraft still kill people in several parts of the world today, including in Muslim-majority societies, and every such killing is a murder and a major sin. A reader who takes anything else from this verse has taken the opposite of what it teaches.

*Fa-yata'allamuna minhuma ma yufarriquna bihi bayna al-mar'i wa zawjihi* — "and they learn from them that by which they cause separation between a man and his wife." Why this example? Because it is the most intimate bond and the most destructive thing to break, and because sorcery has always been sought for exactly this purpose — to make a spouse hate the other, or to bind a marriage or unbind it. The Qur'an selects the worst instance as representative of the whole.

*Wa yata'allamuna ma yadurruhum wa la yanfa'uhum* — "and they learn what harms them and does not benefit them." The transaction is pure loss on both sides: harm acquired, benefit nil.

*Wa la-qad 'alimu la-mani ishtarahu ma lahu fi al-akhirati min khalaq* — "and they certainly knew that whoever purchases it has no share in the Hereafter." *Khalaq* is an allotted portion. And note again: *they certainly knew*. The knowing-rejection pattern that has run through this whole passage is present here too.

*Wa la-bi'sa ma sharaw bihi anfusahum law kanu ya'lamun* — "and wretched is that for which they sold their souls, if only they knew." The commentators noticed the apparent contradiction: the verse has just said they certainly knew, and now it says "if only they knew." The resolution they gave is one of the most useful distinctions in the whole tradition. There are two kinds of knowledge. One is information you possess — you could state the fact, you would assent to it if asked. The other is the knowledge that governs action, the kind that is present to you at the moment of decision and moves you. They had the first and lacked the second. That is the condition of almost every person who knows what is right and does something else, and it is why the Qur'an's remedy for it is never more information.

For practice, three things carry beyond the historical specifics.

The first is the structure of the *fitna*. The two figures warned before every lesson: this is a test, do not disbelieve. That is how trials work in the Qur'an — the test is never set without the means of passing it being supplied alongside. Every prohibited shortcut arrives with its own warning attached, usually in the form of a cost you can already see. The person who takes the shortcut has not been misled about the terms; he has decided the terms do not apply to him.

The second is the shape of the desire, which is what makes this verse contemporary. Sorcery is the attempt to get a result without the means — to obtain love, power, protection, wealth or revenge by a mechanism other than the one Allah prescribed. That desire has not gone anywhere. It has taken new forms: the scheme that promises return without work, the shortcut that promises the outcome without the discipline, the technology that promises influence without relationship, the manipulation that gets the result while bypassing the person's will. The verse's diagnosis is unchanged: what is obtained this way harms and does not benefit, and the purchaser knew it at the time of purchase.

And the third is the specific harm the verse names. The breaking of a marriage is singled out because it is the worst use to which this was put. An honest survey of what human beings actually deploy manipulation, deception and coercion for would find, again and again, the destruction of the bond between two people who trusted each other. The verse names the industry.

## Verse 103

**"If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew!"**

In simple terms: had they chosen belief and God-consciousness instead of sorcery, the reward they would have received from Allah would have been better than anything they gained by it — if only they had the kind of knowledge that acts.

*Wa law annahum amanu wa-ttaqaw* — "and had they believed and been mindful." *Law* introduces a counterfactual that did not happen. The two conditions are the pair the Qur'an joins throughout: *iman* and *taqwa*. Faith, and the lived awareness of Allah that changes conduct. Neither is offered alone.

*La-mathubatun min 'indi Allahi khayrun* — "a reward from Allah would have been better." *Mathubah* comes from a root meaning to return; a reward is what comes back to you from what you sent ahead. And note the phrase *min 'indi Allah* — "from **with** Allah," in His presence. The reward is not merely dispatched by Him; it is held with Him.

"Better" than what? Better than what they acquired by the sorcery, and better than what they paid for it. The comparison is the whole point of the verse. They sold their souls and received harm; had they chosen the other route, the thing they would have received is *khayr* — better, and with Allah.

*Law kanu ya'lamun* — "if only they knew." The second occurrence of this phrase in two verses, and it carries the same distinction noted at the close of verse 102: the knowledge that is missing is not information but the governing, acting kind.

For practice, this verse is short and it is a complete instrument.

The instrument is the counterfactual. "If only they had believed and been mindful." A person can run that sentence on his own life with brutal clarity: if I had taken the lawful route in this matter, where would I be now? Not the pious abstract version — the concrete one, with the actual numbers, the actual relationships, the actual state of the conscience. Almost everyone who has taken a shortcut can perform this calculation, and almost everyone who performs it honestly arrives at the same answer: the lawful path would have been better, slower, and still standing.

The second application is the pairing. *Amanu wa-ttaqaw* — believed and were mindful. Notice that the verse does not say "had they only believed." Belief alone is never offered as the sufficient condition in the Qur'an's practical promises, and the person who has belief and no God-consciousness has the first half of a sentence that is never completed.

And the third is the location of the reward: *min 'indi Allah* — with Allah. The sorcerer's gain is immediate and in the hand. The reward of belief and *taqwa* is held elsewhere, and received later. Every decision a human being makes is a choice between something in the hand now and something held with Allah later. The Qur'an's claim is not that the deferred thing is nobler. It is that it is *better*, and that the comparison is not close.

## Verse 104

**"O believers! Do not say, “Râ’ina.” ˹Herd us!˺ But say, “Unẓurna,” ˹Tend to us!˺ and listen ˹attentively˺. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment."**

In simple terms: the believers are told to stop using a particular word, because it was being used as a vehicle for mockery, and to use a different word that carries the same request without the ambiguity. They are told to listen.

The setting, transmitted in the commentaries on the authority of Ibn 'Abbas, al-Suddi and others. *Ra'ina* was an ordinary Arabic request meaning "tend to us," "be attentive to us," "give us time" — from the root meaning to see and to tend. The believers used it when asking the Prophet ﷺ to slow down or repeat something so they could understand it. The Jews of Madinah, however, used the same word with a twist — in their usage it carried an insult or a curse, and they would say it to the Prophet ﷺ and laugh among themselves. The believers, unaware, adopted the word. Allah forbade it and supplied the replacement.

The precise content of the insult is reported variously in the commentaries and should not be specified beyond this. What is established is the mechanism, and the mechanism is confirmed elsewhere in the Qur'an: 4:46 describes the same people "distorting the word from its [proper] place and saying, 'We hear and disobey'" and "twisting their tongues and defaming the religion" with the word *ra'ina*. That verse settles it: the word was being used as a weapon.

*Wa-qulu unzurna* — "and say: unzurna." *Nazara* means to look, to consider, to wait. The replacement conveyed the same legitimate request — attend to us, give us a moment — without any ambiguity that could be turned into mockery.

Note the structure of the instruction. It does not merely prohibit. It prohibits **and supplies the alternative**. That is a teaching principle of enormous reach, and it is one of the most consistently neglected in practice. A parent, teacher, manager or preacher who removes a word, a habit or an option without providing the replacement has created a vacuum that the old thing will refill. The Qur'an models the better method in a single clause: *la taqulu... wa-qulu*.

*Wa-isma'u* — "and listen." The positive counterpart to the "we hear and disobey" of verse 93. Hearing here means more than auditory reception: in the Qur'an's usage, *sam'* carries obedience, as in the believers' formula *sami'na wa ata'na*, "we hear and we obey" (2:285).

*Wa lil-kafirina 'adhabun alim* — "and for the disbelievers is a painful punishment." The threat is directed at those who used the word to mock the religion, and its severity matches the nature of the act: this was not disbelief but the ridicule of revelation.

For practice, this verse is one of the most immediately useful in the whole surah, and its principle scales.

The first application is the one the verse is literally about: **a Muslim may not use language that can be taken as mockery of the religion, whatever his intention.** The word itself was innocent. That is the point. *Ra'ina* meant nothing wrong in Arabic, and the believers meant nothing wrong by it, and they were told to stop, because it had become a vehicle for contempt. The principle is that the test is not your intention alone but the word's currency.

That has direct contemporary force. The idiom borrowed from people who mock the faith and then used among believers "ironically." The sacred phrase used as an exclamation or punctuation. The joke that depends on a double meaning. The nickname. The meme. In every case the defence is identical — "I didn't mean it like that" — and in every case the verse's answer is the same: it does not matter what you meant, because you are not the only person speaking.

The second application is the discipline of not adopting the vocabulary of those who hold the religion in contempt. This is one of the most reliable mechanisms of assimilation there is. A community begins by refusing the mockers' premises and ends by adopting their words, because the words are shorter and the group using them is more prestigious. Within a generation the vocabulary has changed and so has the thing the vocabulary was protecting. The verse forbids the first step.

And the third is the positive instruction, which is where the verse's gentleness lies: *wa-isma'u*, listen. The whole problem in this passage has been a failure of hearing — "we hear and disobey" at verse 93, the twisted word at verse 104. What is asked for instead is the plain thing: attend, and receive what is being said. Most religious failure is not complexity. It is not listening.

## Verse 105

**"The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord, but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty."**

In simple terms: neither the disbelieving People of the Book nor the polytheists would like any good at all to reach you from your Lord — but Allah chooses whom He wills for His mercy, and His bounty is vast.

*Ma yawaddu alladhina kafaru min ahl al-kitab wa la al-mushrikina* — "neither those who disbelieve among the People of the Book, nor the polytheists, wish." Both groups are named, and the verb *wadda* is a word of affectionate desire, so the statement is that they take no pleasure in your good. The negation covers both parties entirely: *ma... wa la...*, neither this group nor that.

*An yunazzala 'alaykum min khayrin min rabbikum* — "that any good should be sent down to you from your Lord." Not "some good" — *min khayrin*, any good at all, in any amount. That is a strong statement, and it is meant to be: the surah is removing a naivety from the believers.

Two clarifications are required, because this verse is regularly quoted in ways it will not bear.

The first is that it describes a specific historical hostility — the opposition of the Madinan Jewish tribes and the Qurayshi polytheists to the new community — and not a permanent or inherent disposition of any group of people. The surah's own discipline, applied seven times in this stretch, refuses to convert the acts of a party into the character of a people. And the Qur'an states the Muslim's default posture in the clearest possible terms: "Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, Allah loves those who act justly. He only forbids you from those who fight you because of religion and expel you from your homes and aid in your expulsion — [forbids] that you make allies of them" (60:8–9). The default is justice and kindness; the exception is active combatancy, and it is a narrow exception.

The second is that the verse is a description, not an instruction. It tells the believers what to expect. It does not tell them to reciprocate, and the next-but-one verse will command the opposite.

*Wa Allahu yakhtassu bi-rahmatihi man yasha'* — "but Allah singles out for His mercy whom He wills." *Yakhtassu* means to specify, to single out, to choose particularly. The same answer given to the envy of verse 90 is given here again, and it is the Qur'an's standing reply to the objection that the bounty went to the wrong people: the bounty is His and the choice is His. The parallel at 3:74 is nearly identical: "He singles out for His mercy whom He wills. And Allah is the possessor of great bounty."

*Wa Allahu dhu al-fadli al-'azim* — "and Allah is the possessor of great bounty." *Fadl* is supererogatory giving — what is given beyond what is owed. The pairing of mercy and bounty in one verse is worth noticing: nothing you have received was owed to you, and nothing anyone else receives is taken from you.

For practice, the verse works in two directions and both are needed.

The first is realism. A person who expects goodwill from everyone, and who is shocked and embittered when he meets ill-will, has misunderstood the world he is in. The Qur'an states plainly that some people will not wish him well, and it says so in order to inoculate him. The inoculation works: the person who has been told is not surprised, does not take it personally in the destructive sense, and is not diverted from his work by it. That is a large part of why this verse is here.

The second is the cure, which is entirely in the second half. *Allahu yakhtassu bi-rahmatihi man yasha'* — your portion is assigned by Allah, not distributed by your opponents' approval. The person who has internalised that is free of one of the great burdens of human life: the need for the approval of people who will not give it. And it works symmetrically, as verse 90 established — the same truth that frees the envied also cures the envier, because envy requires a scarcity that does not exist when the giver is the possessor of inexhaustible bounty.

## Verse 106

**"If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?"**

This is one of the most consequential and most misused verses in the Qur'an, and it needs to be set out in full, because both the doctrine and its limits are routinely misrepresented.

**What the verse says, and what *naskh* means.**

*Ma nansakh min ayatin aw nunsiha* — "whatever verse We abrogate or cause to be forgotten." *Naskh* comes from a root meaning to efface, to abolish, to transfer, to replace. In the technical language of *usul al-fiqh*, abrogation is defined as the lifting of a legal ruling established by an earlier text by means of a later text.

Three clarifications must be made immediately, because without them the doctrine is unintelligible.

**First: abrogation applies to rulings, not to the truth or validity of the text.** An abrogated verse is still the word of Allah. It is still recited in the prayer. It still carries reward when recited. What has been lifted is the continuing legal obligation, not the revelation's truth or its status as revelation. A person who imagines that abrogation means "that verse was a mistake" or "that verse no longer counts as the Qur'an" has misunderstood the doctrine completely.

**Second: abrogation is not a change of mind.** This is the objection most often raised against it, and the classical answer is consistent and should be given in full. Allah's knowledge is eternal and does not change. What changes is the obligation of the addressed community across time, and that change was known from eternity. The analogy the scholars used is a physician who alters a prescription as the patient's condition changes — not because the physician has learned something new, but because the treatment appropriate to one stage is not appropriate to another. On this reading, abrogation is not a revision of the divine plan. It *is* the plan.

**Third: abrogation is not a contradiction.** Two rulings in sequence, each valid for its time, are not two contradictory rulings. A law that says "fast three days" followed by a law that says "fast the month" is a sequence, not a conflict.

**The three recognised types.**

The jurists distinguished: (1) abrogation of both the ruling and the recitation; (2) abrogation of the ruling while the recitation remains — the most commonly claimed type; and (3) abrogation of the recitation while the ruling remains — the rarest and most disputed. The standard example given for the third is the "verse of stoning," and the narration concerning it is in both Sahihs: 'Umar ibn al-Khattab said, in a sermon, that among what was sent down was the verse of stoning, that they had recited it and memorised it, and that he feared that people would eventually say they did not find stoning in the Book of Allah and so abandon an obligation Allah had sent down. The Sunni understanding of this is that the verse's recitation was abrogated while its ruling remained, that the Qur'an as it stands is complete and protected — "Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian" (15:9) — and that the ruling of stoning is established by massively transmitted Sunnah in any case. The technical classification of that narration is disputed, and it should be cited with that qualification and not as though it cast doubt on the preservation of the text.

**The conditions, and how many verses are actually affected.**

The conditions the jurists laid down are strict. The abrogating text must be later in revelation than the abrogated one. The two must be genuinely irreconcilable, with no possible harmonisation. The ruling must be of a kind that can be abrogated at all — legal rulings, not creedal truths, not reports of past events, not moral universals. And the abrogation must be established by an explicit text or by a report from the Companions, never by reasoning or preference.

And here is the thing most often omitted in discussion of this verse: **the number of verses actually affected is genuinely disputed, and the later scholarship drastically reduced it.** Some early reports attributed high counts to figures like Ibn 'Abbas and Qatadah. Later scholarship applied the conditions strictly and found far fewer. Al-Suyuti counted roughly twenty. Others counted fewer still. Modern scholarship, most thoroughly in the major study of the subject by Mustafa Zayd, reduced the number to a handful — arguing that most of what had historically been classed as abrogation is in fact *takhsis* (specification of a general text), *taqyid* (restriction of an unqualified text), *bayan* (clarification), or a ruling that simply lapsed because its occasion ended. A small modernist current denies abrogation in the Qur'an altogether; that position is outside the classical consensus and is rejected by the mainstream.

The honest picture, then, is this: the doctrine is real and accepted; the number of genuine cases is small and hotly disputed; and any presentation that treats abrogation as a large-scale phenomenon is working from the most expansive of the early reports rather than from the settled state of the discipline.

**The polemical misuse, in both directions.**

Two misuses are common and both should be named.

The first is the claim, made by critics, that abrogation is an admission that the Qur'an is internally contradictory or was revised by its author. The answers above address it: a sequence of rulings is not a contradiction, and no change in the divine knowledge is implied.

The second is far more serious because it is made by Muslims and has consequences. It is the claim that the so-called "verse of the sword" (9:5) abrogated a very large number of the Qur'an's verses — figures of 124 and 140 are cited in some late sources — and that therefore the verses of patience, forgiveness, tolerance and peaceful coexistence are no longer operative. **This claim has been heavily criticised by both classical and modern scholarship, and it does not survive the conditions the jurists themselves laid down.** It fails the requirement of genuine irreconcilability, since texts commanding patience and texts regulating armed conflict are read by the majority of commentators as addressing different circumstances, and it fails the requirement of securely established chronological sequence for each claimed case. A handful of scholars asserted a broad abrogation; many more did not. And it must be said plainly: this claim has no standing as a licence for anything, and it has been used to justify things the religion forbids.

**"Better or similar."**

*Na'ti bi-khayrin minha aw mithliha* — "We bring [something] better than it or similar to it." How can one part of Allah's speech be better than another? The commentators were careful here. "Better" means better **for you** — lighter, more suitable, more merciful, or greater in reward. It does not mean that the earlier verse was deficient in its eloquence or its truth, since all of Allah's speech is perfect. And where the replacement is outwardly heavier, the "better" is understood as greater in reward and benefit.

*A-lam ta'lam anna Allaha 'ala kulli shay'in qadir* — "do you not know that Allah is over all things competent?" The verse's argument closes on power: the reason abrogation is coherent at all is that the Lawgiver is not constrained by His own earlier legislation.

For practice, three things.

The first is the wisdom of gradualism, which is what abrogation actually demonstrates about the religion's method of change. The standard illustration is the prohibition of intoxicants, which came in stages rather than at once — first a statement that the sin in them is greater than the benefit (2:219), then a prohibition on approaching the prayer while intoxicated (4:43), then full prohibition (5:90). Whether each stage is technically abrogation or progressive specification is debated, but the point stands and it is the point the tradition draws: Allah does not take a community from where it is to where it must be in a single step. He takes it in stages. That is a principle anyone changing anything — himself, a family, an institution — should learn from.

The second is that the abrogated ruling was **good and binding while it lasted**. A person living under it was obedient by keeping it and would have been disobedient by abandoning it. That has a quiet application to a life: what Allah required of you at one stage and no longer requires was not wasted. It was obedience at the time, and it counts.

And the third is the caution this verse should teach every reader about the word "abrogated." It is a technical term with strict conditions, and it is invoked far more loosely by polemicists than by scholars. Before accepting any claim that a verse has been abrogated, the correct questions are: who established the chronology, who established the irreconcilability, and does this meet the conditions the jurists laid down? Very often the answer is no, and the verse stands.

## Verse 107

**"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?"**

In simple terms: do you not know that the sovereignty of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah, and that you have no protector and no helper apart from Him?

*A-lam ta'lam anna Allaha lahu mulku al-samawati wa al-ard* — "do you not know that to Allah belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth?" *Mulk* is sovereignty in the fullest sense: ownership, authority, kingship, and the right to command.

Note the structure of this verse with the one before it, because the two are a single argument in two halves. Verse 106 closed on **power**: *Allah is over all things competent*. Verse 107 opens on **authority**: *to Him belongs the dominion*. Both are required for abrogation to be legitimate, and the fact that the Qur'an supplies them in sequence is a mark of how carefully the argument is built. Power without right is tyranny — a ruler who can do anything but has no claim on you. Right without power is impotence — a claim that cannot be effected. Legislation, and the revision of legislation, requires both, and the surah states both before drawing the conclusion.

*Wa ma lakum min duni Allahi min waliyyin wa la nasir* — "and you have not, apart from Allah, any guardian or any helper." Two nouns, and the commentators distinguished them. A *wali* is one who takes charge of your affairs, who looks after you, whose authority over you is real. A *nasir* is one who comes to your aid, who rescues you when you need rescuing. The verse denies both, and the double negation with *min* is emphatic: there is not, from besides Allah, any guardian at all, nor any helper at all.

That is a complete statement of reliance, and it is the practical heart of the verse. Everything human beings substitute for God falls into one of these two categories: something they look to for the management of their affairs (wealth, status, a system, a person, a plan), or something they look to for rescue when things go wrong (the same list, plus family, connections, luck). The verse empties both categories.

The same denial appears throughout the Qur'an — at 2:120, at 29:22 ("And you have not, besides Allah, any protector or any helper"), at 42:31, and at 4:173. And the positive counterpart is the closing supplication of this very surah: "You are our Protector, so give us victory over the disbelieving people" (2:286).

For practice, this verse gives the ground of obedience and the cure for misplaced reliance.

The ground of obedience first, since that is the verse's argument. If the heavens and the earth are His, then His right to command is not derived from your consent, and His right to revise a command is not contingent on your approval. Every objection to a ruling that takes the form "I wouldn't have legislated it that way" is answered by the first clause of this verse. Legislation belongs to the owner.

And the cure, which is in the second clause. The person who has no guardian besides Allah and no helper besides Allah is not abandoned — he is unencumbered. He does not have to manage the approval of a dozen substituted authorities, because none of them has any actual power over his outcome. That is the most freeing thing the Qur'an says about anxiety, and it is stated here as a fact rather than as an exhortation: you have no guardian besides Him and no helper besides Him, so stop looking.

## Verse 108

**"Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way."**

In simple terms: do you intend to badger your messenger with demands the way Moses was badgered? And whoever exchanges faith for disbelief has gone off the middle of the road entirely.

*Am turiduna an tas'alu rasulakum kama su'ila Musa min qablu* — "or do you wish to question your messenger as Moses was questioned before?" The reference is to the whole pattern recorded in this surah: "show us Allah openly" (2:55), "we will never endure one kind of food" (2:61), and above all the cow — questions asked and re-asked, each one narrowing the command and making obedience harder (2:67–71).

The severity of the comparison should not be softened. The believers are being warned against becoming like the people the surah has spent ninety verses indicting. That is the function of these narratives throughout the Qur'an: they are not primarily about the people they describe. "And [warn of] the Day... And there had already come to them news of that which had preceded them as an example" — hmm, that's 39:25-26 in a different context. The clearer statement is 57:16: "And let them not be like those who were given the Scripture before, and a long period passed over them, so their hearts hardened."

The elaboration of this warning appears at 5:101–102: "O you who believe, do not ask about things which, if they are shown to you, will distress you... A people before you asked about them and then became thereby disbelievers." And the hadith already cited at verse 67 states the principle directly: "Leave me as I have left you, for those before you were destroyed only by their excessive questioning and their disagreeing with their prophets" (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

The warning is not against asking. The Qur'an commands asking (16:43) and the Prophet ﷺ said the cure for ignorance is to ask. What is forbidden is the species of question that is designed to produce a ruling you will then resent, and the species that is asked not in order to obey but in order to delay. The distinction was drawn at verse 68 and applies here identically: the question of a person preparing to obey, and the question of a person preparing to postpone, look exactly the same, and they are sorted by what happens after the answer arrives.

*Wa man yatabaddal al-kufra bi-al-imani* — "and whoever exchanges disbelief for faith." The construction *tabaddala X bi-Y* means to take X in exchange for Y, so the meaning is: takes disbelief, and gives faith for it. The same structure as verse 86, where they bought this life with the Hereafter. It is the same transaction described for the third time in this passage, and it is always pure loss.

*Fa-qad dalla sawa'a al-sabil* — "then he has strayed from the level of the way." *Sawa'* is the middle, the even part, the level centre. The image is of a road with a raised crown, and of someone who has left the crown and gone down the side. He has not merely wandered; he is off the surface. It is a stronger statement than "he went astray."

For practice, this verse is one of several places where the Qur'an holds up the People of the Book as a mirror to the Muslims rather than as a contrast to them. That is worth saying, because the polemical passages are very often read as "we are not like them," when the Qur'an's own use of them is far more often "do not become like them."

The second application is the specific one. Questioning of this kind has a very recognisable modern form: the hypothetical that generates a ruling nobody wanted, the edge case raised in a gathering to make the religion look unreasonable, the demand for a ruling on a situation that has not arisen and will not arise. The Prophet ﷺ described the consequence precisely — a people asked such questions and then became disbelievers *thereby*, because the answer arrived, was heavy, and was refused. Every generation produces people who ask the question, receive the answer, and then hold the answer against the religion.

And the third is the exchange. Nobody sets out to trade faith for disbelief. It happens in increments, the way all the transactions in this surah happen: a ruling declined, then another, then a habit of declining, until the exchange has been made without anyone signing anything. The verse's word for the end state is not "he sinned." It is "he is off the road."

## Verse 109

**"Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything."**

In simple terms: many among the People of the Book would love to turn you back to disbelief now that you have believed — out of a self-generated envy, and after the truth has become perfectly clear to them. So pardon them and turn away from them, until Allah brings His command.

*Wadda kathirun min ahl al-kitab* — "many among the People of the Book wished." *Kathirun*, many — not all. The surah's discipline holds again, for the eighth time in this stretch.

*Law yaruddunakum min ba'di imanikum kuffaran* — "that they could turn you back, after your faith, [to being] disbelievers." The word *yaruddunakum* means to return you, to push you back. The ambition is not merely that they should disbelieve; it is that *you* should.

*Hasadan min 'indi anfusihim* — "out of envy from themselves." *Hasad* is envy — the desire that another's good should be removed. The commentators emphasised the phrase *min 'indi anfusihim*, "from their own selves," as indicating that the envy is self-generated and gratuitous. It has no cause outside them. It is not a response to anything the believers did. That is the same diagnosis as verse 90's *baghy* and verse 105's refusal to wish them good: the motive is internal, and it is not tractable by anything the envied person does.

*Min ba'di ma tabayyana lahumu al-haqq* — "after the truth had become clear to them." *Tabayyana* is to become fully evident, to be made distinct. The knowing-rejection pattern appears again: it is not that the truth was obscure. It was clear, and they wished to pull others away from it anyway.

*Fa-'fu wa-sfahu hatta ya'tiya Allahu bi-amrih* — "so pardon and overlook, until Allah brings His command." Two imperatives, and the commentators distinguished them. *'Afu* is to waive the claim — to give up the right to retaliate or punish. *Safh* is to turn the face away — not to raise the matter, not to reproach, not to keep it in the account at all. Together they describe the complete relinquishment of resentment.

**A necessary note on the legal status of this command.** A substantial body of classical commentators held that this instruction of pardon and forbearance was abrogated by later verses concerning fighting and the jizyah — notably 9:29 — and this is one of the cases routinely cited in the literature on abrogation. Other scholars held that it was not abrogated at all but conditioned by circumstance: forbearance where there is no active belligerence, and fighting where there is. A number of modern scholars argue that the two sets of texts address different questions — personal hostility in one case, political and military conduct in the other — and that both remain operative in their own domains. The claim of abrogation here is genuinely disputed, and it should be presented as disputed rather than as settled.

And the guardrail, which is the same one stated at verse 105 and which applies regardless of how that dispute is resolved: **this verse and the debate about it give no individual any licence to harass, harm or abuse anyone.** Whatever the ruling on the conduct of states toward belligerent parties, the individual Muslim's default toward neighbours, colleagues, classmates and fellow citizens is justice and kindness, on the basis of 60:8–9. That is not a modern accommodation. It is the Qur'an's own statement.

*Inna Allaha 'ala kulli shay'in qadir* — "indeed Allah is over all things competent." Note that this is the identical clause that closed verse 106. The connection is deliberate: the same power that establishes and revises the law also brings about the command the believers are told to wait for. The instruction to wait is grounded in the certainty that what is coming will come.

For practice, this verse gives the response to ill-will, and it is not the response most people reach for.

The response is: waive it, and turn away from it. Not answer it, not correct it, not win the argument — waive it and look away. That is a very high demand, and it is demanded of people who were actively being worked against by people who knew better.

The second application is the phrase *min 'indi anfusihim*, which is oddly liberating. The envy is self-generated. It has no cause in you. That means you cannot resolve it by being different, and you should stop trying. Most people who are envied or resented spend an enormous amount of energy attempting to become unobjectionable to the person who resents them, and it never works, because the resentment was never about them. The verse releases the believer from that labour.

And the third is *hatta ya'tiya Allahu bi-amrih* — until Allah brings His command. The believer is not required to avenge himself, and is not permitted to. He is required to wait, and the waiting is not passivity — it is the transfer of the case to the One who has power over it. That is one of the great psychological releases in the religion: you are not obliged to settle your own accounts, and you are not permitted to.

## Verse 110

**"Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."**

In simple terms: establish the prayer and give the zakah — and whatever good you put forward for your own account, you will find it preserved with Allah.

*Wa aqimu al-salah wa atu al-zakah* — "and establish the prayer and give the zakah." The same pair commanded at verse 43 and again at verse 83. Its return here, immediately after a long polemic about another community's failures, is deliberate and the implication is clear. The difference between the community that was indicted and the community now addressed is not lineage, not election, and not inherited status. It is what they do. The surah has just spent a hundred verses describing a people who had revelation, knew it, distorted it, and broke every covenant they made — and it turns to the believers and says: establish the prayer, and give the zakah.

*Wa ma tuqaddimu li-anfusikum min khayrin tajiduhu 'inda Allah* — "and whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with Allah."

Two things in this clause deserve careful attention, and both are among the most instructive in the Qur'an.

The first is *li-anfusikum*, "for yourselves." The good you do is not done *for* Allah in the sense of supplying a need. He has no need. The hadith qudsi recorded by Muslim from Abu Dharr states it in the plainest terms: "O My servants, were the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, to be as pious as the most pious heart of any single one of you, that would not increase My dominion in anything at all." The deed is credited to your own account. Worship is not a service rendered to a needy deity; it is an investment made by a needy creature. That reframing changes everything about how a person approaches it, and it removes the two failures that distort religious practice most: the sense that God is being done a favour, and the expectation of payment.

The second is *tajiduhu*, "you will find it." Not "you will be repaid for it" — you will **find it**. The wording suggests that the deed itself is preserved and waiting, rather than converted into something else and discarded. The Qur'an makes the same point at 99:7–8: "So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it." And the near-identical verse at 73:20 reads: "And whatever good you put forward for yourselves — you will find it with Allah. It is better and greater in reward."

*Tuqaddimu*, "you send ahead," is the same verb used at verse 95 for what their hands sent ahead — *bima qaddamat aydihim*. The two verses are a deliberate pair: the same image, once for the evil that waits and once for the good that waits. Every human being is forwarding packages to a destination he is travelling towards, and the packages arrive before he does.

*Inna Allaha bima ta'maluna basir* — "indeed, Allah is All-Seeing of what you do." *Basir*, seeing, is the same attribute with which verse 96 closed. Nothing done is unseen, and the verse ends by joining the promise to the observation: what you send ahead will be found, and the One with whom it is kept has watched you send it.

For practice, this verse is the perfect close to the batch, and it gives three things.

The first is the return to basics after a hundred verses of complex argument. Whatever else the surah has established — about covenants, about distortion, about envy, about sorcery, about abrogation — the standing obligations have not moved. The prayer and the zakah. The person who has followed the argument and neglected the two things at the end of it has missed the point of the argument.

The second is the "for yourselves." A great deal of religious exhaustion comes from a false model of worship as service rendered to a party who needs it. Replace that model and the exhaustion changes character: the worshipper is not working for someone else's benefit. He is making deposits into the only account that survives him. The Qur'an states this repeatedly — "Whoever does righteousness, it is for his [own] soul" (41:46); "If you do good, you do good for yourselves" (17:7) — and it is one of the most consistent themes in the Book.

And the third is the sending ahead, which is a discipline that can be practised today. Most people make decisions by asking what a choice will produce now. The verse proposes a second question, asked alongside the first: what am I forwarding by this? Every act is a package with an address on it. The only question is which direction it is going, and whether you will be glad to find it when you arrive.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses close the long engagement with the People of the Book and turn toward the internal life of the Muslim community.

The closing of the polemic comes first. A messenger came to them confirming what they already held, and a party of those who had been given scripture flung the Book behind their backs, behaving as though they knew nothing — though they knew (101). What they followed instead was sorcery, which brings the exoneration of Solomon, the devils who fabricated the charge against him, the two figures in Babylon who taught only with a warning attached, and the unanswerable observation that the learners knew at the time of purchase that the buyer has no share in the Hereafter (102). Had they believed and been mindful, the reward with Allah would have been better than anything they got — if only they had the kind of knowledge that acts (103).

Then two short verses on the texture of community life. A word the believers had innocently adopted had to be dropped because it had become a vehicle for mockery, and the replacement was supplied with it (104). They do not wish you well, but Allah singles out for His mercy whom He wills, and His bounty is vast (105).

Then the two verses on law and power: whatever is abrogated or caused to be forgotten is replaced with something better or equal, because Allah is competent over everything (106), and because the dominion of the heavens and the earth is His and there is no guardian or helper besides Him (107).

Then the turn inward. Do not badger your messenger as Moses was badgered, for whoever trades faith for disbelief is off the road entirely (108). They would love to pull you back out of a self-generated envy, after the truth has become clear to them — so waive it, turn away from it, and wait for Allah's command (109). And then the return to the standing obligations: establish the prayer, give the zakah, and whatever good you send ahead for your own account, you will find it preserved with Allah (110).

Three things hold the batch together. First, the diagnosis never changes: the failure throughout is knowing — *ka-annahum la ya'lamun*, *wa la-qad 'alimu*, *min ba'di ma tabayyana lahumu al-haqq*. Nobody in these verses lacks information. Second, two of the hardest verses in the surah sit side by side at its centre, and both of them are regularly misused — the verse on sorcery is misused to persecute people, and the verse on abrogation is misused to cancel the Qur'an's own teachings of patience and justice. Both misuses were named above and both are misuses. And third, the passage ends where the surah always ends: not with an argument won, but with the prayer, the zakah, and the good sent ahead.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators and jurists genuinely differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether *ma* in verse 102 is relative or negative, whether the two figures are angels or men, what the "sending down" to them consisted of, and whether verse 109's command of pardon was abrogated.

Five boundaries have been observed, and they are the most important part of this batch.

On verse 102: the elaborated story of Harut and Marut has been identified as transmitted material widely regarded as Isra'iliyyat and of doubtful authenticity, with the observation that the Qur'anic text itself is sparing and tells no story; and the ruling on sorcery has been stated with the explicit and unqualified guardrail that nothing in this verse authorises any individual to accuse, harm or punish anyone, that accusations of witchcraft have killed enormous numbers of people — overwhelmingly women, the poor and minorities — and that slandering a chaste believing woman is itself one of the seven destructive sins named in the very hadith that names magic.

On verse 106: the doctrine of abrogation has been stated with the three clarifications without which it is unintelligible — that it lifts rulings and not the truth or validity of the text, that it implies no change in the divine knowledge, and that a sequence is not a contradiction. The number of genuine cases has been stated as genuinely disputed and drastically reduced by later scholarship. And the claim that the "verse of the sword" abrogated large parts of the Qur'an has been named, attributed to a handful of scholars, stated to fail the jurists' own conditions, and declared to have no standing as a licence for anything.

On verses 105 and 109: both have been read as descriptions of a specific historical hostility rather than as statements about any people, the Muslim's default of justice and kindness has been grounded in 60:8–9, and it has been stated that neither verse, nor the dispute about their abrogation, gives any individual licence to harass or harm anyone.

On verse 104: the precise content of the insult carried by *ra'ina* has been left unspecified, because it is reported variously in the commentaries, and the mechanism — confirmed by 4:46 — is what the verse establishes.

And throughout: the surah's discipline of particularising rather than generalising has now been traced eight times in this stretch of the text, and no reading of these verses may convert the acts of a party into the character of a people.
