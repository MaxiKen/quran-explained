# Tafsir — Surah Al-An'am (Chapter 6), Verses 140–160

---

## Verse 140

Verse 140 says: *"Lost indeed are those who have murdered their own children foolishly out of ignorance and have forbidden what Allah has provided for them—falsely attributing lies to Allah. They have certainly strayed and are not rightly guided."*

This verse delivers a powerful verdict on two of the most destructive practices of pre-Islamic Arabian society: the killing of children and the invention of arbitrary food prohibitions. Both acts are condemned together in one breath, and the connection is deliberate and profound.

The verse opens with the word *khasira* — to lose, to suffer ruin, to be utterly deprived of good. This is the Arabic of total loss: not a minor failure, but the forfeiture of this world and the next. The people described here made investments that destroyed them. They sacrificed their own children — the most precious gift Allah had given them — and they denied themselves lawful provision — the sustenance Allah had made available to them — and they did both of these things in the name of religion, falsely attributing these actions to divine command.

The practice being referred to is primarily *wa'd al-banat* — the burying alive of infant daughters, which was practiced by certain Arab tribes who feared the shame or financial burden of raising girls. Earlier in this same surah (verse 137), the Qur'an described how Satan's associates made this killing seem beautiful and appealing to them. Here, the verdict is rendered: such people are utterly lost. They committed the greatest act of cruelty — murdering their own flesh and blood — not out of malice, but out of *safah* (foolishness, recklessness, moral shallowness) and *bi ghayri 'ilm* (without knowledge). These two phrases are important. *Safah* refers to a lightness of mind, an inability to weigh consequences properly — the kind of thinking that treats a human life as a disposable burden. *Bi ghayri 'ilm* makes clear that they had no genuine religious knowledge authorising what they did. They invented a justification, called it divine, and acted on it.

The second act condemned alongside infanticide is the forbidding of what Allah had made lawful. The pre-Islamic Arabs had developed an elaborate system of food taboos centred on certain animals — particularly cattle — which they designated as sacred to their idols or prohibited by customs they attributed to their gods. The Qur'an spends several verses in this surah (including verse 143–145) exposing these taboos as pure invention. Here, the verdict is that those who forbid Allah's provision and then falsely attribute that prohibition to Allah are counted among the utterly lost.

Notice the structure of the accusation: they murdered their children AND they made unlawful what Allah provided AND they fabricated lies against Allah. These are presented as three dimensions of the same fundamental error — the replacement of divine guidance with human ignorance disguised as divine authority. When human beings start deciding on their own what is sacred and what is forbidden, what must be killed and what must be protected, without any genuine revelation to guide them, they inevitably go astray.

The verse ends with a double confirmation: *qad dallu* — they have certainly strayed — and *wa ma kanu muhtadin* — they were not among the guided. The use of the past tense here is significant; these are not people on the edge of guidance who might yet find the right path. Their deviation is presented as an established, completed fact.

Ibn Kathir notes that the loss described covers both this life and the Hereafter: in this world, they destroyed their children and deprived themselves of lawful provision; in the next, they will face the consequences of having fabricated lies against Allah.

The lesson of this verse reaches far beyond its immediate historical context. Whenever a society takes the most vulnerable among its members — children, the unborn, the elderly, the poor — and treats them as burdens to be eliminated for economic or social reasons, the same spiritual logic is at work. And whenever individuals or institutions pronounce things forbidden or obligatory without genuine divine authority, they fall into the second error condemned here. The Qur'an is insisting on a fundamental principle: the right to legislate belongs to Allah alone, and overstepping that boundary — in either direction, making the lawful forbidden or the forbidden lawful — is one of the gravest forms of misguidance.

## Verse 141

Verse 141 says: *"He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild—and palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates—similar in shape, but dissimilar in taste. Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful."*

After the condemnation of those who fabricated forbidden things, Allah now turns to a description of His own abundant provision. The transition is deliberate: if those people were lost because they made unlawful what Allah provided, then it is important to understand what Allah actually did provide — and how generously. This verse is, in essence, a reorientation of the reader's gaze from human-invented taboo toward divine abundance.

The Arabic word *ansha'a* — translated here as "produces" — carries a richer meaning than simple creation. It implies causing something to emerge, to grow, to develop upward. It is the word for initiation, for nurturing into existence. Allah does not merely create gardens and leave them; He brings them into being and sustains their growth. The image is of a Provider who is actively involved in the world's flourishing.

The verse lists specific types of agricultural abundance: *jannatin* (gardens), *ma'rushat* and *ghayra ma'rushat* (trellised and untrellised — meaning cultivated gardens where vines are grown on supports and wild or naturally growing ones), palm trees (*nakhl*), crops (*zar'*) of varying tastes, olives (*zaytun*), and pomegranates (*rumman*). Classical commentators like al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir understood *ma'rushat* to refer to plants trained on supports and *ghayra ma'rushat* to those that grow freely without human-constructed frames. Al-Qurtubi expanded the reading to include all cultivated versus uncultivated vegetation.

The description that follows — *mutashabihan wa ghayra mutashabihin* — is one of the Qur'an's elegant observations about the natural world. These fruits are similar in appearance but dissimilar in taste. Look at a red apple and a red pomegranate: both spherical, both red, both growing on trees — yet entirely different on the palate. Look at different varieties of grapes on the same vine. The diversity within apparent similarity is itself a sign of a Creator who does not produce monotony but variety, who builds within creation an inexhaustible richness that reflects His own infinite attributes.

The verse then delivers two instructions and one prohibition.

The first instruction is *kulu min thamarihi* — eat from its fruit when it bears. This is an explicit divine permission, given in direct contrast to those who forbade Allah's provision in the previous verse. Allah is saying: the one who created all of this *wants you to eat from it*. There is no divine reason to voluntarily deprive yourself of these lawful gifts.

The second instruction is *attu haqqahu yawma hasadih* — pay its due on the day of harvest. This is the origin of one of the foundational rulings in Islamic jurisprudence regarding *zakat al-zuru'* (the obligatory portion due on agricultural produce). Scholars have discussed whether the "dues" here refer to the obligatory zakah (one-tenth or one-twentieth depending on the method of irrigation) or to voluntary charity at harvest time, or both. The stronger scholarly position, held by al-Qurtubi and others, is that this refers primarily to the obligatory zakah while also encompassing the spirit of generosity toward those in need at harvest time — when abundance is most visible and the temptation to hoard is strongest. The instruction to pay the due *on the day of harvest* is significant: it encourages immediate generosity rather than deferral.

The prohibition is *wa la tusrifu* — do not waste. The word *israf* in Arabic refers to excess, extravagance, going beyond what is appropriate. Allah then states: *innahu la yuhibb al-musrifin* — He does not love the wasteful. This is one of only a few qualities that the Qur'an explicitly states Allah does not love, which makes it a striking warning. Wasting food, wasting resources, consuming beyond what is needed, discarding in abundance what others lack — all of this falls within the prohibition.

In modern terms, this verse speaks with remarkable directness to one of the defining ethical crises of contemporary life. Global food waste statistics are staggering — hundreds of millions of tonnes of food are discarded annually while hundreds of millions of people face food insecurity. The Qur'anic principle here is not vague advice but a clear moral framework: Allah gave it to you, He asked you to share a portion of it, and He forbids you to throw it away carelessly. Each of these three instructions — eat, give, do not waste — constitutes a complete ethical posture toward the blessings of the earth.

The verse also carries a subtle theological argument embedded within the description of creation. A Creator who designed such variety, colour, and flavour in food — who made pomegranates tart and dates sweet, who made one grape sour and the next beside it rich — is not a God indifferent to human experience. This is a God who cares about what His creation enjoys. The Qur'an is inviting the reader to see Allah's generosity in the very diversity of what grows from the earth.

## Verse 142

Verse 142 says: *"Some cattle are fit for labour, others are too small. Eat of what Allah has provided for you and do not follow Satan's footsteps. Certainly, he is your sworn enemy."*

Having described the abundance of gardens and crops, the Qur'an now turns to animals — specifically the cattle that were at the centre of the elaborate food taboo system in pre-Islamic Arabia. The verse begins by describing two categories: *hamulatan* (those fit for burden, large cattle used for carrying loads and transportation) and *farshan* (those that are close to the ground, smaller animals used primarily for their meat, milk and wool). This is a simple, natural division of animal life — the large beasts of burden such as camels and oxen, and the smaller animals like sheep and goats.

The Qur'an's point in listing these two categories is to establish that all of them — the large and the small, the working animal and the slaughter animal — are part of Allah's provision. None of them were designated by Allah as forbidden. They were created for human benefit across the full range of what cattle offer: transport, farming, meat, milk, wool, and hide.

The instruction that follows — *kulu mimma razaqakum Allahu* — is direct: eat of what Allah has provided for you. This command is not incidental. It is a deliberate counter to the pre-Islamic Arabian practice of making certain categories of cattle forbidden — the *Bahirah*, *Sa'ibah*, *Wasilah*, and *Ham*, which will be referenced in the next two verses. These were animals that the pagan Arabs had designated as sacred or off-limits based on tribal custom and superstition, attributing these rules to divine authority. Allah is cutting through all of that: I created these animals for you. Eat them.

The verse then pivots immediately to a profound warning: *wa la tattabi'u khutwati al-Shaytan* — do not follow the footsteps of Satan. The word *khutwat* (footsteps, steps) is particularly revealing. Satan does not typically appear and say, "Come, let me lead you astray directly." He works in steps — small, incremental moves, each one appearing reasonable, until the person has wandered far from where they should be. In the context of this passage, the footsteps of Satan are the gradual accumulation of invented rules, superstitions, and fabricated prohibitions that over generations came to constitute an entire religious system — one entirely divorced from divine revelation.

The final declaration — *innahu lakum 'aduwwun mubin* — he is your clear, open, declared enemy — is the Qur'an's insistence that the relationship between Satan and humanity is not ambiguous. He is not a neutral figure, nor a tempter who operates from outside the system. He is an enemy who has announced his enmity. The Qur'an references this in multiple places (see also 2:208, 35:6, 36:60), always emphasizing the clarity of this declared hostility so that the human being who follows his steps has no excuse of ignorance.

The practical implication of this verse in contemporary life is significant. The "footsteps of Satan" in food matters today might include not just invented religious prohibitions but the opposite problem: the refusal to take seriously any dietary guidance from divine revelation at all, treating the body purely as a pleasure machine with no obligations. The verse invites the believer to a middle path: eat what Allah has made available, give thanks through the dues required, avoid waste, and do not construct artificial rules around food that Allah did not legislate.

## Verse 143

Verse 143 says: *"Allah has created four pairs: a pair of sheep and a pair of goats—ask them, O Prophet, 'Has He forbidden to you the two males or the two females or what is in the wombs of the two females? Tell me with knowledge, if what you say is true.'—"*

This verse begins a sustained logical challenge to the pagan Arabs' food taboo system — one of the most elegant pieces of rational argumentation in the Qur'an. It operates not through assertion but through question, and the questions are designed so that no honest answer can support the Arab polytheists' position.

The Qur'an identifies *thamaniyata azwaj* — eight pairs of animals. In Arabic, *zawj* means a pair or a spouse — a unit of two that belong together. The pairs referred to here are: a male and female sheep, a male and female goat, a male and female camel, and a male and female ox. These four pairs represent the entire category of domesticated livestock in Arabia. Allah states plainly: He created all eight of these — male and female of each species.

The challenge directed at the Prophet ﷺ to put to the polytheists is: which of these did Allah actually prohibit? Was it the males? The females? Or the young found in the wombs of the females? This question is devastating in its logic. The pagan Arabs had constructed a complex system of taboos:

- The *Bahirah* was a she-camel whose ear was slit after her fifth birth; she was then released and could not be ridden or milked.
- The *Sa'ibah* was a camel released for the idols after a vow; she too could not be used.
- The *Wasilah* was a she-goat that had given birth to females consecutively; the male offspring was considered dedicated to the idols.
- The *Ham* was a stallion camel that had fathered ten offspring; he was released and could no longer be used.

These categories overlapped in confusing ways. In some cases, the male was forbidden and the female was permitted. In others, the offspring were designated differently from the parents. In still others, what was in the womb determined the animal's status. The entire system was a labyrinth of inconsistency.

The Qur'anic challenge cuts right through all of it: *if* Allah actually prohibited something, then identify what it is. Is it the male? Is it the female? Is it the unborn? Because if you say the male is forbidden, then why is the female also forbidden in some cases? If you say the female is forbidden, then why do you also eat from some females? The prohibitions were so internally contradictory that they could not survive this simple question: what exactly did Allah forbid?

The concluding phrase of the challenge — *nabi'uni bi 'ilmin in kuntum sadiqin* — tell me with knowledge, if you are truthful — is a call for evidence. Not tradition. Not custom. Not what your grandfather said. Not what your tribal chief declared. Knowledge. The Qur'an insists that religious law requires a foundation. Something cannot be declared forbidden in the name of God unless God has actually said so. And God's saying so must be traceable through knowledge, not assumed through inherited practice.

This principle has enormous implications. It establishes that religious authority is not self-validating. A tradition's age does not make it divine. A practice's widespread acceptance does not make it Qur'anic. The Qur'an is here establishing, in the midst of a specific argument about food, a broader epistemological principle: religious claims must be supported by genuine knowledge of divine revelation. This principle applies just as much to later Muslim communities as it did to the pagan Arabs. Any religious prohibition invented without Qur'anic or sound prophetic basis is subject to this same challenge.

## Verse 144

Verse 144 says: *"and a pair of camels and a pair of oxen. Ask them, 'Has He forbidden to you the two males or the two females or what is in the wombs of the two females? Or were you present when Allah gave you this commandment?' Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah to mislead others without any knowledge? Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people."*

This verse continues the logical challenge begun in verse 143, completing the list of the four animal pairs by adding camels and oxen. The same question is posed: was it the male that was forbidden? Or the female? Or what was in the womb? The repetition of the question for the second pair of animals is not redundant — it is emphatic. The Qur'an is insisting that the same logic that destroys the food taboo system for sheep and goats equally destroys it for camels and oxen. No matter which animal you examine, the question of *which* category Allah actually prohibited cannot be answered from revelation, because Allah never prohibited any of them.

Then comes an additional question that moves the argument to an entirely new level of force: *am kuntum shuhada'a idh wassakum Allahu bihatha* — "Or were you present when Allah gave you this commandment?" This is a pointed, almost ironic challenge. You are telling people what God has forbidden. Were you there when God spoke? Did you witness this commandment being given? Of course they were not. No one is. Divine commandments are transmitted through prophets. And no prophet had ever brought the *Bahirah*, *Sa'ibah*, *Wasilah*, or *Ham* restrictions. These were human inventions attributed to God without any divine basis.

The question resonates across all religious traditions. Much of what passes for divine command in popular religion is, on examination, a human construction — sometimes centuries old, sometimes well-intentioned, but without genuine prophetic basis. The Qur'an is insisting here on a standard that cuts through custom, culture, and inherited religion: Was this from God? If so, how do we know? Through what transmission?

The verse then moves from question to verdict: *fa man azlamu mimman iftara 'ala Allahi kadhiban li yudilla al-nasa bi ghayri 'ilm* — who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah to mislead people without knowledge? This is one of the Qur'an's strongest expressions of condemnation. The formula *fa man azlamu mimman* (who does more wrong than the one who...) appears several times in the Qur'an, always marking an act of extraordinary moral gravity. Here, the act is fabricating religious law and attributing it to Allah in order to lead people astray.

The phrase *li yudilla al-nasa* — to mislead people — adds a crucial dimension. This is not merely a personal religious error. The people who invented these taboos were actively misleading others. Their fabrications spread through the community and became normalised, leading generation after generation into error. This social dimension of religious fabrication — its capacity to corrupt entire communities, not just individuals — is part of why the Qur'an treats it with such severity.

Ibn Abbas, as reported by al-'Awfi, understood this verse as Allah declaring that He did not prohibit any of the eight pairs of cattle. The challenge "tell me with knowledge" meant: explain how and when Allah prohibited the Bahirah, Sa'ibah, Wasilah, and Ham. Al-Tabari's reading in his *Jami' al-Bayan* similarly emphasises that the challenge is an epistemological one: produce the knowledge, if you are truthful.

The verse closes with: *innallaha la yahdi al-qawm al-zalimin* — Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people. The word *zalimin* here refers to those who persist in placing things outside their proper boundaries — including placing human words in the position of divine law. Guidance, the verse implies, requires a certain orientation of the heart. Those who are committed to fabrication, who have made the justification of their desires into a religion, are not in the posture of receiving divine guidance.

## Verse 145

Verse 145 says: *"Say, O Prophet, 'I do not find in what has been revealed to me anything forbidden to eat except carrion, running blood, swine—which is impure—or a sinful offering in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely your Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.'"*

Having demolished the pagan Arabs' invented food prohibitions, the Qur'an now presents what is actually, genuinely forbidden. The contrast is sharp: after pages of attacking arbitrary fabricated taboos, the list of what Allah really did prohibit is short, clear, and rational.

The verse is introduced with *qul* — "Say" — directing the Prophet ﷺ to state this on Allah's authority. The phrase *la ajidu fi ma uhiya ilayya muharraman* — I do not find in what was revealed to me anything forbidden — is significant. It is not the Prophet ﷺ speaking his personal opinion; it is a statement about revelation. The implication is: if it is not prohibited in revelation, it is permitted. The default ruling regarding food in Islam is permission; prohibition requires explicit divine authority. This principle — *al-ibahah al-asliyyah* — the original permissibility of things — is one of the foundations of Islamic legal reasoning.

Four categories of food are then identified as forbidden:

**First: carrion** (*maytah*) — any animal that died without being properly slaughtered. The prohibition on carrion is rooted in hygiene, dignity, and the principle that the lawful taking of an animal's life requires the invocation of God's name. An animal that died of disease, accident, or neglect cannot be known to have been healthy or properly dispatched.

**Second: running blood** (*daman masfuhan*) — the word *masfuh* means poured out, flowing. This does not refer to the blood that naturally remains in meat after slaughter, which is unavoidable and is not prohibited. It refers to flowing, liquid blood consumed directly — a practice that some pre-Islamic Arabs engaged in as a food. The qualification *masfuh* is important; it shows the precision of the Qur'anic prohibition and why classical scholars did not extend it to the blood traces that remain in cooked meat.

**Third: the flesh of swine** (*lahm al-khinzir*) — which the verse describes as *rijs*, meaning impure, filthy, or abominable. The prohibition on pork is one of the most recognised aspects of Islamic dietary law and is shared with the Torah. The Qur'an does not provide an explicit reason beyond characterising it as *rijs*. Scholars have understood this variously as referring to physical impurity, spiritual connotation, or both. The believer accepts it as a divine instruction, and modern understanding of the biological characteristics of swine may offer some additional perspective, though that is a supplementary observation, not the reason for the ruling.

**Fourth: a sinful offering** (*fisqan uhilla li ghayri Allahi bih*) — any animal over which a name other than Allah's has been pronounced at slaughter. The word *fisq* means transgression, deviation from the boundary. What makes an animal's slaughter lawful in Islam is the combination of proper slaughter and the invocation of Allah's name. When an animal is slaughtered in the name of an idol, a saint, or any other than Allah, it becomes forbidden — not because of anything in the animal itself, but because of the act of dedicating it to other than God.

The scholars noted that this list in verse 145 has a parallel in 2:173, 5:3, and 16:115. In Surah Al-Ma'idah (5:3), the list is more detailed, adding strangled animals, those beaten to death, those that fell from a height, those gored by other animals, and those partially eaten by predators. Classical scholars reconciled this by understanding the Al-An'am verse as a general foundational statement and the Al-Ma'idah verse as providing additional detail.

The verse then addresses the case of genuine necessity: *fa man udturra ghayra baghin wa la 'adin fa inna rabbaka ghafurun rahim* — but if someone is compelled, not out of desire and not exceeding the need, then your Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. This is one of the clearest Qur'anic expressions of the principle of necessity (*darura*) in Islamic law. When a person faces genuine, unavoidable risk to their life and has no lawful food available, they may consume even what is normally forbidden, but only to the extent required to survive. Two conditions are set: no *baghy* (desire, deliberate transgression) and no *'adw* (exceeding the immediate need). The person in necessity is not exploiting the permission; they are surviving.

This clause reveals something profound about the nature of divine law in Islam: it is not a rigid system indifferent to human circumstances. The same God who set the prohibition also provides the exit in genuine emergency. This is not contradiction but mercy — *ghafurun rahim*, All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The verse ends on these two names of Allah deliberately, showing that the permission in necessity flows from the divine attribute of mercy.

## Verse 146

Verse 146 says: *"For those who are Jewish, We forbade every animal with undivided hoofs and the fat of oxen and sheep except what is joined to their backs or intestines or mixed with bone. In this way We rewarded them for their violations. And We are certainly truthful."*

This verse introduces a crucial distinction that the wider argument of the surah depends on. The pagan Arabs had claimed that certain foods were forbidden by God — but as the previous verses demonstrated, those claims were fabricated. Now the Qur'an acknowledges that yes, some additional restrictions beyond the four core prohibitions of verse 145 do exist — but they were given specifically to the Jews, as a consequence of their specific violations, and are not part of the universal Islamic dietary law.

The verse states that Allah forbade for the Jews *kulla dhi zufurin* — every animal with undivided hoofs. The word *zufur* can refer to claws or nails, and the scholarly consensus understands this as referring to animals that do not have split or cloven hooves — including camels, rabbits, and similarly structured animals. This corresponds closely to the dietary restrictions recorded in the biblical book of Leviticus, particularly Leviticus 11, which lists animals with split hooves as permitted and those without as forbidden.

The verse also mentions the prohibition of the fat (*shuhum*) of oxen and sheep, with exceptions: fat attached to the back, the intestines, or mixed in with bone was permitted. Again, this parallels descriptions in Leviticus (3:17, 7:23) regarding prohibited fats. The Qur'an is here confirming the authenticity of these restrictions as genuine divine commands — while also clarifying that they were given to the Jews specifically.

The reason given is striking: *dhalika jazaynahum bi baghyihim* — "In this way We rewarded them for their violations." The word *jazayna* means "We recompensed, We rewarded" — but here, the "reward" is a restriction, not a gift. This is the Qur'anic concept of divinely imposed consequences: because of their transgressions, their lawfulness was narrowed. The broader principle appears elsewhere in the Qur'an: in 4:160, the Qur'an states that *because of the wrongdoing of the Jews, We made forbidden for them good things that had been permitted to them.* The restrictions were a form of divine discipline — not cruelty, but a narrowing of latitude given to a community that exceeded its boundaries.

This raises a profound theological point. Islamic dietary law, which is broader and less restrictive than Jewish dietary law, is not a relaxation of standards for its own sake. It reflects the fact that the Muslim community was not given these additional restrictions because these violations are not attributed to the Muslim community in the same way. The differentiation in divine law across communities is presented as just and proportionate — *wa inna la sadiqun* — and We are certainly truthful.

This also addresses a question that listeners of this surah might have raised: the Arabs knew that Jews did not eat certain meats. If the Qur'an was going to permit all cattle, how did that square with Jewish practice? The answer here is that Jewish restrictions were divine but community-specific — tied to their history and their violations. The Muslim community stands under its own law, given by the same God but calibrated to its own situation.

Contemporary readers sometimes ask whether this verse implies a hierarchy of communities. It does not. The verse presents differentiated law as an expression of divine wisdom and justice — a God who treats communities according to their specific histories and circumstances. The same God can give one community a stricter food law and another community a less restrictive one without either being "favoured" over the other in some simplistic sense. The lesson is that divine law is purposeful and responsive, not one-size-fits-all.

## Verse 147

Verse 147 says: *"But if they deny you, O Prophet, say, 'Your Lord is infinite in mercy, yet His punishment will not be averted from the wicked people.'"*

This brief verse follows the confirmation of Jewish dietary restrictions and serves as a pivot toward a broader argument. If, after all of this evidence — the clear statement of what is actually forbidden, the exposure of fabricated prohibitions, the acknowledgment of Jewish law as divinely given — they still deny the Prophet ﷺ, then he is told how to respond.

The response is not a threat delivered in anger, nor is it a pleading argument. It is a statement of divine reality in two balanced parts.

The first part: *rabbuka dhu rahmatin wasi'ah* — your Lord is vast in mercy, infinite in mercy. The word *wasi'ah* means expansive, wide, all-encompassing. Allah's mercy is not rationed. It is not reserved for particular nations or withdrawn at the first sign of disobedience. The verse opens with mercy — that is the primary character of the God who is speaking.

The second part: *wa la yuraddu ba'suhu 'an al-qawm al-mujrimin* — His punishment will not be averted from the wrongdoing people. The word *ba's* refers to severe force, punishment, the consequences of divine justice. And *la yuraddu* — cannot be turned away, cannot be deflected. This is not a threat made in the heat of confrontation; it is a statement of cosmic reality. The two attributes — mercy and justice — are not opposites in the Qur'anic worldview. They coexist in the same God. Mercy remains vast and open; and at the same time, for those who persist in *ijram* (crime against divine truth), the consequences cannot be escaped.

The verse is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ personally — *rabbuka* (your Lord) — and the "they" who might deny him could refer to the Jewish community (who were just discussed in verse 146) or, more broadly, to anyone who rejects the message. The Prophet ﷺ is being told, in effect: you have presented the truth clearly. If they still reject it, this is not your failure. The door of mercy remains open; the law of justice also stands.

This verse contains an important lesson for those engaged in calling others to truth. The caller is not responsible for outcomes. The responsibility is to convey clearly and honestly. Whether the listener accepts or rejects is between them and God. And God — vast in mercy, irresistible in justice — handles both possibilities.

## Verse 148

Verse 148 says: *"The polytheists will argue, 'Had it been Allah's Will, neither we nor our forefathers would have associated others with Him in worship or made anything unlawful.' Likewise, those before them rejected the truth until they tasted Our punishment. Ask them, O Prophet, 'Do you have any knowledge that you can produce for us? Surely you follow nothing but false assumptions and you do nothing but lie.'"*

This verse confronts one of the most sophisticated-sounding arguments that was used by the polytheists of Makkah — an argument that, at first hearing, might appear theologically profound, but which the Qur'an exposes as a logical and moral evasion.

The argument the polytheists made was this: *law sha'a Allahu ma ashrakna* — if Allah had willed, we would not have committed shirk. Since we do commit shirk, Allah must have willed it. Therefore, our shirk is in accordance with divine will, and we cannot be blamed or punished for it.

This is the argument from divine determinism (*qadar*) used to justify moral irresponsibility. It sounds almost pious — "everything is in Allah's hands" — but it is in fact a manipulation of the concept of divine will to evade accountability. The Qur'an's response is multi-layered.

First, the verse notes that this exact same argument was made by people before them — and they rejected the truth until Allah's punishment came to them. This is the pattern of history: whenever a community has been given clear evidence and still chosen to remain in error, the resort to divine determinism as a justification is a sign not of genuine theological insight but of desperate rationalization. The Qur'an documents this same evasion in the responses of earlier peoples to their prophets (see 7:173, 43:20). The repetition of the pattern across history is itself an argument against it.

Second, and most powerfully, the Qur'an demands evidence: *qul hal 'indakum min 'ilmin fa tukhrijuhu lana* — do you have any knowledge that you can produce for us? This is the same epistemological challenge that appears throughout this passage. The polytheists are claiming that Allah willed their shirk. How do they know this? What is their evidence? Do they have a revelation? Did a prophet tell them this? Do they have a logical argument that demonstrates divine approval of their practices?

Of course they do not. The argument from divine will, when used to justify *disobeying* divine law, is self-contradictory. Yes, Allah is sovereign over all things and nothing occurs outside His knowledge and divine decree (*qadar*). But Allah also sent messengers with clear prohibitions against shirk. The existence of divine decree does not eliminate the moral reality of human choice and human responsibility. A person who drinks and then argues "God willed me to drink, so I cannot be blamed" has not made a theological point; they have made an excuse.

The Qur'an's verdict is direct: *in tattabi'una illa al-zann wa in antum illa takhrusun* — you follow nothing but guesswork and you do nothing but make up things. The word *zann* means conjecture, unverified assumption. And *takhrusun* means to speak without knowledge, to guess wildly, to fabricate without basis. These are not people who have arrived at theological conclusions through careful reflection. They are people who have adopted a comfortable position that requires nothing of them and justifies everything they want to do.

This argument has modern parallels. People today sometimes argue that if a behaviour is widespread, common, or even genetically predisposed, it must be natural and therefore permissible — as though the descriptive reality of what happens overrides the normative question of what ought to happen. The Qur'anic response remains the same: does this constitute knowledge that you can produce? Or is it assumption dressed up as certainty?

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 149

Verse 149 says: *"Say, 'Allah has the most conclusive argument. Had it been His Will, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you.'"*

This verse is one of the most theologically rich short verses in Surah Al-An'am. It responds to the polytheists' abuse of divine will (from the previous verse) by articulating a profound and true understanding of both divine sovereignty and human responsibility.

The verse opens: *qul fa lillahi al-hujjatu al-balighah* — say, the most conclusive argument belongs to Allah. The word *hujjah* means proof, argument, evidence — specifically the kind that settles a case. *Balighah* means one that has fully reached its target, fully penetrated, fully established. Allah has the *definitive* argument.

What is that argument? It operates on two levels.

On the first level, Allah's argument against the polytheists is the series of proofs just presented: the clear evidence of creation, the sending of prophets, the revelation of Scripture, the rational demolition of their fabricated food taboos, and the provision of what is actually lawful and what is actually forbidden. The polytheists cannot claim ignorance because they have received clear, evidenced, knowable guidance. The hujjah has reached them. They simply chose not to accept it.

On the second level, the verse addresses the polytheists' misuse of divine will: *law sha'a lahadakum ajma'in* — had Allah willed, He would have guided all of you. This is a decisive rebuttal to the claim that their shirk proves divine approval. The fact that Allah did not forcibly impose guidance on everyone does not mean He approved of their deviation. It means He chose — in His wisdom — to give human beings genuine moral agency rather than programme them like automatons. He *could* have guided everyone by compulsion. He chose not to. But that choice not to compel is itself part of His wisdom and justice — because a forced faith is not faith.

This verse, understood properly, resolves the apparent tension between divine will and human accountability. Allah's *iradi* will (the divine decree that encompasses all that exists and occurs) is different from His *shar'i* will (the legislative will expressed in revelation and command). The fact that something happens does not mean Allah is pleased with it or approves of it. Murder happens — Allah does not approve of murder. Shirk happens — Allah does not approve of shirk. His sovereignty over all events does not translate into His endorsement of all events.

The *hujjah baligha* — conclusive proof — is thus both the external evidence (prophets and scripture) and the internal truth (the logical coherence of monotheism and the self-contradiction of shirk). The person who rejects this in the name of "it must be God's will that I remain as I am" has not grasped the nature of divine will at all.

## Verse 150

Verse 150 says: *"Say, O Prophet, 'Bring your witnesses who can testify that Allah has forbidden this.' If they falsely testify, do not testify with them. And do not follow the desires of those who deny Our proofs, disbelieve in the Hereafter, and set up equals with their Lord."*

This verse concludes the argument about invented food prohibitions by delivering the definitive legal and moral challenge: bring witnesses. Prove it.

The instruction is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: tell them to produce *shuhada'akum* — your witnesses. People who can actually testify — not merely assert — that Allah forbidden these animals. Testimony in the Islamic legal tradition requires direct knowledge. You cannot testify to something you did not witness. So the challenge is: who witnessed Allah issuing this prohibition? Who can testify with genuine knowledge that this is from God?

No one can. Because it is not from God.

Then comes a moral instruction of great importance: *fa in shahidu fa la tashhad ma'ahum* — if they falsely testify, do not testify with them. The Prophet ﷺ is being told not to lend the weight of his presence or authority to false testimony, even under social or political pressure. This extends to a general principle: the person of integrity does not add their voice to falsehood to avoid conflict or to conform to group expectations. When what is being testified is not true, the right response is silence and separation, not accommodation.

The verse then closes with a comprehensive description of those whose desires must not be followed: those who *deny Our proofs* (*kadhdhaba bi ayatina*), *disbelieve in the Hereafter*, and *set up equals with their Lord*. These three descriptions — denial of signs, disbelief in accountability, and shirk — form a profile of the person whose desires cannot be trusted as a guide. Their desires are built on a foundation that denies divine truth, denies the afterlife, and elevates other than God. Following such desires would inevitably lead the believer away from Allah.

The practical lesson here is timeless. In social, professional, and public life, there is constant pressure to go along with what powerful or popular people are saying — to add your voice to their claims, to not rock the boat, to testify to what you know is untrue in order to belong. The Qur'an's instruction to the Prophet ﷺ — don't testify with them if they testify falsely — is equally an instruction to every believer. Your voice has a value. Your testimony carries weight. Do not lend it to falsehood.

## Verse 151

Verse 151 says: *"Say, O Prophet, 'Come! Let me recite to you what your Lord has forbidden to you: do not associate others with Him in worship. Do not fail to honour your parents. Do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for you and for them. Do not come near indecencies, openly or secretly. Do not take a human life—made sacred by Allah—except with legal right. This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will understand.'"*

After exposing what Allah did *not* forbid (the invented animal taboos) and stating what He *did* forbid (the four food prohibitions), the Qur'an now pivots to a different register entirely. Here begins what classical scholars — and the companion Ibn Abbas (رضي الله عنه) himself — identified as among the most important verses in the entire Qur'an. Ka'b al-Ahbar, a Jewish scholar who became Muslim, said that these commandments in the Qur'an are what the Torah begins with after Bismillah, and that these correspond to the ten commandments revealed to Moses (peace be upon him). Islamic scholars identify verses 6:151–153 as the Qur'an's definitive ethical parallel to the Ten Commandments, presenting commandments sometimes called the "Wasaya of the Qur'an" that affirm the core message of monotheism, justice, kindness to parents, and the sanctity of life.

The verse opens with *qul ta'alaw atlu ma harrama rabbukum 'alaykum* — "Come! Let me recite to you what your Lord has forbidden." The word *ta'alaw* is an invitation — literally "come forward." After all the debate and challenge of the previous verses, this is a moment of gathering. The Prophet ﷺ is not threatening or demanding; he is inviting. And what he recites is not a set of arbitrary food taboos but a universal moral framework.

The prohibitions in verse 151 are:

**First: do not associate others with Allah in worship** (*la tushrika bihi shay'an*). This is the foundational commandment — *tawhid*, the absolute oneness of God. Every other prohibition flows from this. The person who has correctly understood who God is, will understand why parents must be honoured, why children must not be killed, why human life is sacred. Shirk — associating partners with God — corrupts the entire moral framework because it misidentifies the source of all value and authority.

**Second: honour your parents** (*wa bil walidayni ihsanan*) — the word *ihsan* is stronger than mere obedience; it means excellence, kindness, treating them with the utmost goodness. This commandment appears immediately after tawhid in the Qur'an repeatedly (see 4:36, 17:23), establishing a consistent hierarchy: God first, parents second. The Sunnah elaborates extensively: the mother is owed three times the honour of the father (Bukhari and Muslim), and serving parents well during their lifetime is among the highest deeds.

**Third: do not kill your children for fear of poverty** (*wa la taqtulu awladakum min imlaqin*). This is a direct address to the pre-Islamic Arab practice of killing daughters — but also to male infanticide among the very poor. The Qur'an does not merely prohibit the killing; it dismantles the economic argument behind it: *nahnu narzuqukum wa iyyahum* — We provide for you and for them. The provision of children is not the parent's responsibility alone; Allah is the Provider. The fear of poverty that drove infanticide is addressed by a theological truth: the same God who gave you the child will provide for the child. This is not naïve optimism; it is a redirecting of trust. Worrying about provision to the point of killing is an act of distrust in Allah.

**Fourth: do not come near indecencies** (*wa la taqrabu al-fawahish*) — whether open or secret. The word *fawahish* (singular: *fahisha*) refers to acts of severe moral wrongfulness — classically including adultery, fornication, homosexual acts, and other transgressions with wide destructive effects. The instruction *la taqrabu* — do not even come near — is stronger than "do not do." It includes everything that leads toward the act: the environment, the company, the content consumed, the private indulgence that gradually weakens resistance. Both the open and the secret are included — meaning that private indecency is equally prohibited, and the fact that "no one sees" provides no religious exemption.

**Fifth: do not take a human life made sacred by Allah except with legal right** (*wa la taqtulu al-nafs allati harrama Allahu illa bil-haqq*). Human life — *al-nafs* — is *harama Allahu* — declared sacred by God. This sacredness is not a human legal construction that can be revised; it is a divine designation. The prohibition is against its violation except *bil-haqq* — with right, with the lawful authority established by Allah's own law. Classical scholars identified three cases in which Islamic law permits the taking of life: lawful capital punishment after due process for certain crimes, just warfare under proper authority, and retaliation for murder when sought by the family of the victim under judicial oversight. Outside these specific cases, the life of every human being stands under divine protection.

The verse closes with *dhalikum wassakum bihi la'allakum ta'qilun* — this is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will understand. The word *ta'qilun* comes from *'aql* — reason, intellect. The implication is that these commandments are not irrational impositions. They are what a person with genuine understanding would arrive at: that God alone deserves worship, that parents deserve honour, that children deserve life, that the body must be kept from indecency, and that human life is sacred. These are the foundations of any civilised moral order. The Qur'an presents them not as arbitrary rules but as the natural output of sound reason guided by divine revelation.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 152

Verse 152 says: *"And do not come near the wealth of the orphan—unless intending to enhance it—until they attain maturity. Give full measure and weigh with justice. We never require of any soul more than what it can afford. Whenever you speak, maintain justice—even regarding a close relative. And fulfil your covenant with Allah. This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will be mindful."*

The ethical commandments continue in this verse, moving from matters of life and body (in verse 151) to matters of wealth, social justice, speech, and covenant. Together, verses 151–152 constitute what some classical scholars described as the most comprehensive ethical programme in the Qur'an.

**The wealth of the orphan**: *wa la taqrabu mala al-yatimi* — do not come near the orphan's wealth — except in the best way, meaning with the intention and action of enhancing it, until the orphan reaches maturity (*ashuddahu*). The orphan is one of the most consistently protected categories in the Qur'an. Surah Al-Baqarah, Al-Nisa', Al-An'am, Al-Insan, Al-Fajr — in surah after surah, the rights of the orphan are mentioned and their violation condemned. The reason is structural: orphans lack the natural guardian — the father — who would ordinarily protect their property interests. Without that protection, they are vulnerable to exploitation by guardians, relatives, and trustees who handle their wealth.

The instruction here is specific: do not even come near the wealth, except to improve it. This means that a guardian managing orphan funds is held to a high standard: not merely avoiding theft, but actively working for the orphan's financial benefit. The phrase "until they attain maturity" establishes a time-limit and an exit: when the orphan reaches adulthood, the guardianship ends and the wealth is returned.

**Full measure and just weight**: *wa awfu al-kayl wa al-mizan bil-qist* — complete the measure and the scales in justice. This addresses commercial dealings. *Kayl* refers to measurement by volume (grain, liquids), and *mizan* refers to measurement by weight. Shortchanging buyers, inflating quantities when selling, underweighting goods — these are violations of a basic covenant of honest trade. The Qur'an returns to this repeatedly (see 17:35, 83:1–3, where those who cheat in measure are given a chilling warning). In the Prophet's ﷺ time, these were immediate and visible commercial frauds. In the modern economy, their equivalents include manipulated financial statements, hidden fees, false advertising, and misrepresented product quality.

**Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity**: *la nukallifu nafsan illa wus'aha* — We do not place on any soul more than it can bear. This is an embedded reassurance within the commandments. Allah is giving weighty moral obligations — do not steal from orphans, be scrupulously honest in trade, speak justly always. And then He says: I will not ask of you what is beyond you. This is not a general philosophical statement here (though it is true generally); it is a contextual assurance to people who might feel the moral demands are beyond human capacity. The obligations are real, but they are calibrated to what human beings can actually do. Islamic jurisprudence builds an entire framework of facilitation and hardship removal (*taysir* and *raf' al-haraj*) on this verse.

**Just speech**: *wa idha qultum fa'dilu wa law kana dha qurba* — when you speak, speak justly, even if it concerns a close relative. This is one of the most demanding ethical instructions in the Qur'an. It is easy to be just when justice favours your family. It is easy to testify truthfully when the truth helps your own side. The real test of integrity is whether you maintain fairness when your own relatives, friends, or tribe are the subject. The Qur'an does not say "try your best to be fair." It says: be just. Full stop. Even if the person involved is your closest relative.

This instruction has profound implications for legal testimony, business partnerships, community decision-making, and public discourse. Many of the deepest corruptions in Muslim societies and communities — nepotism, tribalism, the covering up of wrongdoing by the powerful — are violations of this verse.

**The covenant with Allah**: *wa bi 'ahdi Allahi awfu* — and fulfil your covenant with Allah. What is this covenant? Scholars have offered several understandings. Ibn Abbas and al-Tabari suggest it refers to all obligations that Allah has placed on the believer — the full weight of religious and moral duty. Others specify it as the covenant of tawhid — the declaration of faith and its implications. A further understanding includes all the specific commandments just listed: they are themselves the content of the covenant. Fulfilling the covenant means living what you have declared — that Allah alone is God, that His law is binding, and that these commandments are obligations.

According to a narration of Abdullah ibn Mas'ud reported in Tafsir Ibn Kathir, he said that whoever wishes to see the special will of the Prophet ﷺ with his seal on it should recite these three verses — in them lies the will and testament bequeathed by the Prophet ﷺ to his community.

The verse closes with *dhalikum wassakum bihi la'allakum tadhakkarun* — this is what He commanded you so that perhaps you will be mindful. The word here is *tadhakkarun* — from *dhikr*, remembrance. These obligations must not be learned once and forgotten. They require ongoing consciousness, repeated remembrance, active keeping-in-mind. The ethical life is not a one-time declaration but a daily practice of recalling what Allah has asked of you.

## Verse 153

Verse 153 says: *"Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way. This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will be conscious of Allah."*

This verse forms the conclusion of the magnificent three-verse ethical commandment that began in verse 151. After listing the specific obligations, Allah steps back and names what all of it constitutes: *hadha sirathi mustaqiman* — this is My Path, perfectly straight.

The Arabic word *sirat* means road, path, a clear way to walk. *Mustaqim* means straight, upright, correct — the same word used in *Al-Fatihah* when we ask for guidance to *al-sirat al-mustaqim*. Every day in prayer, Muslims ask Allah for guidance to the straight path. Here in verse 153, that path is identified by its contents: tawhid, honouring parents, protecting children's lives, staying away from indecency, not violating the sanctity of human life, protecting orphan wealth, honest commerce, just speech, and fulfilling the divine covenant. This is the straight path. It is not an abstraction. It is a set of specific moral commitments lived in daily life.

The instruction is twofold: *fattabi'uhu* — follow it — and *wa la tattabi'u al-subul* — do not follow the other paths. The plural *subul* (paths) contrasted with the singular *sirat* (path) is theologically significant. There is one straight path and many deviating ones. Truth has a unity; error has infinite variations. Every moral system, every invented religious framework, every philosophical school that replaces divine guidance with human reasoning alone is one of the *subul* — and they all share one destination: *fa tatafarraqa bikum 'an sabilihi* — they scatter you away from His Path.

Abdullah ibn Mas'ud reports in the Musnad of al-Darimi that the Prophet ﷺ drew a vertical line and said, "This is the path of Allah," then drew lines on its right and left and said, "These are the subul — on every one of them is a Shaytan inviting to it." He then recited this verse as evidence. The image is vivid and accurate: one straight road, and branching off it in every direction are other roads — each with an inviting voice urging you to take it. The uniqueness of the straight path is not that it is the only road people walk, but that it is the only one that leads to Allah.

The verse closes with its third variation of the concluding formula. In verse 151, it was *la'allakum ta'qilun* (so you will understand/reason). In verse 152, it was *la'allakum tadhakkarun* (so you will be mindful/remember). Here it is *la'allakum tattaqun* (so you will be conscious of Allah, have taqwa). The escalating progression of these three conclusions is deliberate. First, understand. Then, remember. Finally, develop *taqwa* — that deep, comprehensive God-consciousness that is the highest station of the believer. The commandments are not just rules to know and remember; their ultimate purpose is to build *taqwa* — a constant awareness of Allah that shapes every choice, every word, every transaction.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 154

Verse 154 says: *"Additionally, We gave Moses the Scripture, completing the favour upon those who do good, detailing everything, and as a guide and a mercy, so perhaps they would be certain of the meeting with their Lord."*

Having just set out the foundational ethical commandments of Islam (verses 151–153), the Qur'an now places those commandments within the larger history of divine revelation. The Torah given to Moses (peace be upon him) is mentioned here not to contrast Islam with Judaism but to confirm the continuity of divine guidance across prophetic history.

The verse states that the Scripture was given to Moses *tamaman 'ala alladhi ahsana* — completing the favour upon those who do good. The word *tamam* means completion, fulfilment, perfection. The Torah was a complete divine guidance for its time and its community — not a partial or tentative document, but a full expression of divine mercy directed at the Children of Israel through Moses.

The phrase *'ala alladhi ahsana* — upon those who do good — has been understood by scholars in two ways. First, it can mean that the Torah was a completion of the blessing given to Moses himself, who was among those who excelled in worship and gratitude. Second, it can refer to the community: the Torah was a mercy for those among the Children of Israel who responded with *ihsan* — excellence of faith and conduct. Both readings are valid and complementary.

The verse then characterises the Torah as *tafsilal li kulli shay'* — a detailed explanation of everything. This should be understood contextually: everything the Children of Israel needed for their religious and social life was addressed in it. This is a generous Qur'anic description of the Torah, affirming its comprehensiveness as a divine document. The Qur'an does not demean the Torah; it confirms its divine origin and purpose.

It is then described as *hudan wa rahmah* — guidance and mercy — for its community. These two words frequently appear together in Qur'anic descriptions of scripture. Guidance (*huda*) implies showing the right path, giving direction; mercy (*rahmah*) implies the warmth of divine care for the community being addressed. Scripture, in the Qur'anic understanding, is not a legal code imposed coldly from above but an act of divine mercy — God caring enough for human beings to tell them how to live well.

The purpose is stated: *la'allahum bil liqa'i rabbihim yu'minun* — so that they might be certain of the meeting with their Lord. The Torah, like the Qur'an, was designed to cultivate *yaqin* — certainty — in the reality of the Hereafter and the standing before Allah that every human soul will face. Religion is ultimately about that encounter. All of the commandments, all of the guidance, all of the mercy — it is preparation for meeting Allah.

This verse, placed after the ethical commandments of 151–153, suggests a connection: the same foundational moral truths given to the Muslims were also at the heart of what was given to Moses. The core ethical commitments of all the Abrahamic prophets point toward the same reality. This is the Qur'anic vision of religious continuity: one God, successive prophets, one underlying moral framework, culminating in the final revelation.

## Verse 155

Verse 155 says: *"This is a blessed Book We have revealed. So follow it and be mindful of Allah, so you may be shown mercy."*

After mentioning the Torah given to Moses, the Qur'an now returns its attention to itself. The word *hadha* — "this" — points to the Qur'an in the hands of those hearing it or in the experience of those reciting it. *Kitabun anzalnahu mubarak* — a blessed Book that We have sent down.

The word *mubarak* — blessed — carries within it the idea of something from which goodness flows abundantly and continuously. *Barakah* is not a static quality; it is dynamic, outgoing, nourishing. A blessed book is one that gives more than it takes, that enriches the one who engages with it, that produces good fruits in the life of the community that follows it. The Qur'an's *barakah* manifests in every generation that has engaged sincerely with its teachings: in the transformation of individuals, the building of just communities, the civilisational flourishing that followed the first Muslim community and every serious revival since.

The two instructions that follow — *fattabi'uhu* (follow it) and *wa ittaqu* (be mindful of Allah) — are inseparable. Following the Book without taqwa produces mechanical compliance without spirit. Claiming taqwa without following the Book produces sentiment without structure. Together, they form the complete response to divine revelation: let the text shape your actions, and let God-consciousness shape your interior. The promised outcome is *turhamun* — so that you may receive mercy. Mercy is the destination. The Qur'an is the road map, taqwa is the fuel, and following it is the journey.

## Verse 156

Verse 156 says: *"You pagans can no longer say, 'Scriptures were only revealed to two groups before us and we were unaware of their teachings.'"*

This verse is one of three consecutive verses (156–157) that address what might be called the "excuse argument" of the Arabian polytheists. Before the coming of the Qur'an, the Arabs had a ready-made justification for their state of religious ignorance: scripture had been given to Jews and Christians, but not to them, and they had no access to those teachings. The Qur'an is now closing that excuse permanently.

The two groups referred to — *ta'ifatayn* — are the Jews and the Christians, the People of the Book. They had received revealed scriptures: the Torah to Moses and, in the Islamic understanding, the Injil (Gospel, the original divine revelation given to Jesus). The Arabs had for centuries existed in the shadow of these scriptural traditions without being directly addressed by them. This geographical and linguistic distance from revelation was their stated justification: "We were unaware. The scriptures were in other languages, for other peoples. What were we to do?"

The Qur'an's response is clear: that justification is no longer available. The Qur'an has now come in Arabic, to an Arab prophet, for the Arab people — and through them, for all of humanity. The Prophet ﷺ was sent as a messenger *to all people* (34:28), and the Qur'an was revealed in clear Arabic so that its meanings could be directly accessed without translation or intermediary interpretation. The excuse of inaccessibility has been permanently removed.

This verse has a principle within it that extends beyond its immediate historical context. Human accountability before Allah is calibrated to what has been made available. Allah does not hold people responsible for knowledge they had no means of obtaining. But when the message arrives — when the Qur'an reaches a people, when the Prophet's ﷺ mission becomes known — the situation changes. Reception of the message creates responsibility.

## Verse 157

Verse 157 says: *"Nor can you say, 'If only the Scriptures had been revealed to us, we would have been better guided than they.' Now there has come to you from your Lord a clear proof—a guide and mercy. Who then does more wrong than those who deny Allah's revelations and turn away from them? We will reward those who turn away from Our revelations with a dreadful punishment for turning away."*

This verse closes the second excuse: not just "we had no scripture" but the more arrogant claim, "if only we had scripture, we would have responded better than the Jews and Christians did." The Arabs observed that the People of the Book received divine guidance and then divided, argued, and in many cases rejected their own prophets. Some Arabs said — perhaps with a measure of tribal pride — that *they* would have done better.

The Qur'an accepts this claim as a challenge and then immediately answers it: the scripture has now come. The test you claimed you would pass is now before you. *Qad ja'akum bayyinatun min rabbikum wa hudan wa rahmah* — there has come to you from your Lord a clear proof, a guidance and a mercy. The same formula that described the Torah (in verse 154) and the Qur'an (in verse 155) is now applied directly to the situation of the reader: this is your *bayyinah* (clear proof), your *huda* (guidance), and your *rahmah* (mercy). What you said you wanted has arrived.

The verse then asks: *fa man azlamu mimman kadhdhaba bi ayati Allahi wa sadafa 'anha* — who does more wrong than those who deny Allah's signs and turn away from them? The word *sadafa* means to turn one's face away — an active, deliberate movement of rejection. This is not the person who has not heard; this is the person who has heard and turns their back. That turning away, after the *bayyinah* has arrived, is described as the worst form of wrongdoing.

The consequence is stated with a certain rhetorical weight: *sa najzi alladhina yasdifu 'an ayatina su'a al-'adhab bi ma kanu yasdifu'n* — we will repay those who turn away from Our signs with a terrible punishment *because of their turning away*. The punishment is directly proportional to the action: they turned away from guidance, and so they will receive the consequence of that turning away.

## Verse 158

Verse 158 says: *"Are they awaiting the coming of the angels, or your Lord Himself, or some of your Lord's major signs? On the Day your Lord's signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith. Say, 'Keep waiting! We too are waiting.'"*

This verse addresses those who, despite receiving the clear proof described in the previous verses, continue to demand something more dramatic before they will believe. The verse poses this demand ironically: *hal yanzuruna illa an ta'tiyahumu al-mala'ikah* — are they actually waiting for the angels to come? Or for their Lord Himself? Or for the great signs of the Last Day?

This is not an idle question. Some among the Makkan polytheists would say: bring us a visible miracle, bring us angels we can see, then we will believe. The Qur'an's response is that the demand itself reveals the problem. Belief that requires overwhelming, inescapable, undeniable supernatural compulsion is not genuine faith — it is capitulation under pressure. When a person can only "believe" after every last excuse for disbelief has been physically removed, their belief is not the fruit of sincere reflection and humble submission but of having no other option.

The verse specifies a key Qur'anic principle: *yawma ya'ti ba'du ayati rabbika la yanfa'u nafsan imanaha lam takun amanat min qabl* — on the day when some of the Lord's signs come, faith will not benefit a person who did not believe before. This refers to the *ashratu al-sa'ah* — the major signs of the Last Hour. Authentic narrations in Sahih Muslim record that among the major signs before the Day of Judgment are the rising of the sun from the west and the emergence of the Beast (*dabbat al-ard*). Once these signs appear, the door of sincere faith — faith that arises freely from the heart in response to evidence — closes. What remains is only the desperate belief of one who can no longer deny, and such belief, arriving too late and under compulsion, carries no weight.

The same principle applies to the person who did believe but did no good through their faith: *aw kasabat fi imaniha khayran* — or earned good through their faith. Faith that produces no good deeds, no change in conduct, no response to divine instruction, is a faith that was never fully activated. The verse is a warning against deferred virtue — the assumption that one can delay sincere commitment until later, without realising that the window may close.

The closing command — *qul intaziru inna muntaziruna* — say, "Wait. We too are waiting" — is one of the Qur'an's powerful uses of calm, dignified finality. There is no anger here, no desperation. The Prophet ﷺ is told simply to say: we are all waiting. The difference is that the believers wait in faith, with good deeds, prepared to meet their Lord. The others wait hoping either that nothing will come or that they will still have time when it does. History and revelation both counsel otherwise.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 159

Verse 159 says: *"Indeed, you, O Prophet, are not responsible whatsoever for those who have divided their faith and split into sects. Their judgment rests only with Allah. And He will inform them of what they used to do."*

This verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ directly with a statement of profound pastoral reassurance. The mission of a prophet is to convey, to guide, to call. It is not to ensure that every person or every community responds correctly. The Prophet ﷺ is told in clear terms: those who divide religion and form sects — their matter is with Allah, not with you.

The verse describes these people with two acts: *farraqu dinahum* — they divided/fragmented their religion — and *kanu shiya'an* — they became sects/factions. The word *shiya'* (singular: *shi'ah*) in classical Arabic refers to groups that gather around particular partisan loyalties or doctrinal positions in ways that fracture the unity of the community. This verse is not speaking of legitimate scholarly difference (*ikhtilaf al-fuqaha'*) — the natural diversity of opinion among scholars who all agree on fundamentals. It is speaking of those who shatter the unity of the *ummah* by treating their particular group's interpretation as the totality of Islam, condemning all others.

Scholars have identified this verse's applicability across different contexts. Al-Tabari and others noted that in the first instance, it may refer to the previous communities — Jews and Christians — who had divided their scriptures and teachings into competing camps. Ibn Kathir extended it to apply as a warning to the Muslim community as well, given the hadith narrations in which the Prophet ﷺ spoke of his community dividing into groups and warned against partisanship that fragments the *ummah*.

The instruction to the Prophet ﷺ — *lasta minhum fi shay'in* — you have nothing to do with them in this matter — is a release from responsibility, but also a boundary. The Prophet ﷺ had done everything required of him: conveyed the message, established the community, taught the book and wisdom. What people do with that after the fact, including dividing it, is their own moral responsibility and will be answered to Allah directly.

For contemporary Muslims, this verse carries a sober message. Sectarianism, tribalism in faith, the condemning of other Muslims who differ in secondary matters, the fracturing of communities over personalities and politics — these are all among the things that this verse warns against. The diversity of legitimate scholarly interpretation is not what the verse condemns. What it condemns is the poisonous partisanship that makes one's sect the measure of all things and every other Muslim a suspect. That judgment belongs to Allah.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 160

Verse 160 says: *"Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold. But whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished for only one. None will be wronged."*

This is one of the most beloved and frequently cited verses in the entire Qur'an — a statement of divine justice and divine generosity that is as simple as it is profound. It brings Surah Al-An'am's long treatment of theological argument, legal clarification, ethical commandment, and prophetic history to a close on a note of hope balanced by accountability.

The structure of the verse is deliberately parallel and asymmetrical. On the side of good: *man ja'a bil-hasanati falahu 'ashru amthaliha* — whoever comes with a good deed will receive ten times its equal. On the side of bad: *wa man ja'a bil-sayyi'ati fala yujza illa mithlaha* — whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished by only its equal — not ten times, not multiplied by any factor. The asymmetry is the point.

Allah's mercy multiplies good and does not multiply punishment. The reward for one sincere good deed is a minimum of ten times its value. The Qur'an says "minimum" implicitly because authentic hadith, recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, confirm that Allah can multiply good deeds by seven hundred times or more for whomever He wills. The hadith of the "two words light on the tongue, heavy on the scale" (*SubhanAllahi wa bihamdih, SubhanAllahil 'Azim*), the hadith of the value of one prayer in the mosque compared to others — these all expand the picture of divine multiplication of good. The tenfold minimum stated here is the base, not the ceiling.

The treatment of bad deeds, by contrast, is metered. One bad deed, one unit of accountability. No multiplication of punishment, no compounding of consequences beyond the act itself. This is *'adl* — justice — not severity.

The closing declaration — *wa hum la yuzlamun* — and they will not be wronged — is the Qur'anic seal of divine justice. No one will have a good deed taken from them unjustly. No one will have a bad deed added to their account that was not theirs. No one will be given more punishment than their deeds merit. The weighing on the Day of Judgment is perfectly just.

This verse closes the section beginning at verse 140 with the same concern that opened it: the divine standard against which all human actions will be measured. In verse 140, those who invented prohibitions and killed their children were declared utterly lost. Now, in verse 160, the principle of divine accounting is set out: every person, every deed, every choice — it all comes back, measured precisely and recompensed fairly. The believer who understood all the commandments of verses 151–153, who followed the blessed Book of verse 155, who held firm rather than waiting for the overwhelming signs of verse 158 — that person comes on the Day of Judgment with their good deeds multiplied tenfold. This is the closing invitation of the passage: live well, in accordance with the path laid out, and meet Allah with deeds that His mercy will multiply beyond your expectation.

===VERSE-END===
