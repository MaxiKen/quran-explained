# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 91–100

The last batch left a door open and a trajectory closed. Verse 89 said that those who repent after disbelief and mend their ways find God Forgiving and Merciful. Verse 90 said that those who disbelieve after belief and then *increase* in disbelief will not have their repentance accepted. Verse 91 now completes that arc: those who disbelieve and *die* as disbelievers — even an earthful of gold will not ransom them.

Then the surah turns. The turn is sharp, and it is deliberate. From the economics of the Hereafter (gold that cannot buy exit) the text moves to the economics of this life spent rightly: you will not reach *birr* until you spend of what you love (92). From there it opens a legal-historical argument with the People of the Book about food law and what Israel forbade himself before the Torah (93–94), closes it by sending the reader back to Abraham the upright who was no polytheist (95), and then plants the first House at Bakkah — blessed, a guidance for the worlds — with the standing-place of Abraham, the sanctuary, and the obligation of pilgrimage on whoever is able (96–97). The batch ends where the surah's confrontation mode resumes: why do you deny the revelations, why do you turn believers from the way while you are witnesses (98–99) — and a direct warning to the believers themselves: if you yield to a party of those given the Scripture, they will turn you back from belief to disbelief (100).

Five cruxes govern the reading. The first is the ransom of gold at 91 and what it does — and does not — say about charity, deeds, and the finality of dying on disbelief. The second is *birr* at 92 and the famous living commentary of Abū Ṭalḥah and the garden of Bayruḥā'. The third is the food-law dispute at 93: who "Israel" is, what he forbade, and why the Torah is called as witness. The fourth is Bakkah, the first House, and the *istiṭāʿah* that conditions the ḥajj obligation at 96–97. The fifth is the shift of address at 100 — the surah turns from arguing *with* a party of the People of the Book to protecting the believers *from* being led by them.

One standing caution, carried from every previous batch. Charges remain about **conduct and a party** (*farīq* at 100; the acts named at 98–99), never about a people as such. And verse 91, for all its severity, still describes those who *die* as disbelievers — the living who turn and mend remain under 89.

## Verse 91

**"Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers."**

In simple terms: those who disbelieve and die while they are still disbelievers — if any one of them offered enough gold to fill the whole earth as a ransom for himself, it would never be accepted. Theirs is a painful punishment, and they have no helpers.

**This verse closes the arc that began at 86.** Disbelief after belief (86). Curse (87). Eternity without lightening (88). Exception for those who repent and mend (89). Those who increase in disbelief (90). And now: those who disbelieve and *die* as disbelievers. The sequence is a moral timeline. The door is open in life. The door is not open after death on disbelief, by ransom or by any other payment.

**"Die as disbelievers."** *Wa-mātū wa-hum kuffār.* The circumstantial clause is the whole point. Not those who disbelieved and then returned. Those who disbelieved and whose death found them still in that state. Death is the cut-off that 4:18 also names: repentance is not for those who continue the evils until, when death comes to one of them, he says "I repent now."

**"A ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world."** *Mil'u al-arḍi dhahaban.*

*Mil'* is the fill of a vessel — whatever quantity it takes to pack the container full. *Mil'u al-arḍ* is the earth used as that vessel, filled with gold. The image is deliberately impossible. No human being has ever possessed it. The verse is not pricing salvation. It is saying that even a price no one could pay would still not be accepted.

**"It would never be accepted from them."** *Fa-lan yuqbala min aḥadihim.* The same *lan* of strong future negation we met at 3:85 and 3:90. Acceptance is refused. The passive leaves the Refuser unnamed because the Refuser is God.

**"Even if he offered it as ransom."** *Wa-law iftadā bihi.* *Iftidā'* is the paying of a ransom to free a captive. The man of this verse is imagining himself a captive who can buy his way out. The verse says the market is closed. Parallel verses make the same point with different numbers: 5:36 (all that is in the earth and as much again with it); 13:18; 39:47. The teaching is consistent across the Qur'an: after death on disbelief, wealth does not negotiate.

**The hadith that belongs with this verse.** Two authentic reports are regularly joined to it in the tafsir, and both are worth knowing with their proper force.

The first is the question about ʿAbdullāh ibn Judʿān — a pre-Islamic Makkan known for generosity, feeding the poor, and helping the indebted. The Prophet ﷺ was asked whether that would benefit him. He said no — for he never once said, "My Lord, forgive me my sin on the Day of Judgment." The point is not that generosity is worthless. It is that deeds without the foundation of faith in God and the Last Day do not become ransom-currency in the end. The report is in Muslim and is sound; it should be held as illuminating the principle, not as a private verdict on every generous non-Muslim (whose particular case is God's).

The second is the longer eschatological narration from Anas, in Aḥmad and elsewhere: a man of the Fire is asked whether he would ransom himself with the earth's fill of gold; he says yes; he is told he has lied — he was already asked for something far easier than that. The rhetorical force is the same as the verse's: the thing that was available in life (faith, repentance) is incomparably lighter than the gold imagined after death, and it was refused.

**"Painful punishment… no helpers."** *ʿAdhābun alīm… wa-mā lahum min nāṣirīn.*

The root *n-ṣ-r* returns. The disciples were *anṣār* (3:52). The covenant demanded *la-tanṣurunnahu* (3:81). The rejecters at 3:56 were told they would have no *nāṣir*. Now the same word closes the arc of 86–91. The one who dies on disbelief has exhausted the help that faith would have brought, and no created helper remains.

**What this verse is not.** It is not a statement that charity is useless, or that good deeds done by people outside Islam have no meaning in this world. The Qur'an and the Sunnah both recognise worldly and moral realities in acts of kindness. What the verse states is narrower and harder: as *ransom from the punishment of the Hereafter*, for the one who dies a disbeliever, even an earthful of gold will not be accepted. Holding the narrow claim firmly, and refusing to inflate it into a general contempt for human goodness, is part of reading the verse honestly.

**It is also not a licence to despair of the living.** The subject is the one who has *died* as a disbeliever. The living person still asking, still turning, still capable of the *tawbah* and *iṣlāḥ* of verse 89, is not under this verse. Confusing the two has harmed people. The passage itself refuses the confusion by placing 89 before 90 and 91.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The modern form of the fantasy this verse kills is not usually a literal sack of gold at the grave. It is the quieter belief that something else will settle the account at the end — reputation, philanthropy as brand, a life of competence, the love of one's children, the size of what one built. Verse 91 is not against any of those goods. It is against treating them as *iftidā'* — as ransom-currency that can substitute for the one transaction that was available in life: turning to God while turning was still possible.

The practical corollary is severe and kind at once. Severe: there is a deadline. Kind: the deadline is not yet your death, if you are reading this. The gold you cannot assemble is irrelevant. The repentance you can still make is not.

**And the spiritual weight.** Faith is heavier than the earth's fill of gold. That is not a slogan. It is the verse's own arithmetic. A person who dies with faith and little wealth has what the person who dies with an earthful of gold and no faith does not. The religion is not anti-wealth. It is anti-the-illusion-that-wealth-ransoms.

## Verse 92

**"You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish. And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah."**

In simple terms: you will never reach true righteousness until you spend out of what you love. And whatever you spend, God knows it fully.

**After gold that cannot ransom, gold that can.** The turn from verse 91 to verse 92 is one of the surah's cleanest pivots. Verse 91 said that an earthful of gold offered *after* death on disbelief will not be accepted. Verse 92 says that spending *in life*, from what you love, is the road to *birr*. Same metal. Opposite moral location. The religion is not against wealth. It is against wealth in the wrong tense.

**"You will never achieve righteousness."** *Lan tanālū al-birr.*

*Lan* again — strong future negation. *Tanālū* is to attain, to reach, to get one's hands on. *Al-birr* is a dense word. Its root *b-r-r* is about expansive goodness — land that is open and fertile (*barr* as opposed to sea), and then moral expansiveness: piety, righteousness, kindness, the comprehensive good that includes faith and acts. At 2:177 the Qur'an itself defines *birr* at length: faith in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book and the prophets; spending on relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveller, those who ask, and for freeing captives; prayer; zakāh; keeping promises; patience in hardship and in battle. So when 3:92 says you will not attain *birr* until you spend of what you love, it is not inventing a new virtue. It is naming the cost of the virtue 2:177 already described.

Some early readers, including ʿAmr ibn Maymūn as reported by Wakīʿ, took *al-birr* here as a metonym for Paradise — the ultimate good that *birr* leads to. That reading is possible and early. The wider lexical sense (righteousness itself) is also strong. Both preach: you do not reach the thing, or the place the thing leads to, until the spending is from what is loved.

**"Until you donate some of what you cherish."** *Ḥattā tunfiqū mimmā tuḥibbūn.*

Two words are doing precision work.

*Tunfiqū* — you spend, you give out. The root *n-f-q* is the ordinary verb of charitable and obligatory spending throughout the Qur'an.

*Mimmā tuḥibbūn* — *from what* you love. The *min* is partitive. Not necessarily everything you love. From among the things that are dear. Al-Qushayrī's observation is worth keeping as a spiritual gloss rather than as binding tafsir: whoever wants *birr* spends *of* what he loves; whoever wants the All-Good (*al-Bārr*) is prepared to spend everything. The verse itself requires the partitive.

And *tuḥibbūn* — what you love. Not what you can spare. Not what is going off. Not what you had already decided to discard. The test is attached to attachment. If the thing does not cost you inwardly, the verse has not yet been met.

**The living commentary: Abū Ṭalḥah and Bayruḥā'.** When this verse was revealed, Abū Ṭalḥah — one of the Anṣār, among the most generous of the Companions — came to the Prophet ﷺ and said that the garden of Bayruḥā' was the most beloved of his property to him, and that he was giving it in charity for God's sake, hoping for its reward, and that the Prophet ﷺ should dispose of it as God showed him. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the authentic wording, that this was profitable property — *mālun rābiḥ* — and directed that it be put among Abū Ṭalḥah's relatives. The report is in Bukhārī and Muslim from Anas. It is the verse's own first practical tafsir, performed by a man who heard it and moved.

Other Companions are reported to have responded in kind — Ibn ʿUmar freeing a beloved servant-woman; Zayd ibn Ḥārithah bringing a horse he loved. These reports vary in strength; the Abū Ṭalḥah narration is the anchor. The pattern they share is the point: the verse was not heard as poetry. It was heard as an instruction that named the next act.

**"And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah."** *Wa-mā tunfiqū min shay'in fa-inna Allāha bihi ʿalīm.*

Two comforts and one warning sit in this clause.

Comfort one: the amount is not the measure. *Min shay'in* — of anything. A date from a loved store, given rightly, is inside the verse. Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's reported gloss points this way: even a single date spent sincerely can place a person within the promise.

Comfort two: God knows. The spending that no one saw, the attachment that no one measured, the quiet gift that rearranged a private life — *bihi ʿalīm*.

Warning: God knows. The performance-gift, the gift from what was already despised, the gift given for a name — also known. The verse closes the door on both despair (my gift is too small) and theatre (my gift is being watched by the wrong audience).

**How this verse sits after 91.** The person who dies on disbelief cannot buy exit with gold. The person still alive is being told where gold — and every other loved thing — becomes useful: not as ransom after the fact, but as expenditure from love, now. The two verses together are a single lesson about time. Wealth has a tense in which it saves, and a tense in which it does not.

**A necessary caution about reading *birr*.** The verse does not say that people who have not yet spent from what they love have no righteousness at all, or that they are outside Islam, or that their prayer and fasting are void. *Lan tanālū al-birr* names a completeness and a rank — the full attainment of the comprehensive good — conditional on a particular kind of spending. Ordinary obligatory zakāh from ordinary wealth remains obligatory and rewarded. The verse is calling to the higher act that unlocks the higher name. Flattening it into "you are nothing until you give away your house" is not tafsir. It is panic.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The loved thing is not always money. It is time. It is attention. It is the evening that was going to be rest and becomes a hospital visit. It is the contact in one's phone that could open a door for someone else. It is the reputation one risks by telling the truth. It is the child one wanted to keep close and instead teaches to leave the nest. *Mimmā tuḥibbūn* is whatever, if asked for, would make the hand hesitate.

And the hesitation is not the enemy. The hesitation is the evidence that the thing is loved. The verse does not ask for loveless giving. It asks for giving that includes the love — spending the thing *and* the attachment, or enough of both that the self notices.

In a culture that treats decluttering as virtue, the verse is easy to mis-hear. Giving away what you already wanted gone is not this verse. Giving away what you were still using, still planning around, still naming as yours — that is closer.

**And the spiritual centre.** Every loved thing is a potential idol and a potential bridge. The same object, kept as ultimate, blocks *birr*; released for God's sake, becomes the road to it. Verse 92 is the religion's way of refusing to let love of the world be either demonised or deified. Love it enough that giving it costs. Then give.

## Verse 93

**"All food was lawful for the children of Israel, except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.”"**

In simple terms: every food was lawful for the Children of Israel except what Israel forbade for himself before the Torah was sent down. Say: bring the Torah and read it, if you are telling the truth.

**The surah returns to argument with the People of the Book, and the subject is food.** The turn can feel abrupt after the spiritual intensity of 91–92. It is not abrupt in the surah's own logic. The surah has been contesting claims about Abraham, about whose religion is whose, and about what the earlier scripture actually required. Food law was one of the live pressure-points of that contest in Madinah.

**The reported occasion.** The *asbāb* and tafsir literature preserve a consistent cluster: the Jews of Madinah objected that the Prophet ﷺ claimed to follow the creed of Abraham, yet ate camel meat and drank its milk — which, they said, Abraham did not. The Prophet ﷺ replied that it had been lawful for Abraham. They answered that everything they held unlawful now had been unlawful from Noah and Abraham onward. Verse 93 was revealed to cut that claim. Al-Wāḥidī carries versions of this; al-Jalālayn compresses it; Ibn Kathīr develops the legal point. The reports vary in chain strength; the dispute they describe fits the Madinan context and the verse's own challenge ("bring the Torah and read it").

**"All food was lawful for the children of Israel."** *Kullu al-ṭaʿāmi kāna ḥillan li-banī Isrā'īl.*

The starting position is permissibility. Food as a category was *ḥill* — lawful — for them. The Qur'an's default in food law more broadly is that good things are lawful and foul things are forbidden (see 7:157; 5:4–5); this verse applies that instinct historically. Before the Torah, the Children of Israel were not under the full Mosaic food code.

**"Except what Israel made unlawful for himself."** *Illā mā ḥarrama Isrā'īlu ʿalā nafsihi.*

**Who is Israel?** The dominant and strongest reading is that Israel is Jacob — *Isrā'īl* being his honorific title in the Biblical and Qur'anic onomastic tradition, and *Banū Isrā'īl* being his descendants. A minority line reading "Israel" as the children of Israel collectively is weaker against the grammar (*ʿalā nafsihi*, "upon himself," singular) and against the narrative reports.

**What did he forbid himself?** The tafsir tradition reports, commonly, that Jacob was afflicted with the sciatic pain (*ʿirq al-nasā*) and vowed that if he were healed he would forbid himself the most beloved of foods — named in many reports as camel meat and camel milk, in some as other meats. These details are transmitted reports, often with *Isrā'īliyyāt* colouring, and they are not in the Qur'anic text. What the text itself asserts is only: Israel forbade something *for himself*, before the Torah. Hold the text firmly. Hold the medical and menu details loosely.

**"Before the Torah was revealed."** *Min qabli an tunazzala al-tawrāh.* This clause is the hinge of the argument. Whatever Jacob forbade himself was a personal restriction (a vow, an abstinence, a medical or devotional choice in his own law), not a revealed food code binding his descendants as divine law from Abraham's time. The Mosaic prohibitions that later appear in the Torah are a later legislation. They are not proof that camel meat was forbidden to Abraham, and they are not proof that the Prophet ﷺ, claiming Abraham's creed, is inconsistent for eating it.

**"Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true."** *Qul fa'tū bi-al-tawrāti fa-tlūhā in kuntum ṣādiqīn.*

This is one of the Qur'an's most direct evidentiary challenges. The half-bracketed "O Prophet" in the supplied translation correctly marks the addressee of *qul*. The claim under test is the opponents' claim — that their present prohibitions reach back unchanged through Abraham. The method of test is: produce the Torah and read it. If the Torah itself shows a history of legislation (things forbidden at one stage, not at another), the claim of unbroken Abrahamic prohibition collapses.

The challenge also has a second edge. It treats the Torah as a text that can be brought and read in the dispute. That is consistent with the Qur'an's wider posture: it can call the earlier scripture to witness (see 2:41; 5:43–48) while also criticising concealment and distortion (2:75; 3:78). Holding both — scripture as witness, and scripture as sometimes mishandled — is the Qur'an's own pair.

**How this verse connects to 3:65–68 and to 3:95.** The surah has already said that Abraham was neither Jew nor Christian, and that the nearest of people to him are those who followed him, this Prophet, and those who believe (3:68). Verse 93 undercuts one practical objection to that claim (the food objection). Verse 95 will restate the positive: follow Abraham's way. The three verses are one argument in three movements.

**A note on Jewish food law, stated carefully.** Mainstream Judaism's kashrut is a developed legal system with Biblical roots and rabbinic elaboration; it is not "a lie about God" as a system, and this verse is not a licence to say so. What the verse contests is a specific historical claim used in a specific Madinan argument: that the Mosaic prohibitions, or the community's present prohibitions, were already Abraham's law and therefore expose the Prophet ﷺ as inconsistent with Abraham. That claim is what "bring the Torah" is designed to test. The Qur'an's own account of later prohibitions imposed on the Children of Israel as consequence or legislation (4:160; 6:146) is a separate discussion and should not be collapsed into contempt for Jewish practice as such.

**Now into the present.** The ordinary form of the error this verse corrects is the back-projection of a later rule onto an earlier authority, in order to win an argument. "The founders would never have allowed X." "The original community always did Y." Sometimes those claims are true and can be documented. Sometimes they are the present dressed in the past's clothing. Verse 93's method is still the right one: produce the source and read it. A religious argument that cannot survive contact with its own text is not traditionalism. It is invention.

**And the spiritual point hiding in a legal verse.** Jacob forbade himself something he loved — the tafsir tradition's detail, held loosely, would make this a direct echo of verse 92. Whether or not the sciatica story is historical, the juxtaposition is the surah's own: spending or refraining from what one loves, for God's sake, is a known prophetic pattern. The difference is that Jacob's personal restriction did not become, by that fact alone, a revealed prohibition for all his children for all time. Personal *waraʿ* is not automatically communal law. That distinction protects both piety and honesty.

## Verse 94

**"Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah, they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers."**

In simple terms: so whoever invents lies about God after that — they are the ones who are truly wronging.

**A short verse that names the act left standing when the challenge of 93 is refused.**

**"Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah."** *Fa-man iftarā ʿalā Allāhi al-kadhiba min baʿdi dhālika.*

*Iftarā* is to fabricate, to invent and attribute. *Al-kadhib* is the lie. *ʿAlā Allāhi* — upon God, against God, about God. The most serious form of lying in the Qur'an's moral universe is lying *about God*: saying He forbade what He did not forbid, or permitted what He did not permit, or said what He did not say. We have met this structure before in this surah — at 3:75, the claim "we are not accountable for the gentiles," which the verse called attributing lies to God knowingly; at 3:78, the distortion of the Book with the tongue. Verse 94 is the same family.

**"After that."** *Min baʿdi dhālika.* After the challenge to bring the Torah. After the historical correction of verse 93. The one who continues to invent, once the evidentiary door has been opened and the claim has been tested, is in a different moral position from the one who simply inherited a confused report. *Baʿda* again — the surah's consistent marker that timing and prior light raise the stakes (3:82, 3:86, 3:89, 3:90).

**"They will be the true wrongdoers."** *Fa-ulā'ika hum al-ẓālimūn.*

The supplied translation's half-bracketed "true" is interpretive emphasis on the identifying force of *ulā'ika hum* — "those — they are the ones." *Ẓālimūn* are those who put things out of place; here, those who put words in God's mouth that He did not speak. The demonstrative identification is the same form used at 3:82 (*hum al-fāsiqūn*) and 3:90 (*hum al-ḍāllūn*). The surah names states with precision: rebellious, astray, wrongdoing — each matched to an act.

**What the verse does not do.** It does not declare every Jew or Christian a fabricator. It names the one who fabricates lies about God after the matter has been put to the test. The surah's own discipline of *farīq* / *ṭā'ifah* / *minhum* still governs. A reading that turns 94 into an ethnic verdict has left the text.

**Bring it down.** The modern form is familiar: the religious ruling delivered with "God says" when the speaker means "my teacher prefers," or "my politics require," or "my fear suggests." The verse's warning is not only for Madinan polemicists. It is for anyone with enough religious literacy to know the difference between a revealed text and a useful claim, and enough motive to blur them. After light, invention is *ẓulm*.

## Verse 95

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah has declared the truth. So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”"**

In simple terms: say — God has told the truth. So follow the way of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of those who associate partners with God.

**The positive close of the food-law argument.** Verse 93 corrected the history. Verse 94 named the one who invents after correction. Verse 95 states what to do instead: take God's truth as given, and follow Abraham.

**"Allah has declared the truth."** *Ṣadaqa Allāh.* Two words. God has been truthful — in what He revealed about the food laws, about Abraham, about the covenant, about everything under dispute in this surah. The phrase is a seal. The argument is not left as a tie between claims. One side is named as *ṣidq*.

**"So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright."** *Fa-ttabiʿū millata Ibrāhīma ḥanīfan.*

*Millah* is a way of religion, a creed-path. We have met it at 2:135 and at 3:95's own earlier parallel territory in 3:67 — *mā kāna Ibrāhīmu yahūdiyyan wa-lā naṣrāniyyan wa-lākin kāna ḥanīfan musliman*. Abraham was not a Jew and not a Christian; he was a *ḥanīf*, a muslim. The supplied translation's "the upright" for *ḥanīfan* is serviceable; the Arabic is richer. A *ḥanīf* is one who turns away from falsehood toward the straight — away from *shirk* toward pure monotheism — the pre-Mosaic, pre-sectarian orientation of Abraham that the Qur'an claims as its own deepest root.

**"Who was not a polytheist."** *Wa-mā kāna min al-mushrikīn.* The same negation that closed 3:67. Abraham's file is cleared of *shirk* in every direction the surah has been fighting: he is not to be claimed by those who associate, and he is not to be claimed as a partisan of later named communities in a way that erases his *ḥanīf* core. Follow *him* — not the later accretions that argue about him.

**Connection forward to 96–97.** Why does the House at Bakkah come next? Because Abraham is not only a creed. He is a builder and a pilgrim's ancestor. The God who says "follow Abraham" immediately points at the House Abraham raised and the rites that still go there. Creed and sanctuary are one argument.

**Bring it into the present.** "Follow Abraham" in a modern mouth can become a vague spirituality that loves the name and drops the content — monotheism without law, orientation without obligation, "Abrahamic" as a diplomatic adjective. The Qur'an's Abraham is sharper: *ḥanīf*, not a *mushrik*, a man who submitted, who built, who was willing to spend what he loved (his son, in the trial the Qur'an narrates elsewhere), and whose *millah* is defined by what he turned *from* as much as by what he turned *to*. Following him costs. Verse 92 has already said what kind of cost.

## Verse 96

**"Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people."**

In simple terms: the first House set up for people is the one at Bakkah — blessed, and a guidance for all the worlds.

**The surah plants a flag in geography.** After Abraham the *ḥanīf*, the House associated with Abraham. After arguments about whose religion is older, a claim about whose sanctuary is first.

**"The first House established for humanity."** *Awwala baytin wuḍiʿa li-l-nās.*

*Bayt* here is the House of worship — the Kaʿbah — not a residential house. *Wuḍiʿa li-l-nās* — set down, appointed, established *for people*. The beneficiaries are not a tribe. They are *al-nās*, humankind. The universality is in the grammar before it is in the commentary.

**"First"** is a genuine crux, and the tradition has been careful with it.

The strongest early reading, anchored in a hadith of Abū Dharr in Bukhārī and Muslim, is that the first mosque placed on the earth was al-Masjid al-Ḥarām, and the next was al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, with forty years between them. "First" on this reading is chronological primacy among houses of worship.

A complementary reading emphasises primacy of rank and blessing rather than only of time: the most important, the archetypal, the one that defines what a House of God is. The two readings are not enemies. A house can be first in time and first in rank.

Reports that Adam first built it and that Abraham and Ishmael rebuilt it after the Flood are widely transmitted in the tafsir and sīrah literature. They are not at the level of the Abū Dharr hadith, and the Qur'an itself at 2:127 narrates Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundations without narrating the pre-history. Hold the Adam reports as traditional narrative; hold 2:127 and 3:96 as the firm text.

**"At Bakkah."** *Bi-Bakkah.*

Bakkah is Makkah. The name appears in this form here; *Makkah* appears at 48:24. Classical explanations of the variant include: that Bakkah names the specific site of the Kaʿbah while Makkah names the wider town; that the *bā'* and *mīm* interchange in the dialectal history of the name; that Bakkah is connected to *bakka* in the sense of crowding or breaking, because of the throngs that press there or the tyrants the House breaks. None of these etymologies is certain; the identification of the place is not in serious dispute within the tradition. Some modern comparative notes point to "Baca" in Psalm 84 as a possible echo of an old sanctuary-name; that parallel is suggestive and contested, and should not be leaned on as proof. The Qur'an's own claim is geographical and liturgical, not dependent on the Psalm.

**"A blessed sanctuary."** *Mubārakan.* Blessed — the site is a locus of *barakah*: increase, permanence, the kind of good that multiplies beyond ordinary measure. The blessing is not tourism. It is the quality the rites and the history and the divine appointment put into the place.

**"And a guide for all people."** *Wa-hudan li-l-ʿālamīn.*

The supplied translation's half-bracketed "all" before "people" matches the force of *al-ʿālamīn* — the worlds, all peoples, throngs of beings. The House is not guidance only for Arabs, or only for the residents of the Ḥijāz. It is named as guidance for the worlds — continuous with Abraham's prayer that the House be a place of security and that a messenger be raised from its people (2:125–129), and continuous with the *qiblah* change that made this House the prayer-direction of the final community (2:142–150).

**Why this verse is here, after the Abraham argument.** The People of the Book claimed Abraham. The surah has answered with Abraham's *ḥanīf* monotheism (3:67, 3:95). Now it answers with Abraham's House. If you follow Abraham, the House he was commanded to raise is not optional scenery. It is part of what following him means. Ibn Kathīr makes this point sharply: they claim his religion and do not make ḥajj to the House he built.

**Bring it into the present.** Sacred geography is easy to sentimentalise and easy to commercialise. Verse 96 does neither. It makes a claim of primacy and universality that obliges. A House that is *hudan li-l-ʿālamīn* is not a heritage site. It is a direction for prayer, a destination for pilgrimage, and a standard against which later sanctuaries and later nationalisms are measured. The modern temptation to make every local shrine equal to this House, or to make this House a brand, both miss the verse. It is first. It is for mankind. It is blessed. It guides.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 97

**"In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe. Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people. And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation."**

In simple terms: in it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it is safe. Pilgrimage to the House is a duty God has laid on people who are able to make the way to it. And whoever disbelieves — God is free of need of all the worlds.

**Four clauses, each a pillar, and the verse is one of the legal foundations of Islam.**

**"In it are clear signs."** *Fīhi āyātun bayyināt.* Clear signs — evidences that mark the House as what the surah has claimed: Abrahamic, appointed, blessed. The tradition enumerates many (the Ḥajar, Zamzam, Ṣafā and Marwah, the security of the ḥaram, the destruction of the Elephant army in the year of the Prophet's birth, and others). The verse itself immediately specifies one.

**"And the standing-place of Abraham."** *Maqāmu Ibrāhīm.*

The *Maqām* is the stone associated with Abraham's standing — in the dominant narrative, the stone he stood on while raising the walls of the Kaʿbah higher, with Ishmael handing him stones, his footprints left in it. The stone is still taken as a station of prayer after ṭawāf, on the command of 2:125: *wa-ttakhidhū min maqāmi Ibrāhīma muṣallā* — take the standing-place of Abraham as a place of prayer. The supplied translation's "standing-place of Abraham" is exact.

Whether every detail of how the footprint was made is historically recoverable is less important than what the *Maqām* does theologically: it makes Abraham present in the rite. The pilgrim does not only visit a building Abraham built. He prays at a stone Abraham stood on. Memory becomes posture.

**"Whoever enters it should be safe."** *Wa-man dakhalahu kāna āminan.*

Security is both a legal ruling and a observed reality the Qur'an points to. The ḥaram is a sanctuary: game is not to be hunted, trees not cut, blood not shed there in the ordinary way (the specific fiqh is developed in the sunnah and the schools). The Qur'an elsewhere asks whether the Makkans have not seen that the ḥaram is secure while people are snatched from around it (29:67). The Prophet ﷺ, on the day of the Conquest, affirmed that Makkah was made a sanctuary by God on the day He created the heavens and the earth, and that fighting in it had been permitted to him only for a brief hour of that day. The reports are in the ṣaḥīḥ collections.

"Should be safe" in the supplied translation catches the normative force — this is what entry is meant to mean. It is not a promise that no one inside the ḥaram will ever suffer; it is the appointment of the place as sanctuary, with the obligations that follow.

**"Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people."** *Wa-li-Llāhi ʿalā al-nāsi ḥijju al-bayti man istaṭāʿa ilayhi sabīlan.*

This is the Qur'anic foundation of ḥajj as a pillar.

The grammar is worth hearing. *Li-Llāhi ʿalā al-nās* — for God, as a right upon the people. Ḥajj is not a festival the community invented and God endorsed. It is a debt owed *to* God *by* humankind. Then the scope: *man istaṭāʿa ilayhi sabīlan* — whoever has the ability of a way to it.

**What is *istiṭāʿah*?** The verse does not list the conditions. The sunnah and the jurists did. The core agreed sense is the capacity to reach the House: provisions, transport, physical ability, and the safety of the road, with variations among the schools on details (whether a maḥram is required for a woman, how debt interacts with ability, what counts as a safe route in a given age). The partitive and conditional structure matters theologically: the obligation is universal in address (*ʿalā al-nās*) and conditional in application (*man istaṭāʿa*). God does not charge a soul beyond its capacity (2:286); this verse is one of the places that principle becomes concrete.

**"And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of any of His creation."** *Wa-man kafara fa-inna Allāha ghaniyyun ʿani al-ʿālamīn.*

The supplied translation's half-bracketed "any of His creation" renders *ʿani al-ʿālamīn* — free of need of the worlds. Two readings of *man kafara* are classical and compatible:

One: whoever denies the obligation of ḥajj — rejects it as a duty — has disbelieved in that sense, and God is free of need of his rite.

Two: whoever is able and does not perform it is described with the language of *kufr* in some stern hadith (the well-known report "whoever dies without having performed ḥajj, let him die if he wishes as a Jew or a Christian" is debated in authenticity and should not be asserted as a *ṣaḥīḥ* legal verdict without its chains being weighed); the verse's own force is that God's self-sufficiency means the rite does not enrich Him. The pilgrim does not do God a favour. God has given the pilgrim a path.

Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī and others discuss whether leaving ḥajj while affirming its obligation is major disbelief or grave sin; the majority distinction between rejecting the duty (disbelief) and neglecting it out of laziness while affirming it (grave sin) is the safer and more precise reading. The verse's closing clause works either way as theology: God is *ghanī*. He does not need the worlds. The worlds need the House.

**How 96–97 complete the Abraham argument.** Follow Abraham (95). His House is the first House, blessed, guidance for the worlds (96). In it are signs, his standing-place, security; and ḥajj to it is God's right on those who can (97). The creed, the geography, and the rite are one chain. A community that claims Abraham and does not face his House has not finished claiming him.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Most readers will make ḥajj once, if they are able, or will live in the long hope and saving toward it. The verse's daily reach is wider than the once-in-a-lifetime journey.

*Istiṭāʿah* is a useful spiritual category beyond fiqh. What am I actually able to do for God, and what am I calling inability that is really unwillingness? The provisions and the safe road have analogues: the hour I have, the health I have, the freedom I have. Verse 97 does not demand the journey from the person who cannot make it. It does demand honesty about ability.

And the closing clause — God is free of need — is medicine for two opposite diseases. For the proud pilgrim: your ḥajj did not enrich Him. For the despairing absentee: His self-sufficiency means your inability did not impoverish Him either; the door of mercy is not a tax office.

**And the spiritual centre.** The *Maqām* makes the point the whole batch has been circling since 92. Abraham stood on a stone and raised a House. The pilgrim stands where he stood and prays. Spending what one loves, building what one is commanded, facing what one is given as direction — the religion is not an idea. It has footprints.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 98

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Why do you deny the revelations of Allah, when Allah is a Witness to what you do?”"**

In simple terms: say — O People of the Book, why do you deny God's revelations, when God is Witness to what you do?

**The surah resumes direct address, and the tone is prosecutorial again.** After the sanctuary verses, the argument returns to the People of the Book with two "why?" questions (98–99), then pivots to warn the believers (100).

**"Why do you deny the revelations of Allah."** *Lima takfurūna bi-āyāti Allāh.*

*Āyāt* are signs and verses — the revelations themselves, and the evidences that attend them. *Kufr* here is rejection, covering, denial. The question is not seeking information. It is the Qur'an's form of indictment: given what you have, why this?

**"When Allah is a Witness to what you do."** *Wa-Allāhu shahīdun ʿalā mā taʿmalūn.*

Witness returns. The batch began under the shadow of the prophets' covenant sealed by witness (3:81). It now places God as *shahīd* over the acts of denial. The one who rejects the *āyāt* does so on a stage already occupied by a Witness. There is no private denial. The grammar *ʿalā mā taʿmalūn* — over what you do — makes the witness practical, not abstract. Acts. Not only beliefs.

**Connection to the surah's running charge.** The surah has said *wa-antum tashhadūn* (3:70), *wa-hum yaʿlamūn* (3:71, 75, 78), *shahidū anna al-rasūla ḥaqq* (3:86). Denial after witness is the pattern. Verse 98 compresses it into one question.

**Bring it into the present.** The verse is addressed to the People of the Book in a specific Madinan conflict. Its transferable force is the structure: rejection of clear signs, under the gaze of a Witness who does not miss acts. Anyone who has received light and covers it is inside the structure, whatever their census label. The surah's own discipline still applies — a party, an act, not a people.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 99

**"Say, “O People of the Book! Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah—striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, while you are witnesses ˹to its truth˺? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.”"**

In simple terms: say — O People of the Book, why do you turn believers away from God's path, trying to make it look crooked, when you are yourselves witnesses? God is never unaware of what you do.

**The second "why," and it names a different act.** Verse 98 was denial. Verse 99 is obstruction — turning others away.

**"Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah."** *Lima taṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāhi man āmana.*

*Ṣadd* is to bar, to turn aside, to block a path. The target is *man āmana* — the one who has believed. The act is not only private unbelief. It is active interference with someone else's faith.

**"Striving to make it appear crooked."** *Tabghūnahā ʿiwajan.*

The supplied translation's half-bracketed "appear" is a careful gloss. *ʿIwaj* is crookedness, deviation from the straight. *Tabghūnahā ʿiwajan* is to want it crooked, to seek it as bent — to desire that the path be seen, or made, as something twisted. The Qur'an uses this collocation elsewhere (7:45; 11:19; 14:3): those who bar from God's way and want it crooked. The half-bracketed "appear" emphasises the public, rhetorical dimension — making the straight look bent — which is often how the obstruction works. The Arabic also covers wanting the path itself to be bent. Both forces are live.

**"While you are witnesses to its truth."** *Wa-antum shuhadā'.*

The supplied translation's half-bracketed "to its truth" makes explicit what the bare *shuhadā'* implies in context: you are witnesses — you know, you have seen, you are in a position to testify — and you obstruct anyway. The witness theme of the surah reaches another peak. Witnesses who block the path they could attest to are in a worse position than outsiders who never saw.

**"And Allah is never unaware of what you do."** *Wa-mā Allāhu bi-ghāfilin ʿammā taʿmalūn.*

Not *shahīd* only, as in 98. Now the negation of *ghafah* — heedlessness, inattention. God is not unaware. The pairing is deliberate: Witness (98), never unaware (99). The two verses close every escape — no unwatched denial, no unwatched obstruction.

**Reported backdrop.** The tafsir tradition connects these verses to Madinan efforts by some among the People of the Book to unsettle new Muslims, revive old tribal hostilities (the Aws and Khazraj memory appears in the next verses' occasion literature), and make the new path look confused or crooked. Specific names and incidents vary across reports; the pattern the verse names does not depend on any single anecdote.

**Now into the present.** Obstruction with the appearance of care is the modern form. The path is made to look crooked by selective quotation, by scandal amplified beyond its measure, by the confident voice that says "I used to be on that path and I can tell you what it really is." Verse 99's conditions are specific: the obstructer is a witness, and the target is a believer, and the aim is crookedness. Applying it requires the same precision the surah has demanded all along. It is not a licence to call every critic an obstructer. It is a diagnosis of a particular bad faith — knowing, and blocking anyway.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 100

**"O believers! If you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture, they would turn you back from belief to disbelief."**

In simple terms: O you who believe — if you obey a group of those who were given the Scripture, they will turn you back after your belief into disbelievers.

**The address pivots.** For two verses the Prophet ﷺ was told to say things *to* the People of the Book. Now the believers are addressed directly about a party of them. The surah protects its own community without withdrawing its earlier precision: the danger is *farīq* — a group — not the whole.

**"O believers."** *Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū.* The ordinary Qur'anic call that marks a community instruction. What follows is not information about others. It is a warning about the self.

**"If you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture."** *In tuṭīʿū farīqan min alladhīna ūtū al-kitāb.*

*Tuṭīʿū* — if you obey, if you yield, if you give your compliance. The verb is about following as authority. *Farīqan* — a party, a faction. The surah's precision-word returns. Not "the Jews." Not "the Christians." A group from among those given the Book.

**"They would turn you back from belief to disbelief."** *Yaruddūkum baʿda īmānikum kāfirīn.*

*Yaruddūkum* — they would turn you back, return you. *Baʿda īmānikum* — after your belief. *Kāfirīn* — as disbelievers. The risk named is not mild confusion. It is reversion — the undoing of the faith already reached. The *baʿda* is the same moral marker we have been tracking: after light, the fall is a different kind of fall.

**Reported occasion.** Early reports connect this warning to specific Madinan moments when some among the People of the Book tried to re-ignite the old hatred between Aws and Khazraj, or to pull individual Muslims back toward former positions. Ibn ʿAbbās and others are cited in the *asbāb* material; details vary. The verse's legal-spiritual force does not depend on recovering the exact conversation. It depends on the structure: obedience to a faction that wants you reversed will reverse you.

**What the verse is not.** It is not a ban on learning from the People of the Book, or on kindness to them, or on the just dealing 60:8 protects, or on the marriage and food permissions of 5:5, or on the praise of the upright among them at 3:113–115 and 3:199. The verse is about *obedience* (*iṭāʿah*) to a *party* whose direction of pull is toward *kufr*. A community that cannot tell the difference between scholarship, neighbourliness, alliance in justice, and religious obedience to a reversing faction has lost the surah's own distinctions.

**Connection to 3:72 and 3:69.** Earlier the surah described a party that said "believe at the face of the day and reject at its end" (3:72), and a wish from some of the People of the Book that the believers be led astray (3:69). Verse 100 is the believers' side of that threat: if you yield, the wish succeeds. The surah has now warned both directions — do not stage the reversal (71–72), and do not submit to those who want it (100).

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The modern form of *iṭāʿah* to a reversing faction is not usually formal conversion under a Madinan elder. It is the slow transfer of authority over what counts as true, shameful, possible, or kind — away from revelation and toward a circle that treats revelation as the problem. The faction may not carry a scripture. It may carry a feed, a faculty, a brand of sophistication, a family pressure, a political absolute. The test verse 100 supplies is directional: if yielding to them turns you back from what you believed, the yielding is the error the verse named.

And the protection the verse offers is not isolation. It is the recovered right to choose whom one obeys in matters of faith. *Farīqan* — a party. The surah does not say "trust no one who was given a Book." It says: do not obey the party that will undo you.

**And the spiritual centre of the batch's ending.** Verses 91–97 took the reader through ransom, spending, food law, Abraham, and the House. Verses 98–100 return to the conflict and end on a warning inward. The House is for the worlds. The community that faces it can still lose itself by obeying the wrong faction. Ability to reach Makkah (97) and ability to refuse a reversing obedience (100) are both *istiṭāʿah* of a kind. God is free of need of the worlds either way. The believers are not free of need of vigilance.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses move through three terrains that the surah refuses to keep separate: the end, the hand, and the House.

**Verses 91–92 are an economics lesson in two tenses.** Gold offered after death on disbelief does not ransom (91). Spending in life from what one loves is the road to *birr* (92). The same metal, opposite moral location. Abū Ṭalḥah's garden is the living footnote. The earthful of gold that cannot buy exit, and the loved property that can buy *birr*, are one theology of time.

**Verses 93–95 are the food-law and Abraham argument.** All food was lawful to the Children of Israel except what Israel (Jacob) forbade himself before the Torah (93). Whoever invents lies about God after that is the wrongdoer (94). God has told the truth — follow Abraham the *ḥanīf*, who was no polytheist (95). The challenge "bring the Torah and read it" is the method; Abraham is the destination.

**Verses 96–97 plant the House.** The first House set up for humankind is at Bakkah, blessed, a guidance for the worlds (96). In it are clear signs and Abraham's standing-place; entry is safety; ḥajj is God's right on those who can find a way; God is free of need of the worlds (97). Creed becomes geography; geography becomes rite; the *Maqām* makes Abraham's footprint a place of prayer.

**Verses 98–100 return to conflict and turn the warning inward.** Why do you deny the revelations, when God is Witness (98)? Why do you bar believers from the path, wanting it crooked, when you are witnesses — and God is never unaware (99)? O believers, if you obey a party of those given the Book, they will turn you back after belief into disbelief (100). The surah's precision-word *farīq* is still in force. The danger is a party and an obedience, not a people and a presence.

**Threads carried and extended.**

- **Witness** reaches from the prophets' covenant (3:81) through denial after witness (3:86) to God as *shahīd* (3:98) and the obstructers as *shuhadā'* (3:99).
- **Root *n-ṣ-r*** closes the disbelief-arc with *mā lahum min nāṣirīn* (3:91), continuous with 3:52, 3:56, 3:81.
- ***Baʿda*** — after — continues as the surah's moral clock: after belief (90, 100), after the challenge (94), after death as the cut-off (91).
- **Abraham** binds the batch: follow his *millah* (95), face his House (96), pray at his *Maqām* (97), having already been told he was *ḥanīf muslim* and no polytheist (3:67).
- **Spending what is loved** (92) sits next to Jacob's self-restriction from what he loved (93, in the reported detail) and next to Abraham's whole life of spending what he loved. The batch is quietly a single lesson in attachment released for God.

**Where the supplied translation has chosen, it has been flagged.** Half-bracketed additions — "O Prophet," "of worship," "all," "appear," "to its truth," "any of His creation," "true" — are interpretive aids, mostly exact and helpful. "Donate some of what you cherish" for *tunfiqū mimmā tuḥibbūn* is warm contemporary English for a phrase whose classical centre is spending from what one loves; "righteousness" for *birr* is serviceable and narrower than the Qur'an's own range at 2:177. "Yield" for *tuṭīʿū* at 100 catches compliance well. None of these is distortion; each is a choice.

**The verse to leave with is 92**, in the light of 91. An earthful of gold will not ransom a death on disbelief. A piece of what you love, spent now, is on the road to *birr*. The religion has always known which tense of wealth matters.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them.

Five places in this batch required particular care.

**The first is 3:91 and the efficacy of deeds.** The verse refuses ransom after death on disbelief. I have stated that firmly, and I have refused the inflation of the verse into general contempt for human goodness or into despair about the living. The Ibn Judʿān report illuminates the principle; it is not a private verdict the reader is authorised to extend to every generous person outside Islam.

**The second is 3:92 and *birr*.** *Birr* is larger than a single English word; 2:177 remains the Qur'an's own definition. The verse names a condition for attaining the fullness of that good, not a cancellation of everyone who has not yet given away a house. Abū Ṭalḥah is the authentic living tafsir; other companion reports are secondary.

**The third is 3:93 and Israel’s self-prohibition.** Israel as Jacob is the dominant reading. The sciatica-and-camel details are transmitted reports with *Isrā'īliyyāt* colouring and are not in the text. The verse contests a specific historical claim in a Madinan argument; it is not a licence for contempt toward Jewish food law as a lived system.

**The fourth is 3:96–97, Bakkah, and ḥajj.** Bakkah is Makkah; the first-House claim is anchored by the Abū Dharr hadith among other evidence; Adam-as-first-builder is traditional narrative held more loosely than 2:127. On *man kafara* at the end of 97, I have preferred the majority distinction between rejecting the obligation (disbelief) and neglecting it while affirming it (grave sin), and I have noted that God is *ghanī* either way.

**The fifth is 3:100.** The warning is against *obedience* to a *party* that pulls toward reversion. It is not a ban on contact, justice, learning, or the Qur'an's own praise of righteous People of the Book. The surah's *farīq* precision is the guardrail.

**On the translation.** Half-bracketed interpretive aids are generally helpful and have been noted where they add force the Arabic leaves implicit ("appear," "to its truth," "of worship," "any of His creation"). "Cherish" for *tuḥibbūn* is warm and accurate. "Righteousness" for *birr* is narrower than 2:177's range — flagged. "Yield" at 100 is a strong reading of *tuṭīʿū*. The translation is not wrong; it has chosen, and the reader is entitled to know what was chosen.

Two boundaries observed throughout. No living individual has been placed under 91's ransom-refusal or 100's reversion; the verses describe states and conditional trajectories. And no reading in this batch closes the door of 3:89 on a person who still turns and mends. Death on disbelief is one subject. Life still available is another.
