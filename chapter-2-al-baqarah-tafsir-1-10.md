# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 1–10

We are now entering the opening passage of Surah al-Baqarah, the longest surah of the Qur'an and, by the agreement of most scholars, a Madani surah revealed over roughly a decade after the Prophet's ﷺ migration to Madinah. Its first twenty verses are widely regarded as among the earliest Madani revelation, and they function as the doorway to everything that follows. Before any law, any story or any argument, Allah divides humanity into three groups and shows how each of them meets the same message: the believers, described in verses 2 to 5; the entrenched disbelievers, described in verses 6 and 7; and the hypocrites, whose description begins in verse 8 and runs to verse 20. Almost the entire surah afterwards is an elaboration of that opening. The surah takes its name from the story of the cow in verses 67 to 73, though it is not "about" that story; the name is a label, as the names of surahs generally are. The ten verses we are about to examine move from the mysterious letters that open the surah, to the Qur'an's own claim about itself, to the five qualities that define a person of taqwa, and then to the two ways human beings fail: defiant rejection and religious pretence.

## Verse 1

**"Alif-Lãm-Mĩm."**

In simple terms, the surah opens with three Arabic letters recited individually — *Alif*, *Lam*, *Mim* — not as a word and not as a sentence. They are part of the revealed text and are recited exactly as they appear. Their precise meaning is one of the genuinely unsettled questions of Qur'anic interpretation, and a faithful tafsir must say so rather than pretend otherwise.

These are known as the *huruf muqatta'at*, the "disjoined letters," and they open twenty-nine surahs of the Qur'an. Grammatically they are read as the *names* of letters — you say "Alif, Lam, Mim," not "Alm" — and in Arabic parsing each is treated as an indeclinable unit. The early grammarians and commentators asked what their grammatical role is, since a sentence normally needs a subject and a predicate. Al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan discuss several possibilities: that each is the subject of an omitted predicate, or the predicate of an omitted subject — so that the sense is something like "These letters — this Book is composed of them." That reading is attractive because the very next verse announces the Book, and it is a view many later commentators found convincing.

On their meaning, the scholars of the early generations and the classical mufassirun held a range of positions, and it is important to lay them out honestly rather than pick one and hide the rest.

The first and most cautious position is that these letters belong to the *mutashabihat* — the verses whose full meaning is known only to Allah — and that we recite them, believe they are revelation, and entrust their meaning to Him. Statements to this effect are transmitted from several of the earliest authorities, and the principle behind them is the Qur'an's own division of revelation into the clear (*muhkam*) and the ambiguous (*mutashabih*) in 3:7.

The second position is that they are names by which Allah named these surahs — identifying marks of the surahs, in the way a thing is given a name.

The third position is that each letter points to one of Allah's names or attributes. Under this reading, the *Alif* has been said to stand for *Allah*, the *Lam* for *al-Latif* (the Subtle) or *al-Latif bi-khalqih*, and the *Mim* for *al-Malik* (the Sovereign) or *al-Majid* (the Glorious). Al-Razi collected dozens of such suggestions in his commentary, and variations of them appear in the work of al-Suyuti and others. These are possibilities that the letters, taken as isolated symbols, can support; they are not meanings established by a chain of transmission from the Prophet ﷺ, so we cannot affirm any particular one of them with certainty. A statement often quoted in this connection — that "every Book has a secret, and the secret of the Qur'an is the opening letters of the surahs" — is widely circulated but its attribution to any particular Companion is not firmly established.

The fourth position, and the one that most substantive commentators have found most satisfying, connects these letters to the inimitability of the Qur'an. On this reading, the surah opens by placing before the Arabs the raw material of their own language — the very letters with which they composed their celebrated poetry and oratory — and then immediately presents a Book built from those same letters that they cannot match. The point is sharpened later in this very surah, in the challenge of verse 23: "produce a surah like it." Al-Zamakhshari argued for something close to this: that the letters are mentioned to arrest the listener's attention and to signal that what follows is made of the alphabet they themselves use, yet is beyond their capacity. Al-Razi and al-Shawkani both give this reading serious weight.

Two observations are worth noting alongside this. First, the letters used across all twenty-nine openings number fourteen — exactly half of the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet — a pattern the classical commentators noticed, though what should be concluded from it is not agreed upon. Second, the Arabic used is the clearest and most eloquent Arabic of its time, which is precisely what makes the challenge in verse 23 meaningful.

Modern discussion of these letters goes in several directions, and we should treat each according to its evidence. Some contemporary Muslim writers have built elaborate numerical schemes on them — the most famous being the claim that the letters encode a "nineteen-based" mathematical pattern. Mainstream Islamic scholarship has overwhelmingly rejected such claims as methodologically unsound, and we should note that the last of them led its proponent to reject established parts of the religion, which is a warning in itself. Western academic scholarship has offered its own hypotheses: that the letters may be abbreviations of divine names, or remnants of the *basmalah*, or scribal and liturgical markers used to group manuscripts, or devices connected to the recitation and structuring of the text. These remain hypotheses. They are not established facts, and none of them is necessary to make sense of the letters. What can be said with confidence is that the letters are part of the revealed text as it was transmitted by mass transmission (*tawatur*), that they are recited in prayer exactly as they appear, and that the Qur'an itself did not explain them.

Now what should you do with this verse, practically? Recite it. The recitation of these letters is an act of worship for which you are rewarded, whether or not you can explain them. And there is a posture being taught here, which matters more than any theory: the Qur'an opens some of its surahs in a way that stops a human being at the very first syllable and reminds him that this speech is not his own. A reader who meets the Qur'an expecting to master it, to mine it for proof-texts, or to reduce it to data is in the wrong posture from the first verse. The correct posture is the one of a student before something greater than himself — listening before interpreting. In a time when everyone has instant access to the text and to a thousand opinions about it, that first lesson may be the one we most need.

## Verse 2

**"This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺,"**

In simple terms, Allah declares that the Qur'an is a genuine, complete, written revelation about which there can be no real doubt, and that it functions as guidance — but guidance that actually lands and benefits those who approach God with reverent awareness of Him.

The verse opens with *dhalika*, a demonstrative normally used for something distant — "that" rather than "this." Rhetoricians noted the effect immediately: the far demonstrative is being used for something present in order to indicate elevation of rank. It is as if the Book is being pointed to as something exalted and far above ordinary speech. The same device appears at the close of the passage in verse 5 with *ula'ika*.

*Al-kitab* comes from the root meaning to write and to gather together, and a *kitab* is a complete, collected, written composition. The great majority of commentators understood it here as the Qur'an itself, and al-Tabari preferred that reading. Because the word is definite, the sense is not "a scripture" in general but *the* Book — the one already known and promised.

Then comes *la rayba fihi*, "no doubt in it." Two things about this phrase reward attention. Grammatically, it is the strongest form of negation available in Arabic — the *la* of absolute categorical negation, denying doubt of every kind, not merely of one sort. Semantically, *rayb* is not neutral uncertainty; it is doubt tinged with anxiety and suspicion, the unsettledness that makes a person unable to rely on something. So the claim is that this Book contains nothing that should leave a person inwardly unsettled about it.

But an obvious objection arises: plenty of people *do* doubt the Qur'an. The commentators answered in complementary ways. Some said the negation is a statement about the Book itself — that it is intrinsically free of defect, contradiction or obscurity that would justify doubt, which is precisely the claim the challenge of inimitability in verse 23 tests. Others, including al-Zamakhshari, understood it as information carrying the force of instruction: "there is to be no doubt about it" — meaning, do not doubt it. Both readings are legitimate, and together they say: the Book gives no grounds for doubt, and you are not to entertain any.

The third phrase is the one with the most weight: *hudan lil-muttaqin*, "a guidance for those mindful of Allah." Here the Qur'an makes a claim and a condition in the same breath. It is guidance — but guidance in the sense that benefits and delivers is for *al-muttaqin*. This raised a well-known question, because elsewhere the Qur'an calls itself "a guidance for humanity" (2:185) and "a guidance and good news for the believers" (2:97), and the Prophet ﷺ is described as "a warner to all the worlds" (25:1). The standard reconciliation, given by al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and later authorities, is that the Qur'an is guidance for everyone in the sense of showing the way — it lays out the road unmistakably, so that no one can claim ignorance. But the guidance that actually takes root in a person, that produces faith and leads to success, requires a receptive heart, and that receptiveness is *taqwa*. This is the same distinction the scholars drew in explaining al-Fatihah's *ihdina*: the guidance of direction is universal; the guidance of enablement is particular. Note also that *hudan* is indefinite, and an indefinite noun in this position in Arabic conveys greatness — "a guidance" of a tremendous kind.

So what is *taqwa*? The root *w-q-y* means to guard and to shield. Taqwa is placing a protective shield between yourself and the displeasure of Allah by doing what He commanded and avoiding what He prohibited. The early authorities described it in vivid, usable ways. When 'Umar ibn al-Khattab asked Ubayy ibn Ka'b about taqwa, Ubayy asked him in turn whether he had ever walked a path lined with thorns. 'Umar said he had. Ubayy asked what he did. He said he rolled up his garments and moved carefully. Ubayy said: "That is taqwa." Talq ibn Habib, one of the early scholars of Basra, gave the fuller definition: "Taqwa is that you act in obedience to Allah, upon a light from Allah, hoping for the reward of Allah; and that you leave disobedience to Allah, upon a light from Allah, fearing the punishment of Allah." Others among the Salaf summed it as: fulfilling what Allah has enjoined and avoiding what He has prohibited. Two features of these definitions deserve highlighting: taqwa is active, not merely a feeling; and it is exercised "upon a light" — that is, on the basis of knowledge, not anxiety or scrupulosity.

Notice the connection back to al-Fatihah. There the believer prays, "Guide us along the Straight Path." Here, at the opening of the surah that immediately follows, Allah answers: *this* is that guidance — *hudan*. The structure of the Qur'an's own arrangement makes the point: the prayer of the servant and the response of the Lord are placed side by side.

Now bring this into your own life, because this verse is the answer to a question many people quietly carry: "I read the Qur'an and nothing happens." The verse locates the obstacle not in the Book but in the reader's posture. Taqwa is the condition. A person who opens the Qur'an to win an argument, to find a verse that supports what he has already decided, or to collect merit without any intention of changing, is not in the posture the verse describes — and the guidance does not land. The practical test is simple and searching: when you read a verse that asks something difficult of you, do you feel addressed, or do you feel like an observer? The muttaqi feels addressed. That is why the same page that bores one reader breaks another open.

## Verse 3

**"who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,"**

In simple terms, having named the Book and its audience, Allah now describes those people of taqwa by five qualities, and this verse gives the first, third and fourth of them: they believe in what is beyond their senses, they establish the prayer, and they give out of what Allah provided them.

The first quality is belief in *al-ghayb*, the unseen. *Ghayb* is whatever is absent from human perception — everything that you cannot see, hear or measure but which Allah has informed you of. That includes Allah Himself, His angels, His revealed Books, His messengers, the Day of Resurrection, Paradise and the Fire, the recording of deeds, and every matter that reaches us only through revelation. The early authorities explained it plainly: al-Tabari transmits explanations to the effect that the *ghayb* is what is hidden from the servants and what they do not witness.

Two boundaries have to be drawn here, because this phrase is misunderstood in opposite directions. The first boundary: absolute knowledge of the unseen belongs to Allah alone. The Qur'an says, "With Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them except Him" (6:59), and "Say: None in the heavens and the earth knows the unseen except Allah" (27:65). So believing in the unseen does not mean claiming to know it; it means believing what the Knower of the unseen has told you about it. The second boundary: because the unseen is only knowable through revelation, Islam closes the door on every independent claim to hidden knowledge. The Prophet ﷺ warned against going to fortune-tellers and soothsayers, and an authentic narration recorded by Ahmad and Abu Dawud states that whoever goes to a diviner and believes what he says has disbelieved in what was revealed to Muhammad ﷺ. Belief in the unseen is therefore not credulity; it is trust in a specific, verified source — revelation — and a refusal to trust unverifiable substitutes like astrology, clairvoyance or conspiracy.

The classic definition of faith comes from the well-known hadith of Jibril, recorded by Muslim from 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, in which the Prophet ﷺ defined *iman* as believing in Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, the Last Day, and in the divine decree, both its good and its bitter. Every item on that list except Allah is unseen — which is why the very first quality of the muttaqin is exactly this.

The second quality in this verse is *yuqimuna al-salah*, "they establish the prayer." The Arabic verb is significant: the Qur'an does not say they *perform* the prayer, it says they *establish* it — *iqama*. That verb carries the sense of setting something up on its proper footing and maintaining it: performing the prayer with its conditions, its pillars, its humility, at its times, and keeping it up consistently rather than sporadically. The early commentators glossed *iqama* as completing the prayer's bowing, prostration, recitation and attentiveness. The importance of this quality is underlined in the Sunnah: the Prophet ﷺ said, in a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasa'i, that the first thing a servant will be called to account for on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer, and that if it is sound, the rest of his deeds are sound. A related narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi and others describes the prayer as the covenant between a person and Islam.

The third quality is *wa mimma razaqnahum yunfiqun*, "and they spend out of what We provided them." Almost every word here is doing work, and the commentators lingered over each.

*Mimma* — "out of what" — is partitive. They spend a *portion*, not everything. This quietly corrects two errors at once: it refutes the idea that faith requires total renunciation of wealth, and it refutes the miser's postponement of giving until he has "enough." You give from what you already have.

*Razzaqnahum* — "We provided them" — attributes the wealth to Allah, not to the spender. This does two things to the giver's soul. It removes pride: you are handing over what was handed to you, so there is no room for condescension or for reminding people of your generosity. And it removes fear: the One who gave you this can give you more. The hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim captures it: "Spend, O son of Adam, and I will spend on you." And in Sahih Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ said that charity does not decrease wealth.

*Yunfiqun* — "they spend" — is general. The commentators differed over whether it refers specifically to the obligatory zakah or to spending in general, including voluntary charity and maintenance of one's family. Since zakah in its detailed form was legislated in Madinah while this is among the earliest Madani revelation, some held that the word here primarily covers voluntary and general spending and that zakah entered under it once it was prescribed; others held that the general wording includes zakah and more. The stronger view is that the wording is general and covers both: the obligation and the supererogatory, the public gift and the quiet one.

There is a striking feature of the Arabic root worth noticing, because it links this verse to the hypocrites who are described a few verses later. The root *n-f-q* carries the sense of something exiting or passing through — which is why maintenance is called *nafaqah* and why a tunnel is called a *nafaq*. From the same family comes *munafiq*, the hypocrite, explained by the early lexicographers as one who enters Islam through one opening and exits through another, like the jerboa slipping out of its burrow. The connection is a quiet piece of moral architecture in this passage: the believers let their *wealth* exit for the sake of Allah, while the hypocrites let their *faith* exit.

Notice finally the architecture of the whole list in verses 3 and 4: belief in the unseen, then the prayer, then spending, then belief in the revelation, then certainty about the Hereafter. The two beliefs bracket the two deeds. Creed is not an appendix to practice, and practice is not a substitute for creed; the person of taqwa is held together by both.

What does this look like today? Belief in the unseen is tested precisely where nobody is watching: the expense report you could inflate, the work you could claim without doing, the promise you could quietly break. Prayer, when "established" rather than merely performed, is what interrupts a day that would otherwise run entirely on autopilot. And spending out of provision reframes money itself — from something you own to something you are administering. Someone who gives regularly and quietly is not merely being generous; he is repeatedly testifying that the wealth was never really his.

## Verse 4

**"and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter."**

In simple terms, the people of taqwa also believe in the Qur'an that was revealed to Muhammad ﷺ, and in the genuine revelations that came before him, and — with an emphasis the verse itself makes — they are absolutely certain about the life to come.

The first half of the verse states a requirement that surprises many people, Muslim and non-Muslim alike: belief in previous revelation is a condition of Islamic faith. The Torah given to Musa, the Zabur given to Dawud, the Injil given to 'Isa, and the scriptures given to earlier messengers are all affirmed by the Qur'an as genuine revelation from Allah in their original form. This is not a concession; it is repeated throughout the Qur'an, as in 2:136 and 4:136, and a Muslim is not a Muslim who rejects any messenger of Allah.

But this belief has a shape, and the shape matters. Islam does not ask a Muslim to affirm the texts as they exist today; it asks him to affirm the revelation as Allah sent it, and it makes the Qur'an the criterion over what came before — *muhayminan 'alayh*, in the language of 5:48. The Sunnah supplies the practical rule in a remarkably direct way. Abu Hurayrah reported that the People of the Book used to recite the Torah in Hebrew and then explain it in Arabic to the Muslims, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "Do not believe the People of the Book, and do not deny them; rather say: 'We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to you, and our God and your God is one.'" That narration, recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, is built on this very verse — which is to say the verse is not abstract theology, it was a live instruction about how to behave when someone reads you a passage from an earlier scripture. The rule: affirm the divine origin, suspend judgement on specific details you cannot verify, and refer the matter to what Allah has sent down to you.

Within Islamic scholarship there is a genuine and well-known disagreement about how to understand the condition of earlier scriptures. Some classical authorities held that the previous texts underwent *tahrif al-nass*, corruption of the text itself. Others — among them, notably, Fakhr al-Din al-Razi — argued for *tahrif al-ma'na*, meaning that the texts were substantially preserved but their meaning was distorted in interpretation and practice. Both positions exist in the tradition, both are held by credible scholars, and the honest thing is to state the disagreement rather than flatten it. What all Muslims agree on is that the Qur'an is the final and authoritative criterion, and that whatever in earlier tradition agrees with it is accepted and whatever contradicts it is not, while the messengers themselves are all honoured and believed in.

The second half of the verse shifts grammatical gear, and the shift is the point. Everything so far has been stated with verbs: *yu'minun*, *yuqimun*, *yunfiqun* — they believe, they establish, they spend. Here the verse becomes a nominal sentence: *wa bil-akhirati hum yuqinun* — "and in the Hereafter, it is they who are certain." In Arabic, moving from a verb sentence to a nominal sentence, and fronting the pronoun *hum*, produces emphasis and, in this position, restriction: certainty about the Hereafter belongs to them in a way it belongs to no one else. The verse is not merely adding a fifth item to a list; it is singling this one out.

Why? Because certainty about the Hereafter is what converts belief into a life. The word *yaqin* denotes knowledge that has settled — not a probability you are leaning towards, but something established in the heart to the point that it orders your decisions. The Qur'an grades it: *'ilm al-yaqin* (the knowledge of certainty), *'ayn al-yaqin* (the eye of certainty) and *haqq al-yaqin* (the truth of certainty), in Surah al-Takathur, verses 5 to 7. And *al-akhirah* — the "last" — is called that because it comes after this first life.

This quality also closes a circle opened back in al-Fatihah. There the believer prays to the One who is "Master of the Day of Judgment." Here, the muttaqin are defined as the people for whom that Day is not a doctrine they hold but a certainty they live by.

Now consider what certainty about the Hereafter does in practice, because this is where the verse stops being abstract. A person certain about the Hereafter can afford to lose money, because the ledger is not closed in this life. He can turn down the promotion that requires dishonesty, because the cost-benefit calculation now includes a term no employer can see. He can forgive the person who will never repay him, because the account is with Allah. He can spend on a stranger, because the return is not scheduled for this quarter. He can bear the loss of reputation for a principle, because the audience that ultimately matters has not yet given its verdict. Conversely, most of the moral compromises people make are, underneath, a failure of *yaqin* rather than of information — a person knows the Day is coming and simply does not live as though he is certain. The remedy the Qur'an gives for that is not more argument; it is the reminders, the prayer, and the company of people who are themselves certain.

## Verse 5

**"It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful."**

In simple terms, this verse closes the description that began at verse 2: the people just described are the ones who truly stand upon guidance from their Lord, and they are the ones who genuinely succeed.

Grammatically, verses 2 to 5 form a single sentence. "This is the Book... guidance for those mindful of Allah" is the opening; "those who believe in the unseen..." up to "the Hereafter" is one long descriptive clause; and this verse is the predicate that the whole description has been building towards. That structure is itself part of the meaning, and it produces one of the most beautiful circles in the Qur'an. The sentence begins with guidance (*hudan lil-muttaqin*), passes through the qualities that define the muttaqin, and concludes: "it is they who are upon guidance from their Lord." Guidance leads to taqwa; taqwa produces these qualities; and these qualities bring a person back to a firmer, deeper guidance. Guidance is not a one-way transaction; it is a circuit, and every step taken on it earns the next one. This is why the Qur'an elsewhere says, "Those who strive in Our cause, We will surely guide them to Our ways" (29:69), and why the hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim states that whoever draws near to Allah a hand-span, Allah draws near to him an arm's length.

The verse opens with *ula'ika*, the plural demonstrative of distance — "those." As with *dhalika* in verse 2, the far demonstrative is used for people who are present, and the classical rhetoricians read the device as one of elevation: they are being pointed out as having attained a high station.

Then comes a small preposition that carries real weight: *'ala hudan* — "upon guidance." Not "with" guidance, not "towards" guidance, but *upon* it. The image is of being firmly mounted or firmly established: they are standing on the guidance, carried by it, positioned above it in the sense of being supported by it. Guidance is pictured as solid ground rather than as a passing idea. It is worth putting this next to the two groups that follow in this surah — those who are sealed in verse 7 and those who "purchase misguidance" in verse 16 — to see that the Qur'an pictures each group's relationship to truth as a physical posture.

The guidance is then attributed to their *Lord*: *min rabbihim*. Given everything the word *rabb* means — owner, master, but above all the One who nurtures and brings to completion — the phrasing is tender. They are guided by the One who has been personally caring for them all along.

The final phrase is *wa ula'ika hum al-muflihun*, "and it is they who are the successful." *Al-falah* comes from a root meaning to split or to till, which is why a farmer is a *fallah* — one who splits the soil so it yields. In the vocabulary of the Qur'an, *falah* means attaining what is sought and being saved from what is feared: it holds gain and safety together. That is why the same sentence pattern appears at the close of so many passages in this surah — "there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" (2:38, 2:62, 2:112, 2:262, 2:274, 2:277). The successful person is not merely the one who gains; he is the one who gains *and* is safe.

The construction *hum al-muflihun* — an independent pronoun followed by a definite noun — is a form of restriction in Arabic. It says: the truly successful are these, i.e., real success belongs to them. This claim needs to be handled carefully, because it is easily misread. It does not mean that believers never prosper in worldly terms or that others never do. History is full of believers who were poor and persecuted, and of unbelievers who were wealthy and powerful. What the verse restricts is *falah* in its complete sense: the success that survives death. A person who gains the whole world and loses the Hereafter has, in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, lost. And a believer who struggles, is mocked, and dies obscure is, by this definition, successful — a claim the Qur'an makes explicitly elsewhere: "Those who are mindful of Allah will be above them on the Day of Resurrection" (2:212).

This is the verse most worth memorising from this passage, because it quietly replaces the definition of success your environment is constantly selling you. Consider what the modern definition consists of — income, visibility, followers, the house, the title, the body — and then hold it against this one: are you upon guidance from your Lord? That question has the advantage of being answerable today, of not depending on anyone's approval, and of remaining true after you die. A person who measures himself by it stops being at the mercy of comparison, because guidance is not a limited resource that someone else's success can take from him.

## Verse 6

**"As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe."**

In simple terms, the surah now turns to a second group — people so entrenched in rejection that warning them and not warning them come to the same thing: they will not believe.

The Arabic verb is *kafaru*, from the root meaning to cover or conceal: the one who disbelieves covers over a truth he recognises. Two features of the wording matter. First, the verb is in the past tense — "those who *disbelieved*" — describing a state that has become settled. Second, the conclusion is in the present-future — "they will not believe" — describing what follows from that state. The combination says: this is a condition that has hardened, not a single mistake.

Who exactly is meant? The commentators differed, and the difference is worth stating. One view, held by a number of classical authorities, is that the verse refers to specific leaders of Quraysh whose hearts Allah knew would never accept faith — men like Abu Jahl, Abu Lahab and their circle, the very people who were later killed at Badr. Reports along these lines are found in the commentaries of al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir. The other view reads the verse as a general principle: anyone about whom Allah knows that he will die in disbelief — for such a person, warning and non-warning are equivalent. These views are not mutually exclusive; the specific can be the first instance of the general. What matters is that no one can identify such a person with certainty, since the final state of a heart is unseen.

The theological question raised here is unavoidable and should not be skipped: if Allah already knows, and has stated, that they will not believe, is the call to them meaningless? The answer given by the mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah rests on a distinction Allah's *knowledge* of a thing is not the same as His *compulsion* of it. Allah knows from eternity what each person will freely choose; His knowledge follows what the person will do, it does not force him to do it. The verse, on this reading, is news about an outcome, not a description of coercion. And crucially, it is not a licence to stop calling people to Allah. The Prophet ﷺ received this verse and then spent the remaining years of his life calling Quraysh, the tribes, the delegations and the neighbouring rulers with undiminished urgency. The narration recorded in the two Sahihs captures his state of heart: "My example and your example is like a man who kindled a fire, and when it lit up everything around it, the moths and other creatures began falling into it, and he tried to hold them back but they overcame him and kept falling in." The Qur'an itself consoles him elsewhere: "Perhaps you will destroy yourself with grief that they do not become believers" (26:3), and "Indeed, you do not guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills" (28:56).

The rhetorical structure of the verse is worth noticing too. "Whether you warn them or you do not warn them" is an exhaustive pairing — it closes off the alternatives completely, so that no qualification is left dangling. And the verb used is *andhara*, to warn, rather than *bashshara*, to give glad tidings. That is deliberate: a message that carries a warning of punishment is appropriately described as a warning.

Now, the contemporary lesson here is sharper than it first appears, and it cuts in two directions.

The first direction is reassurance for anyone engaged in da'wah, teaching or parenting. You are responsible for conveying the message clearly and kindly; you are not responsible for the outcome. Parents who raised children with everything they had and now watch those children drift need to hear this verse and stop destroying themselves. The Prophet ﷺ himself could not guide his own uncle Abu Talib, whom he loved and cared for, and the Qur'an told him plainly that guidance is not in his hand. That is not a failure of your effort; it is the boundary Allah placed between the call and the response.

The second direction is a warning against a misuse of the verse. You and I cannot know who belongs to this category, so the verse gives no one permission to write anybody off. No matter how hostile, how dismissive, how far gone a person appears, you have no revelation about his final state. The practical rule the verse establishes is: keep delivering the message, and hand the outcome over. Conversely — and this is where the verse turns and looks at the reader — persist long enough in refusing the truth and refusing can become the settled state of the heart. That is not a threat aimed at others; it is a warning about the trajectory of your own refusals. The next verse describes what that hardening looks like from the inside.

## Verse 7

**"Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment."**

In simple terms, Allah has closed the hearts and the hearing of these people so that guidance cannot enter, and He has placed a covering over their eyes; because of that, a tremendous punishment awaits them.

The first word to understand is *qalb*, "heart." In modern English the heart is the organ of feeling, and reading the verse that way produces nonsense. In the Qur'an's vocabulary, the heart is the seat of intellect, perception, intention and faith — the faculty by which a person understands and chooses. So a "sealed heart" is a mind and will that can no longer receive the truth, not a person who has stopped having emotions. The Qur'an makes this explicit elsewhere: "They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear" (7:179).

*Khatama* means to seal, to stamp closed — the image is of a seal placed on a document so that nothing can be added to it or taken from it. Notice, though, that the verse does not use the same verb for all three faculties. It says the hearts and the hearing are *sealed*, but then changes construction: "and upon their sight is a covering" (*wa 'ala absarihim ghishawah*). The commentators, including al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan, treated this variation as deliberate eloquence. A seal closes something from within, so it suits the heart and the faculty of hearing, which receive and process. A *ghishawah* — a veil or covering — sits over something from without, and that suits the eye, which is an exposed organ blocked by a covering over it. Each faculty is disabled in a manner appropriate to how it works.

Why hearts, hearing and sight specifically? Because hearing and sight are the two doors through which knowledge enters, and the heart is the chamber that receives it. With the doors obstructed and the chamber sealed, guidance has no route in. Note also the grammatical detail that "hearing" (*sam'*) is singular — a single faculty — while "sights" (*absar*) is plural, matching the paired eyes, and "hearts" is plural, matching the people.

Now the question that has occupied theologians for over a thousand years: does Allah seal the heart of a person who then has no choice, and if so, how is that just? This is one of the places where the schools genuinely parted company, and it would be dishonest to present only one side.

The Mu'tazila, reasoning from their principle that Allah cannot create evil or punish someone for what He Himself caused, refused to read the verse as an act of Allah. They reinterpreted it: that Allah *described* them as sealed, or *judged* them to be sealed, or that the sealing goes back to Satan's decoration of their deeds. Al-Zamakhshari's commentary, though it belongs to the great classics of Arabic rhetoric and grammar, reflects this Mu'tazili reading, and students need to know that when they use it.

Ahl al-Sunnah — the Ash'aris, the Maturidis and the scholars of hadith — read the verse as it appears: Allah does create the seal. But they insisted, with strong Qur'anic evidence, that it is a consequence of the person's own rejection and not an arbitrary act imposed beforehand. The Qur'an's own cross-references make the sequence clear. "Rather, Allah sealed them for their disbelief" (4:155) — the sealing is *because of* their disbelief. "When they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate" (61:5) — their deviation comes first in the sentence. "No! Rather, the stain has covered their hearts because of what they used to earn" (83:14) — the stain is *earned*. "Have you seen the one who has taken his desire as his god, and Allah left him astray upon knowledge, and sealed his hearing and his heart" (45:23) — the sealing follows his taking desire as a god. And in this very surah, verse 10: "In their hearts is a sickness, and Allah increased them in sickness" — again, a response to an existing state.

So the established Sunni position is that the seal is a created consequence of a person's own sustained, knowing rejection of the truth — a judicial hardening, not a pre-emptive one. The person is not sealed first and then unable to believe; he refuses, and refuses, and refuses, and in refusing loses the capacity he was given. The analogy the scholars used is of a warning label: a person who ignores a warning often enough genuinely stops seeing it.

Two further cautions. First, no human being may declare a specific individual sealed, because the state of a heart is unseen; we judge only by what is apparent. Second, this is not only a doctrine about "them." The slow version of this process is a real danger for a believer, and the Sunnah describes it in almost physiological terms. In a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah from Abu Hurayrah, the Prophet ﷺ said that when a servant commits a sin, a black spot is placed upon his heart; if he repents and desists, his heart is polished; but if he persists, the spot spreads until it covers the heart entirely — and that, the Prophet ﷺ said, is the "stain" (*ran*) Allah mentioned in 83:14. Another hadith, reported by al-Tirmidhi and regarded as sound, describes faith wearing out inside a person's heart the way a garment wears out, and asks Allah to renew faith in our hearts.

Finally, *'adhabun 'azim* — a tremendous punishment. The adjective *'azim* denotes something vast and overwhelming, and the proportion is deliberate: the punishment is tremendous because what was refused was tremendous. These were not people who never heard; they were people who heard the clearest possible invitation, recognised it, and turned away.

Now bring this into our own lives, and be honest about it. Very few of us reject revelation outright; what we do instead is stop responding to it. The Qur'an is recited and nothing changes. A verse that once stings stops stinging. A sin that once caused distress becomes routine. That dulling is the process this verse is describing, and it is worth asking what is doing the dulling in your case. For many people today it is not philosophical doubt but saturation: the endless scroll, the algorithmic feed that only confirms what you already think, the noise that leaves no room for a verse to land, the content consumed in secret that makes the heart ashamed to approach its Lord. The heart's capacity for guidance is not usually destroyed in a single act of rebellion; it erodes.

The good news embedded in the same tradition is that the erosion is reversible up to the last breath. The remedies the scholars named are the ones the revelation itself names: the Qur'an, which is described as "a healing for what is in the breasts" (10:57) and "a healing and a mercy for the believers" (17:82); the remembrance of Allah, since "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (13:28); repentance, which polishes; righteous company, which re-sensitises the conscience; and acting on whatever you already know, on the well-known principle among the scholars that whoever acts upon what he knows, Allah grants him knowledge of what he did not know. And there is always the supplication the Prophet ﷺ made constantly: "O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion."

## Verse 8

**"And there are some who say, “We believe in Allah and the Last Day,” yet they are not ˹true˺ believers."**

In simple terms, the surah now introduces a third group — people who announce their faith in correct Islamic language while their hearts do not actually believe. They are the hypocrites, and the Qur'an's verdict on them is emphatic: they are not believers at all.

The historical setting explains why this group appears here. This is Madinah. In Makkah the Muslims were a persecuted minority and there was no advantage in pretending to join them, so open hypocrisy was not a meaningful phenomenon. After the Hijrah, when the Prophet ﷺ became the ruler of Madinah and especially after Badr gave the Muslims real standing, people began entering Islam outwardly for safety, for tribal alignment, for status, or in order to undermine the community from inside. Their most prominent figure was 'Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul. The classical commentators, including al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir, tied these verses to that milieu; a specific occasion of revelation for each individual verse here is not firmly established, and we should not manufacture one.

The wording of the verse is precise. *Wa mina al-nasi man yaqulu* — "and among the people are those who say." The Qur'an does not say "those who believe"; it says "those who *say*." Their claim is verbal, and the verse measures the claim against the reality. Notice too what they claim: "We believe in Allah and the Last Day." Those are exactly the two anchor beliefs that opened the description of the believers in verses 3 and 4. The hypocrites had the vocabulary perfectly right. This is the first lesson of the verse: correct creedal statements, correctly pronounced, are not the same thing as faith. A person can say everything right and be empty.

The negation at the end is unusually strong. *Wa ma hum bi-mu'minin* contains an extra preposition (*bi-*) that Arabic grammar does not strictly require, and grammarians noted that this extra particle after *ma* serves to intensify the negation. It is not simply "they are not believers"; it is a firm, total denial — they are not believers in any real sense.

This verse sits at the centre of one of the earliest and most consequential debates in Islamic theology: what is *iman*? The schools answered differently, and the verse was used as evidence by each side. The Khawarij held that committing a major sin expels a person from faith entirely. The Murji'a held that faith is mere inward affirmation, and that deeds are not part of it at all. The position that became the mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah — articulated by the scholars of hadith and by al-Tabari at length in his discussion of this verse — is that faith is affirmation in the heart, statement with the tongue and action with the limbs; that it increases with obedience and decreases with sin; and that a believer who commits a major sin is a deficient believer, not an unbeliever.

That last point is essential and is often missed. This verse is about people who do not believe in their hearts *at all* while pretending to. It is not about the struggling believer who sins, feels bad about it, and keeps returning. The difference between those two people is not the sin; it is the state of the heart. The Kharijite error was to collapse the second into the first, and the Murji'ite error was to make the outward claim sufficient regardless of the heart. The verse refutes both.

The scholars also drew a distinction that is indispensable for reading this passage and the hadith literature alongside it. There is *nifaq in belief* — outward Islam with inward disbelief — and this places a person outside the religion and, as Surah al-Nisa' states, in the lowest depth of the Fire (4:145). And there is *nifaq in practice* — the traits of hypocrisy without the loss of faith. The hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah describes this second kind: "The signs of the hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; and when he is entrusted, he betrays." In another version: "Four traits — whoever has all of them is a sheer hypocrite, and whoever has one of them has a trait of hypocrisy until he abandons it: when he is entrusted he betrays, when he speaks he lies, when he makes a covenant he breaks it, and when he disputes he is foul." Al-Nawawi and other scholars explained that this second kind is a grave sin and a route towards the first, but it does not expel a person from Islam.

Now bring this into the present, because this is the most directly diagnostic verse in the batch. The hypocrite's defining feature is the gap between the public self and the private self, and there are few eras in which that gap has been easier to manufacture. There is the man who posts Qur'anic verses and underpays his staff. The woman who performs meticulous religiosity in public and is cruel to her mother in private. The professional who leads the prayer at the office and signs off on a fraudulent report. The activist who invokes the language of justice for an audience and abandons it the moment it costs him. The online believer whose feed is a cathedral and whose browser history is something else.

And there is a subtler version: the person who is religious for the reaction. He performs the visible good because he wants the approval, and he would not do it in an empty room. The Prophet ﷺ called this "the lesser shirk," showing off, in a narration recorded by Ahmad, and said he feared it for his community more than he feared the Dajjal's tribulation in some narrations — a comparison that shows how seriously this was taken.

The honest self-examination this verse demands is not "am I a hypocrite?" in the abstract, but the concrete question: what do I do when no one is watching and nothing is at stake? That question is not meant to produce despair; it is meant to close the gap before it becomes a chasm.

## Verse 9

**"They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it."**

In simple terms, these people try to outwit Allah and the Muslim community by putting on a show of faith, but the trick lands on no one except themselves — and the tragedy is that they are not even aware of it.

The Arabic verb is telling: *yukhadi'un*. This is a derived verb form in Arabic that conveys attempting, striving and contending rather than a single completed act, so the sense is not simply "they deceive" but "they work at deceiving, they try again and again to outwit." The hypocrisy being described is an ongoing project, a sustained campaign of managing appearances, not a one-off lie.

Then there is the obvious theological problem, and the commentators addressed it head on: how can anyone try to deceive Allah, when nothing is hidden from Him? Al-Tabari and others explained it in two complementary ways. The first is that the attempt is directed at the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and at the believers, and it is *described* as an attempt against Allah because deceiving His Messenger is deceiving the One who sent him — the same logic as the Qur'an's statement, "Those who pledge allegiance to you are only pledging allegiance to Allah" (48:10). The second is that the phrasing captures what they are effectively doing: they behave as though they could hide from the One who sees everything, and the Qur'an describes their action by its intended object rather than by its actual reach. Either way the point is the same — the object of their scheme is infinitely beyond their schemes, so the scheme collapses back onto them.

And that collapse is the heart of the verse: *wa ma yakhda'una illa anfusahum* — they deceive no one but themselves. How? In several ways at once. They are exposed in this life, for the Qur'an elsewhere tells the Prophet ﷺ that if He willed, Allah would show them to him and he would know them by their marks (47:30), and their private mockery keeps leaking into public. They lose the very thing they were pretending to have, because the faith they performed outwardly never took root inwardly, so it cannot benefit them. They are promised the lowest depth of the Fire, in 4:145 — a worse destination than that of the open disbelievers. And they trade away the genuine love, trust and support of the believing community for a counterfeit version of it. Getting the benefits of belonging to a community while quietly working against it sounds like cleverness; in practice it is the purchase of a great punishment at the price of a small, temporary advantage.

The final clause is the most haunting: *wa ma yash'urun* — and they do not perceive it. The word *shu'ur* belongs to a family of meanings connected with fine perception and awareness; these people lack even that level of awareness. The Qur'an uses several different words for human failure here: *la ya'lamun* (they do not know — a failure of information), *la ya'qilun* (they do not reason — a failure of intellect), and *la yash'urun* (they do not perceive — a failure of awareness). The hypocrite's problem is not that the facts are unavailable; it is that he does not notice what he is doing to himself.

This is one of the places where modern knowledge genuinely illuminates a verse rather than being forced into it. Psychology has a great deal to say about self-deception, motivated reasoning and cognitive dissonance: people are remarkably skilled at constructing narratives in which their actions are reasonable and they themselves are good, and the less aware they are of doing it, the more completely it works. There is also a well-documented experimental finding that the emotional response to dishonesty diminishes the more a person repeats it, so the discomfort that would otherwise stop them fades — a "slippery slope" that has been measured in brain activity as well as in behaviour. Note carefully what this does and does not establish. It does not prove the verse, and the verse does not need proving. What it does is make the Qur'an's description legible in contemporary terms: the Qur'an says their deception returns to them and they do not perceive it, and the finding is that a person who deceives repeatedly becomes progressively less able to perceive the harm. The compatibility is real; the dependence is not.

Make this concrete. The employee who fakes the numbers on a report and tells himself everyone does it. The student who plagiarises and calls it research. The spouse who hides messages and says it is nothing. The person who performs religiosity for an audience and calls it da'wah. The common thread in all of them is the same word the verse uses: they do not perceive. Each would be genuinely shocked to be described as harming himself, and that shock is the symptom.

There is one more thing the verse quietly teaches, and it is the cure. Deception that a person cannot see in himself is deception that needs an external mirror. That is partly why the Qur'an addresses the community as well as the individual, why the Sunnah insists on sincere companionship and honest advice, and why the believer is told to keep checking himself: not to live in suspicion, but because self-deception is the one disease that presents as health.

## Verse 10

**"There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies."**

In simple terms, there is a real disease in the hearts of the hypocrites; because of their persistence and their lying, Allah lets that disease grow; and a painful punishment awaits them.

The verse opens with a nominal sentence — *fi qulubihim maradun*, "in their hearts is a sickness" — rather than saying "their hearts are sick." In Arabic, the nominal construction presents the condition as settled and established rather than as something happening to them, and the indefinite noun (*maradun*, "a sickness") leaves the nature of it open while asserting its reality. They have a disease of a certain kind, and it is in the very organ that is supposed to receive guidance.

What is the disease? The early authorities named it in overlapping ways, and their answers are complementary rather than contradictory. Mujahid explained it as doubt (*shakk*). 'Ikrimah and al-Hasan al-Basri explained it as hypocrisy (*nifaq*). Others said it is doubt together with hypocrisy, or a general corruption of the heart's orientation. Later commentators, notably al-Razi and al-Qurtubi, generalised the principle rather than the label: the heart's health is its capacity to recognise and receive the truth, and its sickness is whatever veils it from that — which can be doubt, envy, arrogance, love of praise, desire, or attachment to this world. On that reading, the verse is not only a description of seventh-century Madinan hypocrites; it is a diagnosis of a condition any human heart can develop.

The Qur'an's own vocabulary confirms that it treats the heart as an organ with a clinical condition: hearts that are sound, sick, dead, sealed, rusted, hardened, or at rest. And it prescribes the medicine in its own pages: "O mankind, there has come to you an admonition from your Lord and a healing for what is in the breasts" (10:57), and "We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers" (17:82). The disease described in this verse is treated by the very Book that describes it — which is one reason the Qur'an keeps being called a healing and not merely information.

Then comes the clause that raises the same theological issue as verse 7, and it must be handled with the same care: *fa-zadahumu Allahu maradan* — "so Allah increased them in sickness." Note the particle *fa*, which indicates consequence: the increase follows from their state. The increase is attributed to Allah because He is the creator of everything, including this; but the Qur'an's own grammar presents it as a response to what they did, and the verse ends by naming the cause outright: *bima kanu yakdhibun* — because of what they used to lie. The same pattern appears elsewhere: "As for those in whose hearts is disease, it increased them in filth upon their filth" (9:125); "When they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate" (61:5).

How the increase works, on the mainstream Sunni reading, can be described at two levels that do not conflict. At the level of created causality, every rejected sign further deadens the heart — so as more of the Qur'an was revealed and they rejected it, or as more occasions for honesty arose and they lied, the disease deepened. Several commentators noted this progressive reading: the hypocrites worsened each time a fresh portion of revelation came and they refused it. And at the level of divine decree, it is Allah who creates that consequence. The Mu'tazila again read the attribution differently, referring the increase to the person's own action; that difference has already been described in the commentary on verse 7 and need not be repeated except to note that it applies here too.

The punishment is described here as *'adhabun alim* — a painful punishment — where verse 7 described the disbelievers' punishment as *'azim*, tremendous. The Qur'an varies its descriptors across contexts rather than ranking groups mechanically, and it is not sound to build a hierarchy on the difference. What is certain is that the hypocrites' final destination is stated unambiguously elsewhere: "Indeed, the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of the Fire" (4:145). The reason is not hard to see. They had the truth in front of them, claimed it, and used it as camouflage.

The closing clause deserves a final word, because it binds the whole passage together: their punishment is *because of what they used to lie*. The verb is in a form indicating continuous, settled practice — they were habitual liars, not occasional ones. And the link being made is causal and psychological as much as legal: lying is what produced the sickness, and continuing to lie is what makes it worse. Hypocrisy cannot be sustained without a constant supply of lies — to the Prophet, to the community, to one's family, and above all to oneself — and a life built on that cannot stay healthy.

Here, again, modern research illuminates without proving. That same line of work on dishonesty found that the emotional arousal associated with lying diminishes as dishonesty is repeated, and that people escalate their dishonesty over time when it goes unpunished — a measurable version of the hardening this verse describes as an increase in disease. The finding is compatible with the Qur'anic account; it is not the basis of it, and it would be wrong to rest any interpretation on a research programme that will keep developing.

Now bring this verse home, because of all ten this is the one a self-aware reader should find uncomfortable. The practical form of this disease appears in ordinary, non-dramatic symptoms. Feeling nothing when the Qur'an is recited. Irritation rather than gratitude when someone reminds you of Allah. Relief rather than grief when a religious person is exposed. Finding reasons to be absent whenever the community gathers for good. Being fluent about other people's hypocrisy and silent about your own. A private life you would be horrified to have made public — not because of embarrassment, but because of what it reveals. Those are the early symptoms, and the verse's warning is that they do not stay early on their own.

The cure runs in the opposite direction to the disease, step by step. Stop lying, including the small social lies and the lies you tell yourself about why you did what you did. Sit with the Qur'an in the posture verse 2 described — as someone being addressed. Ask Allah directly, in the words of the supplication reported by Muslim from Zayd ibn Arqam that the Prophet ﷺ used to make: "O Allah, grant my soul its taqwa and purify it — You are the best of those who purify it; You are its Guardian and its Master." And make the supplication he made constantly: "O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion." A heart can be sick and get well; the verse is a warning precisely because the outcome is not fixed until it is fixed by death.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Taken together, these ten verses lay the foundation of the entire surah and, in miniature, of the whole Qur'anic message. Verse 1 opens with letters no human being can explain, establishing immediately that this speech is not of human manufacture. Verses 2 to 5 identify the Book, set its condition — taqwa — and then describe the people who meet that condition: they believe in the unseen, pray, spend, accept all of Allah's revelation, and are certain about the Hereafter; and the promise is that they are firmly upon guidance and are the truly successful. Verses 6 and 7 turn to the opposite extreme: those who reject so completely that their capacity to receive truth has been sealed as a consequence of their own refusal. Verses 8 to 10 introduce the most dangerous category of all — the people who use the language of faith while hollow inside, who deceive themselves without noticing, and whose hearts carry a disease that grows with every lie.

Three lessons run through the whole passage. First, guidance is a relationship, not a transaction: it is given to those who approach it with reverent awareness, and it deepens in those who respond to it. Second, rejection and pretence are not opposites but destinations reached by different roads: open defiance hardens the heart, and concealed disbelief rots it — and in both cases the person's own choices are the road. Third, the organ that everything depends on is the heart, and it has a condition that can be diagnosed, neglected, treated or lost. The surah will go on to give laws, histories, arguments and rulings, but it begins here, with the state of the heart that will have to receive them all.
