## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:21

> O humanity! Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you, so that you may become mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Commentary:**

### A Universal Call to Worship

After describing the three responses to revelation—the mindful, the openly rejecting, and the hypocritical—the Qur’an turns directly to all humanity: “O humanity!” This address is wider than the community of believers. It includes those who worship Allah, those who reject Him, those who associate partners with Him, and those who are uncertain. The invitation is therefore not based on ancestry, class, language, or prior religious affiliation. Every human being is addressed because every human being is created, sustained, and dependent upon the same Lord.

The command is *uʿbudū rabbakum*: worship your Lord. Worship in the Qur’an is more comprehensive than ritual acts alone. It includes loving Allah, submitting to Him, obeying His commands, seeking His help, fearing His displeasure, hoping in His mercy, and directing all ultimate devotion to Him. Prayer, fasting, charity, honest work, family responsibility, and justice become worship when they are performed in obedience to Allah and according to the guidance He has revealed. Conversely, a person may perform outward rituals while allowing pride, wealth, status, or desire to occupy the place of ultimate allegiance in his heart.

### The Lord Who Created You

Allah identifies Himself as *rabbukum*—your Lord. The word *Rabb* carries meanings of creator, owner, sustainer, nurturer, and one who brings a thing to completion. The reason given for worship is not an arbitrary demand from a distant power. Allah created humanity, created previous generations, sustains every creature, and provides the conditions in which life is possible. Since He alone gives existence and maintains it, He alone has the right to receive ultimate worship.

The reference to “you and those before you” also dissolves the illusion of self-sufficiency. No person created himself, and no generation is self-originating. Every human achievement rests upon gifts inherited from earlier people and upon capacities Allah placed within the human being. The command to worship the Creator is therefore an invitation to return reality to its proper order: the creature acknowledges dependence, and the Creator is recognized as the true source of life and provision.

The Qur’an frequently argues from creation to worship. “Were they created by nothing, or were they themselves the creators?” (52:35). It also reminds people that Allah created them and what they do (37:96), and asks whether the One who creates is like the one who does not create (16:17). These arguments do not reduce faith to a single philosophical formula, but they expose the irrationality of receiving existence from Allah while directing ultimate devotion to created things.

### Worship Produces Taqwā

The purpose clause is “so that you may become mindful”—*laʿallakum tattaqūn*. Taqwā is not only fear; it is a protective awareness of Allah that shapes choices. A person who knows that he lives under Allah’s care and will return to Him develops restraint, gratitude, and moral vigilance. Worship is meant to cultivate that awareness, not merely to produce repeated motions or inherited identity.

The verse therefore connects theology with transformation. Believing that Allah created us should make us humble. Knowing that He sustains us should make us grateful. Knowing that He will judge us should make us honest. If worship does not gradually produce these qualities, the worshipper must examine the condition of his heart and the quality of his practice. Allah is not benefited by human worship; human beings are purified and protected through it.

This universal call also provides the foundation for human equality. All people share one Creator and one origin, and no community can claim an independent source of life or a hereditary right to divine favour. Superiority is not established by race, wealth, or lineage, but by taqwā (49:13). The next verse will point to the earth, sky, rain, and fruits as signs of this sustaining Lord, and will warn humanity not to set up equals alongside Him.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 16:36, 21:25, and 51:56 on the universal call to worship Allah alone.
- Qur’an 52:35–36 and 16:17 on creation as evidence for the Creator’s right to worship.
- Qur’an 49:13 on the common human origin and taqwā as the true measure of honour.
- Qur’an 37:96 on Allah’s creation of human beings and their actions.
- Qur’an 23:1–11 on worship producing humility, restraint, trustworthiness, and success.
- For the comprehensive meaning of *ʿibādah*, see Ibn Taymiyyah’s al-ʿUbūdiyyah and the discussions of 2:21 in Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:22

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you.
>
> So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺.

**Commentary:**

### The Earth as a Habitable Home

The preceding āyah commanded humanity to worship the Lord who created them. This verse presents signs of that lordship. Allah made the earth *firāshan*—a spread, resting place, or habitation—and the sky a canopy. The language does not mean that the earth is perfectly flat or that the verse is offering a technical description of its shape. It describes the human experience of a world made suitable for life: a place upon which people can walk, build, cultivate, travel, and find shelter.

The earth’s habitability is not an invitation to treat it as an object of careless consumption. It is a provision and a trust. Allah says that He created what is on earth for human benefit (2:29), but the Qur’an also forbids waste, corruption, and destruction. Benefiting from creation must therefore remain within gratitude, justice, and restraint. The one who recognizes the earth as Allah’s provision does not confuse stewardship with absolute ownership.

The sky is described as a canopy or protective roof. It is a visible sign of order: the vast expanse above human beings, the cycles of clouds and rain, and the atmosphere through which life is sustained. The Qur’an uses such descriptions to awaken reflection, not to replace scientific investigation with a superficial claim. “Do they not look at the sky above them—how We built it and adorned it, and how it has no cracks?” (50:6). The created order points beyond itself to the wisdom and power of its Creator.

### Rain and Provision

Rain descends from the sky and brings forth fruits as provision. Water is one of the most accessible signs of divine mercy. It reaches the soil, revives apparently dead land, and enters countless biological processes that human beings neither create nor control. The Qur’an repeatedly compares the revival of the earth by rain with the resurrection of human beings (30:50; 35:9). The analogy is not that the earth is divine, but that an observable process makes the unseen promise of resurrection intelligible.

The verse calls the fruits *rizq*, provision. This word includes what Allah grants for sustenance, but it also teaches that provision is not reduced to money. Food, water, health, family, knowledge, security, and opportunities to do good are all forms of provision. A person earns, plants, trades, and plans, but the ability of seed, soil, rain, and human effort to produce nourishment remains within Allah’s sustaining decree.

The mention of provision also carries an ethical implication. Since Allah is the ultimate provider, a person should not allow fear of poverty to push him into theft, exploitation, hoarding, or unlawful income. He should work through lawful means while knowing that provision is not secured by disobedience. The Qur’an promises that whoever is mindful of Allah will be given a way out and provided from where he does not expect (65:2–3).

### Do Not Set Up Equals

The conclusion is the central theological lesson: “Do not knowingly set up equals to Allah.” The word *andād* means rivals, counterparts, or beings treated as though they share Allah’s unique rights. Association can occur through idol worship, but it can also appear when a person gives a created being the kind of ultimate fear, hope, obedience, reliance, or love that belongs exclusively to Allah. The Qur’an says that some people love their rivals as they should love Allah, while the believers love Allah more deeply and exclusively (2:165).

The warning is qualified by “knowingly.” This is not permission to ignore ignorance or to treat every mistake as deliberate polytheism. It is a warning against recognizing Allah as Creator while consciously directing worship to others. The ancient polytheists often admitted that Allah created and provided, yet still invoked idols as intermediaries and objects of devotion (10:31; 29:61–65). The Qur’an asks why they worship what can neither create nor provide while Allah alone does both.

The order of the verse is decisive. It first mentions creation, habitability, rain, and provision, then forbids rivals. The evidence of lordship leads to the obligation of exclusive worship. This is the foundation of *tawḥīd*: Allah alone creates and sustains (*tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah*), therefore Allah alone deserves worship (*tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah*). Acknowledging the first while refusing the second is an incomplete response to the signs surrounding every human life.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:29, 16:10–18, and 21:30 on the earth, sky, water, and provision as signs of Allah’s lordship.
- Qur’an 10:31 and 29:61–65 on people acknowledging Allah as Creator while associating others with Him in worship.
- Qur’an 13:17, 30:50, and 35:9 on rain reviving the earth and pointing to resurrection.
- Qur’an 2:165 on loving Allah and the danger of treating created beings as rivals.
- Qur’an 7:31 and 17:26–27 on gratitude, avoiding waste, and responsible use of provision.
- Qur’an 65:2–3 on taqwā, lawful conduct, and Allah’s promise of provision.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:23

> And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.

**Commentary:**

### The Challenge of the Qur’an

After establishing Allah’s right to be worshipped, the Qur’an turns to the source of the message itself. It addresses those who doubt what has been revealed to “Our servant”—the Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ). The title *ʿabd*—servant—honours the Prophet because the highest human station is not independence from Allah but complete servitude to Him. The Qur’an uses this title for the Prophet in moments of revelation, night journey, and divine distinction (17:1; 53:10), showing that prophethood and servitude belong together.

The challenge is precise: if the doubters think the Qur’an is a human composition, they should produce one sūrah like it. The Qur’an does not merely ask them to write something eloquent, long, or emotionally moving. It challenges them to match its combined qualities—its Arabic expression, structure, depth, coherence, legal and moral insight, truthful unseen information, transformative power, and freedom from contradiction—within even a single sūrah. The challenge is narrowed in stages: produce a whole Qur’an (17:88), ten sūrahs (11:13), and finally one sūrah (2:23; 10:38).

### A Challenge to Every Claimed Supporter

The instruction to call “your helpers other than Allah” includes every resource the opponents believed could assist them: eloquent poets, religious authorities, learned people, political allies, and the idols they invoked. The phrase removes the excuse that a person could not answer alone. If the Qur’an were merely the Prophet’s invention, then the collective expertise of its opponents should have been able to produce a comparable passage. Yet the challenge remains in the Qur’an, publicly recited before people intensely motivated to refute it.

The challenge is not an argument that every Arabic speaker must be a specialist in literary criticism before he can believe. Many people recognize the Qur’an’s truth through its message, moral clarity, spiritual effect, and the Prophet’s character. Nor does the challenge mean that no human sentence can ever resemble a particular phrase in a superficial way. Similarity in vocabulary is not the same as producing a discourse with the Qur’an’s total qualities. The point is the sustained inability to meet the challenge in the relevant sense, despite the strongest motive to do so.

### Revelation and Honest Doubt

The verse begins conditionally: “If you are in doubt.” This shows that the Qur’an engages doubt with evidence rather than treating every question as an act of rebellion. Sincere doubt can arise from incomplete information, emotional pain, historical confusion, or exposure to competing claims. The remedy is honest inquiry. What the Qur’an condemns is the posture that uses doubt as a permanent excuse while refusing to examine the evidence or obey what has already become clear.

The challenge also moves the question from the personality of Muhammad (ﷺ) to the nature of the message. Opponents could accuse him of poetry, sorcery, or fabrication, but the Qur’an repeatedly asks them to engage the text itself. Its message was not hidden from its first audience. It confronted their theology, economic practices, tribal pride, and moral corruption; it was not designed merely to flatter them. A book that transformed its first hearers while challenging their deepest interests cannot be explained simply as a strategy for personal gain.

### The Qur’an’s Continuing Evidence

The inimitability of the Qur’an is not restricted to a single moment in seventh-century Arabic. Its language remains part of its evidence, but its guidance also continues to expose false assumptions, reform lives, preserve a coherent worldview, and unite worship with ethics. The Qur’an invites reflection upon its internal consistency: “Had it been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much contradiction” (4:82). It also appeals to the Prophet’s long public life and asks why his opponents do not reason (10:16).

The correct response to the challenge is not pride in possessing a slogan. It is to read the Book seriously and allow its claim to be tested. If it is from Allah, it cannot be approached as ordinary literature alone. It deserves linguistic study, contextual understanding, comparison with the Prophet’s Sunnah, and, above all, a willingness to follow the truth when it corrects the reader. The next āyah states the consequence for those who remain unable to answer the challenge and unwilling to submit.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 10:16, 10:38, 11:13, 17:88, and 52:33–34 on the progressive challenge to produce the like of the Qur’an.
- Qur’an 4:82 on reflection and the absence of contradiction in revelation.
- Qur’an 17:1 and 53:10 on the honour of the Prophet’s servitude to Allah.
- Qur’an 28:49 on inviting opponents to bring a guidance better than the two revealed Books if their claim is true.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the Prophet’s greatest miracle being revelation and the continuing nature of the Qur’anic sign.
- For classical discussions of *iʿjāz al-Qurʾān*, see al-Bāqillānī’s Iʿjāz al-Qurʾān, al-Rummānī’s al-Nukat fī Iʿjāz al-Qurʾān, and the tafsīrs of al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr on 2:23.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:24

> But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.

**Commentary:**

### The Challenge and Its Consequence

The previous āyah presented the Qur’an’s challenge: produce one sūrah comparable to it if its divine origin is doubted. This verse states that the challenge will never be met—*wa-lan tafʿalū*, “and you will never do so.” The wording is striking because it does not merely say that the opponents had failed in the past. It declares that the failure is definitive. Human beings may create powerful speeches and literature, but no human production can become the Qur’an.

The challenge is not an invitation to treat revelation as a literary competition detached from guidance. The inability to imitate the Qur’an is one sign of its source, but the Qur’an’s purpose is to lead human beings to worship Allah and prepare for accountability. A person who acknowledges the Qur’an’s unique expression yet refuses its moral call has recognized an evidence without submitting to what it proves. Knowledge becomes beneficial only when it leads to truthfulness and obedience.

### “Fear the Fire”

The command “then fear the Fire” is a warning and a mercy. It tells the hearer that the question is not merely academic. If the Qur’an is truly from Allah, rejecting it is not a harmless disagreement about style. It places a person in opposition to the One who created him and sent guidance for his salvation. The Qur’an combines proof with warning because human beings can recognize an argument and still refuse to change; fear of consequence can awaken the will when intellectual recognition alone has not done so.

The Fire is described as being prepared for the disbelievers. Other passages state that it is prepared for those who reject Allah and His messengers (3:131; 48:13). The Qur’an also makes clear that the relevant disbelief is not a passing question or a message that never reached a person in a clear form. Allah judges with complete knowledge of what each person received, understood, intended, and chose. The warning is therefore severe without making human beings judges of the hidden circumstances of others.

### People and Stones as Fuel

The Fire’s fuel is described as people and stones. The wording intensifies the image: those who were created from earth and those who made or worshipped stone objects will discover that neither human power nor false gods can protect them. The Qur’an says of the idols and their worshippers, “Indeed, you and whatever you worship besides Allah are fuel for Hell” (21:98), referring to the idols that were treated as objects of devotion. Some early reports also interpreted the stones as idols, while other exegetes understood the phrase more generally. The essential point is that the material world cannot shield anyone from Allah’s judgment.

The image also exposes the absurdity of idolatry. A stone that was carved, carried, and placed by human hands is unable to rescue its worshipper; on the Day of Judgment, the very object of false reliance becomes part of the terrifying scene. The Qur’an repeatedly asks whether the things people invoke can create, hear prayers, benefit, or avert harm (10:31; 35:13–14). Reliance upon created things in their proper, limited capacity is not the same as worshipping them or treating them as rivals to Allah.

### The Place of the Warning in the Qur’an’s Mercy

The Qur’an’s warnings should not be isolated from its abundant invitations to forgiveness. Allah says, “O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of Allah’s mercy” (39:53), and He accepts the repentance of those who turn back before death. The Fire is “prepared” as a real consequence, but warning a person of it while the door of repentance remains open is an act of compassion. The most dangerous response is neither fear nor questioning; it is arrogant indifference.

The verse also prevents the reader from turning the challenge into a boast of cultural superiority. The Qur’an is not saying that Arabs are naturally better than other peoples. Its first challenge was made in Arabic because that was the language in which the revelation came and in which its opponents claimed mastery. Its guidance, warning, and invitation are for all humanity. The decisive distinction is not language, race, or literary ability but whether a person responds to truth with faith and obedience.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:23, 3:131, and 48:13 on the challenge and the Fire prepared for rejection of Allah and His messenger.
- Qur’an 10:31, 21:98, and 35:13–14 on the inability of false objects of worship to benefit or protect.
- Qur’an 66:6 on protecting oneself and one’s family from the Fire.
- Qur’an 39:53 on the open door of repentance before death.
- Qur’an 17:15 and 28:59 on Allah’s justice and the establishment of proof through clear warning.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on “people and stones” and the force of *wa-lan tafʿalū*.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25

> Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.

**Commentary:**

### Warning Is Joined to Good News

After the warning of the Fire, Allah commands the Prophet (ﷺ) to give good news to those who believe and do good. The Qur’an balances fear with hope so that the believer is neither complacent nor despairing. Fear protects from arrogance and heedlessness; hope draws the heart toward Allah’s mercy and reward. The Prophet’s mission itself is repeatedly described as bringing good news and warning (18:56; 33:45–46). A complete religious education must preserve both dimensions.

The verse joins *īmān*—faith—with righteous action. This does not mean that human deeds purchase Paradise independently of Allah’s mercy. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that no one enters Paradise solely by his deeds, not even him, except through Allah’s mercy (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Deeds are nevertheless the evidence of sincere faith and the means through which Allah’s mercy is sought. Faith without obedience becomes a claim, while action without faith loses its eternal direction.

### Gardens Beneath Which Rivers Flow

The promised home is *jannāt*—gardens—described with rivers flowing beneath them. The imagery speaks to human beings who know thirst, heat, drought, and the labour required to sustain crops. Paradise is not merely an escape from pain; it is a perfected home of life, beauty, abundance, security, and nearness to Allah. The Qur’an describes rivers of pure water, milk whose taste does not change, wine that does not intoxicate, and clarified honey (47:15). These descriptions communicate realities through familiar forms while reminding us that the delights of the Hereafter surpass the limits of worldly experience.

The gardens also signify permanence. Worldly beauty decays, rivers dry, wealth changes hands, and homes are eventually abandoned. The garden of Paradise is not threatened by drought, disease, war, age, or death. It is a provision from the Ever-Living Lord, and its safety is complete because the greatest danger—the loss of Allah’s pleasure—has been removed.

### Familiar Appearance, Renewed Delight

Whenever the people of Paradise are given fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before.” The fruit resembles what they recognize, yet its taste and experience are renewed. Classical exegetes have explained this as either fruits resembling one another in appearance while differing in taste, or repeated provision that is familiar in form but continually delightful. Both readings point to a mercy: Paradise is not alien to the human longing for beauty and recognition, but it purifies and perfects that longing.

The statement also suggests that the pleasures of Paradise do not become dull through repetition. Worldly pleasure often diminishes through habituation. The most desired meal becomes ordinary, the most beautiful scene becomes familiar, and the strongest excitement fades. In Paradise, Allah gives familiar forms with ever-fresh enjoyment. The believer recognizes the generosity of his Lord while receiving it again as though it were new.

### Pure Spouses and Lasting Life

The promise of “pure spouses” includes freedom from physical impurity, illness, betrayal, resentment, and every quality that makes worldly relationships painful. Paradise does not erase affection or companionship; it removes the defects that corrupt them. The Qur’an says that Allah will remove whatever rancour is in the hearts of its people and that they will be brothers facing one another upon couches (15:47). The relationships of Paradise are therefore marked by dignity, peace, mutual joy, and security.

The promise “they will be there forever” is the decisive contrast with the world. The greatest worldly blessing remains fragile because it can be lost through death. In Paradise, the believer does not fear the return of grief, separation, illness, or punishment. The Qur’an describes the people of Paradise as receiving the greeting “Peace be upon you” and never hearing vain or sinful speech (56:25–26). The final reward is not simply an abundance of objects; it is an entire existence purified from fear and loss.

The verse therefore gives the believer a destination that can sustain effort. Prayer, charity, patience, and moral restraint are not meaningless sacrifices. They are steps toward a home whose beauty cannot be adequately captured by the language of this world. The Prophet (ﷺ) reported that Allah has prepared for His righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has imagined (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The descriptions are true, but the reality is greater than our images.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 18:56 and 33:45–46 on the prophetic mission as good news and warning.
- Qur’an 3:133–136, 9:72, and 47:15 on Paradise, its gardens, rivers, and divine pleasure.
- Qur’an 15:47 and 56:25–26 on purified relationships and freedom from rancour and harmful speech.
- Qur’an 32:17 and 50:35 on delights beyond human imagination and the increase Allah grants.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on entering Paradise through Allah’s mercy and on its unimaginable delights.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on the similarity and renewed taste of Paradise’s fruits.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:26

> Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—

**Commentary:**

### No Created Thing Is Too Small for a Lesson

Allah does not “shy away” from using the example of a mosquito or something smaller. The statement answers an objection that some people raise against divine revelation: why would a majestic scripture mention small, ordinary, or apparently insignificant creatures? The Qur’an teaches that value is not measured by size or human prestige. A mosquito possesses an astonishingly complex form, life cycle, and relation to its environment, but even if a creature appears insignificant to human beings, it remains a creation of Allah and can carry a lesson.

A parable is not intended to flatter the audience’s sense of importance. It makes an unseen truth visible through a familiar image. The Qur’an uses a spider’s house to describe the weakness of relying on false protectors (29:41), a fly to expose the incapacity of idols (22:73), and a seed or garden to illuminate the consequences of charity (2:261–266). The point is not that the object of comparison is equal to the reality being explained. The point is that a small object can reveal a great truth when Allah directs attention to it.

Classical exegetes discussed whether “what is smaller” means smaller than the mosquito in physical size or smaller in the apparent insignificance of the example. The exact scientific identification is not necessary for the moral lesson. The verse teaches that revelation is free to choose the image most effective for guidance, and human beings are not entitled to reject a truth merely because its illustrative vehicle seems beneath their dignity.

### The Same Parable Tests Different Hearts

The believers respond: “It is the truth from our Lord.” They do not assume that every feature of the parable is already understood before they accept it. Their trust is not blind; it is based on their knowledge of Allah’s wisdom, the truthfulness of the Qur’an, and the consistency of its message. They reflect upon the parable and seek its benefit. When an aspect remains beyond them, they say, “Our Lord, increase us in knowledge” (20:114), rather than making their limited understanding a measure of divine wisdom.

The disbelievers ask mockingly, “What does Allah mean by this?” An honest question can be a path to guidance, but the tone described here is argumentative dismissal. They are not asking in order to learn; they are using the example as a pretext to undermine revelation. The same sign therefore becomes a test. It reveals the believer’s humility and the disbeliever’s arrogance. The issue is not the mosquito; it is the condition of the heart that refuses to learn from anything that does not reinforce its preferred image of what revelation should be.

The Qur’an makes clear that its parables require reflection. “These examples We present to people, but none understand them except those of knowledge” (29:43). Knowledge here is not merely information; it includes the humility to ask what the example is doing, what false assumption it corrects, and what action it demands.

### Guidance and Misguidance Under Divine Wisdom

The verse says that through the test Allah guides many and leaves many to stray, but He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—*al-fāsiqūn*. *Fisq* means departing from the proper boundary. The verse does not depict Allah as misleading sincere seekers who want the truth. Rather, the parable exposes and deepens the direction chosen by those who persist in rebellion. The receptive person gains guidance from it; the arrogant person uses it to reinforce his rejection.

This is a recurring Qur’anic principle. Revelation is mercy and guidance for those who seek Allah, but it can become a proof against those who turn away. “As for those who are guided, He increases them in guidance and grants them their mindfulness” (47:17). Conversely, when people knowingly reject the signs, their rejection compounds and their ability to benefit from further reminders decreases. Divine guidance and human response are both affirmed: Allah grants guidance, and people are responsible for whether they receive or resist it.

The phrase also guards against blaming the Qur’an for the disease of its rejecters. The light is not at fault because a person shuts his eyes. A teacher is not at fault because a proud student mocks a lesson. Yet Allah remains the ultimate Lord of guidance, and no one can claim that faith was achieved by independent power. The proper response is gratitude for guidance, humility before revelation, and prayer for steadfastness.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 22:73, 29:41–43, and 2:261–266 on parables drawn from small or ordinary things.
- Qur’an 16:74 on not making false comparisons with Allah.
- Qur’an 20:114 and 47:17 on seeking increase in knowledge and guidance.
- Qur’an 14:4 and 39:23 on guidance and misguidance through the different responses of people to revelation.
- Qur’an 8:29 and 64:16 on taqwā as a means of discernment and guidance.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on the mosquito parable, *fisq*, and the distinction between a sincere question and a mocking objection.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:27

> those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.

**Commentary:**

### The Rebellious Defined

The previous verse identified the rebellious as those whom Allah leaves to their chosen misguidance. This verse describes them through three connected violations: they break Allah’s covenant, sever bonds He commanded to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. Rebellion in the Qur’an is not merely making an isolated mistake. It is crossing the boundaries of a known obligation and treating the bonds established by Allah as disposable.

The covenant includes the fundamental obligation built into human nature and reaffirmed by revelation: to recognize Allah as Lord, worship Him alone, believe His messengers, and obey His guidance. It also includes the specific covenants made by communities and individuals—promises, contracts, oaths, and responsibilities. The Qur’an commands, “Fulfil the covenant of Allah when you have pledged, and do not break oaths after confirming them” (16:91). A religious commitment that is spoken publicly but abandoned whenever it becomes inconvenient is not a light matter; it reveals a person’s relationship with truth and responsibility.

The covenant is not only a burden; it is a mercy. It gives human beings a stable moral framework and protects them from making every decision according to appetite or power. When a person breaks it, he does not become free in the deepest sense. He becomes subject to impulse, social pressure, and the interests of whoever can offer the strongest immediate reward. The Qur’an’s language of covenant restores the seriousness of promises in a world that often treats words as disposable tools.

### Severing What Allah Commands to Join

The second violation is “breaking whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained.” This includes family relationships, obligations of mutual care, the bonds of faith, justice, neighbourliness, and the wider relations that preserve human society. The Qur’an repeatedly commands kindness to parents and relatives, care for orphans and the needy, and justice even toward opponents. It condemns those who cut the ties of kinship and spread corruption (13:25; 47:22–23).

This does not mean that every relationship must be maintained in the same form regardless of abuse or danger. Islam does not require a person to remain available to a violent or exploitative individual. Maintaining a bond can sometimes mean setting a safe boundary, refusing wrongdoing, or offering limited contact rather than enabling harm. The principle is that relationships must not be severed through pride, tribalism, greed, or a desire to punish people whom Allah has commanded us to treat with justice and mercy.

The phrase also has a spiritual dimension. The Qur’an links believers to one another through faith and commands them to reconcile disputes (49:10). Deliberately spreading suspicion, inciting factional hatred, and turning people against one another destroys the bonds by which a community lives. A person may be eloquent in religious speech and still be a corrupter if his influence consistently produces estrangement, betrayal, and hostility.

### Corruption and the Meaning of Loss

The third violation is spreading *fasād*—corruption—on earth. As verse 11 indicated, corruption includes damage to religion, human life, justice, trust, family, public order, and the natural world. The Qur’an does not treat private spirituality as a substitute for ethical responsibility. A person cannot claim closeness to Allah while systematically harming people and institutions under his care.

The final verdict is that they are the real losers—*al-khāsirūn*. The Qur’an’s idea of loss is deeper than financial failure. It is the loss of faith, moral integrity, relationships, time, and finally the soul. “Say, ‘The losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection’” (39:15). A person may accumulate wealth and influence while losing the capacity to love truth, trust others, repent, and return to Allah. That is the loss that cannot be repaired by worldly compensation.

The verse is therefore a bridge between belief and social conduct. The rebellious person is not identified only by what he says about theology; he is identified by what he does with promises, relationships, and power. Taqwā preserves covenants, repairs bonds, and prevents corruption. The believer asks whether his worship is making him more trustworthy and more beneficial to people. If it is not, he must return to the covenant rather than defend the contradiction.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 5:1 and 16:91 on fulfilling covenants, contracts, and promises.
- Qur’an 13:25 and 47:22–23 on breaking ties and severing relationships commanded by Allah.
- Qur’an 17:26, 30:41, and 28:77 on social, economic, and environmental corruption.
- Qur’an 49:10–12 on repairing relationships, avoiding suspicion, and protecting communal bonds.
- Qur’an 39:15 on the deepest form of loss: losing oneself and one’s family in the Hereafter.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on maintaining ties of kinship and the serious consequences of severing them.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:28

> How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Commentary:**

### The Argument from One’s Own Existence

“How can you deny Allah?” is a rhetorical question that calls the denier to examine the most immediate evidence available: his own existence. Human beings were not self-created. They began in a condition of non-life, received life, will experience death, will be raised again, and will return to Allah for judgment. The sequence moves from origin to destination and shows that resurrection is not an isolated claim; it completes the pattern already visible in the human condition.

The word “lifeless” does not require a single narrow interpretation. It can refer to the state before human life, the lifeless substance from which the body is formed, or the non-living stage of the human being before Allah gives him a soul and life. The Qur’an uses several descriptions of human origin—dust, clay, fluid, and a created drop—without contradiction, because they refer to different stages or aspects of the creative process (22:5; 23:12–14). The central point is dependence: a person who did not bring himself into existence cannot reasonably treat the Giver of life as absent.

### Death Is a Transition, Not Annihilation

The verse names death as part of the divine sequence. Death is real, painful, and a separation from the present world, but it is not the final disappearance of the human person. Allah causes death as He causes life, and He has authority to restore what He originated. The Qur’an says, “He is the One who gives life and causes death, and to Him you will be returned” (10:56). The certainty of death also levels worldly pretensions: wealth, status, and power cannot exempt anyone from the appointment fixed by Allah.

The return to life after death is not a second creation that would be more difficult for Allah. The Qur’an answers those who think decayed bones cannot be restored: “Say, ‘He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He has full knowledge of all creation’” (36:79). Human beings may find resurrection unimaginable because they have never witnessed the entire event, but Allah’s ability is not measured by the limits of human experience.

### The Stages of Life and Accountability

The sequence can also be read as a concise account of human purpose. The first life is a gift and a test; death ends the period of action; resurrection begins the period of recompense; and return to Allah means standing before the Judge. The Qur’an says, “He created death and life to test you as to which of you is best in deed” (67:2). Life is therefore neither a meaningless accident nor a private possession. It is an entrusted opportunity that will be evaluated.

This perspective changes how a believer understands success and failure. A person may lose property but not his reward, suffer oppression but not be forgotten, or enjoy worldly status without being safe from judgment. Every hidden deed is preserved. The return to Allah means that injustice will not remain permanently unresolved and that sincere goodness will not be wasted. It also removes the illusion that a person can escape accountability by dying before his actions receive their worldly consequences.

### A Call to Gratitude and Repentance

The verse is not only an argument for existence; it is a call to gratitude. If Allah gave life when we possessed nothing, continued to sustain us, and promises to raise us after death, then worship and obedience are the natural response. The person who recognizes this sequence cannot coherently claim absolute ownership of his body, time, or wealth. They are gifts entrusted for a fixed period.

It is also a call to repent before the return. The Qur’an repeatedly urges people to turn back while action is still possible. After death, belief and remorse no longer function as voluntary obedience. The wise person allows the certainty of return to govern present choices, not waits until the return makes every choice visible but irreversible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 10:56, 22:66, 23:12–16, and 30:40 on Allah giving life, causing death, and resurrecting.
- Qur’an 36:77–79 on the answer to doubts about the resurrection of decayed bodies.
- Qur’an 40:11 on the stages of death and life.
- Qur’an 67:2 on life and death as a test of deeds.
- Qur’an 75:36–40 on the human being not being left without purpose and the power to recreate him.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on the sequence of non-life, life, death, resurrection, and return in 2:28.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:29

> He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Commentary:**

### Creation as Provision and Trust

After reminding humanity that Allah gives life, causes death, and will resurrect, the Qur’an points to the created world. “He created everything in the earth for you” means that Allah placed within the earth resources, benefits, and signs that make human life possible. Food, water, animals, minerals, plants, shelter, and the conditions for human flourishing are all part of a created order that precedes and sustains human activity.

The phrase does not mean that every individual thing is lawful to consume or use in every manner. The Qur’an distinguishes between what is good and what is harmful, lawful and unlawful, beneficial and destructive. “Eat from what is lawful and good on the earth” (2:168), it says, while forbidding waste and corruption (7:31; 7:56). “For you” therefore indicates provision and beneficial use under Allah’s guidance, not absolute ownership or permission to exploit creation without limits.

Human beings are recipients and stewards, not independent masters. The Qur’an says that He made people successors on earth and tests them in what He has given them (6:165). A steward uses a trust according to the owner’s instructions. This principle applies to land, animals, wealth, technology, and natural resources. Gratitude requires responsible use, preservation, fairness in distribution, and restraint from the kind of consumption that harms future generations.

### The Seven Heavens

The verse then says that Allah turned toward the heaven and formed it into seven heavens. The Qur’an affirms the existence of seven heavens, but it does not require the reader to force the expression into a modern scientific category or to pretend that the exact nature of these heavens is known. They belong to the unseen realities disclosed by revelation, and the manner of their structure is not fully described to us. Other passages speak of the seven heavens in ordered layers (67:3; 71:15) and of Allah’s creation being free from disorder.

The phrase “then” does not imply that Allah needed time, effort, or a change from ignorance to knowledge. Allah’s creation is by His command, and time itself is part of the created order. Qur’anic sequence can describe the order in which realities are presented or brought into being without suggesting that Allah is subject to creaturely limitation. The verse’s final clause immediately affirms that His knowledge encompasses all things.

The heavens and the earth are not independent powers competing with Allah. They are signs, servants, and effects of His creative command. Their scale should produce humility rather than worship of nature. The Qur’an calls people to contemplate the alternation of night and day, the ships that sail, the rain, living creatures, winds, and clouds (2:164). Reflection on the universe should lead to recognition of the One who orders it, not to treating the created order as divine.

### Knowledge Completes the Argument

The final statement, “He has perfect knowledge of all things,” connects creation with divine wisdom. Allah does not create blindly or discover the world after creating it. He knows every particle, process, creature, hidden intention, and future consequence. This gives moral weight to the preceding commands. The One who provides the earth and heavens knows what benefits His servants, what harms them, and what obligations fit their created nature.

It also reassures the believer that the world is not abandoned to chaos. Human beings may not understand every event, and creation includes processes that appear destructive or mysterious, but Allah’s knowledge is complete. “He knows what enters the earth and what emerges from it, what descends from the sky and what ascends into it” (57:4). Nothing is outside His awareness, and no act of stewardship or abuse is lost from His record.

The verse therefore holds together three truths: creation is a gift, human use is accountable, and divine knowledge is complete. The proper response is gratitude expressed through lawful enjoyment, care for the earth, reflection upon the heavens, and worship of the Creator rather than the created.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:22, 6:165, and 16:10–18 on created provision and human responsibility.
- Qur’an 2:168 and 7:31, 7:56 on lawful use, moderation, and avoiding corruption.
- Qur’an 17:44, 21:30, 67:3, and 71:15 on the heavens and their ordered creation.
- Qur’an 2:164 and 57:4 on the signs of Allah in the created world and His encompassing knowledge.
- Qur’an 31:20 on Allah’s blessings, visible and hidden, being placed at human disposal.
- For the classical treatment of the seven heavens and the sequence of creation, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:29.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”

**Commentary:**

### The Appointment of Humanity on Earth

The verse begins the Qur’an’s account of Adam’s creation and humanity’s earthly responsibility. Allah tells the angels that He is placing a *khalīfah* on earth. The word is connected to succession and coming after. It can describe successive generations—human beings replacing one another across time—and, in a related sense, a steward entrusted to exercise responsibility under the authority of the true Owner. It must not be misunderstood as making human beings divine or independent rulers whose will is automatically sacred.

Human beings are placed on earth with capacities that include knowledge, choice, creativity, moral responsibility, and the ability to build or destroy. The appointment is therefore an honour and a burden. The earth is not a playground without accountability, nor is it a prison in which human life has no purpose. It is the arena in which people worship Allah, cultivate what is beneficial, establish justice, resist corruption, and are tested by what they choose to do.

The Qur’an confirms human dignity—“We have certainly honoured the children of Adam” (17:70)—but it also describes the trust of responsibility as weighty. The human being accepts a moral trust that the heavens, earth, and mountains feared to bear (33:72). Dignity and accountability are joined. Human authority is legitimate only when it remains subordinate to Allah’s commands and protects the rights of His creation.

### The Angels’ Question

The angels ask whether Allah will place on earth a being who spreads corruption and sheds blood while they glorify and sanctify Him. Their question is not an objection, quarrel, or attempt to correct Allah’s decision. Angels do not disobey Allah (66:6), and their words are best understood as a request to understand the wisdom of the appointment. They recognize the possibility of violence and disorder in a creature possessing desire and choice, while affirming their own constant worship.

Some early reports suggested that the angels had observed corruption from earlier earthly creatures, or that they inferred it from the nature of a being with competing desires. These reports are not necessary to the verse and should not be stated as certain history unless supported by revelation. The essential point is that the angels identify a real risk in human freedom: the same capacities that allow knowledge, mercy, civilization, and worship can be misused for oppression and bloodshed.

The angels’ words also teach the etiquette of inquiry. They do not accuse Allah of error; they present the concern and remain ready to receive His answer. Honest questions are not the same as rebellious challenges. A servant may ask for wisdom while already trusting the Lord’s judgment. Iblīs, by contrast, will later reject a command because his pride makes him unwilling to submit.

### “I Know What You Do Not Know”

Allah’s answer is concise: “I know what you do not know.” He does not deny the angels’ observation about human corruption; He reveals that their knowledge is incomplete. Humanity will indeed shed blood, but from humanity will also come prophets, martyrs, scholars, truthful servants, people who repent, and communities that establish mercy and justice. Adam will be taught knowledge, and among his descendants will be those who recognize Allah freely and worship Him through trial.

The answer establishes a general principle. A limited perspective may identify a real danger but still fail to grasp the wisdom of Allah’s wider plan. Human beings often judge one event by its immediate appearance, while Allah knows its causes, consequences, hidden benefits, and final place in the moral order. This does not mean that people should call every injustice good or stop resisting corruption. It means that moral action must be joined with humility: we oppose wrongdoing while acknowledging that Allah’s knowledge exceeds ours.

The verse also implies that human beings are not defined only by their capacity for violence. The angels’ question could have reduced humanity to its worst potential. Allah directs attention toward what they do not know—the capacity for knowledge, repentance, obedience, and moral achievement. A just view of humanity recognizes both vulnerability and possibility.

### Worship and Stewardship Together

The angels mention glorification and sanctification, while Allah speaks of a creature appointed on earth. The passage does not set spiritual worship against earthly responsibility. Human beings fulfil their purpose by worshipping Allah while acting responsibly within creation. Prayer without justice can become empty ritual; activism without worship can become another form of ego and power. The khalīfah is successful when devotion to Allah governs his use of knowledge and authority.

The verse therefore asks the reader to consider what kind of successor he is. Does he leave the earth more just, more protected, and more beneficial, or does he multiply harm? Does his knowledge lead to humility and service, or to domination? Humanity’s honour is not a licence to exploit. It is a call to represent obedience, mercy, and responsible care within the limits established by the Creator.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 6:165 and 17:70 on human succession, dignity, and testing through what Allah grants.
- Qur’an 33:72 on the weight of the trust accepted by humanity.
- Qur’an 66:6 on the angels’ obedience and their not disobeying Allah.
- Qur’an 38:71–72 and 51:56 on Adam’s creation and the purpose of creation in worship.
- Qur’an 30:41 and 7:56 on human corruption and the prohibition of spreading it on earth.
- For the interpretation of *khalīfah* and the angels’ question as inquiry rather than objection, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:30.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?”

**Commentary:**

### Knowledge as a Distinctive Gift

Allah responds to the angels’ question by revealing a capacity they had not been shown: He taught Adam “the names of all things.” The verse places knowledge at the centre of human dignity and responsibility. Adam is not honoured because of physical strength, wealth, race, or independent power. He is honoured through knowledge granted by Allah and through the ability to understand, name, classify, communicate, and use what he has been taught.

The phrase *asmāʾ kullihā*—“the names of all things”—has been interpreted in several ways. Many early exegetes understood it broadly: Allah taught Adam the names of created things, their qualities, uses, and relationships, including the capacity for language. Other scholars emphasized that “all” refers to what was relevant to the particular test and demonstration in the passage. The verse does not require us to claim that Adam possessed every piece of specialized knowledge that human beings would later discover. It establishes the origin of human learning in divine instruction.

Human language is more than a tool for labelling objects. It allows people to preserve knowledge, teach generations, formulate laws, tell stories, make promises, and reflect upon abstract realities. The Qur’an later says that Allah taught the human being expression (*bayān*) (55:3–4) and taught by the pen (96:4–5). These gifts make civilization possible, but they also create accountability. The more knowledge a person receives, the greater his responsibility to use it truthfully and beneficially.

### A Demonstration Before the Angels

Allah presents the named things to the angels and asks them to name them if their earlier concern amounted to complete knowledge of what humanity would become. The question is not a challenge to Allah’s knowledge; it is a demonstration for the angels and for all readers. Their concern about human corruption was real, but their perspective was partial. They did not know the capacities Allah had placed in Adam or the righteous servants who would emerge from his descendants.

The scene is also a lesson about the limits of comparative judgment. It would be wrong to praise human knowledge as though it were self-created, and it would be wrong to judge humanity only by its destructive potential. Knowledge is a gift from Allah, and its proper use can lead to worship, justice, medicine, reconciliation, and care for creation. Its misuse can produce weapons, deception, exploitation, and corruption. The lesson of Adam’s knowledge is therefore not human arrogance but grateful responsibility.

The angels are not being humiliated for lacking information that Allah had not given them. Allah’s creatures have different functions and different forms of knowledge. The point is that excellence is not measured by possessing every possible quality. The angels have their own perfection in obedience and worship; Adam is taught a capacity suited to his earthly role. A sound community likewise recognizes different forms of ability rather than reducing all worth to one narrow standard.

### Knowledge Must Lead to Humility

The verse can be misused to celebrate knowledge without morality. The Qur’an does not regard information alone as wisdom. Pharaoh’s people possessed organization and power; Iblīs possessed awareness of Allah’s command; yet neither knowledge nor intelligence saved them when pride prevented submission. True knowledge produces recognition of the One who teaches and increases humility. “Only those of His servants who have knowledge truly fear Allah” (35:28).

Adam’s knowledge will soon be followed by the angels’ acknowledgement of their limits. The sequence teaches that knowledge is honourable when it is received as a trust. The scholar, teacher, scientist, and administrator should therefore say, in effect, “Allah taught me,” rather than treating learning as a reason to despise those who know less. Knowledge that creates arrogance has missed its purpose; knowledge that leads to service fulfils the Adamic gift.

The verse also establishes the importance of teaching. Allah teaches Adam, and Adam is then made to demonstrate what he has learned. Human beings fulfil part of their stewardship by transmitting beneficial knowledge accurately. Concealing knowledge, falsifying it, or using it to manipulate the vulnerable is a betrayal of the gift. The Qur’an will later condemn those who conceal clear guidance (2:159), while praising people who teach and act upon the Book.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 55:3–4 and 96:4–5 on Allah teaching humanity expression and knowledge through the pen.
- Qur’an 35:28 on beneficial knowledge producing reverence of Allah.
- Qur’an 17:36 on not following or claiming what one does not know.
- Qur’an 58:11 on Allah raising those who believe and those given knowledge in rank.
- Qur’an 2:32, which records the angels’ acknowledgement that their knowledge is only what Allah taught them.
- For the interpretations of the names taught to Adam, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:31.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:32

> They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Commentary:**

### The Humility of the Angels

The angels respond with *subḥānaka*—“Glory be to You,” or “You are free from every imperfection.” Their first response is to declare Allah’s transcendence before admitting the limits of their knowledge. They do not defend themselves, pretend to know what they do not know, or challenge the wisdom of Allah’s decision. The scene presents a model for every student, scholar, leader, and believer: when a limit in understanding becomes clear, the proper response is humility rather than embarrassment or stubbornness.

“We have no knowledge except what You have taught us” is not a denial of the knowledge Allah had already given them. It is an acknowledgement that all their knowledge is created, granted, and bounded. Angels know what Allah teaches them; human beings know what Allah teaches them through revelation, reason, experience, and the created order. No creature possesses knowledge independently of Allah, and no amount of learning removes dependence upon Him.

This statement also protects the believer from two errors. The first is intellectual pride, which treats human discovery as self-sufficient and forgets the source of the ability to discover. The second is anti-intellectualism, which treats learning as a threat to faith. The angels’ response shows that knowledge and worship belong together. The more accurately a person knows his own limits and the scope of Allah’s knowledge, the more reverent and honest his learning should become.

### Allah Is All-Knowing and All-Wise

The angels name Allah as *al-ʿAlīm*—the All-Knowing—and *al-Ḥakīm*—the All-Wise. His knowledge encompasses everything: what is visible and hidden, what has happened and what will happen, the possibilities within a creature, and the consequences of every choice. His wisdom means that His acts are not random or purposeless. He places things where they belong, gives each creature an appropriate function, and brings about outcomes according to a perfect knowledge of causes and consequences.

The angels had identified one aspect of human nature—the capacity to spread corruption and shed blood—but Allah knew the whole reality. He knew Adam’s capacity for learning, repentance, worship, and the emergence of prophets and righteous communities. This does not mean that human corruption is secretly good or that evil becomes wisdom in itself. It means that Allah’s complete plan contains realities beyond the angels’ initial view, while He remains just in judging the corruption human beings freely choose.

The pairing of knowledge and wisdom is important. Knowledge without wisdom can be used destructively; wisdom without knowledge is impossible. Human beings may possess facts but not understand their place, timing, or consequence. Allah’s commands are therefore not arbitrary restrictions. They proceed from complete knowledge of human nature and perfect wisdom about what purifies and protects it, even when a particular command’s full rationale is not immediately known to us.

### Admitting “I Do Not Know”

The angels’ confession establishes the legitimacy of saying, “I do not know.” In an age of instant answers and public performance, people can feel pressure to speak on every subject. The Qur’anic model is different. It is better to acknowledge ignorance than to fabricate religious claims, medical advice, historical certainty, or legal rulings. Speaking without knowledge about Allah is specifically condemned (7:33), and the Qur’an commands people to ask those who possess knowledge when they do not know (16:43).

This humility does not prevent investigation. The angels receive the demonstration and are prepared to learn. Likewise, the believer can ask questions, study evidence, consult qualified scholars, and revise an opinion. Humility is not intellectual passivity; it is freedom from the need to appear omniscient. It allows learning to continue because the learner is not defending an image of perfection.

### Knowledge as a Path to Worship

The angels’ answer also teaches the correct end of knowledge. Their statement begins with glorification and ends with recognition of Allah’s names. They do not treat knowledge as a possession that raises them above worship. Knowledge should lead to *tasbīḥ*, gratitude, truthful action, and service. “My Lord, increase me in knowledge” (20:114) is a prayer for knowledge that remains under divine guidance.

The verse consequently honours both the teacher and the learner. The teacher imitates Allah’s act of teaching by conveying what is known with honesty and care. The learner imitates the angels by receiving knowledge without arrogance and crediting its source. When knowledge is joined to wisdom and humility, it becomes a means by which humanity can fulfil its trust on earth rather than a tool of corruption.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:33 and 16:43 on not speaking about Allah without knowledge and asking qualified people when one does not know.
- Qur’an 20:114 on praying for increase in knowledge.
- Qur’an 35:28 and 58:11 on knowledge, reverence, and rank.
- Qur’an 6:59 and 67:14 on Allah’s complete knowledge of the visible and hidden.
- Qur’an 17:44 and 59:24 on creation glorifying Allah and His perfect names.
- For the angelic response and the relationship between divine knowledge and wisdom, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 2:32.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:33

> Allah said, “O Adam! Inform them of their names.” Then when Adam did, Allah said, “Did I not tell you that I know the secrets of the heavens and the earth, and I know what you reveal and what you conceal?”

**Commentary:**

### Adam Demonstrates the Gift He Received

Allah commands Adam to inform the angels of the names. Adam does so, and the knowledge that Allah taught him becomes evident. The scene is not a contest in which Adam independently defeats the angels. It is a demonstration of divine teaching. Adam’s excellence is real, but its source is Allah. The verse therefore honours human learning while keeping it within the framework of dependence and gratitude.

This distinction matters whenever people speak about knowledge and achievement. Human beings can discover, classify, invent, and teach, but the capacities that make discovery possible are themselves created gifts. The Qur’an says, “Allah brought you out of your mothers’ wombs knowing nothing, and He gave you hearing, sight, and hearts so that you might be grateful” (16:78). Learning is a process of receiving, organizing, and applying gifts that no person created for himself.

Adam’s ability to name also confirms that human language is a means of understanding the world. Names allow a person to distinguish, communicate, preserve memory, and teach another generation. Yet names can be used for truth or deception. The value of language depends on whether it accurately represents reality and serves justice. The hypocrites in the opening passage used words to conceal, whereas Adam uses knowledge to clarify.

### Allah Knows the Secrets of the Heavens and Earth

Allah then reminds the angels and Adam that He knows the unseen realities of the heavens and the earth. The Arabic expression includes what is hidden from creatures and what is beyond their present perception. Allah knew Adam’s capacities before they were demonstrated, knew the future corruption and righteousness of his descendants, and knew the complete wisdom of placing humanity on earth. His knowledge is not acquired through observation, nor does it increase after a period of ignorance. It is perfect and eternal, in a manner befitting Him.

This divine knowledge is broader than the information revealed to human beings. People may know the names of many things and still not know the full purpose, consequences, or hidden relations of those things. Allah knows both the outward and the inward, the beginning and the end, and every possibility within the created order. “With Him are the keys of the unseen; no one knows them except Him” (6:59).

The verse connects the unseen with what creatures reveal and conceal. Allah knows public words, private thoughts, motives, fears, intentions, and plans. Nothing is hidden by darkness, distance, secrecy, encryption, or social reputation. “Whether you conceal your speech or state it openly, He surely knows what is hidden in the hearts” (67:13). This is a warning to the hypocrite and a comfort to the sincere person whose good deed is unseen by people.

### What People Reveal and Conceal

The reference to what is revealed and concealed may include the angels’ spoken question and what they did not express, as well as the future inner states of Adam’s descendants. It shows that Allah’s answer is based on complete knowledge, not on the partial picture available to the angels. Human beings often evaluate a person by a moment, a public statement, or a visible failure. Allah knows the intention behind the act, the opportunities received, the pressures endured, and the repentance that follows.

This does not remove the need for human standards of evidence. People are commanded to judge outward conduct fairly and not to claim access to hidden hearts. Allah’s comprehensive knowledge should make us cautious about accusation, while His commands should make us responsible for the evidence available to us. We do not know the unseen, but we do know that lying, betrayal, oppression, and concealment of testimony are wrong when established by reliable evidence.

### Knowledge and Moral Accountability

The demonstration of Adam’s knowledge also prepares the reader for the moral responsibility of humanity. Knowledge increases the ability to act, but it also increases accountability. A person who knows that an action is unjust cannot excuse himself as though he had no guidance. The Qur’an distinguishes those who know from those who do not, and repeatedly warns against concealing truth after it has become clear (2:159; 3:187).

At the same time, Allah’s knowledge is a source of hope. He knows the sincere effort that people make, the private struggle against sin, and the good intention that never receives public recognition. “Whatever good you put forward for yourselves—you will find it with Allah” (73:20). The believer therefore seeks knowledge, uses it honestly, and trusts the All-Knowing Lord to judge both visible actions and concealed realities with perfect wisdom.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 6:59, 67:13–14, and 69:18 on Allah’s encompassing knowledge of the unseen and the hidden.
- Qur’an 16:78 and 96:4–5 on the human capacities for hearing, sight, understanding, and learning.
- Qur’an 2:159 and 3:187 on the responsibility not to conceal clear truth.
- Qur’an 17:36 and 49:12 on the limits of human knowledge and the prohibition of unfounded suspicion.
- Qur’an 73:20 on Allah knowing and recording the good people put forward.
- For the demonstration of Adam’s knowledge and its connection with the angels’ limited perspective, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Saʿdī on 2:33.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:34

> And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful.

**Commentary:**

### A Prostration of Honour, Not Worship

Allah commanded the angels to prostrate before Adam, and they all obeyed. In the law of earlier communities, prostration could be an act of greeting or honour, whereas prostration as worship belongs only to Allah and is forbidden to be directed to anyone else in Islam. The command was therefore not an invitation to worship Adam. It was an honour bestowed upon Adam and a test of obedience to Allah’s command. The angels’ prostration ultimately expressed submission to Allah, who alone issued the order.

The Qur’an records similar acts of honour in earlier narratives, such as the prostration of Joseph’s family before him (12:100). These examples do not establish a general permission for Muslims to prostrate to people. The final Sharīʿah reserves prostration as worship for Allah alone. The lesson here is the angels’ obedience and Adam’s honoured position, not a ritual to be imitated.

The phrase “they all did” emphasizes the complete obedience of the angels. They did not ask whether the command suited their rank, speculate about Adam’s future descendants, or compare Adam’s created substance with their own. They heard and obeyed. Their response contrasts with the refusal of Iblīs, who allowed pride to turn a command into a dispute about status.

### Iblīs Was Not an Angel

The exception “but not Iblīs” does not mean that Iblīs was an angel who became a different species. The Qur’an explicitly says, “He was one of the jinn, and he departed from the command of his Lord” (18:50). Iblīs was present among the angels and addressed by the command because he was included in the assembly and was capable of obedience, but his origin and nature were different. Angels do not disobey Allah, whereas jinn, like human beings, possess choice and can obey or rebel.

This distinction is important because evil should not be attributed to the angelic order. Iblīs’s presence among the angels did not make him an angel; proximity to righteous people does not transform a rebellious heart. A person may sit in a place of worship, speak religious language, or possess knowledge of revelation while still refusing submission. What matters is not the appearance of association but the inward response to Allah’s command.

### Refusal and Arrogance

The Qur’an identifies two linked qualities in Iblīs: he refused and acted arrogantly. His refusal was not caused by lack of evidence. He had witnessed Allah’s command and knew the authority of the One who issued it. His problem was that his self-estimation overruled obedience. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records his argument: “I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay” (7:12). He turned a divine command into a comparison of materials and claimed that his own judgment was superior.

Arrogance in the Qur’an is not merely confident posture or a desire for excellence. The Prophet (ﷺ) defined it as rejecting truth and looking down upon people (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 91). Iblīs displayed both: he rejected a known command and considered Adam beneath him. The danger is universal. Whenever a person says, “I know better than revelation,” or refuses a truth because it would require him to lower his pride, he follows the logic of Iblīs even if he does not use the same words.

### “Becoming Unfaithful”

The translation’s “becoming unfaithful” renders *kāfirīn* in the context of Iblīs’s rejection. Kufr includes covering or rejecting truth, and Iblīs’s disbelief was expressed through deliberate disobedience and arrogance. He did not merely make an accidental mistake. He knew the command, refused it, defended the refusal, and then sought permission to mislead Adam’s descendants. This is why the Qur’an treats his response as a model of rebellion rather than a momentary lapse.

The verse nevertheless teaches that rank and proximity do not guarantee salvation. Iblīs had access to the heavenly assembly and knowledge of Allah’s command, but knowledge without submission increased his guilt. The believer therefore asks Allah for humility and obedience, not merely information. The proper response to a divine command is not to demand that it conform to the ego but to seek understanding while remaining willing to submit.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 12:100 on the prostration of honour in the story of Joseph.
- Qur’an 17:61, 18:50, and 20:116 on the command to prostrate and Iblīs’s identity as one of the jinn.
- Qur’an 7:11–18 and 38:74–85 on Iblīs’s refusal, argument, and arrogance.
- Qur’an 4:117 and 16:49 on the angels’ obedience and prostration to Allah.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 91, on arrogance as rejecting truth and looking down on people.
- For the legal and theological distinction between worshipful prostration and prostration of honour, see Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 2:34.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:35

> We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”

**Commentary:**

### Abundance Before Restriction

Allah places Adam and his wife in Paradise and gives them broad permission: eat freely from its provisions. The prohibition concerns one tree only. The structure of the command is significant. Divine law is not presented as a world in which all good things are forbidden. Allah begins with vast generosity and sets a single boundary as a test of obedience. The Qur’an repeatedly describes lawful provision as good and tells people not to forbid the adornments and wholesome things Allah has made lawful (7:32).

The verse does not name the tree, and the Qur’an does not require us to know whether it was a particular species. Early reports offered various possibilities, but none is established by a decisive Qur’anic statement or an authentic prophetic explanation. The identity of the tree is less important than the command not to approach it. The test concerns trust in Allah’s wisdom and willingness to respect a boundary even when the prohibited object appears attractive.

The wording is “do not approach this tree,” not merely “do not eat from it.” This is a protective form of prohibition. It closes the paths that lead directly to disobedience. Islam uses the same principle when it forbids not only unlawful sexual acts but also approaching them (17:32), and when it commands believers to lower their gaze and protect their modesty. Avoiding the edge of a forbidden matter is often wiser than testing how close one can come without falling.

### Adam and His Wife as a Pair

Allah addresses Adam and gives him a wife, showing that human companionship and family life belong to the original human pattern. The Qur’an describes spouses as a source of tranquillity, affection, and mercy (30:21). The passage does not make the woman a scapegoat for the fall. Later verses address both Adam and his wife, and the Qur’an elsewhere says that Satan caused both of them to slip (2:36; 7:20–22).

This is important because some religious traditions developed a narrative in which the woman alone bears responsibility for humanity’s fall. The Qur’anic account does not support that accusation. Both were warned, both were tempted, both ate, and both later repented. Neither sex carries an inherited spiritual guilt because of the event. Responsibility in Islam is individual: “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164).

### A Boundary That Protects Freedom

The prohibition was not evidence that Allah wanted to deprive Adam and his wife of joy. It defined the moral space in which their freedom operated. Freedom does not mean the absence of all limits; it means the ability to choose within a meaningful order. Without boundaries, there is no obedience, restraint, or moral responsibility—only appetite following appetite.

The command also teaches that the lawful is more abundant than the forbidden. Satan often reverses this perspective, making the single prohibited object appear more desirable than the vast provision surrounding it. The believer resists that distortion by remembering Allah’s wisdom and by training desire to accept the limits that protect it. The Qur’an says that whoever is mindful of Allah will find that He makes a way out and provides from where he does not expect (65:2–3).

### “You Would Be Wrongdoers”

The warning “or else you will be wrongdoers” translates *fa-takūnā mina al-ẓālimīn*. Wrongdoing here means placing something where it does not belong—violating the command and harming one’s own standing through disobedience. It does not imply that Adam and his wife became permanently evil or that their nature was corrupted beyond repair. The next verses will show repentance and forgiveness.

The verse thus introduces a central Qur’anic principle: divine commands are boundaries of mercy. The believer may not understand every wisdom behind a prohibition, but he knows that Allah is All-Wise and that obedience protects the human being. The test is not whether a person can invent a justification for crossing the boundary. It is whether he trusts his Lord enough to remain within it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:19–22 and 20:117–121 on the command, the tree, and Satan’s temptation.
- Qur’an 7:32 and 16:114 on Allah’s broad provision and the prohibition against declaring lawful blessings forbidden without authority.
- Qur’an 17:32 on avoiding not only sin but the paths leading toward it.
- Qur’an 6:164 on individual responsibility and the absence of inherited guilt.
- Qur’an 30:21 on marriage as tranquillity, affection, and mercy.
- For the unnamed tree and the shared responsibility of Adam and his wife, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:35.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:36

> But Satan deceived them—leading to their fall from the ˹blissful˺ state they were in, and We said, “Descend from the heavens ˹to the earth˺ as enemies to each other. You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.”

**Commentary:**

### Satan’s Deception

Satan caused Adam and his wife to slip—*fa-azallahumā al-shayṭān*. The verb suggests making someone stumble or removing him from a stable position. Satan did not possess independent power over them, nor did he force their bodies to eat. He deceived them through suggestion, false assurance, and the promise that disobedience would produce lasting life or an angelic status (7:20; 20:120). The Qur’an presents temptation as a real danger, but it does not remove moral responsibility from the person who chooses to follow it.

Satan’s method is still recognizable. He rarely begins by openly inviting a person to reject Allah. He beautifies a small step, makes the forbidden seem harmless, promises an imaginary benefit, and encourages delay in repentance. He may present disobedience as freedom, sophistication, self-care, or the only route to happiness. The Qur’an warns that Satan promises and creates false hopes, while his promises are deception (4:120). Recognizing the pattern helps a believer resist temptation before it becomes a settled habit.

The account also preserves the dignity of Adam and his wife. They were deceived and fell, but they did not persist in defending the act or blame Allah. Their story is not one of an irredeemable human nature. It is the first illustration of human vulnerability, the reality of temptation, and the possibility of repentance.

### Descent to Earth and Enmity

The command to descend marks the transition from the protected setting of Paradise to earthly life. It is a consequence of disobedience, but it should not be reduced to the idea that earthly existence is a mistake or that humanity is trapped in an alien place. Allah had already told the angels that He would place a khalīfah on earth (2:30). Earthly life is therefore part of the divine plan for human testing, worship, moral choice, and stewardship, even though Adam’s disobedience determined the circumstances of that transition.

The statement that they would be enemies to one another includes the enmity of Satan toward Adam and his descendants, and the conflicts that arise when human beings follow Satan’s path. The Qur’an makes Satan’s hostility explicit: “Surely Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy” (35:6). Human beings should not treat evil as a harmless abstract force. They are warned that the tempter wants their ruin, while they are commanded to resist him through faith, remembrance, and obedience.

The enmity among human beings is not a permission for permanent hostility. It is a description of the conflict introduced by disobedience and competing desires. The Qur’an commands justice, reconciliation, mercy, and the protection of life. The existence of an adversary does not justify becoming an aggressor. It calls for moral vigilance and resistance to oppression without abandoning the limits Allah has set.

### Residence and Provision for a Fixed Time

Earth will be a place of residence and provision for an appointed period. The words indicate both comfort and limitation. Human beings can build homes, cultivate land, form families, seek knowledge, and enjoy lawful provision, but no earthly arrangement is permanent. Every person has a fixed term, and every generation eventually hands the earth to another. “Every soul will taste death” (3:185), and “wherever you may be, death will overtake you” (4:78).

The temporary nature of earthly life is not meant to produce neglect of the world. The Qur’an teaches people to seek the Hereafter while not forgetting their share of the world and to do good as Allah has done good to them (28:77). The correct response is responsible use: take provision without worshipping it, build without arrogance, enjoy without waste, and prepare for the return.

The verse also shows that provision remains available after the fall. Allah’s judgment is accompanied by continued mercy. Humanity will face hardship, labour, and temptation, but it will also receive food, shelter, knowledge, family, revelation, and opportunities for repentance. Earth is a test, not an abandonment.

### No Doctrine of Inherited Original Sin

Islam does not teach that Adam’s descendants inherit the guilt of Adam’s disobedience. Adam sinned, recognized his mistake, and Allah accepted his repentance (2:37). Each person is born upon a natural disposition and is accountable for his own choices. “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164). The descent to earth is therefore a shared human condition, not a permanent inherited condemnation.

The lesson is more hopeful and more demanding: human beings are weak before temptation, but they are not without guidance or the ability to return. Satan’s deception is real, yet Allah’s mercy is nearer to the repentant person than his own sin. The next āyah will describe the words Allah taught Adam so that he could turn back.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:20–25, 20:120–123, and 35:6 on Satan’s deception and enmity.
- Qur’an 4:120 on Satan’s promises as deception and false hope.
- Qur’an 2:30 on humanity’s prior appointment to live responsibly on earth.
- Qur’an 3:185 and 4:78 on the fixed term and unavoidable reality of death.
- Qur’an 28:77 on seeking the Hereafter without neglecting lawful worldly responsibility.
- Qur’an 6:164 on individual responsibility and the rejection of inherited guilt.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Allah Teaches the Way Back

After Adam and his wife’s disobedience, Allah “inspired” Adam with words of repentance. The verse shows that the ability to return to Allah is itself a mercy from Allah. The sinner may recognize his wrong, but Allah teaches him how to express regret, seek forgiveness, repair what can be repaired, and begin again. Repentance is not a human invention that forces Allah to forgive; it is a door Allah opens and loves when His servant enters it.

The words are reported in the Qur’an in the supplication: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will certainly be losers” (7:23). The prayer contains the essential elements of sincere repentance. Adam and his wife acknowledge the act without euphemism, attribute no injustice to Allah, ask for forgiveness and mercy, and recognize that their own loss is the consequence of their wrong.

Their response contrasts with Iblīs’s response. Iblīs disobeyed, defended himself, blamed circumstances, and persisted in rebellion. Adam sinned, admitted his need, and returned. The difference between the two is not that one creature was incapable of error. It is the difference between humility and arrogance after error. A believer is not defined by never falling; he is defined by refusing to make the fall his permanent identity.

### The Meaning of Tawbah

Allah is called *al-Tawwāb*—the Accepter or Repeatedly Returning One—and *al-Raḥīm*—the Most Merciful. *Tawbah* means turning back. The name *al-Tawwāb* carries the meaning that Allah repeatedly accepts the repentance of those who repeatedly return sincerely. It also reminds us that Allah turns toward the servant with mercy before the servant’s repentance could have any value. He makes recognition possible, softens the heart, and provides the opportunity to repair.

Sincere repentance has identifiable features. A person stops the sin, regrets having committed it, resolves not to return to it, and, if another person’s right was violated, restores that right or seeks the injured person’s pardon. Repentance is not merely repeating words while planning to continue the same injustice. Nor is it delayed indefinitely until the person reaches the point where death makes his regret involuntary. Allah says that repentance is accepted from those who do wrong in ignorance and then turn back soon afterward, but not from those who persist until death arrives (4:17–18).

The prophetic tradition describes Allah’s joy at the repentance of His servant with an analogy of a traveller who loses his mount and provisions in a desert and then unexpectedly finds them again (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2747). The comparison does not imply emotional change or need in Allah. It communicates the greatness of divine approval and mercy in language that human beings can understand.

### No Permanent Stain from Adam’s Sin

The acceptance of Adam’s repentance is central to the Islamic understanding of human nature. Adam was not left permanently condemned, and his descendants did not inherit his guilt. The Qur’an does not teach that a human being must be redeemed from an inherited sin through another person’s sacrifice. It teaches that every person is responsible for his own deeds and that Allah forgives the one who turns to Him sincerely.

This does not make sin insignificant. Adam’s disobedience had consequences, and repentance did not erase the earthly realities that followed. Forgiveness removes guilt before Allah, but worldly consequences may still need to be endured or repaired. A person who repents from theft may be forgiven by Allah but must return the property; one who harms another must seek reconciliation where possible. Mercy and responsibility remain together.

### The Hope of the Repentant

The verse is a source of hope to every believer. Satan wants the sinner to move from sin to shame, from shame to despair, and from despair to further sin. Allah interrupts that chain by teaching the words and path of return. No one should make peace with wrongdoing, but no one should imagine that a sincere return is useless. “Indeed, Allah loves those who are constantly repentant” (2:222).

Repentance also changes how believers treat other sinners. They must not approve evil, but they should leave room for reform and avoid turning a person’s past into a permanent verdict. The one who has repented may become more humble and sincere than the one who never noticed his own weakness. Adam’s story therefore begins not only with the danger of temptation but with the mercy that makes human growth possible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:23 on Adam and his wife’s supplication of repentance.
- Qur’an 4:17–18, 25:70, and 39:53 on sincere repentance, forgiveness, and the limits of delayed repentance.
- Qur’an 2:222 on Allah’s love for those who repeatedly turn back and purify themselves.
- Qur’an 20:121–122 on Adam’s lapse, repentance, and Allah’s choosing and guiding him.
- Qur’an 6:164 on each soul bearing its own responsibility.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2747, on Allah’s joy at the repentance of His servant.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:38

> We said, “Descend all of you! Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

### Earthly Life with Guidance

The command to descend is repeated, but now it is accompanied by a promise. Humanity will live on earth, where temptation, conflict, labour, and death are real, yet Allah will not abandon people without direction. “When guidance comes to you from Me” includes the messengers, scriptures, signs, and reminders through which Allah shows humanity how to worship Him and live rightly. Earth is not a place of guidance-free punishment; it is the arena of a guided test.

The repetition also emphasizes that the experience of Adam and his wife is the beginning of a human condition shared by all their descendants. People will be born into the world, encounter choices, and face the enmity of Satan. But they are not born carrying Adam’s guilt. They receive a natural disposition toward recognizing truth and a succession of guidance suited to their circumstances. The Qur’an says that no people were left without a warner (35:24), while the final revelation is sent as guidance for all humanity.

### Following the Guidance

The promise is conditional: whoever follows Allah’s guidance will have no fear and will not grieve. Following guidance is more than hearing it or admiring it. It means accepting its truth, trusting Allah’s wisdom, obeying its commands, repenting after failure, and allowing it to govern one’s choices. The Qur’an says, “Whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray nor suffer” (20:123), using the same Adamic transition to show that the human future is defined by response to revealed direction.

The verb “follows” also suggests movement. Guidance is a path, not a decorative possession. A person may have knowledge of the road and still remain lost if he refuses to walk. Conversely, someone who knows little but sincerely follows the guidance available to him may be directed further. Allah does not require a person to know what he has not been given, but He does require him to respond faithfully to what becomes clear.

### No Fear and No Grief

The promise of no fear and no grief does not mean that believers will never feel fear, sadness, or hardship in the world. Prophets and Companions experienced loss, persecution, illness, and anxiety. The promise refers ultimately to the Hereafter, and in this life to the spiritual security that comes from knowing one’s affairs are with Allah. Fear concerns what may happen in the future; grief concerns what has already passed. Allah promises to protect the guided from ultimate terror about the future and lasting regret about the past.

The same promise appears throughout the Qur’an. The angels reassure those who affirm Allah as their Lord and remain steadfast: “Do not fear and do not grieve; rather, rejoice in the good news of Paradise” (41:30). The believers may shed tears and feel pain, but their pain does not become despair because they know that Allah sees it, rewards patience, and will bring them to a final home where fear and grief disappear.

Guidance also gives emotional direction in the present. It teaches the believer how to respond to success without arrogance, failure without hopelessness, loss without self-destruction, and danger without abandoning principle. The heart may shake, but it does not lose its ultimate anchor. This is why remembrance of Allah brings tranquillity (13:28), and why patience and prayer are described as sources of help (2:153).

### The Mercy of Being Warned

Allah’s promise is evidence of mercy. He could have left humanity to infer its purpose from the created world alone, but He sends explicit guidance and appoints messengers to explain it. The Qur’an says that messengers were sent as bearers of good news and warnings so that people would have no argument against Allah after the messengers (4:165). Guidance makes the human test fair and meaningful.

The verse therefore turns the story of Adam away from despair. The descent to earth is not the end of the relationship with Allah. It is followed by revelation, repentance, worship, and the possibility of returning to Paradise through obedience. The believer does not romanticize the world, but neither does he hate the life Allah has entrusted to him. He treats it as the place where guidance must be followed until the promised return.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 20:123–124, the parallel passage on Adam’s descent, guidance, and the consequences of turning away.
- Qur’an 35:24 and 4:165 on messengers and the establishment of proof.
- Qur’an 13:28 and 2:153 on tranquillity through remembrance, patience, and prayer.
- Qur’an 41:30–32 on the angels’ reassurance to those who remain steadfast.
- Qur’an 7:35 and 24:54 on following messengers and receiving guidance.
- For the relationship between the descent and the human mission on earth, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Saʿdī on 2:38.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:39

> But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”

**Commentary:**

### The Opposite Response to Guidance

Verse 38 promised security to those who follow Allah’s guidance. This verse gives the opposite outcome to those who disbelieve and deny His signs. The contrast is not between people with different ethnic identities or social labels. It is between two responses to truth: acceptance that leads to obedience, and rejection that treats Allah’s signs as false or irrelevant.

The word “signs”—*āyāt*—includes the revealed verses and the signs in creation and within the human self (41:53). The Qur’an’s signs are not merely miraculous interruptions of nature. They include the coherence of revelation, the truthfulness of the messenger, the order of creation, moral conscience, historical lessons, and the recurring evidence of human dependence. To deny the signs is to encounter evidence of Allah’s lordship and deliberately refuse its implication.

### “Residents of the Fire”

The Qur’an describes them as *aṣḥāb al-nār*—companions or inhabitants of the Fire. The expression indicates a settled destination, not a temporary visit. The Fire is a real consequence of knowingly rejecting Allah and dying upon that rejection. The Qur’an elsewhere says that those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers will not be forgiven in the Hereafter (4:18; 4:116), while it opens the door to forgiveness for the living who repent sincerely (39:53).

The permanence of the punishment is not a denial of Allah’s mercy. Mercy includes warning, guidance, repeated opportunities to repent, and the sending of messengers. It also includes justice: the person who knowingly rejects the Creator, persists in wrongdoing, and meets death without repentance cannot demand the reward promised to those who believed and followed. Allah knows the degree of evidence each person received and judges with complete fairness. Human beings must therefore avoid assigning a particular individual’s final fate without knowledge of that person’s circumstances and end.

The Qur’an’s descriptions of the Fire are meant to awaken responsibility, not to satisfy curiosity about punishment. Fire represents pain, humiliation, deprivation, and separation from the mercy that the rejecter persistently refused. The warnings are repeated because people are prone to postpone consequences. A person may know that death is certain and still live as though accountability were distant. Revelation makes the future morally present.

### The Meaning of “Forever”

The word “forever”—*khālidūn*—is used in the Qur’an for the lasting punishment of those who reject faith and die upon disbelief. This must be distinguished from the punishment of sinful believers, whom Allah may forgive or punish according to His justice and mercy. The Qur’an’s theological distinction is important: disbelief is not simply one more sin among others because it rejects the foundation upon which repentance and worship stand.

Yet fear of the Fire must not lead to despair. The Qur’an says, “Do not despair of Allah’s mercy” (39:53), and the Prophet (ﷺ) taught that Allah accepts repentance so long as the soul has not reached the point of death. The warning is addressed to the living, precisely because the living can still respond. The wise person treats the verse as an invitation to turn back before the final state becomes fixed.

### A Balanced Reading

The closing of this opening section preserves balance. The Qur’an gives an expansive description of guidance, then a sober warning against rejecting it. It does not conceal the consequences of unbelief, but it also does not close the door to anyone who sincerely seeks truth. The believer combines hope in Allah’s generosity with fear of becoming heedless, arrogant, or false.

The passage from verses 21–29 has argued from creation, provision, life, death, and resurrection to Allah’s right to worship. Verses 30–39 have shown Adam’s honour, human vulnerability, Satan’s hostility, repentance, and the promise of guidance. The result is a complete moral framework: humanity is created by Allah, tested on earth, supplied with guidance, and responsible for its response.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:18, 4:116, and 98:6 on disbelief, repentance, and the lasting punishment of those who die rejecting faith.
- Qur’an 39:53 on the open door of repentance before death.
- Qur’an 41:53 on signs in the horizons and within human beings.
- Qur’an 18:105–106 and 22:51 on rejecting Allah’s signs and the consequences of that rejection.
- Qur’an 20:123–124 on following guidance or turning away from it.
- For the distinction between the punishment of disbelief and the punishment of sinful believers, see Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr and al-Nawawī’s commentary on the relevant chapters of Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:40

> O children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine, and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺.

**Commentary:**

### A New Address to a People of Revelation

With this āyah, Sūrah al-Baqarah turns from the universal call to humanity and begins an extended address to the Children of Israel. The transition is significant. The Qur’an has established the basic human obligations—worship the Creator, follow guidance, and prepare for the Hereafter. It now addresses a community with a long history of revelation, prophets, covenant, deliverance, failure, and mercy. Their history is not narrated merely as an archive; it is a moral mirror for the community hearing the Qur’an and for every later people entrusted with revelation.

“Children of Israel” refers to the descendants of Jacob, whose title was Israel. The address is not a condemnation of every individual Jew, nor does it erase distinctions between sincere and corrupt members of a community. The Qur’an itself says that among the People of the Book are upright people who recite Allah’s revelations and prostrate (3:113–115). The passage criticizes specific patterns of covenant-breaking, concealment, arrogance, and rejection; it must not be transformed into a license for ethnic hatred or collective injustice.

### Remembering Favour

Allah commands them to remember His favours. Remembering in the Qur’an is more than recalling historical information. It means bringing blessings into present consciousness so that gratitude produces obedience. The favours include rescue from Pharaoh, provision in the wilderness, water from the rock, revelation through Moses, forgiveness after the calf, and the raising of prophets and scripture among them. Later verses in this sūrah will list many of these favours in detail (2:47–61).

A community’s memory determines its character. If it remembers only its grievances, it may become permanently resentful; if it remembers only its achievements, it may become proud; if it remembers Allah’s mercy and its own responsibility, it becomes grateful and morally alert. The Qur’an’s repeated command to remember is therefore a call to recover the truth about history: blessings were gifts, deliverance carried obligation, and privilege was never a substitute for obedience.

The instruction also applies to Muslims. The Children of Israel are being addressed directly, but the Qur’an preserves the account for the final community to learn from it. Muslims remember the Qur’an, the Prophet’s mission, guidance after ignorance, safety after fear, and the blessings of faith. Gratitude is not complete until it appears in conduct. “If you are grateful, I will surely increase you” (14:7), while ingratitude turns blessings into evidence against the one who received them.

### Fulfil Your Covenant

The command “fulfil your covenant” refers to the covenant Allah took from them: to worship Him alone, follow His revelation, obey His messengers, uphold justice, and convey the truth entrusted to them. The Qur’an records a covenant made with the Children of Israel that included worshipping Allah, kindness to parents and vulnerable people, prayer, alms-giving, and good speech (2:83). It also describes the covenant taken at Mount Sinai and the obligation to hold firmly to the Torah (2:63; 5:12).

A covenant is a binding moral responsibility, not a ceremonial identity. The Children of Israel could not claim the honour of being a people of scripture while deliberately concealing or altering what the scripture required. The Qur’an later condemns those who accepted some of the Book and rejected other parts (2:85). The warning applies to every community that selects revelation according to convenience—embracing teachings that support its status while quietly setting aside commands that challenge its power or desire.

The phrase “I will fulfil Mine” expresses Allah’s promise of mercy, support, forgiveness, and reward when people honour their side of the covenant. It does not establish an unconditional guarantee that a group is safe regardless of belief and conduct. The Qur’an says that Allah’s covenant is not extended to wrongdoers (2:124), and that honour before Him belongs to those with taqwā (49:13). Divine favour is generous, but it carries responsibility.

### Stand in Awe of Allah Alone

The final command is to stand in awe of Allah alone—*wa-iyyāya farhabūn*. This is not a call to live in paralyzing terror. It is a demand that ultimate fear belong to Allah rather than to Pharaoh, rulers, scholars, crowds, economic interests, or the threat of losing social status. A person who fears people more than Allah may know the truth but conceal it when truth becomes costly.

Healthy fear of Allah includes reverence, alertness, and awareness of accountability. It protects a person from the fear of creation because no created being controls the final judgment. The Qur’an asks, “Do you fear them? Allah is more worthy of your fear, if you are believers” (9:13). This does not cancel prudence or legitimate concern for safety. It prevents fear from becoming an excuse for betrayal of divine guidance.

The verse brings memory, covenant, and fear together. Remembering past mercy should produce gratitude; gratitude should lead to fulfilling the covenant; and the fear of Allah should make that fulfilment stronger than the fear of people. The address begins a sustained examination of how a community can receive revelation, benefit from divine favour, and still lose its way when memory becomes pride and covenant becomes a mere slogan.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:47–61 on the favours granted to the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 2:63, 2:83, 2:124, and 5:12 on covenant, responsibility, and the limits of inherited privilege.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on the upright among the People of the Book, showing that the critique is not a blanket judgment upon every individual.
- Qur’an 9:13 and 14:7 on fearing Allah above people and responding to favour with gratitude.
- Qur’an 49:13 on honour being measured by taqwā rather than lineage.
- For the covenantal address and its historical setting, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:40.
