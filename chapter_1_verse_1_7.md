## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Beginning with Allah’s Name

The opening formula, *Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*, is called the *basmalah*. It teaches the servant how to begin every worthy act: by remembering Allah, seeking His blessing, and acknowledging that ability and success come from Him. A person who begins in Allah’s name places the action under a moral covenant. He should not invoke Allah before an act that is forbidden, unjust, or harmful and then imagine that the formula makes it righteous.

The name *Allāh* is the proper name of the One who alone deserves worship. It gathers the meanings of divinity, lordship, perfection, and authority. The servant does not begin by invoking a vague force or a created intermediary. He begins with Allah, the Creator, Sustainer, and final Judge. This opening prepares the heart for the praise and requests that follow in Sūrah al-Fātiḥah.

### Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm

The two names *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm* come from the root *r-ḥ-m*, associated with mercy, tenderness, care, and compassionate action. *Al-Raḥmān* conveys the vastness and abundance of Allah’s mercy, which encompasses creation and provision. *Al-Raḥīm* conveys the continuing and special mercy by which Allah guides, forgives, accepts worship, and rewards His believing servants.

The repetition of mercy at the beginning of the Qur’an corrects a distorted image of religion. Allah is not approached only through fear. His mercy surrounds the servant before the servant can perform a deed, and it remains available when the servant repents after failure. The balance of hope and fear appears throughout the Qur’an: Allah warns against punishment while opening the door to forgiveness and return.

### Mercy and Justice Belong Together

Sūrah al-Fātiḥah will soon name Allah as Master of the Day of Judgment. Mercy therefore does not mean that wrongdoing has no consequence. Allah’s mercy includes sending guidance, allowing repentance, accepting sincere worship, and multiplying good deeds; His justice ensures that oppression is not ignored. The believer begins with mercy and proceeds toward accountability without separating the two.

This has ethical consequences. Whoever seeks Allah’s mercy should show mercy to people and living creatures. The Prophet ﷺ said that those who show mercy will be shown mercy by the Most Merciful, as recorded in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and other collections. Mercy is not weakness or approval of abuse. It includes protecting the vulnerable, correcting wrongdoing wisely, and refusing to become cruel while opposing cruelty.

### The Basmalah in Worship and Daily Life

The basmalah is recited before Qur’an recitation, eating, drinking, entering the home, slaughtering lawfully, and many ordinary actions. It transforms routine acts into opportunities for remembrance. Saying Allah’s name before eating reminds the servant that provision is a gift; saying it before work reminds him to earn lawfully; saying it before learning reminds him to seek knowledge with humility.

The Prophet ﷺ taught children to mention Allah’s name when eating and to eat with the right hand and from what is in front of them, in the authentic reports of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The formula should therefore be accompanied by manners, gratitude, and restraint.

### Beginning with Dependence

The grammatical structure places Allah’s name first: *bi-ism Allāh*. The letter *bāʾ* can convey seeking assistance and blessing. The servant begins by saying, in meaning, “I begin with Allah’s name and through Allah’s help.” This is the first lesson of *tawakkul*: human effort matters, but it is never independent of the Lord’s enabling.

A person who begins in Allah’s name should remember that his work will be judged by Allah. A contract, lesson, journey, meal, or conversation begun with the basmalah must not be used for deception. Remembrance is not a decoration placed upon an unethical act; it is a summons to sincerity.

### The Opening to the Mother of the Book

Sūrah al-Fātiḥah is called *Umm al-Kitāb* and *Umm al-Qurʾān*, the Mother of the Book or Mother of the Qur’an, because it gathers the central themes of revelation: praise of Allah, His mercy and authority, worship, dependence, guidance, gratitude, and the paths of salvation and error. The basmalah opens this summary by teaching that every relationship with revelation begins with Allah’s mercy.

Whether the basmalah is counted as the first verse of al-Fātiḥah is a known issue of Qur’anic recitation and jurisprudence. The user’s supplied translation numbers it as verse 1, and this commentary follows that numbering. The difference does not concern whether the formula is part of the written muṣḥaf or whether it is recited before the sūrahs; it concerns the verse-counting tradition and the audible recitation in prayer.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:156, 40:7, and 6:54 on the breadth of Allah’s mercy and His prescription of mercy.
- Qur’an 17:110 and 59:22–24 on Allah’s names and calling upon Him by them.
- Qur’an 39:53 and 2:160 on repentance and Allah’s forgiveness.
- Qur’an 11:41 and 27:30 on beginning in Allah’s name.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on mentioning Allah before eating; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, the hadith “Those who show mercy, the Most Merciful will show mercy to them.”
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395, the hadith qudsī concerning Sūrah al-Fātiḥah as a prayer divided between Allah and His servant.
- For *basmalah*, *Allāh*, *al-Raḥmān*, *al-Raḥīm*, and the verse-counting discussion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 1:1.
## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,[1]

**Commentary:**

### Al-Ḥamd Belongs to Allah

The phrase *al-ḥamdu lillāh* means that every complete and true form of praise belongs to Allah. *Ḥamd* combines praise for what Allah is and gratitude for what He gives. A person may thank someone for a particular favour, but Allah deserves praise because He possesses perfect names and attributes and because every blessing ultimately comes from Him.

The definite article in *al-ḥamd* gives the phrase breadth: all praise, in its deepest and truest sense, belongs to Allah. Created beings may be praised for honesty, beauty, courage, or generosity, but these qualities are gifts and limited reflections. Allah is the source, owner, and perfect possessor of every good quality.

### Praise before Request

Sūrah al-Fātiḥah begins with praise before it asks for guidance. This is a pattern in supplication. The servant first recognizes who Allah is, then asks from a position of humility and trust. Praise is not flattery designed to manipulate Allah. It is truthful recognition that corrects the servant’s view of himself and of the world.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that when a person begins supplication by praising Allah and sending blessings upon him, he has placed the request in its proper order. The famous hadith qudsī about al-Fātiḥah describes the first half as praise and glorification belonging to Allah, then the second half as the servant’s requests. Prayer is therefore a conversation of worship, not a list of demands.

### Rabb: Lord, Sustainer, and Educator

Allah is *Rabb al-ʿālamīn*, Lord of all worlds. *Rabb* includes creator, owner, sustainer, governor, and educator. Allah brings creation into existence, maintains it at every moment, provides for it, and guides it through stages of growth. His lordship is not distant control; it includes care, wisdom, and purposeful nurture.

The word *Rabb* also means that Allah has the right to legislate and direct. If He sustains the body, teaches the soul, and gives every blessing, then worship and obedience belong to Him. Human beings do not own themselves absolutely or possess an independent right to define good and evil against the revelation of their Lord.

### The Worlds

*Al-ʿālamīn* is the plural of *ʿālam*, a created realm or world. It includes human beings, jinn, angels, animals, the heavens, the earth, and realities known only to Allah. The plural widens the heart beyond human self-importance. Allah is not merely the God of one ethnicity, nation, language, or religious community. He is Lord of every realm and every creature.

This universal lordship is the foundation of human dignity and justice. No ruler, race, or class owns creation. Every person stands as a servant before the same Lord, even though people differ in faith, responsibility, ability, and worldly circumstance. A Muslim’s claim to guidance must lead to humility rather than racial or tribal superiority.

### Gratitude in Ease and Difficulty

Saying *al-ḥamdu lillāh* in ease is natural; saying it in difficulty requires deeper trust. The believer does not praise the pain itself or pretend that loss is pleasant. He praises Allah while grieving because Allah remains wise, merciful, and worthy of worship. He knows that a trial may contain purification, warning, protection, or reward beyond what he can see.

The Prophet ﷺ praised Allah in all circumstances and taught that the affair of a believer is good in prosperity and adversity when he responds with gratitude or patience, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This does not cancel seeking treatment, justice, or relief. It gives those efforts a truthful spiritual foundation.

### Praise and Moral Conduct

Praise is not complete when it remains on the tongue while the limbs contradict it. If Allah is praised as Lord of all worlds, the servant should not oppress His creatures, cheat in His markets, or damage His earth without concern. Gratitude for food includes avoiding waste; gratitude for wealth includes charity; gratitude for knowledge includes honest teaching.

The verse therefore moves from theology toward ethics. A person who recognizes Allah as *Rabb* becomes a caretaker rather than an absolute owner. He uses power as a trust and treats other creatures with mercy.

### The Prayer’s Universal Opening

Every recitation of al-Fātiḥah renews this relationship. The worshipper does not begin by praising his own intelligence, nation, or achievements. He praises Allah, then asks for the path that Allah alone can show. This protects the prayer from self-centredness and places the individual inside a universal community of servants.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 1:1–4 on basmalah, praise, mercy, and divine authority.
- Qur’an 6:1, 17:44, and 45:36–37 on praise belonging to Allah as Lord of all creation.
- Qur’an 2:21–22 and 16:78–81 on Allah’s lordship, provision, and the duty of gratitude.
- Qur’an 49:13 on human diversity and honour through taqwā rather than lineage.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that the believer’s affair is good in prosperity and adversity; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the hadith on praising Allah after eating and drinking.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395, the hadith qudsī explaining the division of al-Fātiḥah between Allah and His servant.
- For *ḥamd*, *Rabb*, and *ʿālamīn*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 1:2.
## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful,

**Commentary:**

### Mercy Repeated

The names *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* are repeated from the basmalah immediately after Allah is praised as Lord of all worlds. The repetition is not redundant. It places mercy at the centre of the servant’s understanding of lordship. Allah creates, sustains, commands, and judges, but His lordship is never detached from compassion and wisdom.

The repetition also prepares the heart for the mention of the Day of Judgment in the next verse. Fear of accountability must remain balanced by hope in mercy. The servant does not worship a distant power with no concern for creation. He worships the Lord whose mercy surrounds life and whose special mercy accepts repentance and rewards obedience.

### The Breadth of al-Raḥmān

*Al-Raḥmān* is a majestic name associated with vast, encompassing mercy. Allah’s general mercy appears in the existence and provision of every creature: believers and non-believers breathe, eat, receive opportunities, and benefit from the order of the world. Rain, family affection, health, knowledge, and the ability to repent are among the signs of this broad mercy.

This general mercy should produce gratitude, not entitlement. A person does not own the blessings he receives simply because they arrive regularly. He recognizes the Giver and uses them responsibly. The existence of mercy in the world also obliges people to show mercy to one another and not hoard every blessing for themselves.

### The Special Mercy of al-Raḥīm

*Al-Raḥīm* conveys continuing and particular mercy. Allah guides believers, forgives their sins, strengthens them in hardship, accepts their worship, and grants them reward in the Hereafter. His special mercy does not mean that believers are never tested. It means that tests can become means of purification and that no sincere return is ignored.

The believer invokes this name when asking for forgiveness, healing, guidance, and protection. He also tries to embody mercy in his family, work, community, and treatment of animals. The Prophet ﷺ was described as a mercy to the worlds (21:107), and his Sunnah shows that mercy includes truth, justice, and protection from harm.

### Mercy and the Human Heart

Human beings often become harsh when they feel powerful or frightened. Reciting *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* corrects both states. The servant remembers that he lives by mercy and will need mercy, so he does not treat weakness as a reason for cruelty. He disciplines children without humiliation, corrects mistakes without contempt, and opposes injustice without becoming unjust.

Mercy is not sentimental approval of every desire. A doctor may cause temporary pain to heal, a judge may impose lawful accountability to protect society, and a parent may set a boundary to prevent harm. The compassionate action is the one that seeks genuine good with wisdom and does not take pleasure in another’s suffering.

### Mercy and Repentance

The names create a door for the sinner. A person who has lied, neglected prayer, harmed a spouse, or consumed unlawful wealth may feel that return is impossible. The Qur’an repeatedly tells him not to despair of Allah’s mercy (39:53). Repentance requires stopping the sin and repairing rights, but it begins with confidence that Allah welcomes the servant who returns sincerely.

The Prophet ﷺ gave examples of Allah’s mercy exceeding human imagination, including the joy of Allah at a servant’s repentance in the hadith qudsī recorded by Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. These teachings do not make sin harmless. They prevent despair from becoming another form of unbelief.

### Mercy toward Creation

Allah’s mercy is a reason for ecological and social responsibility. The earth, animals, water, and human communities are not disposable. The Prophet ﷺ praised giving water to a thirsty creature and warned against torturing animals. He also commanded care for neighbours, relatives, orphans, workers, and prisoners.

A person who recites the Merciful’s name while enjoying cruelty should examine his worship. Mercy to creation does not replace tawḥīd, but tawḥīd should make the servant merciful where mercy is due.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 1:1–4 on basmalah, lordship, mercy, and judgement.
- Qur’an 7:156, 6:12, and 40:7 on Allah’s encompassing mercy.
- Qur’an 39:53 and 25:70 on repentance and hope in divine mercy.
- Qur’an 21:107 and 90:12–17 on the Prophetic mercy and mercy toward the vulnerable.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith qudsī on Allah’s joy at repentance; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim, reports on mercy to animals and the Prophet’s compassion.
- For *al-Raḥmān*, *al-Raḥīm*, general and special mercy, and the relation between mercy and justice, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 1:3.
## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment.

**Commentary:**

### Allah’s Absolute Authority

Allah is *Mālik* or *Malik* of the Day of Judgment. Both recitations are authentically transmitted and complementary: He is the Owner and the King. In this world, people may possess property temporarily and rulers may exercise limited authority, but on the Day of Judgment all claims of independence disappear. Allah alone has absolute ownership and command.

The phrase *Yawm al-Dīn* refers to the Day of recompense, judgement, and repayment. *Dīn* here is not merely a religious identity. It is the day when every person receives the consequence of belief, worship, injustice, patience, and rejection. The servant recites this verse while remembering that the final account belongs to Allah, not to human reputation.

### Mercy before Judgement

Sūrah al-Fātiḥah has already named Allah as the Most Compassionate and Most Merciful. It then mentions judgement. The order teaches balance. The believer hopes in mercy without becoming careless and fears judgement without despairing of mercy. Allah’s judgement is not the judgement of a human court that can be bribed, deceived, or confused.

Mercy before judgement means that Allah gave creation life, guidance, prophets, time, repentance, and opportunities for good. Justice after mercy means that people cannot treat those gifts as though they carried no responsibility. The servant begins every prayer with both hope and accountability.

### The Day of the Unveiling

The Day of Judgment will reveal what worldly life conceals. The oppressed may have no voice before a corrupt authority, while the dishonest may be praised. On that Day, Allah brings every hidden intention and deed to light. No wealth, family, office, or social reputation can purchase a different record.

The Qur’an describes the Day as one in which even an atom’s weight of good or evil is seen (99:7–8). This is a comfort to those whose quiet goodness is ignored and a warning to those who think small injustices do not matter. The servant’s private conduct is part of his public account before Allah.

### Human Power Is Temporary

On earth, kings and institutions may claim authority, but their power is limited by time, knowledge, health, and death. On the Day of Judgment, Allah asks, “To whom belongs the dominion today?” and the answer is, “To Allah, the One, the Prevailing” (40:16). The reality is always His; the Day merely makes it undeniable.

This awareness frees the believer from servility to created power. He respects legitimate authority and fulfils lawful commitments, but he does not obey a ruler, employer, family, or crowd in disobedience to Allah. He knows that every authority will itself stand before the Master of the Day.

### Accountability and Moral Seriousness

Belief in the Day of Judgment gives weight to choices that have no immediate worldly reward. A person pays an honest wage, keeps a promise, lowers his gaze, forgives an injury, or refuses a bribe because Allah will judge it. He does not say, “No one will know.” The Master knows, and the day of account is certain.

This belief also prevents despair in the face of injustice. The believer pursues lawful justice in this world but knows that no case is lost if human courts fail. Allah’s final judgement restores rights without confusion or bias.

### Intercession and the Day

On the Day of Judgment, intercession occurs only by Allah’s permission. No prophet, angel, or righteous person controls the outcome independently. The servant therefore worships Allah alone, follows the Messenger ﷺ, and hopes for the intercession Allah grants rather than relying upon family status or spiritual claims.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the truly wise person is one who remembers death and prepares for what follows it, in a report recorded by Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. Remembering the Day does not make a person withdrawn from life; it makes his life honest and purposeful.

### The Daily Rehearsal

Every recitation of this verse is a rehearsal for the final meeting. The worshipper stands before Allah now in prayer and will stand before Him then in judgement. The posture invites self-accounting: what have I done with the mercy I received, and what will I meet when Allah opens the record?

The verse also helps the servant forgive appropriately. He does not take divine judgement into his own hands, but he does not pretend that injustice is harmless. He leaves final recompense to Allah while fulfilling justice and mercy in the world.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 1:1–4 on lordship, mercy, and the Day of Judgment.
- Qur’an 40:16, 82:17–19, and 99:7–8 on Allah’s exclusive dominion and the full account of deeds.
- Qur’an 20:109 and 34:23 on intercession by Allah’s permission.
- Qur’an 3:185 and 21:35 on death, resurrection, and recompense.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the Resurrection and intercession; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, the hadith on remembering death and preparing for it.
- For *Mālik*, *Malik*, *Yawm al-Dīn*, ownership, kingship, and recompense, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 1:4.
## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.

**Commentary:**

### The Centre of the Sūrah

This verse is the turning point of Sūrah al-Fātiḥah. The first four verses speak about Allah—His name, praise, lordship, mercy, and judgement. Now the servant speaks directly to Him: “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help.” The language moves from knowledge about Allah to a covenant of worship and dependence.

The Arabic fronting of *iyyāka* gives emphasis and exclusivity: You alone. The servant does not say merely, “We worship You,” which would state an action without the same grammatical emphasis. He declares that worship belongs exclusively to Allah and that ultimate reliance belongs exclusively to Him.

### ʿIbādah: The Whole Life of Worship

*ʿIbādah* includes prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, charity, remembrance, supplication, love, fear, hope, obedience, and every lawful act done for Allah’s sake. It is not limited to rituals in a mosque. Honest work, caring for parents, maintaining a family, seeking beneficial knowledge, and defending a person’s rights can become worship when the intention and method are sound.

The servant does not worship wealth, reputation, desire, ancestors, rulers, or spiritual intermediaries. He may love people, respect teachers, use medicine, and cooperate with authorities, but he does not give any created thing the devotion and ultimate obedience owed to Allah.

### Seeking Help from Allah

*Istiʿānah* means seeking help. Allah alone is the independent source of benefit, protection, guidance, and success. Created beings can help one another through means Allah created: a doctor treats, a teacher teaches, a friend supports, and an institution provides safety. The believer thanks these means while knowing that their ability is dependent and limited.

This protects against two extremes. One person treats people as independent powers and forgets Allah. Another refuses all human help and calls that reliance. The Prophet ﷺ used practical means, consulted his Companions, accepted assistance, and asked Allah for help. Tawakkul joins effort to dependence upon Allah.

### Worship before Asking

The verse places worship before seeking help. The servant first affirms his purpose, then asks for the strength to fulfil it. He does not treat Allah as a means to worldly desires while refusing obedience. At the same time, Allah’s mercy allows a sinful or struggling person to ask. Supplication can be the first step back into worship.

The two halves support one another. Worship without seeking help can become self-righteousness, as though a person obeys through his own power. Seeking help without worship can become a demand for blessings without submission. The believer says, in meaning, “I belong to You, and I need You to remain faithful.”

### Singular Address and Plural Commitment

The servant addresses Allah in the singular—“You”—but speaks of worship and help in the plural—“we.” This reflects the community of believers who worship one Lord together. Prayer is personal, yet the worshipper joins all people who face the qiblah and recite the same words.

The plural also teaches solidarity. A Muslim does not ask Allah only for private success while ignoring the needs of other servants. He seeks help to become a source of help, and he worships in a community that protects the weak and encourages righteousness.

### The Covenant of Tawḥīd

This verse is the practical expression of *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah*, the oneness of Allah in worship. The Qur’an’s prophets called people to worship Allah alone and reject false deities (16:36). The declaration is not merely a theory about God’s existence. It governs whom the heart loves ultimately, whom it fears ultimately, and whose command it obeys when human desires conflict.

Hidden rivals can enter the heart through showing off, attachment to approval, fear of a leader, or belief that wealth guarantees safety. The servant repeatedly renews this verse to place every attachment beneath Allah.

### The Hadith of the Young Companion

The Prophet ﷺ taught Ibn ʿAbbās: “When you ask, ask Allah; when you seek help, seek help from Allah,” in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. The hadith does not deny ordinary cooperation. It teaches that the heart’s ultimate request and reliance belong to Allah, even when the hand uses created means.

The hadith qudsī concerning al-Fātiḥah also describes this verse as the servant’s portion. Allah responds to “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help” by affirming that this is between Him and His servant and that the servant will receive what he asks.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 1:1–5 on Allah’s names, lordship, judgement, worship, and dependence.
- Qur’an 16:36, 51:56, and 98:5 on the exclusive purpose of worship.
- Qur’an 5:2 and 8:24 on cooperation, responding to Allah, and helping one another in righteousness.
- Qur’an 3:159 and 42:38 on consultation and the lawful use of human means.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2516, the hadith “When you ask, ask Allah; when you seek help, seek help from Allah.”
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395, the hadith qudsī explaining the division of al-Fātiḥah and the servant’s request.
- For *iyyāka*, *ʿibādah*, *istiʿānah*, tawḥīd, and the balance between reliance and means, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 1:5.
## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path,

**Commentary:**

### The Greatest Request

After praising Allah and declaring exclusive worship and dependence, the servant asks for the greatest gift: guidance—*ihdinā*. The request is plural because the believer asks for himself and the believing community. It includes being shown the truth, recognizing it, loving it, following it, remaining firm upon it, and being brought safely to its final reward.

Guidance is not needed only by those who know nothing. The prophets and the most righteous servants continually asked Allah for firmness and increase. A person can know the path yet drift through desire, pride, distraction, or bad companionship. Every prayer renews the need for Allah to keep the heart on course.

### The Two Dimensions of Guidance

The scholars distinguish between guidance that explains and points toward the path—*hidāyat al-irshād*—and guidance that enables the heart to accept and follow it—*hidāyat al-tawfīq*. Prophets, teachers, parents, and books can explain, but Allah grants the inner ability to believe and act. The servant seeks knowledge while asking Allah to make it transformative.

The Prophet ﷺ conveyed the guidance clearly, but he could not place faith in the heart of a loved one without Allah’s permission (28:56). This distinction prevents arrogance in those who teach and despair in those whose efforts seem not to change others.

### The Straight Path

*Al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* is the clear, upright path that leads to Allah. It is singular because truth has one essential direction: tawḥīd, revelation, obedience, justice, and sincere worship. The path has breadth in lawful details and recognized scholarship, but it does not split into rival truths concerning Allah’s right to worship.

The Qur’an describes this path as the way of Allah and commands believers not to follow competing paths that divide them (6:153). The path is not merely a set of ideas. It is walked through prayer, honesty, charity, family responsibility, patience, and repentance.

### Guidance Is a Daily Need

A person asks for guidance while already praying, because guidance must be renewed. Today’s clarity does not guarantee tomorrow’s humility. The servant may be tested by wealth, fear, praise, grief, or a new temptation. The request asks Allah to protect him from losing what he knows and to open what he still does not understand.

The Prophet ﷺ frequently made supplication for a firm heart. The hearts of human beings change, and the believer does not trust his own strength alone. He takes means: Qur’an, Sunnah, sound companionship, learning, reflection, and repentance.

### Guidance in Belief and Conduct

The straight path includes correct belief about Allah, His angels, Books, messengers, and the Hereafter. It also includes the practical path of worship and character. A person who speaks correctly about creed while abusing a spouse or consuming unlawful wealth has not fully walked the path. Guidance joins theology with conduct.

The path also avoids extremes. It is not harshness that makes lawful life impossible, nor negligence that treats commands as optional. It balances worship with family, rights with mercy, courage with restraint, and hope with fear.

### A Path Shared by the Community

The plural request reminds the worshipper that guidance includes how people live together. A person asks Allah to guide the community toward justice, truthful leadership, protection of the weak, and reconciliation. He cannot sincerely ask for the Straight Path while wishing oppression upon everyone outside his group or ignoring injustice within his own family.

The community supports guidance through teaching, accessible worship, honest institutions, and compassionate correction. Scholars have a duty to clarify, and ordinary believers have a duty to learn and practise without claiming infallibility.

### Guidance and Free Will

Asking Allah for guidance does not remove human responsibility. The servant chooses to listen, study, obey, repent, and resist temptation. Allah’s enabling grace is a gift, but the person must turn toward it. The Qur’an says that those who strive in Allah’s cause are guided to His ways (29:69).

A person should not say, “If Allah wanted to guide me, I would not need to act.” The prayer itself is an act of turning, and its answer often comes through the next opportunity to obey.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 1:5–7 on worship, guidance, the Straight Path, and the paths of blessing, anger, and error.
- Qur’an 6:153 and 42:52–53 on the Straight Path and the guidance brought by revelation.
- Qur’an 28:56 and 29:69 on human conveyance, divine enabling, and striving.
- Qur’an 3:8 and 24:46 on asking Allah for firm hearts and guidance.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on saying *āmīn* after al-Fātiḥah and reports of the Prophet ﷺ supplicating for firmness; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, the hadith of Ibn ʿAbbās on asking Allah for help.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.[2]

**Commentary:**

### The Path of the Blessed

The Straight Path is made concrete through people who walked it. The servant asks not for an abstract ideal but for the way of those Allah has blessed—*ṣirāṭ alladhīna anʿamta ʿalayhim*. The Qur’an identifies these blessed people as the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous (4:69). Their lives show how guidance appears in worship, courage, patience, family life, justice, and repentance.

Following them does not mean copying every cultural detail of an earlier community. It means joining their essential submission to Allah. Ibrāhīm’s sincerity, Mūsā’s courage, Maryam’s devotion, ʿĪsā’s call to tawḥīd, Muhammad’s mercy and perseverance, and the steadfastness of the righteous all illuminate the path.

### Blessing Is More Than Comfort

Allah’s blessing—*niʿmah*—includes faith, revelation, sound understanding, righteous companionship, health used in obedience, lawful provision, and the ability to return after sin. A person can possess little worldly comfort yet be richly blessed in guidance. Conversely, abundant wealth can become a trial if it leads to arrogance or neglect.

The servant asks for blessings that bring him nearer to Allah, not merely for ease that feeds desire. If prosperity helps him give, worship, and serve, it is a blessing. If hardship produces patience and repentance, it can also become a means of blessing, though the believer is still permitted to seek relief.

### Not the Path of Anger

The phrase “those You are displeased with”—*al-maghḍūb ʿalayhim*—describes people who knowingly turn away from truth and persist in rejection or disobedience. The Qur’an’s language is a moral category, not an ethnic label. Classical tafsīr and a report from ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim mention the Jews as a historical example of knowing truth and then rejecting or concealing it, while the Qur’an itself also praises righteous Jews and people of the Book.

Muslims must not use this phrase to express hatred toward every Jew, Christian, or member of another community. Allah judges individuals by knowledge, intention, and action. The warning also applies to Muslims who know a command and deliberately abandon it for pride, wealth, or group loyalty.

### Not the Path of Error

“Those who are astray”—*al-ḍāllīn*—describes people who lose the path through ignorance, confusion, or worship without sound guidance. Classical exegetes mention Christians as a historical example in the report of ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, while the category itself is broader. A person can be sincere yet mistaken, and he should be taught with wisdom rather than mocked.

The Qur’an distinguishes between a person who lacks access to clear knowledge and one who rejects it arrogantly. This is why Muslims invite, teach, and clarify rather than declaring the final fate of individuals. Misguidance is dangerous, but the remedy is truthful guidance and mercy.

### Knowledge and Action Together

The two rejected paths represent two recurring failures. One knows truth but refuses to act; the other acts with enthusiasm but lacks sound knowledge. The Straight Path joins *ʿilm* and *ʿamal*: knowledge that leads to obedience and action guided by revelation. A person may perform many rituals while following custom rather than evidence, or possess information while refusing to submit. Both need guidance.

The believer asks Allah to protect him from these failures. He studies the Qur’an and Sunnah, follows qualified scholarship, and asks whether his knowledge produces humility, justice, and repentance.

### The Final Request Is Communal

The verse is recited in the plural, so the worshipper asks for the whole community to follow the blessed path. This includes good leadership, truthful scholarship, strong families, care for the poor, and institutions that protect justice. He also asks Allah to prevent the community from knowing truth but betraying it, or from acting passionately without knowledge.

The community must avoid both arrogance toward people who differ and relativism that denies truth. It invites with wisdom, treats peaceful people justly, and remains firm in tawḥīd.

### Al-Fātiḥah in Every Prayer

The Prophet ﷺ called al-Fātiḥah the “Mother of the Qur’an” and taught that there is no prayer for the one who does not recite its opening chapter, according to the authentic hadith. Every obligatory prayer therefore renews the request for the Straight Path. The believer does not assume that one declaration of faith makes further guidance unnecessary.

When the imām or individual completes the sūrah, the worshippers say *āmīn*, meaning “O Allah, answer.” The Prophet ﷺ taught that when *āmīn* coincides with the angels’ *āmīn*, previous sins are forgiven, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This is a mercy attached to sincere prayer, not a replacement for repentance and good action.

### Guidance after the Prayer

The answer to al-Fātiḥah appears throughout the Qur’an. Allah gives the servant revelation, the prophetic model, warnings against the paths of anger and error, and opportunities to practise. The prayer should therefore continue outside the mosque. The person who asks for the path of the blessed seeks their truthfulness, generosity, patience, and courage in his own life.

The sūrah begins with Allah’s name and praise and ends with a plea for guidance. It teaches that the servant’s journey starts with recognition of Allah’s mercy, passes through worship and dependence, and continues by following the people whom Allah has guided.
