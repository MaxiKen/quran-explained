# Sūrah 7 — al-Aʿrāf (The Heights)

*206 verses; Meccan. Al-Qurṭubī: the sūrah is Meccan "except eight verses — His saying, 'And ask them
about the town' (7:163) to His saying, 'and when We raised the mountain above them' (7:171)" — the
passage those two markers enclose. The rest of the sūrah came down in Mecca before the Hijrah, in the
same season as the other long Meccan sūrahs that carry the prophets' stories [Qurṭubī]. Al-Nasāʾī
reports from ʿĀʾishah that the Messenger of God ﷺ recited Sūrat al-Aʿrāf in the Maghrib prayer,
splitting it between the two rakʿahs — a report Abū Muḥammad ʿAbd al-Ḥaqq graded ṣaḥīḥ [Qurṭubī].
**[Saʿdī]** likewise marks it Meccan.*

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* ·
**[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr
al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** Al-Aʿrāf — "the heights," the ridge between Paradise and the Fire on which stand those
whose good and evil deeds are equal (7:46–48). **[Maʿārif]** surveys it: most of what the sūrah treats
is *al-maʿād* (the return to the Hereafter) and *al-risālah* (prophethood); the first, second and part
of the third section establish the mission of the Prophet and the reality of the requital, from the
beginning of the sūrah to about its sixth section, and from the eighth to the twenty-first sections
the sūrah gives a detailed account of past prophets — Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, Shuʿayb and Moses — of
what befell their communities, and of the reward and punishment that followed [Maʿārif]. **[Saʿdī]**
notes the same arc: the sūrah opens with the Book revealed to warn, then walks through the destroyed
towns, the scale, the story of the first creation, the prophets of the nations, and closes with Moses
and the covenant, ending on remembrance of God and the prostration of the angels who are near Him.

---

## 7:1

*"Alif Lam Mim Sad."*

**Meaning.** The sūrah opens, as several of the long sūrahs do, with separated letters: *Alif Lām Mīm
Ṣād* — four letters of the Arabic alphabet, recited as letters and not read as a word. **[Ṭabarī]**
sets out the interpretations reported for it: some said its meaning is *Anā Allāh afḍal*, "I am God,
the most excellent" — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and from Saʿīd b. Jubayr; some said it spells out the
letters of the divine name *al-Muṣawwir*, "the Fashioner," as al-Suddī held; some said it is one of the
names of God by which He swore an oath — from Ibn ʿAbbās by way of ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭalḥa; and some said it
is a name of the Qurʾān itself, from Qatāda. **[Jalālayn]** states the position that does not commit
either way: *God knows best what He means by these letters.* **[Qurṭubī]** handles them briefly,
pointing back to the long discussion at the opening of al-Baqarah, and gives the grammar: the letters
stand as the subject (*mubtadaʾ*) and *kitābun* is its predicate, so that the sense is "Alif Lām Mīm
Ṣād is a Book sent down to you"; al-Kisāʾī read the sense as "this is a Scripture." **[Ibn Kathīr]**
also refers the reader back to his treatment of the *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* at the beginning of al-Baqarah.

**Language.** The letters are recited by name — *alif, lām, mīm, ṣād* — and not joined, which is why
they carry no declension and no meaning within themselves to the reciting tongue; the very next words
supply the sense, since what follows is a description of the Book [Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** The same letters open 2:1 (*Alif Lām Mīm*) and 3:1 (*Alif Lām Mīm*); Ṣād alone
opens 38:1; and *alif lām mīm ṣād* stands in the group of openings that are followed immediately by
speech about the revelation: "Alif Lām Mīm: the Book in which there is no doubt" (2:1–2); "Alif Lām
Mīm: a Book whose verses are made firm" (11:1); "Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād" (7:1) — and then "*a Book sent down
to you*."

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** notes the pattern that runs through the *muqaṭṭaʿāt*: the separated
letters are followed in almost every case by mention of the Book, of revelation, or of the Qurʾān
(R, Z) — as if the letters were the threshold of the revelation, a summons to listen. **[Ṭabarī]**
keeps the several opinions in view without closing them, and **[Jalālayn]** hands the matter to God;
the reader is left standing at the door of the sūrah with the letters on his tongue and the Book in
front of him, which is where the sūrah wants him to stand.

## 7:2

*"A Book sent down to you — so let there be no tightness in your breast on account of it — that you may warn thereby, and a reminder for the believers."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** begins plainly: this Qurʾān, O Muḥammad, is a Book God sent down to you; and
the command laid on the Prophet is that his breast not be straitened by the warning he must deliver —
not by the burden of delivering it, not by doubt that it is from his Lord — so that he goes on with
patience, as the messengers of resolve went on, for God is with him. **[Saʿdī]** reads the verse as a
statement of the Book's greatness: it is a mighty Book that contains everything the servants need,
the divine requirements and the purposes of the law, set out firmly and in detail; *ḥaraj*, the
tightness, is constriction and doubt and confusion — let there be none of it, because this Book is the
sending down of One wise and praised; therefore let the breast be expanded by it, the soul be calmed,
the commands and prohibitions be proclaimed openly, and no censurer or opponent be feared. The purpose
of the sending down is double, as the verse names it: *that you may warn thereby* — mankind, admonishing
them and reminding them so that the proof stands against the stubborn — and *a reminder for the
believers*, who by it recall the straight path, its outward and inward works, and what stands between
a servant and walking that path; and God said the like: "And remind, for reminding benefits the
believers" (51:55) [Saʿdī].

**Language.** *Ḥaraj* in the speech of the Arabs is straitness, narrowness — already explained at 6:125,
"strait and narrow"; al-Ṭabarī notes the explanation there suffices for here. **[Qurṭubī]** gives the
range of glosses: the *ḥaraj* is constriction in conveying, because it was reported of the Prophet ﷺ
that he said, "I fear they will crush my head and leave it like a loaf" — Muslim recorded it; so the
verse is a prohibition whose sense is the removal of the constraint, meaning: do not let your breast be
straitened because they do not believe, for yours is only the delivery. **[Ibn Kathīr]** reports the
same choices: Mujāhid, Qatāda and al-Suddī said the *ḥaraj* is doubt, and it was also said it is
hesitation about conveying the Qurʾān and warning with it. **[Jalālayn]** adds the grammatical
attachment: the phrase *that you may warn thereby* is connected to *sent down*, that is, it was sent
down for the purposes of warning — "so that you convey it, lest you be called a liar" — and it is a
reminder for those who believe.

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** draws out the consolation in the verse: the One who sent the Book down on the
Prophet has Himself arranged that he remain protected, which removes the anguish. Others said the
constraint on the heart was the pain the Prophet ﷺ felt, out of his affection for people, when they
disbelieved after hearing the Qurʾān and its injunctions; the answer given him is that his obligation
is only the call and the conveying of the message — once that is done, it is not his responsibility
to see who becomes a Muslim and who does not. **[Qurṭubī]** records Mujāhid's and Qatāda's gloss of
*ḥaraj* as doubt, with the careful qualification that this is not the doubt of unbelief but the doubt
that comes with distress — as in "We know that your breast is straitened by what they say" (15:97) —
and he rejects as far-fetched the reading that makes the address outwardly to the Prophet and inwardly
to his community. The pronoun in "on account of it" is the Qurʾān; some said it is the warning, with
the sentence then needing to be read out of order; some said it is the denial the deniers heap on it
[Qurṭubī].

**Cross-references.** "Perhaps you would kill yourself with grief that they do not become believers"
(26:3); "Do not let your soul go out in sighs after them" (35:8); "And be patient as the messengers of
resolve were patient" (46:35); "We know that your breast is straitened by what they say" (15:97);
"And remind, for reminding benefits the believers" (51:55); "It is a reminder for you and your people"
(43:44) [Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī] [Ibn Kathīr].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** reads the constriction as moral, not intellectual: the doubt or
anxiety the Prophet might experience would not be about the truth of the revelation but about the
response he might receive from his people when he warns them — and *reminder* (*dhikrā*) is used here
for the Qurʾān specifically, as in 20:3 and 36:69, though elsewhere the word names the various divine
reminders, and in 40:54 it describes the Scripture given to Moses. **[Saʿdī]** closes the point for
every later reader: the Book was not sent down to burden the heart but to expand it; whoever carries
the reminder as a reminder and not as a weight finds in it the freedom of the path rather than the
tightness of the breast.

## 7:3

*"Follow what has been sent down to you from your Lord, and do not follow, apart from Him, any protectors. Little do you remember!"*

**Meaning.** **[Saʿdī]** puts the verse as an address to the servants, turning them toward the Book:
follow the Book that was meant to be sent down for your sake, and it is from your Lord — the One who
wants to complete His nurturing of you — so that if you follow it your formation is perfected, the
blessing is completed upon you, and you are guided to the best of works and the highest of character.
*And do not follow, apart from Him, any protectors* — that is, do not take them as allies, do not follow
their whims, and do not abandon the truth for their sakes; *little do you remember* — for had you
remembered and known your own interest, you would never have preferred the harmful to the beneficial
and the enemy to the friend [Saʿdī]. **[Jalālayn]** is compact: say to them: follow what has been
revealed to you from your Lord — the Qurʾān — and do not take, besides Him, any patrons, obeying them
in disobedience to Him; and it notes the variant recitation *tadhakkarūn* / *yadhakkarūn*, and the
reading *tadhkurūn*.

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** draws two *masāʾil* from the verse. First, "follow what has been sent down
to you from your Lord": the Book and the Sunna together — "and whatever the Messenger gives you, take
it; and whatever he forbids you, desist" (59:7) — an order that a group said covers the Prophet ﷺ and
his community, though the apparent sense is that it is addressed to all people; follow the religion of
Islam and the Qurʾān, make lawful what it makes lawful, forbid what it forbids, carry out its command
and avoid its prohibition. And the verse proves the abandoning of personal opinions where a text
exists. Second, "and do not follow, apart from Him, any protectors": *apart from Him* means other than
Him, the pronoun returning to the Lord; the meaning is: do not worship anything alongside Him, and do
not take as a friend anyone who has swerved from the religion of God — and whoever is content with a
doctrine, its adherents are his protectors. Mālik b. Dīnār is reported to have read *wa-lā tabtaghū min
dūnihi awliyāʾ*, "and do not seek, apart from Him, any protectors" [Qurṭubī].

**Language.** **[Jalālayn]** explains *qalīlan mā tadhakkarūn*: the *mā* is extra, merely emphasising
the littleness — "how little you are admonished." **[Saʿdī]** builds his warning on the same littleness:
remembrance is what makes a man see the difference between the beneficial and the harmful, and the
verse charges the people with a want of it.

**Cross-references.** "Follow that which has been revealed to you from your Lord" (6:155; 39:55);
"Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it" (59:7); "Say: obey God and obey the Messenger" (24:54);
the parable of the spider: "The parable of those who take protectors apart from God is that of the
spider that makes a house, and truly the frailest of houses is the spider's house, if they but knew"
(29:41) [Study Quran]; "no protector have you besides Him" (6:51; 11:20; 13:11; 18:26; 29:22; 32:4)
[Study Quran].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** notes that the verse turns from the Prophet, addressed in 7:2, to the
Prophet's community or to people in general (R); the protectors forbidden here may be the associates
and friends of the early Companions who incited them to polytheism (Ṭ, Z), and the phrase may also be
rendered "apart from it" — apart from that which has been sent down to you (R, Z). **[Ibn Kathīr]**
makes the obedience concrete: follow and imitate the unlettered Prophet, who brought you a Book revealed
for you from the Lord of everything; do not disregard what the Messenger brought you for something
else, for that is to leave the judgment of God for the judgment of another — and he compares *little
do you remember* with "most of mankind will not believe even if you desire it eagerly" (12:103) and
"if you obey most of those on the earth they will lead you away from God's path" (6:116). **[Qurṭubī]**'s
ruling stands as the practical edge of the verse: where God has spoken, opinion retires — the one who
is content with a doctrine has already chosen his protectors.

## 7:4

*"How many a town have We destroyed, so that Our might came upon it by night, or while they took their noon rest!"*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** gives the verse as a warning spoken to those who worship other than God and
set up gods and idols alongside Him: fear My wrath, lest My punishment alight on you as it alighted on
earlier communities who disobeyed My messengers and worshipped other than Me — and how many a town's
people did exactly that. *By night* means Our punishment came on them at night, before they came to the
morning; *or while they took their noon rest* means by day, at the hour of the midday pause.
**[Saʿdī]** puts the same in the key of heedlessness: the punishment came upon them in the hour of
their inattention and their delusion, crouching over them while destruction had never crossed their
hearts; when it came they could not fend it off, and the gods they had hoped in availed them nothing,
and they could not deny the wrongdoing and disobedience they had been doing. **[Jalālayn]** supplies
the grammar: *kam*, "how many," is predicative and is the direct object of *ahlaknāhā*, "We destroyed
it" — the town meaning its inhabitants — and the two adverbs describe the two halves of the day,
"sometimes it came upon them at night and sometimes by day"; *qāʾilūn* is from *al-qaylūla*, the
midday rest, even if no sleep occurs in it.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]**: *kam* is for abundance, as *rubba* is for fewness; it stands in the
position of a subject with *ahlaknā* as its predicate — "and many a town," and towns are the places
where people gather, "We destroyed them" — and the accusative reading is also possible, with the verb
understood after *kam* rather than before it, since an interrogative particle is not governed by what
precedes it; the first reading is strengthened by "and how many generations We destroyed after Noah"
(17:17), and *ahlaknā* may also be taken as an adjective of the town, since *kam* in sense is the town
itself. **[Jalālayn]** notes the same double character of the destruction's hour, which has its own
lesson: no hour is safe from the reckoning.

**Cross-references.** "How many a town We destroyed while it was wrong-doing, so that it has fallen
into ruin" (21:11); "So when they felt Our might, behold, they were running from it" (21:12);
"And how many a town We destroyed, and We raised up after them another people" (21:11; 6:6; 17:17);
"And your Lord would never destroy the towns until He sends a messenger in their midst" (28:59);
"What, then, do the townsmen feel secure that Our might will not come upon them by night while they
sleep? Or that Our might will not come upon them in the daytime while they play?" (7:97–98) [Study
Quran] [Saʿdī].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** frames the verse in the sūrah's architecture: towns destroyed for
their wrongdoing are mentioned by way of warning throughout the Qurʾān, and vv. 59–136 of this sūrah
contain the first of several lengthy passages recounting the fate of successive disbelieving towns —
the account here begins with the people of Noah and ends with the people of Pharaoh — with other
accounts at 11:25–100, 26:105–189, 27:45–58 and 54:9–42. That the punishment came by night, or while
they took their ease at midday, means that once God has warned people through His messengers, the
chastisement of those who reject the message may come not only after death but in this world at any
time, and it may come without further warning (cf. 7:97) [Study Quran]. **[Ibn Kathīr]** gives the
reason plainly — they were destroyed for defying their messengers and rejecting them, and the disgrace
of this world led them to the disgrace of the next — and cites the like of it: "And indeed, messengers
before you were mocked, but those who scoffed were surrounded by the very thing they used to mock"
(6:10).

## 7:5

*"And their plea, when Our might came upon them, was nothing but to say, 'We were wrongdoers.'"*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads the verse as the confession of the destroyed town: when the punishment
came, the only claim its people had left was the admission against themselves that they had been
wrongdoers — sinning against their own souls, wronging their Lord, and opposing His command and His
prohibition. He notes the Arabic: *daʿwā* here is *duʿāʾ*, calling out; and the word has two faces in
the language — invocation, and the claiming of a right — with this verse and "and that cry of theirs
did not cease" (21:15) belonging to the first. **[Saʿdī]** sets the verse beside its parallel: just as
God said of the towns cut off before them, "When they felt Our might, behold, they were running from
it: do not run, but return to that wherein you lived at ease and to your homes, that you may be
questioned — they said, 'Woe to us, we were wrongdoers,' and that cry of theirs ceased not until We
made them a mown field, stilled" (21:12–15). Their groaning was not repentance in time; it was the
recognition of the condemned. **[Jalālayn]**: and their only plea, their only words, when Our might
came upon them was to say, "We were evildoers indeed." **[Study Quran]** notes the pattern in the
Qurʾān: the disbelievers are sometimes presented as denying or excusing their error even when called
to account (4:97; 6:23–24; 16:28), and sometimes as admitting their wrongdoing once it is too late for
repentance to help them (21:46; 23:106; 26:97; 40:85; 68:29–31; 74:43–47).

**Language.** The sentence is built as an exception: *their plea was nothing but to say* — the
"nothing but" strips the men of every defence and leaves them with a single sentence, and it is a
sentence against themselves **[Ṭabarī]**.

**Cross-references.** "They said, 'Woe to us, we were wrongdoers'" (21:14); "And that cry of theirs
did not cease until We made them a mown field, stilled" (21:15); "Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves"
(7:23); "They will say, 'Yes, indeed, the promise of our Lord has come to pass'" (7:53);
"Say: I fear, if I disobey my Lord, the punishment of a dreadful day" (6:15) [Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī].

**Reflection.** **[Ṭabarī]**'s point is that the confession was true — they were wrongdoers — but the
hour in which a man can profit from such a confession is the hour before it is forced out of him.
**[Saʿdī]** draws the warning for the living: the verse is set where it is, after the account of the
ruined towns, so that the reader says the words *we were wrongdoers* in the time of safety, when the
admission still does its work, and not in the time when it is only the record of a loss.

## 7:6

*"Then surely We shall question those to whom it was sent, and surely We shall question the messengers."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** states the sense at once: We shall question the nations to whom My messengers
were sent — what did you do with what came to you from Me, of command and prohibition? Did you act on
what I commanded, desist from what I forbade, obey My command, or disobey and oppose it? — and We shall
question the messengers whom I sent to the nations: did they deliver My messages and carry to them
what I ordered them to carry, or did they fall short and fail to deliver? That was how the people of
interpretation read it: Ibn ʿAbbās said, "God asks the people about how they answered the messengers,
and He asks the messengers about what they conveyed" [Ṭabarī]. **[Saʿdī]** gives the questions their
Qurʾānic frame: We shall ask the nations about the answer they gave their messengers — as He said,
"On the day when He calls them and says, 'What answer did you give the messengers?'" (28:65) — and We
shall ask the messengers about their delivery of their Lord's messages and about the response their
nations gave them. **[Jalālayn]** is equally concise: We shall question those communities about their
response to the messengers, and to what extent they put into practice what was conveyed to them; and
We shall question the messengers about the conveying of it.

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** draws the doctrinal consequence: the verse is a proof that the disbelievers
are brought to account — "then it is upon Us to reckon them" (88:26) — and he sets beside it "and the
criminals will not be asked about their sins" (28:78), explaining that the Hereafter has stations:
a station where they are questioned for the reckoning, and a station where they are not; the denial in
28:78 belongs to the hour when they are already settled in the punishment. Their questioning is a
questioning of reproof, of exposure and of rebuke, while the questioning of the messengers is a
questioning that calls them to bear witness and to speak out — about how the people answered them —
which is the sense of "that He may ask the truthful about their truthfulness" (33:8). He also records
another construction: that "those to whom it was sent" may mean the prophets, and "the messengers" the
angels who were sent to them.

**Cross-references.** "On the day when He calls them and says, 'What answer did you give the
messengers?'" (28:65); "And the criminals will not be asked about their sins" (28:78); "Then it is upon
Us to reckon them" (88:26); "that He may ask the truthful about their truthfulness" (33:8); "Every
soul shall know what it has sent ahead and what it has left behind" (82:5) [Qurṭubī] [Saʿdī].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** takes up the apparent contradiction between the questioning here and
the verses that say the guilty will not be questioned (28:78) or that on that day neither man nor jinn
will be questioned about his sin (55:39): al-Rāzī resolves it by saying that people will not be
questioned about their actions as such — God already knows them, and they are already recorded in the
book each will receive (17:71; 69:19) — but they will be questioned about why they did those deeds and
what turned them away from the good ones (R). The commentators add that the questioning of 7:6 is a
means of rebuke (R, Ṭ, Z), and that the messengers are asked only about delivery, since the Qurʾān
states that the duty of the Prophet, like that of every prophet, is simply to deliver the message
(3:20; 5:92, 99; 13:40; 16:35, 82; 29:18): the reaction of the people lies beyond the Prophet's
control, and so beyond his accountability [Study Quran]. **[Saʿdī]** leaves the reader with the same
weight in a different form: the questions of that day are not the questions of an uninformed examiner
but of a judge who lets the record speak.

## 7:7

*"Then surely We shall narrate to them with knowledge, and We were never absent."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** explains: We shall inform the messengers and those to whom they were sent,
with certain knowledge, of what they did in the world with regard to what I commanded and forbade —
*and We were never absent* from them, nor from the deeds they were doing. **[Saʿdī]** expands the
audience: We shall recount to all creation what they did, with a knowledge that belongs to Him of their
works, and We were never absent at any time — as He said, "God has recorded it, and they have forgotten
it" (58:6), and "and We were never unaware of the creation" (23:17). **[Jalālayn]**: We shall narrate
to them with knowledge — We shall inform them with previous knowledge of what they did — for We were
not absent when the messengers conveyed the message, nor were We absent during the time of the earlier
communities and what they did.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** raises the question that a reader will ask: how can God question the messengers
and those sent to, when He also says He narrates to them with knowledge of their deeds? His answer:
this is not a question of seeking guidance, nor a question that waits to learn what He did not know —
it is a question of rebuke and confirmation whose meaning is a statement, like a man saying to another,
"Did I not do you good, and you did me evil? Did I not keep your ties, and you cut me off?" So the
questioning of those to whom the messengers were sent will take the form: "Did My messengers not come
to you with the clear proofs? Did I not send you warners to warn you of My punishment and My
retribution on this day for whoever disbelieved in Me and worshipped other than Me?" — as He said He
will say to them on that day: "Did I not covenant with you, O children of Adam, that you should not
worship Satan, for he is a clear enemy to you, and that you should worship Me? This is a straight path"
(36:60–61) [Ṭabarī]. **[Qurṭubī]** records Ibn ʿAbbās's reading of the same verses: the book of his
deeds speaks against him.

**Cross-references.** "God has recorded it, and they have forgotten it" (58:6); "And We were never
unaware of the creation" (23:17); "Did I not covenant with you, O children of Adam" (36:60–61);
"This is Our Book that speaks against you with truth; We were recording what you used to do" (45:29);
"And there is no absent thing in the heaven or on the earth" (34:3) [Ṭabarī] [Saʿdī].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** draws the two attributes the verse carries: if God's recounting of
people's earthly deeds indicates His Omniscience, the statement that He was never absent indicates His
Omnipresence and nearness — "and He is with you wherever you are, and God sees whatsoever you do"
(57:4). And it records a ḥadīth in which the Prophet ﷺ said: "There is not one of you to whom his Lord
will not speak without an interpreter between them on the Day of Resurrection; and He will say to him,
'Do you remember the day on which you did this and that?' so that He may remind him of what he did in
the world" (Ṭ). The verse is set in the sūrah, then, as the other face of the questioning: the questions
and the narration are one act, and no man will be asked about anything that God was not present to see
[Study Quran].

## 7:8

*"And the weighing on that Day is the truth. So whoever's scales are heavy — it is they who are the successful."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** begins with the grammar of the sentence: *al-wazn*, "the weighing," is the
infinitive from "I weighed such and such, I weigh it, a weighing and a weight," like "I promised him,
a promise and a pledge"; it is raised as the subject and *al-ḥaqq* is its predicate, and the sense of
the whole is: and the weighing on the day when We question those to whom the messengers were sent and
the messengers themselves — that weighing is the truth, meaning the justice. He records the readings
of the early authorities: Mujāhid said *al-wazn* in this place is *al-qaḍāʾ*, the decree; Mujāhid also
said *al-ḥaqq* here is justice; others, among them al-Suddī, said it is the weighing of deeds; and
al-Ṭabarī settles on the view reported from ʿAmr b. Dīnār — that it is the known balance with which
deeds are weighed, the good of them and the bad — since the reports from the Prophet ﷺ confirm that
this is a scale by which works are weighed. **[Saʿdī]** states the point of the *ḥaqq*: the weighing on
the Day of Resurrection will be with justice and equity, in which there is no deviation and no
wrongdoing in any respect; then whoever's scales are heavy — his good deeds outweighing his evil ones —
those are the successful, meaning those saved from the hated and attaining the loved, who have secured
the great profit and the lasting happiness. **[Jalālayn]** fills in the picture: the weighing of the
deeds, or of the scrolls of deeds, will be on that day — the day of the questioning, namely the Day of
Resurrection — a true and just weighing; and it notes that the balance has a tongue and two palms as
scales, as reported in a ḥadīth; those whose scales are heavy with good deeds are the triumphant.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ṭabarī]** collects reports on what the balance is and how it will work.
ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr said: a man, huge and tall and a great eater and drinker, will be brought and he will
not weigh a mosquito's wing — and another report from him: a man tall and great will be set in the
balance and will not rise to the weight of a fly's wing. Ḥudhayfa said: the one in charge of the
balances on the Day of Resurrection is Gabriel — "O Gabriel, weigh between them!" — and some will
recover from others; and there is no gold or silver there: if the wrongdoer has good deeds, they are
taken from his good deeds and given to the one he wronged, and if he has no good deeds, some of the
sins of his companion are loaded onto him, and the man returns carrying the like of mountains — and
that is the meaning of "the weighing on that Day is the truth" — *(weak)*, since the isnād of this
report runs through ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz b. Abān, whom al-Ṭabarī's editor describes as a liar who used to
fabricate ḥadīths. Ibn Jurayj said: ʿAmr b. Dīnār told me concerning "the weighing on that Day is the
truth," "we see a balance and two pans," and he heard ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr say, "the great tall man is
placed in the balance and does not stand against the wing of a fly." Then the famous report: ʿAbdullāh
b. ʿAmr said, "A man of my community will be called out before all creation on the Day of Resurrection,
and ninety-nine scrolls will be spread out for him, each scroll as far as the eye can see; God will
say, 'Do you deny any of this?' He will say, 'No, my Lord.' He will say, 'Did My recording angels
wrong you?' He will say, 'No.' He will say, 'Do you have any excuse? Any good deed?' The man will be
overawed and say, 'No.' He will say, 'Rather, you have good deeds with Us, and no injustice will be
done to you today.' Then a card will be brought out containing, 'I bear witness that there is no god
but God, and that Muḥammad is His servant and messenger.' The man will say, 'My Lord, what is this
card against these scrolls?' He will say, 'You will not be wronged' — and the scrolls are placed in
one pan and the card in the other, and the scrolls fly up and the card weighs heavy." Al-Tirmidhī
added, "and nothing is heavy alongside the Name of God," and graded it *ḥasan gharīb*; Aḥmad recorded
it in his *Musnad*, and Ibn Mājah, and al-Ḥākim in *al-Mustadrak* where he graded it sound on the
criteria of Muslim, al-Dhahabī concurring — and al-Ṭabarī's editor calls its isnād sound [Ṭabarī]
[Qurṭubī]. And from Abū al-Dardāʾ, the Prophet ﷺ said: "There is nothing placed in the balance heavier
than good character" — al-Tirmidhī graded it *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ* [Ṭabarī] [Study Quran].

**Belief.** **[Qurṭubī]** sets out the dispute over what is weighed. Ibn ʿUmar said the scrolls of the
servants' deeds are weighed — and this is the sound view, and what the reports came with; others said
the balance is the Book in which the deeds of creation are written; Mujāhid said the balance is the
good and the evil deeds in their very selves; and Mujāhid, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Aʿmash said the weighing
and the balance mean justice and judgment, the mention of a balance being a figure of speech — as one
says, "this speech is in the weight of this," that is, it equals it, even where no weighing takes
place. Al-Zajjāj said this is admissible in the language, but the better is to follow what came in the
sound chains of transmission mentioning the balance; and al-Qushayrī praised al-Zajjāj for it, adding
that if the balance were carried over to metaphor, one would have to carry the Path over to metaphor
too, and Paradise and Hell to what befalls spirits without bodies, and the satans and jinn to blameworthy
character and the angels to praiseworthy faculties — whereas the first community unanimously took these
apparent meanings without interpretation, and what the community unanimously refuses to interpret
becomes a text. He records that the Muʿtazila denied the scale, arguing that accidents cannot be
weighed since they do not subsist in themselves, and that some theologians answered that God turns
accidents into bodies and weighs them — which is not sound with us; the sound position is that the pans
grow heavy and light by the scrolls in which the deeds are written, as in the report of the man whose
balance was nearly lightened by his good deeds, whereupon a parchment bearing "There is no god but God"
was placed in it and it grew heavy. From Ṣafwān b. Muḥriz, who said a man asked Ibn ʿUmar how he had
heard the Messenger of God ﷺ describe the intimate conversation: he said, I heard him say, "The believer
is drawn near his Lord on the Day of Resurrection until He places His covering over him and has him
acknowledge his sins — 'Do you know this? Do you know that?' — and he says, 'Yes, my Lord' — and God
says, 'I concealed them for you in the world, and I forgive them for you today' — and he is given the
scroll of his good deeds; while the disbelievers and hypocrites are called out before all creation:
'These are the ones who lied against God'" — and his words "he is given the scroll of his good deeds"
are a proof that the deeds are written in scrolls and weighed [Qurṭubī]. **[Qurṭubī]** also notes the
form *mawāzīn*: it is the plural of *mīzān*, originally *miwzān*, the *wāw* turned to *yāʾ* because of
the *kasra* before it; it may be that there are many balances for a single worker, each weighing one
kind of his deeds; or that it is a single balance expressed in the plural, as one says, "so-and-so went
out to Mecca on mules" and "to Baṣra in ships," or as in "the people of Noah denied the messengers"
(26:105) though only one messenger came to them; or *mawāzīn* may be the plural of *mawzūn*, the weighed
things, meaning the deeds themselves. **[Ibn Kathīr]** adds that the deeds will be placed on the
balance though they are not material objects — God will give those deeds physical weight on the Day of
Resurrection, which al-Baghawī said was reported from Ibn ʿAbbās — and he cites 21:47, "and if there be
the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it," and 4:40, "surely God wrongs not the weight of a speck
of dust," then 101:6–11 and 23:101–103 with their heavy and light scales. **[Study Quran]** notes that
a ḥadīth says, "Nothing is heavier in the balance than good character" (Ṭ), and another indicates that
belief in the One God and in the prophethood of Muhammad outweighs even a vast record of error and sin
(R).

**Cross-references.** "And We shall set up the balances of justice on the Day of Resurrection, so that
no soul will be wronged in anything" (21:47); "Then as for him whose scales are heavy, he will live a
pleasant life; and as for him whose scales are light, his home will be the abyss" (101:6–9);
"Then those whose scales are heavy, they are the successful; and those whose scales are light, they are
those who lose themselves, in Hell abiding" (23:101–103) [Ibn Kathīr]; "So whoever does an atom's
weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (99:7–8)
[Study Quran].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** sets the image of the balance in its larger Qurʾānic frame: the
weighing of good and evil deeds appears elsewhere as a figure of divine judgment (21:47; 23:102–3;
101:6–9), and the figure is consistent with the repeated Qurʾānic commands to weigh with justice and
integrity in trade (6:152; 7:85; 11:84–85; 17:35; 26:182–83; 55:8–9), and with the theme that God
created all things in due measure and "set the balance" (al-mīzān) for the created order (42:17; 55:7;
57:25). Taken together, the representation suggests a universal balance that is in the nature of
things, forming the basis of just dealing among people and according to which human actions will be
measured and recompensed in the next world — while, like the questioning in v. 6, it is not that God
needs information or a criterion outside Himself: it is a rebuke that forces the wrongdoer to
acknowledge his wrongdoing and surrender his excuses (Z), and an assurance to every soul that it is
not being wronged, since all deeds are measured by one scale. **[Ṭabarī]** frames the same assurance
from the other side: God weighs His servants' deeds — the man and the scrolls of his good deeds in one
pan, the scrolls of his evil deeds in the other — as an argument for His creatures, like the speaking
of their hands and feet and the other proofs He will bring against them; and to whoever denies that,
the answer is that God has told us He makes the scales of some heavy and of others light, and the
reports from the Messenger of God ﷺ have come one after another confirming it.

## 7:9

*"And whoever's scales are light — it is they who have ruined their own souls, because they wrongfully treated Our signs."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]**: whoever's scales of good deeds are light, not made heavy by the affirmation
of God's oneness, by faith in Him and His messenger, and by following His command and prohibition —
those are the ones who have cheated themselves of their share of God's abundant reward and honour, and
that is the meaning of "because they wrongfully treated Our signs": because they denied God's proofs
and signs, neither affirming their truth nor being certain of their reality — as Mujāhid said, "his
good deeds." **[Saʿdī]** unfolds the loss: whoever's scales are light, his evil deeds outweighing and
the verdict going to them, those are the ones who have lost their own souls, since the enduring bliss
has slipped away from them and the painful punishment has come to them — and this because they
*wrongfully treated Our signs*, not submitting to them as it was their duty to submit. **[Jalālayn]**:
and whoever's scales are light because of evil deeds, those are the ones who have ruined their souls
by sending them toward the Fire, because they wrongfully — that is, knowingly — denied Our signs.
**[Qurṭubī]** gives the grammar of *khisrān*: the scales being light is the loss; and the verse joins
the two halves of the scale — the one who prospers and the one who is ruined — in a single frame, so
that each reading of the one is a reading of the other.

**Cross-references.** "And those whose scales are light, they are those who lose themselves, in Hell
abiding" (23:103); "Look how they invent lies against God; that suffices as a manifest sin" (4:50);
"Then as for him whose scales are light, his home will be the abyss — and what will make you know
what it is? A fiercely blazing fire" (101:8–11) [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī]; "And whoever does an atom's
weight of evil will see it" (99:8).

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** notes the same rebuke and the same assurance as in v. 8: the balance
that is light for the wrongdoer and heavy for the doer of good is one balance, so no one falls short
by another's measure and no one prospers by another's weight — the justice of the Day of Weighing is
what makes it a day of accounting for all. **[Saʿdī]** draws out the phrase "they have ruined their
own souls": the loss spoken of is self-inflicted, and the verse's charge is that of a man who has
wrecked his own house and then asks after the reason.

## 7:10

*"And We have indeed established you in the earth and made for you therein means of livelihood. Little do you give thanks!"*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** reads the opening as God's favour: We have made the earth a broad pavement
and a cradle for you, O people, and set in it the means by which you live. **[Qurṭubī]** puts the same
in fuller terms: We made it for you a dwelling place and a bed, and prepared for you in it the causes
of livelihood; *maʿāyish* is the plural of *maʿīsha*, meaning what a man lives by, of food and drink
and whatever makes life go on — the verb being *ʿāsha yaʿīshu*, giving *ʿayshan wa maʿāshan wa
maʿīshan wa maʿīshatan wa ʿīshatan*; al-Zajjāj said the *maʿīsha* is what one uses to arrive at life.
**[Saʿdī]** draws the favour out: God speaks as the Benefactor, reminding His servants of the dwelling
and the livelihood — "We established you in the earth": We made it ready for you so that you are able
to build on it and plough it and turn its benefits to use; "and made for you therein livelihoods": from
what the trees and plants bring out, and the minerals of the earth, and the kinds of crafts and trades
— for it is He who prepared it and subjected its causes; "little do you give thanks" to God, who has
blessed you with the varieties of blessing and turned away from you the calamities. **[Jalālayn]**
supplies the language: We gave you power, O children of Adam, in the earth, and appointed for you in it
livelihoods — *maʿāyish*, the plural of *maʿīsha*, the means by which you subsist — and little thanks
you show, with the *mā* emphasising the littleness. **[Ibn Kathīr]** gathers the bounties under the
same head: all the bounties in the heavens and the earth are for the benefit of mankind; God made the
earth a fixed place for dwelling, placed firm mountains and rivers on it, made homes, and allowed
people to use its benefits; He made the clouds work for them so that their sustenance comes out of the
earth, and He created the ways and means of earnings, commerce and the professions — yet most of them
give little thanks; and he cites "And if you count the blessings of God, you will not be able to number
them; indeed, man is unjust, ungrateful" (14:34).

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** treats the word *maʿāyish* at length: it is *mafʿila* according to al-Akhfash
and many grammarians; al-Aʿraj recited it with *hamza*, *maʿāyiʾish*, and Khārija b. Muṣʿab reported
the like from Nāfiʿ; al-Naḥḥās said the *hamza* is a mistake that is not permitted, since the singular
is *maʿīsha*, so the *alif* of connection is added while it and the *yāʾ* are both unvowelled, and one
of them must be given a vowel since elision is not possible and the *alif* cannot be vowelled — so the
*yāʾ* takes the vowel that belonged to it in the singular, as in *manāwir* and *maqāwim* from *mawāwin*
and *maqāwim*. **[Ṭabarī]** notes only the plain sense of the verse, that God established them in the
earth and set their livelihoods in it, and the charge of the last words.

**Cross-references.** "It is He who made the earth a bed for you and the sky a canopy, and sent down
water from the sky" (2:22); "And the earth — We have spread it out, and how well We have laid it out"
(51:48); "And We placed within it firm mountains above it, and We gave you therein that by which you
live" (15:19–20); "Is He who created the heavens and the earth and sent down for you water from the
sky, with which We cause to grow pleasant gardens" (27:60) [Ibn Kathīr] [Saʿdī].

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** notes that the verse is addressed to people generally, and that
God's establishing them (*makkannā*) may mean He has given them a dwelling place on the earth (Ṭ; cf.
2:36; 6:98; 7:24), and may also mean He has given them, or delegated to them, a certain power in the
world (JJ). The verse, it observes, serves as an introduction to the story of Adam's creation and fall
that follows immediately, and at the end of two separate Qurʾānic accounts of that same incident God
mentions that He is establishing a dwelling for human beings on the earth (2:36; 7:24) — so the
livelihoods and the establishment here are the outward face of the creature's appointed place, and the
little thanks of the last words is the first note of the long account of human heedlessness that the
sūrah is about to tell.
