# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 121–130

This is the hinge of the surah. Having spent a hundred and twenty verses dismantling the claim of the Children of Israel to a standing inherited by descent, the surah now supplies the alternative genealogy, and it locates it in Abraham. It begins by defining what genuine adherence to scripture is, and noting that the People of the Book who actually practise it are the ones who believe (121). It restates the old address and the old warning about the Day when no proxy, ransom, intercession or rescue will avail (122–123). And then it turns to Abraham: tested, and made a leader for all people, with the stipulation that the covenant does not pass to wrongdoers — which settles the whole descent question in a sentence (124). Then the House: made a place of return and a sanctuary, entrusted to Abraham and Ishmael for purification (125). Then Abraham's prayer for the city and Allah's answer about provision (126). Then the two of them raising the foundations and praying the prayer of people who fear their work will not be accepted (127). Then the prayer for steadfastness, for a submitting community, and for the rites to be shown (128). Then the prayer for a messenger — answered, the tradition holds, in Muhammad ﷺ (129). And the closing verdict: who would turn away from the way of Abraham except someone who has made a fool of his own self (130)?

---

## Verse 121

**"Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it. As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers."**

In simple terms: those who were given revelation and who recite it as it ought to be recited — with adherence, not merely with the voice — are the ones who truly believe. Those who reject it are the losers.

*Alladhina ataynahum al-kitaba yatlunahu haqqa tilawatihi* — "those to whom We gave the Book, who recite it with its true recitation." The crux is the phrase *haqqa tilawatihi*, and the commentators were emphatic about it because the alternative reading would make the verse false.

If *tilawa* here meant only vocal recitation, the verse would be contradicted by the surah itself. Verse 113 has just described the Jews and the Christians declaring one another to be upon nothing, and the clause that made that indefensible was *wa hum yatluna al-kitab* — "while they **recite** the Book." They recited it. Vocal recitation is exactly what they did, and it did not produce belief. So *haqqa tilawatihi* cannot mean pronunciation.

What it means is recitation-with-adherence: following it, acting by it, holding what it makes lawful to be lawful and what it forbids to be forbidden, not displacing its words from their places, and not selecting the parts that suit. This is the reading transmitted from Ibn 'Abbas and followed by the majority. And it makes the verse the completion of verse 113 rather than its contradiction: 113 told us what recitation without adherence looks like, and 121 tells us what recitation *is*.

*Ula'ika yu'minuna bihi* — "those believe in it." What does "it" refer to? Two readings are transmitted. On the first, "it" is the scripture they were given: those who truly follow the Torah genuinely believe in it. On the second — and this is the stronger reading in context — "it" is the message of Muhammad ﷺ: those who truly adhere to the earlier scripture are the ones who believe in this revelation, because their own Book leads them to it.

The second reading is what the whole passage has been arguing. The Qur'an confirms what they have (2:41, 2:89, 2:101); they recognise it as they recognise their own sons (2:146); and so proper adherence to their scripture terminates in acceptance of him. On this reading the verse is a claim with real content: **the People of the Book who genuinely follow their Book become Muslims.** Not all of them — the surah has been unsparing about those who do not — but the ones described here, the ones who recite it *haqqa tilawatih*, do.

*Wa man yakfur bihi fa-ula'ika hum al-khasirun* — "and whoever disbelieves in it, those are the losers." *Khasirun* is the same word used at verse 27 for the covenant-breakers and at verse 64 for what they would have been but for grace.

For practice, this verse belongs to Muslim readers before it belongs to anyone else, and the tradition has always read it that way.

There is a hadith recorded by Muslim from Abu Malik al-Ash'ari in which the Prophet ﷺ said: "Purity is half of faith... and the Qur'an is a proof for you or against you."

That is this verse compressed into a sentence. The recited Book is either for you or against you. There is no third condition in which a person recites the Qur'an regularly and it does nothing — recitation that is not adhered to accumulates *against* the reciter, because it is evidence that he knew and did not act. That is why the tradition speaks of the Qur'an as an intercessor on the Day of Resurrection for the one who lived by it, and as a prosecutor against the one who did not.

The second application is the test the verse supplies, and it is concrete. Haqqa tilawatih is not a mystical state. It means: when the Book says something is forbidden, you treat it as forbidden. When it gives a ruling that falls on you, the ruling falls on you. When it commands something inconvenient, you do it. When it praises something you do not possess, you want it. A person can check this in ten minutes against the last passage he recited.

And the third is the promise hidden in the verse, which is the reason it is placed here. Proper adherence to whatever light you have leads to more light. The People of the Book who truly followed what they had were brought by it to Muhammad ﷺ, and the transmitted accounts of the earliest converts among them — 'Abdullah ibn Salam among them — describe exactly that trajectory. The converse is equally true and equally sobering: light that is not followed does not stay neutral. It dims.

## Verse 122

**"O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others."**

In simple terms: a repeat of the address that opened the catalogue of favours — remember what I gave you, and that I favoured you above the peoples.

The wording here is identical to verse 47, where this address opened the catalogue of ten favours. The repetition is not redundancy and it is worth naming what it is doing.

The surah used this address at verse 47 to begin, and it returns to it here having completed its case. Between the two occurrences lie seventy-five verses of favours enumerated, covenants taken and broken, prophets denied and killed, scripture distorted, a covenant flung aside every time it was made, and a claim to exclusive salvation answered with a demand for proof. And now the same address opens a new section — the passage on Abraham — because the argument is about to shift from "what you did with what you were given" to "where your standing actually comes from."

The repetition therefore functions as a hinge. It says: the subject is the same, and the question is now going to be answered at a deeper level.

The two halves of the address were treated at verse 47 and should be read with that treatment. *Ni'mati* — My favour — is singular in form but comprehensive in sense, meaning the whole body of what was given. *Wa anni faddaltukum 'ala al-'alamin* — "and that I favoured you above the worlds" — was read there as a statement about a real historical preference in grace, not about an inherent quality in the people, and the argument for that reading is the whole of what follows in this surah: a favour conferred is a test, it can be withdrawn, and it confers no immunity and no essential superiority. The definitive statement on human ranking is 49:13: "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you."

For practice, this verse is best read as a pause rather than as a new instruction. The surah is about to take the question of privilege by descent apart at the root, in verse 124, and it wants the reader to arrive at that verse having heard the claim once more in its strongest form.

The application is small and immediate: before you argue with anyone about what they think they are owed, state their case as well as they would. The surah does.

## Verse 123

**"And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given."**

In simple terms: guard against a Day when no person can stand in for another, no ransom will be accepted, no intercession will help, and no one will be rescued.

*Ittaqu yawman* — "and fear a Day." *Taqwa* again: the surah's standing purpose-word, and the verb of guarding against something real and approaching.

*La tajzi nafsun 'an nafsin shay'an* — "no soul will avail another soul anything." *Tajzi* is from the root meaning to recompense or to suffice, so the sense is that no soul will be able to discharge anything on another's behalf.

Then four negations, and each one removes a specific thing that human beings rely on.

**1. *Wa la yuqbalu minha 'adl*** — "nor will any *'adl* be accepted from it." *'Adl* is the crux. The word means equivalence, justice, a substitute, or a ransom. The commentators predominantly read it here as a ransom — something offered in place of the person — on the grounds that the verse is describing a day on which nothing can be substituted for the self. Nothing you can offer will be taken instead of you.

**2. *Wa la tanfa'uha shafa'ah*** — "nor will intercession benefit it." On intercession, the position was set out at verse 48 and should not be restated at length: intercession is real in Islam, established by massively transmitted hadith, but it operates only by Allah's permission and only for those with whom He is pleased (2:255, 20:109, 21:28), and it does not avail those who die in rejection (74:48). What is negated here is intercession that helps *without* permission — which is precisely what the polytheists imagined when they took their idols as intercessors (10:18, 39:3).

**3. *Wa la hum yunsarun*** — "nor will they be helped." No rescue from outside.

Note the small but deliberate variation from verse 48, which is otherwise nearly identical. Verse 48 read "nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be taken from it," while this verse reads "nor will compensation be accepted from it, nor will intercession benefit it." The two negations have been swapped in order and their verbs differ. This is the Qur'an's habit of repetition-with-difference noted at verse 92: the content is the same, the framing shifts to suit the surrounding argument. Here the emphasis falls on intercession *benefiting*, which is the sharper formulation, because the preceding verse has just spoken of people who recite scripture and assume their recitation will carry them.

The fullest picture of what a person will attempt on that Day is at 70:11–14: the criminal will wish to ransom himself with his children, his spouse, his brother, his nearest kindred who sheltered him, and everyone on earth entirely — and it would not save him.

For practice, the verse's method is to remove supports one by one so that what is left has to stand on its own.

Run the four negations against your own reliance. Who or what am I counting on? A parent's piety — removed by the first negation. Wealth, or a charity given, or a bargain struck — removed by the second. A pious relative or a teacher who will speak for me — permitted only by permission, which is the third. A party, a community, a nation, a connection — removed by the fourth. Four of the five things human beings actually rely on are eliminated in a single verse, and the fifth, intercession, is made conditional on something outside the person's control.

That is not meant to produce terror. It is meant to produce the one thing that works: a person who knows he will stand alone stops outsourcing his standing and starts building it. And the building is not mysterious — the surah has spent a hundred and twenty verses describing it.

There is a second and gentler application. The verse is addressed to a community that had been told repeatedly that it was favoured above the peoples. It now tells them that on that Day the favour will not function as a proxy. That is the closing of the argument that opens this section, and it is why verse 124 follows immediately.

## Verse 124

**"˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”"**

In simple terms: Abraham was tested by his Lord with commands, and he carried them out completely. Allah told him He would make him a leader and exemplar for all people. Abraham asked whether this would extend to his descendants, and the answer was: My covenant is not reached by wrongdoers.

*Wa idh ibtala Ibrahima rabbuhu bi-kalimatin* — "and when his Lord tested Abraham with words." *Ibtala* is to test, to try, to put to the proof — the noun *bala'* is the same word used at verse 49 for what Pharaoh's people endured, where I noted the two transmitted readings of affliction and of blessing. Here the sense is unambiguously a trial.

What were the *kalimat*, the "words"? The text leaves them unspecified, which is itself a decision, and the commentaries transmitted several identifications: the rites and obligations of the religion which he was commanded to establish and did; the great trials — the fire, the migration, the sacrifice of his son, the circumcision; and, in one transmitted strand, the practices of natural purity and bodily cleanliness. All of these are reports rather than text. What the verse establishes is not the list but the sequence: he was tested, and *fa-atammahunna* — he completed them.

*Atamma* is emphatic: not "he did them" but "he brought them to completion, he fulfilled them wholly." The imamate in the next clause is granted after the completion, and in the Qur'an's own logic the two are connected. This is stated explicitly elsewhere: "And We made from among them leaders guiding by Our command **when they were patient** and were certain of Our signs" (32:24), and again at 21:73: "And We made them leaders guiding by Our command, and We inspired to them the doing of good deeds, establishment of prayer, and giving of zakah; and they were worshippers of Us."

The hadith that governs this is recorded by al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah from Mus'ab ibn Sa'd, from his father: a man asked the Prophet ﷺ which people are most severely tested, and he said: "The prophets, then those most like them, then those most like them. A person is tested according to his religion: if he is firm in his religion his trial is harder, and if he is weak in his religion his trial is lighter. Trials do not cease to afflict a servant until they leave him walking on the earth with no sin upon him." Its grading is discussed; the substance is attested elsewhere, and it states the principle this verse enacts.

*Qala inni ja'iluka lil-nasi imaman* — "He said: I am making you a leader (*imam*) for the people." *Imam* is from the root meaning to be in front, to be followed. The word in the Qur'an is used both ways — for the leaders who guide by Allah's command (21:73, 32:24) and for the leaders who invite to the Fire (28:41). The office is one; its direction is what differs.

And the decisive point about it here: **the imamate is granted, not inherited and not seized.** Abraham did not claim it. It is announced to him. And when he asks for it to extend to his descendants, the answer is not refused but qualified.

This verse is cited in the classical Sunni and Shi'i discussions of the imamate, and the difference should be stated accurately rather than smoothed. Sunni readers take it as establishing that religious leadership is a divine grant conditioned on conduct rather than a hereditary office, and note that the exclusion is of the wrongdoers. Shi'i readers cite it in support of the doctrine of a divinely designated, protected imam. Both read it within their own frameworks. What is agreed and what the plain text states is that the office is conferred by Allah and that it does not pass by descent to those who do wrong.

*Qala wa min dhurriyyati* — "he said: and from my descendants?" Note what he asks and what he does not. He does not ask for himself; that has already been settled. He asks for his children. The Qur'an presents this as the model of the believing parent's concern, and it echoes it at 14:40: "My Lord, make me an establisher of prayer, and [many] from my descendants."

*Qala la yanalu 'ahdi al-zalimin* — "He said: My covenant is not reached by the wrongdoers." *Yanalu* is to reach, to attain, to obtain. The covenant — of imamate, of prophethood, of the special divine commission — is not something a wrongdoer arrives at.

Two readings of *al-zalimin* are transmitted. The first takes it generally: those who do wrong. The second, supported by the Qur'an's own usage, takes it as the idolaters specifically, on the basis of 31:13: "Indeed, association [with Him] is great injustice" — so that the gravest *zulm* is *shirk*, and the exclusion certainly covers it. The two readings are compatible; the second is the narrower application of the first.

**And here is the verse that settles the entire argument of the surah so far.** The Children of Israel claimed standing by descent from the prophets. This verse says that the covenant given to Abraham's family is not inherited — it is granted, and wrongdoers do not reach it. Descent from Abraham confers nothing on a wrongdoer, and the absence of that descent deprives nothing from one who is not. In one sentence the surah removes the ground from every claim of inherited religious privilege, including — and this is the point — the claims Muslims have made since.

For practice, three things.

The first is the destruction of hereditary claim, and it is the most important thing in this batch. Your father's piety is not yours. Your family's standing is not yours. Your community's history is not yours. Your nation's relationship to the religion is not yours. The covenant is reached by the one who reaches it, and wrongdoers do not reach it. That is simultaneously the most frightening sentence in the verse and the most democratising: nobody is in by birth, and nobody is out by birth.

The second is the order of the verse. Testing, then completion, then the station. *Ibtala... fa-atammahunna... ja'iluka imaman.* The leadership came after the trial, not before it, and not instead of it. Every person who wants a position — in the religion, in a community, in a family, in a field — should note the sequence the Qur'an records here, because it is the opposite of the sequence the world offers.

And the third is the hope inside the answer. Abraham asked "and from my descendants?", and the reply was not no. It was a qualification: wrongdoers are excluded. Which means the righteous among them are included — and the whole of the Abrahamic promise that follows in verses 126 to 129 rests on that inclusion. When Allah qualifies rather than refuses, the qualification is the shape the gift takes.

## Verse 125

**"And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, “˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.” And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺."**

In simple terms: the House at Makkah was made a place people return to again and again and a sanctuary of safety; believers are told to take the standing-place of Abraham as a place of prayer; and Abraham and Ishmael were charged with purifying the House for the people who perform its rites.

*Wa idh ja'alna al-bayta mathabatan lil-nasi wa amnan* — "and when We made the House a place of return for the people, and a sanctuary of security." *Mathabah* is from the root meaning to return: it is called that because people do not finish with it. They leave and they come back, and those who have been long for it. *Amnan* is security, and it is not incidental — the sacred precinct is inviolable, and its inviolability covers life and property within it.

The Sunnah is emphatic about this. "Indeed Allah made Makkah sacred, not the people. It is not lawful for anyone who believes in Allah and the Last Day to shed blood therein, nor to cut down a tree therein. And it was not lawful for anyone before me, and it will not be lawful for anyone after me; it was made lawful for me only for an hour of a day" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). And: "This land was made sacred by Allah on the day He created the heavens and the earth, and it is sacred by the sanctity of Allah until the Day of Resurrection" (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

*Wa-ttakhidhu min maqami Ibrahima musallan* — "and take, from the standing-place of Abraham, a place of prayer." *Maqam Ibrahim* is understood by the majority as the stone on which Abraham stood while building, and the transmitted tradition holds that it bears the impress of his feet. That tradition is transmitted rather than a verified archaeological claim, and it should be cited as transmitted; the legal ruling attached to it does not depend on it either way. The ruling is established by the Sunnah: in the long hadith of Jabir describing the Farewell Pilgrimage, recorded by Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ circumambulated the House, then came to the Maqam and recited this very verse, placing the Maqam between himself and the House, and prayed two rak'ahs.

*Wa 'ahidna ila Ibrahima wa Isma'ila an tahhira baytiya* — "and We charged Abraham and Ishmael: purify My House." *'Ahidna ila* is a solemn commissioning, not a casual instruction. The verb is the cognate of *'ahd*, covenant, and the form carries the weight of a charge laid upon someone.

Note *baytiya* — **My** House. The possessive is the point, and in a surah that has been dismantling claims of ownership over revelation it matters enormously. The House belongs to Allah. It belonged to Him before Quraysh kept its keys, it belonged to Him while the idols stood around it, and it belongs to Him now. No tribe, no dynasty, no state and no custodian owns it. Every generation needs to hear that, and every generation has produced people who needed to hear it.

*Tahhira* is the dual imperative: you two, purify. What purification? The commentaries gave both, and the second is the stronger: physical cleanliness, and — more importantly — purification from idols and from associating partners with Allah. Given that Abraham is the prophet who smashed his people's idols (21:57–58, 37:91), and given that idolatry later filled the precinct around the Ka'bah, the command reads as both an instruction to keep it pure and a commission that would be completed centuries later when the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah and destroyed the idols around it.

*Li-al-ta'ifina wa al-'akifina wa al-rukka'i al-sujud* — "for those who circumambulate, those who devote themselves therein, and those who bow and those who prostrate." Four groups, and note the shape: two rites specific to the House (circumambulation and devoted staying, the latter understood variously as residence or as *i'tikaf*), and two that name the prayer in its two defining postures. The House is purified *for* worshippers, which is the other half of the command: cleansing from is for the sake of cleansing for.

For practice, this verse gives three things.

The first is the *mathabah*, and it is one of the most beautiful words in the religion. The House is a place of return because people do not finish with it. Every pilgrim knows the phenomenon: you arrive, you leave, and within months you are planning to return. The tradition treats that longing as a sign rather than a sentiment — the Prophet ﷺ said, concerning the pilgrimage, that the reward of the accepted one is Paradise, and the House was made a place people come back to. A person who has been and wants to go again is experiencing the thing the verse names.

The second is the security. The precinct is a sanctuary, and the sanctity covers what happens inside it. That is a standing rebuke to every generation that has turned the precinct into a place of fear, and it is why the classical law is so severe about conduct within it.

And the third is *baytiya* — My House. It is worth holding this against every attempt, ancient or modern, to treat the Ka'bah as the property of a people or a project. It is not. It was entrusted to two men to purify for whoever circles it, stays in it, bows in it and prostrates in it — and the list of who qualifies contains no ethnicity, no nation and no sect.

## Verse 126

**"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day.” He answered, “As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”"**

In simple terms: Abraham prayed for the city's security and for its people to be provided with fruits, specifying those among them who believe. Allah answered that the disbeliever would be granted enjoyment briefly, then driven to the punishment of the Fire.

*Rabbi ij'al hadha baladan aminan* — "My Lord, make this a secure city." Note the movement from verse 125, where the *House* was made a sanctuary by Allah's act, to this verse, where the *city* is made secure by Abraham's request. The two belong together, and the Qur'an elsewhere confirms that both were granted: "Have We not established for them a safe sanctuary to which are brought the fruits of all things as provision from Us?" (28:57), and "Have they not seen that We made [Makkah] a safe sanctuary while people are being taken away all around them?" (29:67).

*Wa-rzuq ahlahu mina al-thamarati man amana minhum billahi wa al-yawmi al-akhiri* — "and provide its people with fruits — those of them who believe in Allah and the Last Day." The wording has a small ambiguity that the commentators discussed: did Abraham ask for provision for the city's people generally and then specify the believers, or did he ask specifically for the believers? The dominant reading is the second — the provision was requested for those who believe — and the answer then explains that Allah's provision in this life extends beyond that request.

*Qala wa man kafara fa-umatti'uhu qalilan* — "He said: and whoever disbelieves, I will grant him enjoyment for a little." *Ummatti'uhu* is from the root for enjoyment and use, the same root as the *mata'* of verse 36. And *qalilan* — little, brief. The same word used at verse 79 for the "small price" the forgers sold the truth for. Provision in this life, for the one who rejects, is real, and it is small.

*Thumma adtarruhu ila 'adhabi al-nari* — "then I will drive him to the punishment of the Fire." *Adtarruhu* is from the root meaning to compel, to drive by necessity — the form is emphatic and the meaning is that there will be no possibility of refusal. *Wa bi'sa al-masir* — "and wretched is the destination."

Now, the two great practical points.

The first is the observable sign, which the Qur'an itself makes at 28:57. Makkah sits in a barren valley with no agriculture, and Abraham asked for its people to be provided with fruits — and fruits are brought there from everywhere, continuously, in every era, as an observable fact. The Qur'an presents this as a sign rather than as a scientific claim, and that is exactly how it should be presented. Anyone who has been there has seen it. It is a sign that can still be checked.

The second is far more pastorally urgent, and it is the reason this verse matters to most readers.

**Provision in this life is not a verdict.** Allah answers Abraham's prayer for the believers by explaining that He also provides for those who reject — briefly, and without that provision being any indication of His pleasure. The converse follows with equal force: the believer who is not provided for has not been abandoned, and the disbeliever who is richly provided for has not been approved.

That single distinction resolves an enormous amount of human confusion and an enormous amount of quiet spiritual damage. The person who looks at the corruption, comfort and success of people who have no regard for God and concludes that religion is a losing proposition has made exactly the mistake this verse corrects. *Fa-umatti'uhu qalilan* — I grant him enjoyment, briefly. The word that qualifies the enjoyment is *qalil*, and what follows the brief enjoyment is stated without euphemism.

And the corollary for the believer: the absence of provision is not the absence of favour. This is the same principle the surah established at verse 83, where the covenant listed care for the poor as an obligation — because the poor are not thereby abandoned by God, and the community is made responsible precisely because the distribution in this life is not the distribution in the next.

## Verse 127

**"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ “Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: while Abraham and Ishmael were raising the foundations of the House, they prayed that Allah accept it from them.

*Wa idh yarfa'u Ibrahimu al-qawa'ida mina al-bayti wa Isma'ilu* — "and when Abraham was raising the foundations of the House, and Ishmael." Note the imperfect verb *yarfa'u*, "he was raising": the grammar places the reader inside the scene rather than after it. *Al-qawa'id* is the plural of *qa'idah*, a foundation — from a root meaning to sit, because the foundation is what sits beneath.

There is a question about whether this describes building from new or rebuilding on foundations that already existed. The transmitted reports hold that the site and its foundations were ancient, going back before Abraham, and that he rebuilt upon them; this is the standard understanding. It is corroborated by the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, in which the Prophet ﷺ said to 'A'ishah: "Do you not see that when your people built the Ka'bah they fell short of the foundations of Abraham?" She asked why he did not restore it to those foundations, and he said: "Were it not that your people are recent in their disbelief, I would have done so." The narration is authentic and it establishes both that the original foundations were Abraham's and that Quraysh's rebuilding did not follow them exactly.

*Rabbana taqabbal minna* — "Our Lord, accept from us." This is the clause the tradition has never stopped commenting on, because of who is saying it.

Abraham — the one called *imam* four verses earlier, the one Allah took as an intimate friend, the one whose way is the religion — is building the House of God, at God's command, with his own son, and he prays: **accept it from us.**

The commentators drew the principle directly: the fear that a good deed will not be accepted increases with the stature of the doer and does not decrease. The Qur'an states the same condition as a mark of the believers at 23:60: "And they who give what they give while their hearts are fearful, because they will return to their Lord." And 'A'ishah is reported to have asked the Prophet ﷺ about that verse, asking whether it meant the man who steals, fornicates and drinks while fearing Allah. He said: "No, O daughter of al-Siddiq. It is the man who prays, fasts and gives charity while fearing that it will not be accepted from him." That narration is recorded by al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah, and its grading is discussed among the specialists; it is cited here for its meaning, which is attested by the abundant practice of the early generations.

*Innaka anta al-sami'u al-'alim* — "indeed You are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing." And note how perfectly the two Names fit the request. Acceptance requires that the prayer be *heard* and that the intention be *known*. Abraham invokes the two attributes that his request depends on. That is the etiquette of supplication throughout the Qur'an: name the attribute your request rests on.

For practice, this verse contains the single most corrective attitude in the religion, and almost nobody has it.

It is the difference between doing a good work and assuming it counts. Most people operate on the assumption that the deed and its acceptance are the same event — I prayed, therefore it is accepted; I gave, therefore it is credited; I built, therefore it stands. The Qur'an's model here is a man building the House of God with his own hands who is asking, as he builds, whether it will be taken.

There is a second, quieter application in the scene itself. A father and a son, working together on the same thing, praying the same prayer — *minna*, "from us," in the dual. The religion is transmitted in exactly this way, or it is not transmitted: not by instruction alone but by shared work, with the older and the younger both doing it and both asking that it be accepted. The parent who wants his child in the religion should note that Abraham did not send Ishmael to build while he supervised.

And the third is the timing. They prayed *while raising*, not after finishing. The supplication accompanies the work rather than following it. That is why the tradition recommends du'a during action rather than only at its end — because the request is not for the completed structure but for the acceptance of everything that went into it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 128

**"Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: make us both constantly submissive to You, and raise from our descendants a community submissive to You; teach us our rites, and turn to us in acceptance.

*Rabbana wa-j'alna muslimayni laka* — "Our Lord, make us both *muslimayn* [submitting ones] to You." The dual form. And here is the question the commentators all addressed: Abraham and Ishmael were already submitting. Why pray to be made so?

The answer they gave is one of the most useful things in the religion. The prayer of the established believer is not for a beginning but for an ending and an increase. It is a prayer for constancy — keep us submitting until we die — and for completion — make our submission whole rather than partial. This is why the Qur'an's righteous pray prayers they seem to have already had answered: Yusuf, a prophet, prays "cause me to die as one who submits and join me with the righteous" (12:101); and the believers pray "Our Lord, let not our hearts deviate after You have guided us" (3:8).

The practical consequence is large. Many people stop asking for the things they have, on the grounds that asking implies not having. The Qur'an's model is the opposite: you ask most urgently for the thing you can least afford to lose. Possession is not the end of the prayer; it is the beginning of a different prayer about the same possession.

*Wa min dhurriyyatina ummatan muslimatan laka* — "and from our descendants a community submitting to You." Note the word: *ummah*, a community, not a scattering of individuals. Abraham prays for a people. This is the direct counterpart of verse 124, where his request concerning his descendants was qualified by the exclusion of wrongdoers — here he prays for the submitting community, and it is granted.

*Wa arina manasikana* — "and show us our rites." *Manasik* is from a root meaning worship through prescribed acts, and especially sacrifice; in usage it is the rituals of the pilgrimage. And note the verb: *arina*, **show** us. He asks to be taught by demonstration rather than by description. That is exactly how the rites were transmitted, and the Prophet ﷺ said during his Farewell Pilgrimage: "Take your rites from me" — the hadith is recorded by Muslim from Jabir. Worship in Islam is embodied; it is learned by watching someone do it, and that is why the Sunnah is not an optional supplement to the text.

*Wa tub 'alayna* — "and turn to us in acceptance." And again the question arises: from what? The commentators answered it by pointing at the practice of the Prophet ﷺ. He said, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari from Abu Hurayrah: "By Allah, I seek the forgiveness of Allah and I turn to Him in repentance more than seventy times a day."

That hadith settles the matter completely. *Tawbah* is not only a remedy for sin; it is the standing posture of the creature before the Creator. The one whose sins had been forgiven, past and future, turned to Allah more than seventy times a day, which means that what he was doing was not being cured of something but maintaining a relationship. A person who thinks repentance is only for when he has done something wrong has misunderstood what it is for.

*Innaka anta al-tawwabu al-rahim* — "indeed You are the Accepter of Repentance, the Most Merciful." The same pair that closed verse 37 for Adam and verse 54 for the calf-worshippers. The surah has now used it three times, and each time it is on the lips of someone returning.

For practice, this verse is a complete curriculum for what to ask for, and it is worth taking apart.

Ask for constancy, not for a first conversion — *wa-j'alna muslimayni laka*. Ask for your descendants as a community, not merely as individuals — *ummah*, not just children. Ask to be *shown* how to worship rather than told, and go and watch someone do it — *arina manasikana*. And turn to Allah constantly rather than only after failures — *wa tub 'alayna*.

There is one further application that is easy to miss. Abraham prays for a community *from his descendants* — and three verses earlier he was told that his covenant is not reached by wrongdoers. Held together, the two verses describe the shape of religious transmission in every generation: the prayer is answered in those of the descendants who submit, and it is not answered in those who do wrong. A family, a community, an institution continues in the religion not by having descended from religious people but by the ones who actually submit. Every generation has to do it again.

## Verse 129

**"Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. Indeed, You ˹alone˺ are the Almighty, All-Wise.”"**

In simple terms: raise up from among them a messenger who will recite Your signs to them, teach them the Book and the wisdom, and purify them.

This is the most famous of Abraham's prayers and the one the tradition has always identified as answered in Muhammad ﷺ.

*Rabbana wa-b'ath fihim rasulan minhum* — "Our Lord, and raise among them a messenger from themselves." Note the verb: *ib'ath*, from the same root used for the resurrection at verse 56 — *thumma ba'athnakum min ba'di mawtikum*. The sending of a messenger is described in resurrection vocabulary, and the resonance is deliberate: a messenger is sent to a community that is dead in a real sense, and he raises it. It is the same metaphor the Qur'an uses at 6:122: "Is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him a light by which to walk among the people like one who is in darkness, never to emerge from it?"

*Minhum* — from among them. This matters, and the Qur'an makes the principle explicit: "And We did not send any messenger except [speaking] in the language of his people, to make clear to them" (14:4). A messenger from among them is one of them, known to them, speaking their tongue, whose lineage and character they can verify. That is why the Qur'an repeatedly reminds the Quraysh that Muhammad ﷺ was one of them, had lived among them for forty years before the revelation, and that they had no grounds to claim he was a stranger or a fraud (10:16).

*Yatlu 'alayhim ayatika wa yu'allimuhum al-kitaba wa al-hikmah* — "reciting to them Your signs and teaching them the Book and the wisdom."

Three functions, and the second contains one of the most consequential terms in the whole religion.

*Al-hikmah*, "the wisdom." What is it? The majority of the scholars of *usul al-fiqh* identified it as the Sunnah — the teaching, example and rulings of the Prophet ﷺ, distinct from the recited Qur'an and revealed alongside it. This identification is not a minority reading; it is the standard one, and al-Shafi'i's argument for the authority of the Sunnah in *al-Risalah* rests substantially on this verse and its parallels. Other readings take *hikmah* as understanding of the religion, or as the rulings, or as wisdom generally. The readings are compatible — the Sunnah is the vehicle of all of them — but the dominant scholarly identification is the Sunnah, and it is why this verse is cited as a proof text for the Sunnah being revelation.

*Wa yuzakkihim* — "and purify them." *Yuzakki* is from the same root as *zakah*: to purify and to cause to grow, together. And this third function is the one most often dropped.

The messenger's work is threefold: he **recites** (conveys the revelation), he **teaches** (explains the Book and the wisdom), and he **purifies** (transforms the person). Modern religious institutions excel at the second and neglect the third. Teaching without purification produces people who know a great deal and are unchanged by it — which is precisely the condition this surah has been diagnosing since verse 44. The Qur'an states the stakes of the third function at 91:9–10: "He has succeeded who purifies it, and he has failed who corrupts it."

*Innaka anta al-'azizu al-hakim* — "indeed You are the Almighty, the All-Wise." And note again the fit: sending a messenger requires power (*'izza*) and requires wisdom (*hikmah*) — power to accomplish it, and wisdom to choose whom, when, and to whom.

Now the fulfilment, which the Qur'an states in almost the same words. "Certainly did Allah confer [great] favour upon the believers when He sent among them a Messenger from themselves, reciting to them His verses and purifying them and teaching them the Book and wisdom, although they had been before in manifest error" (3:164). And again at 62:2: "It is He who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves, reciting to them His verses and purifying them and teaching them the Book and wisdom, although they were before in clear error."

Set 2:129 beside 3:164 and 62:2 and the claim is unmistakable: the prayer of Abraham, in its exact terms, is the description of Muhammad ﷺ. A narration to the effect that the Prophet ﷺ said he was the supplication of his father Abraham is transmitted in the later collections, with discussion of its grading; it is not found in the two Sahihs, and it should be cited with that qualification. The identification does not depend on the narration, because the Qur'an makes it in the two fulfilment verses.

For practice, three things.

The first is the answered prayer as a fact about how God works. A man prayed, in a barren valley, for a messenger to be raised from his descendants to recite, teach and purify — and roughly two and a half millennia later, in that valley, the messenger came. No human being had arranged it. The lesson is not merely devotional: it is that the things a person asks for are heard, and that their fulfilment may take a scale of time he will never see. That reframes what it means for a prayer to go unanswered.

The second is the threefold function, applied to anyone who teaches. If you teach the religion — to a child, a student, a congregation, a readership — the model is recite, teach, purify. Most religious education delivers the second and wonders why it does not take. The missing element is not more information. It is *tazkiyah*: the deliberate work of helping a person become someone the knowledge can live in.

And the third is *minhum* — from among them. The messenger was raised from the people he was sent to. That is a principle for anyone who works with communities: the most effective carrier of a message to a people is one of that people, who knows the language and the wounds and the humour, and whose character they have had a lifetime to verify. Abraham did not pray for a stranger to be sent to his descendants. He prayed for one of them.

## Verse 130

**"And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous."**

In simple terms: no one turns away from the way of Abraham except someone who has made a fool of his own self. Allah chose him in this life, and in the next he is among the righteous.

*Wa man yarghabu 'an millati Ibrahima* — "and who turns away with aversion from the religion of Abraham?" *Raghiba 'an* is to turn from something with dislike. *Millah* is the religion as the way of a people — the same word used at verse 120, where the Jews and Christians wanted the Prophet ﷺ to follow *millatahum*. The echo is deliberate: they invite him to their *millah*, and the surah responds with the *millah* that precedes and encompasses them both.

*Illa man safiha nafsahu* — "except one who has made a fool of his self." The construction is a form of censure: *safiha* is a verb of blame and *nafsahu* is its object or specification. *Safih* means lacking in sound judgement, deficient in restraint — the opposite of *halim*. And it is the word the Qur'an uses for someone who cannot be trusted to manage his property: "And do not give the foolish [*sufaha'*] your wealth, which Allah has made for you a means of sustenance" (4:5).

That gives the clause its bite. The person who turns from the way of Abraham is not accused of being stupid. He is accused of mismanaging the one thing he was given to manage — himself. He has been entrusted with a self, and he is running it badly.

*Wa la-qadi istafaynahu fi al-dunya* — "and We certainly chose him in this life." *Istafa* is to select, from a root meaning to be pure and clear; the word carries both election and purification. The Qur'an places Abraham at the head of the chosen line: "Indeed, Allah chose Adam and Noah and the family of Abraham and the family of 'Imran over the worlds" (3:33).

And the fullest description of him is at 16:120–121: "Indeed, Abraham was a leader [and] model, devoutly obedient to Allah, inclining toward truth, and he was not of those who associate others with Allah. [He was] grateful for His favours. He chose him and guided him to a straight path."

*Wa innahu fi al-akhirati la-mina al-salihin* — "and indeed he, in the Hereafter, will be among the righteous." And the Qur'an gives him a title it gives no one else: "And Allah took Abraham as an intimate friend" (4:125). The Prophet ﷺ said: "Indeed Allah took me as an intimate friend [*khalil*] just as He took Abraham as an intimate friend" (Muslim, from Jundub).

Now, the term that governs this verse and the two that follow: the *millat Ibrahim*, and the word attached to it — *hanif*.

*Hanif* comes from a root meaning to incline, to turn aside. In the Qur'an it denotes the one who turns away from every false religion toward pure monotheism — and it is used of Abraham at 2:135, 3:67, 3:95, 4:125, 6:79, 6:161, 16:120, 16:123, 22:31 and 98:5. The decisive statement is 3:67: "Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was one inclining toward truth [*hanifan*], a submitter [*musliman*], and he was not of the polytheists."

That is the argument the surah is building toward, and it is why this batch ends here. The Children of Israel claim standing by descent from Abraham; the Christians claim him too. The Qur'an's answer is that Abraham predates both, belongs to neither, and that the thing he actually was — one who turned from all falsehood to the pure worship of one God — is precisely what the message of Muhammad ﷺ restores. That argument is completed at verse 135, which opens the next batch.

For practice, two things.

The first is the *safiha nafsahu* diagnosis, which is unusually precise as a piece of self-examination. Where in your life are you mismanaging what you were entrusted with? The word is used for a person who cannot be trusted with property, and the property here is the self. Turning from the way of Abraham is not presented as rebellion so much as incompetence: you have one life, one self, one death, and you are running it as though none of that were true.

And the second is the model itself, which is worth stating concretely because "the way of Abraham" can sound abstract. Concretely, it is: to reason your way to God from what you can observe (6:75–79); to break the idols of your own people rather than accommodate them (21:57–58); to argue with power without flattering it (2:258 in this very surah); to leave everything at a command (37:102); to pray for a city you will not live to see (2:126); to build a house for God with your son and ask whether it will be accepted (2:127); and to pray for your descendants' descendants, centuries before they exist (2:128–129).

That is the *millah*, and the verse's question is a fair one: who would turn away from it, except someone who has made a fool of his own self?

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses turn the surah's argument from the particular to the universal, and they do it by supplying a different genealogy.

The People of the Book who genuinely adhere to what they were given are the ones who believe — because recitation that is adhered to leads where the recitation-points (121). They are reminded of the favour and of the Day on which no proxy, ransom, intercession or rescue will function (122–123). And then the surah goes behind them all, to Abraham.

Abraham was tested and completed the test, and was made a leader for all people — and when he asked for the station to extend to his descendants, the answer was that the covenant is not reached by wrongdoers (124). That sentence removes the ground from every claim of inherited standing, theirs and ours. The House was made a place of return and a sanctuary, and Abraham and Ishmael were charged to purify it — *My* House, belonging to no tribe (125). Abraham prayed for its security and its provision, and was told that provision in this life is given to believer and rejecter alike, briefly, and is not a verdict (126). The two of them raised the foundations while praying that the work be accepted — the greatest of men building the house of God and unsure it would be taken (127). They prayed for constancy, for a submitting community from their descendants, to be shown the rites, and for turning in repentance (128). They prayed for a messenger from among them who would recite, teach and purify — a prayer the Qur'an itself declares fulfilled (129). And the verdict: who turns from the way of Abraham except one who has made a fool of his own self? He was chosen in this life, and in the next he is among the righteous (130).

Three things hold the batch together. First, **descent confers nothing and lineage is not a claim** — stated once negatively at verse 124 and once positively at verse 130, where the way that is proposed belongs to a man who was neither Jew nor Christian. Second, the religion is **received and practised before it is possessed** — the true recitation of verse 121, the shown rites of verse 128, the acceptance begged for at verse 127. And third, the whole passage rests on **prayer that outlives the one who prays it**: for a city, for a community, for a messenger — all of it answered on a scale of centuries none of the petitioners saw.

**Other works**
- Works of *usul al-fiqh* — on *al-hikmah* as the Sunnah and on the authority of the Sunnah as revelation, following the argument of al-Shafi'i in *al-Risalah*
- Works of creed — on the imamate as a divine grant, on the Sunni and Shi'i readings of verse 124, and on the election of the prophets
- Works of *fiqh* — on the inviolability of the sacred precinct and on the rites of the pilgrimage
- Works of *tazkiyah* — on the purification of the self as the third function of the messenger
- Grammar and lexicography: al-Raghib al-Isfahani, *al-Mufradat*; Ibn Manzur, *Lisan al-Arab*

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: which mosque-site matters in verse 125, what the *kalimat* of Abraham's testing were, whether *al-zalimin* in verse 124 means wrongdoers generally or idolaters specifically, and whether *al-hikmah* in verse 129 is the Sunnah or something broader.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the identifications of the words with which Abraham was tested, the tradition that the Maqam bears the impress of his feet, and the report that the site's foundations predate him. The two narrations cited from al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah have been flagged as having discussed gradings, and the narration identifying the Prophet ﷺ as the supplication of Abraham has been noted as absent from the two Sahihs, with the identification rested instead on the Qur'an's own fulfilment verses, 3:164 and 62:2.

Three boundaries have been observed. On verse 124: the Sunni and Shi'i readings of the imamate have both been stated accurately rather than one being presented as the other's error, and the point on which the plain text is unambiguous — that the office is conferred by Allah and not inherited by wrongdoers — has been separated from the disputed questions. On verse 126: the provision of fruits to Makkah has been presented as the Qur'an's own stated sign (28:57), which is observable, and not as a scientific claim. And on verse 125: the House has been stated to belong to Allah and to no tribe, dynasty, state or custodian, in a passage whose whole argument is against treating revelation and its sites as anyone's property.
