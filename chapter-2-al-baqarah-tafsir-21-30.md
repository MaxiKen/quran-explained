# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 21–30

We are coming to a turning point in the surah. Verses 1 to 20 sorted humanity into three groups — the believers, the defiant disbelievers and the hypocrites — and ended by declaring Allah's power over everything. Now, in verse 21, the address changes: instead of speaking about each group, Allah speaks to all of them at once, calling the whole human race to worship the One who created them. What follows is the case for that worship, built in four movements. Verses 21 and 22 give the argument from provision — the earth beneath you, the sky above you, the rain and the fruit — and draw the obvious conclusion: do not make rivals to the One who gave you all of it. Verses 23 and 24 issue the standing challenge, the test by which anyone can check the Qur'an's claim for himself, followed by the warning. Verse 25 answers that warning with good news for the believers. Verses 26 and 27 then explain why the very same message guides some people and ruins others, and they name exactly who is left astray. Verses 28 and 29 return the argument to the observable world — your own origin and the ordered heavens — and verse 30 opens the story of Adam, which will run to verse 39 and which gives the answer to the question every reader has been carrying since verse 21: what is a human being for?

## Verse 21

**"O humanity! Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you, so that you may become mindful ˹of Him˺."**

In simple terms, Allah now addresses every human being and gives a single command: worship your Lord — the One who created you and everyone who came before you — because that worship is what produces taqwa, the reverent awareness of Him that this whole surah opened by praising.

The first thing to notice is the address: *ya ayyuha al-nas* — "O humanity." For twenty verses the Qur'an has been speaking about categories of people; now it speaks to all of them together. The placement is deliberate. Having shown the three ways human beings respond to revelation, the Qur'an calls all three back to the one thing that would repair each of them. The call is not to a tribe, a nation, a religion or a generation; it is to the human race, which is fitting for a Book that describes itself elsewhere as "a reminder for all the worlds" (25:1).

*I'budu rabbakum* — "worship your Lord." Two features of the wording carry weight. The first is *rabbakum*, "your Lord" — not merely "the Lord." The possessive establishes a relationship: He is the One who sustains, raises and brings you along. So the command to worship is grounded not in raw power but in the care you have already received, which makes it a response to love and gratitude rather than to fear alone. The second is that the command is plural — it is given to people collectively, reminding us that worship is something done by a community and not only by isolated individuals.

Then the reason: *alladhi khalaqakum wa alladhina min qablikum* — "Who created you and those before you." Why mention the generations before? The commentators gave several reasons that reinforce one another. It emphasises that creation is not a single ancient event but an unbroken, repeated act across all of history, which quietly establishes the possibility of resurrection: the One who has brought generation after generation into existence from nothing has no difficulty doing it again. It universalises the call — every human being who ever lived was created by Him, so no one is outside the address. And it humbles the hearer: you are one generation among countless others, neither first nor last, which undercuts the arrogance that so often stands behind refusal.

The purpose clause is the most important part of the verse: *la'allakum tattaqun* — "so that you may become mindful of Him." Note the particle *la'alla*, which in human speech expresses hope or expectation and therefore carries a hint of uncertainty. When the Qur'an uses it of Allah, the scholars explained it as meaning "in order that" — because hoping implies not knowing, and not knowing is impossible for Allah. So the verse states a purpose, not a wish: worship is prescribed *in order to produce* taqwa.

That single clause settles a question many people carry. Worship is not an end in itself, and Allah does not need it. He said so plainly in 51:56–57: "I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me. I do not want from them any provision, nor do I want them to feed Me." And in the hadith qudsi recorded by Muslim from Abu Dharr, Allah says: "O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were as pious as the most pious heart of any single one of you, that would not increase My dominion in anything at all." Worship is for *you*. It is the training programme by which taqwa is built.

And this closes a circle the surah opened at verse 2. There, the Qur'an was "a guidance for those mindful of Allah" — taqwa was the *precondition* for benefiting from guidance. Here, worship is the *means* of acquiring taqwa. Put the two together and you have the engine of a religious life: guidance produces taqwa, taqwa produces worship, worship deepens taqwa, and deeper taqwa opens more guidance. It is a circuit, not a line. The same purpose clause appears with fasting in 2:183 — "so that you may become mindful" — and the logic is stated outright in 22:37 about the sacrificial animals: "It is neither their meat nor their blood that reaches Allah; rather, it is your taqwa that reaches Him."

Now, what does worship actually do to a person? It is repeated, embodied training in awareness. Five times a day, a person stops what he is doing, faces a fixed direction, and stands before his Lord. That interruption is not incidental; it is the mechanism. A mind that is stopped and reoriented at regular intervals develops a habit of noticing, and that habit is precisely taqwa — the reflex of remembering Allah before you act rather than after. This is why the Qur'an pairs worship with its purpose so consistently, and why a person who performs the movements without the awareness has the form without the goal.

There is a final wisdom in the verse's structure that is worth noticing, because it is the Qur'an's standard method of argument. The command begins from something no one in the audience disputes — everyone, believer or idolater, accepted that a Creator had brought them into being — and then moves to the conclusion: then worship Him alone. The Qur'an does not begin by demanding assent to something contested; it begins from common ground and builds. Anyone reading this verse should ask himself whether he has accepted the premise while declining the conclusion, because that inconsistency is exactly what verses 22 and 28 will press on.

## Verse 22

**"˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you. So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺."**

In simple terms, the verse names three blessings — the earth made comfortable to live on, the sky raised as a protective structure, and the rain that brings out the food you eat — and then draws the conclusion: the One who gave you all of that is the One who deserves your worship, so do not set up rivals to Him, and you know better.

*Alladhi ja'ala lakum al-arda firashan* — "Who made the earth a *firash* for you." The word *firash* means a bed, a spread-out couch, something made soft and comfortable to lie on. The choice of word matters. The earth is not described as a platform or a surface; it is described as something prepared, laid out and made restful — the way a host spreads a bed for a guest. The commentators drew out the implication: this enormous sphere is made firm, stable and subdued enough for you to walk on, sleep on, build on, farm on and bury your dead in, without it shaking or swallowing you. Note the repetition of *lakum*, "for you" — the whole verse is framed as gift rather than as accident. The Qur'an states the principle behind this elsewhere: "He has subjected to you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, all from Him" (45:13), and "Do you not see that Allah has subjected to you whatever is in the heavens and the earth?" (31:20).

*Wa al-samaa bina'an* — "and the sky as a *bina'*." *Bina'* means a building, a constructed structure, something deliberately raised and held firm. The Qur'an develops this image in several places: "We made the sky a protected ceiling" (21:32); "He created the heavens without pillars that you can see" (13:2 and 31:10); "Do they not look at the sky above them — how We built it and adorned it, and it has no rifts?" (50:6). The classical commentators understood it as a real, vast, ordered structure raised without visible support and held by Allah's command — and 22:65 adds the note that makes the favour explicit: "He holds back the sky from falling upon the earth except by His permission."

Some modern writers have connected the "protected ceiling" and the absence of "rifts" to what we now know about the atmosphere — that it shields the surface from radiation and burns up most incoming meteors. That is a legitimate reflection on the text and a reasonable thing to mention in a sermon, but it should be labelled as reflection rather than as the verse's meaning. The classical meaning is clear and sufficient: the sky is a built, firm, protective structure, and the One who holds it deserves your gratitude. It is also worth saying plainly that Islam reads this verse as a reason for responsible stewardship rather than for exploitation: the earth is described as a *bed prepared for you*, which makes you a guest in someone else's house, not an owner with unlimited rights.

*Wa anzala mina al-samaa'i ma'an fa-akhraja bihi mina al-thamarati rizqan lakum* — "and sent down water from the sky, and brought forth with it fruits as provision for you." This sequence — water from above producing life from below — is one of the most repeated signs in the Qur'an, appearing in 16:10–11, 20:53, 30:24, 50:9–11 and elsewhere. And it is doing double duty here. Literally, it describes your food: the rain falls on dead ground and green life comes out of it. But the Qur'an uses that same image throughout as its favourite analogy for the resurrection, as in 35:9 and 50:11 — "Thus is the bringing forth [of the dead]." So this verse is quietly preparing the argument that verse 28 will make explicit.

Then the conclusion: *fa-la taj'alu lillahi andadan wa antum ta'lamun* — "so do not set up rivals to Allah, while you know." *Andad* is the plural of *nidd*, meaning a peer, a counterpart, a rival — one who is set alongside another and treated as his equal. What are these rivals? For the original audience, they were the idols. But the early authorities broadened the category, and the hadith literature supports them. Al-Tirmidhi records the narration from 'Adi ibn Hatim — a former Christian who became a Companion — who objected that the People of the Book did not literally worship their rabbis and monks; the Prophet ﷺ explained that they did, because those leaders made lawful for them what Allah had made unlawful and unlawful what Allah had made lawful, and the people followed them — and that was their worship of them. The chain of that narration has been discussed by hadith scholars, though its content is supported by the Qur'an's own usage in 9:31. So the principle is established regardless: a *nidd* is anything or anyone that is loved, feared, obeyed or hoped in the way that belongs to Allah alone.

The closing phrase, *wa antum ta'lamun*, carries the bite. It can be read two ways, and both are held: "while you know" — that is, you already possess the knowledge that He alone created all this, so you have no excuse — or "knowingly," meaning do not do it deliberately. The first reading is the stronger one and is supported by the Qur'an's repeated observation about the polytheists of Arabia: they *did* acknowledge Allah as Creator. "If you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would surely say: Allah" (31:25, and similarly 29:61 and 43:9). Their crime was not ignorance of the Creator; it was giving His due to others while knowing better.

Now make this concrete, because the rivals of our century are rarely made of stone. A *nidd* is whatever occupies the place that belongs to Allah. It is the career you will compromise your integrity for — that is a rival. It is the approval of people you fear more than His displeasure — that is a rival. It is the political identity, the nation, the ideology, the platform, the bank balance, or the person whose rejection you cannot bear. The test the scholars proposed is simple: what do you love most, fear most, hope in most, and obey when it conflicts with His command? Whatever that is has become a *nidd*.

And this is why the lesser form of this sin is so dangerous and so badly under-examined: showing off in worship. The Prophet ﷺ warned about it repeatedly, and the early Muslims took the warning so seriously that they used to make the supplication transmitted in the hadith collections: "O Allah, I seek refuge with You from associating partners with You knowingly, and I seek Your forgiveness for what I do not know." That second half is the honest part. Most people who set up rivals would never call it that, and most of us have rivals we have not noticed. That is precisely why the supplication asks forgiveness for the unknown ones.

## Verse 23

**"And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true."**

In simple terms, Allah offers the doubters a test: if you genuinely suspect that this Book is the work of a human being, then produce a single chapter like it — and call on anyone and everyone you like to help you — if your claim is true.

Notice the opening: *wa in kuntum fi raybin* — "and if you are in doubt." The word *rayb* is the same one used in verse 2, where the Qur'an declared "no doubt in it." The two verses are in deliberate conversation: the Book states its own certainty, and then addresses the doubter on his own terms — "if, hypothetically, you doubt, here is how to settle it." That is not the language of a text afraid of scrutiny. It is the language of a text that invites a test and defines in advance what would count as failure.

The next phrase is the heart of the verse: *mimma nazzalna 'ala 'abdina* — "about what We have sent down upon Our servant." Two details. The first is the verb *nazzala*, the intensive form, which in Arabic signals sending down repeatedly and in stages — an accurate description of a revelation that came over twenty-three years in response to events, questions and circumstances, rather than all at once. The second is *'abdina*, "Our servant." The Prophet ﷺ is named by the title of servanthood, which is the noblest description he has, and the Qur'an uses it at the highest moments of his life — "Glory be to Him who took His servant by night" (17:1), and "He revealed to His servant what he revealed" (53:10).

But why call him "Our servant" *here*, in the middle of the challenge? Because it is part of the argument. The challenge is being issued to people who claimed the Qur'an was human speech — the work of a poet, a soothsayer or a man taught by others. The reply is that the man through whom it came is only a recipient. He is a servant, and a servant does not author what his Master sends. This is reinforced elsewhere by the emphasis on his unlettered state: "You did not recite any scripture before it, nor did you write one with your right hand — for then the falsifiers would have had cause to doubt" (29:48). The argument from his having never composed a line of verse in a society obsessed with poetry is a real argument, not a devotional flourish.

*Fa'tu bi-suratin min mithlihi* — "then produce a surah like it." There is a genuine disagreement about the pronoun here, and it affects how the challenge is understood. Most commentators took it to refer to the Qur'an: "produce a surah like it [i.e., like this Qur'an]." Others took it to refer to the servant: "produce a surah like it [i.e., from someone like him — an unlettered man like Muhammad]." Both readings are transmitted, and they converge on the same demand: produce one chapter that is genuinely comparable.

Note the modesty of the demand. Not the whole Book. Not ten chapters. One. And the Qur'an will go further in 17:88: "Say: If mankind and the jinn gathered together to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it, even if they helped one another." Read the challenge verses together — 52:33–34, 11:13, 10:38, 17:88 and this one — and you can see the bar being lowered each time: the whole Book, then ten surahs, then one surah, then one surah with all of humanity and the jinn collaborating. Each stage makes the demand easier and the failure more complete. And the demand is not to produce something *better*; it is only to produce something *equal*.

*Wa-d'u shuhada'akum min duni Allahi in kuntum sadiqin* — "and call your helpers apart from Allah, if you are truthful." The word *shuhada'akum* is read two ways by the commentators, and both are transmitted: as "your witnesses," those who will testify that what you produced is equal to the Qur'an, or as "your helpers," those who will assist you in producing it. The root covers both senses — one who is present, one who testifies, one who aids. And *min duni Allah* throws the door wide: call on anyone other than Allah — your gods, your poets, your allies, and even the jinn, whom the Arabs believed inspired their poets. "If you are truthful" refers to their claim that the Qur'an is merely human speech.

What, then, makes the Qur'an inimitable? This is one of the great theological questions of Islam, and the answers are worth knowing because they prevent the question from being trivialised. A minority view, associated with the Mu'tazili thinker al-Nazzam and known as *sarfah*, held that Allah simply turned away the Arabs' ability to meet the challenge — that they were capable but prevented. Mainstream scholarship rejected this, and for a good reason: if Allah prevented them, the challenge would be meaningless, and the Qur'an's own argument would collapse. The majority position is that the Qur'an is inimitable in itself. The great theorists of this doctrine each located the miracle somewhere slightly different: al-Rummani catalogued multiple aspects; al-Khattabi argued it lies in the unique combination of wording, meaning and arrangement; and 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani, whose theory became the most influential, located it in the *nazm* — the specific ordering and composition, such that the inimitability is not in any individual word but in the way meanings are built together. That is why the challenge is to produce a *composition*, not merely a beautiful sentence.

Two honest qualifications belong here. First, Western academic scholarship has raised questions about the historical framing of the challenge — scholars such as Nöldeke, Bell and, in a more radical vein, Wansbrough proposed alternative accounts of the Qur'an's emergence and literary context. These are hypotheses, not established facts, and they remain contested within the field; the traditional position is that the challenge is a genuine, public, unrepeated claim that no one has met. Second, some modern Muslim apologists have tried to ground inimitability in the Qur'an's scientific statements. This is a mistake, and it is worth naming: a scientific claim can be superseded, and making it the foundation of *i'jaz* puts the doctrine on borrowed ground. The classical foundation is literary, spiritual, legislative and existential — a Book that transformed an entire civilisation's language, law and conscience within a single generation, produced by a man who had never composed a poem.

The practical lesson for a modern reader is about the character of the faith itself. This verse makes Islam a religion that submits its central claim to a test. No one is asked to believe without evidence; the evidence offered is public, repeatable and, in principle, falsifiable — produce one comparable chapter. Fourteen centuries on, the Arabic-speaking world, including its critics and hostile scholars, has not produced a surah that its own literary community accepted as comparable. That is not a proof in the mathematical sense, and a Muslim should not overstate it as one; but it is a striking fact about a challenge that has been open, unanswered and unretracted for a very long time.

## Verse 24

**"But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers."**

In simple terms, the verse completes the argument: if you cannot meet the challenge — and you never will — then the conclusion follows that this Book is from Allah, and so you should fear the Fire whose fuel is human beings and stones, prepared for those who reject it.

The structure is a compressed syllogism, and it is worth unpacking because the verse moves fast. Premise one: you cannot produce a surah like it. Premise two: therefore the Book is not human speech. Conclusion one: therefore it is from the Creator. Conclusion two: therefore rejecting it has consequences. Instruction: fear the consequence. All of that in one verse.

*Wa lan taf'alu* — "and you will never do it." The negation here is *lan*, the particle of emphatic future negation, which in Arabic denies something across the whole of the future rather than at a single moment. This is not only a challenge; it is a prediction. And the prediction is remarkable for how exposed it is. A single successful surah would have falsified the Qur'an permanently, and there were, in seventh-century Arabia, thousands of people with every motive to produce one — poets whose livelihoods and status were being destroyed, chiefs whose ancestral religion was being dismantled, and a hostile opposition spanning the whole peninsula. The classical and modern scholars alike have treated the unfulfilled prediction as a standing argument, and it should be presented as such: it is a public claim that has been open to refutation for fourteen centuries.

Two qualifications keep the argument honest. Theologically, the statement is an expression of Allah's knowledge — He knew they would not do it — and, as with verse 6, this is foreknowledge rather than compulsion; they remained entirely free to try, and their failure was their own. Historically, some academic scholars have proposed that the challenge verses reflect a later literary framing rather than a recorded event; that is a hypothesis to be weighed against the evidence, not an established fact, and the traditional reading remains the stronger one.

*Fa-ittaqu al-nara* — "then fear the Fire." The verb is from the same root as *taqwa*, and it means to place a shield between yourself and what you fear. The instruction is not "despair" but "take protective action." Fear, here, is a motive for movement, not a state to be inhabited.

*Allati waquduha al-nasu wa al-hijarah* — "whose fuel is people and stones." Human beings are themselves the fuel of this Fire. That is a deliberately horrifying image, and the Qur'an repeats it in 66:6: "O you who believe, protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones."

The mention of *stones* drew three explanations from the commentators, and they are not exclusive. The first, and the most rhetorically devastating, is that the stones are the idols they worshipped — so that the objects of their worship become the fuel of their punishment. The Qur'an states this openly in 21:98: "Indeed, you and what you worship besides Allah are the firewood of Hell." (There is a well-known qualification to that verse supplied by 21:101: those who were themselves worshipped but were righteous servants of Allah — such as Jesus and the angels — are excluded and are far removed from it.) The second explanation is that the fire is so intense that it consumes even stone, which conveys its severity. The third, transmitted in some early reports, is that these are stones of a sulphurous kind that burn with particular intensity and foulness. All three are held; the first carries the sharpest moral point: what you elevated in place of God is what will burn you.

The verse closes with *u'iddat lil-kafirin* — "prepared for the disbelievers." That one word, *u'iddat* (it has been prepared, made ready), settled a real theological disagreement. The mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah holds that Paradise and the Fire are already created and exist now, citing this verse alongside 3:131 and 3:133 ("Paradise... prepared for the righteous"), and authentic narrations in which Paradise and Hell were shown to the Prophet ﷺ — as in the eclipse prayer, when he saw them brought near to him. The Mu'tazila held that they are not yet created and that Allah will bring them into being on the Day of Resurrection. The disagreement is genuine and well documented, and it rests on how these and similar texts are read.

A second disagreement attaches to the Fire's duration. The mainstream position, based on the explicit statements of the Qur'an and the consensus of the early community, is that the Fire is eternal for those who die in disbelief. A small number of later scholars — views attributed in particular to Ibn Taymiyyah and his student Ibn al-Qayyim — argued that the punishment of the Fire would eventually come to an end, basing this on certain narrations and a reading of Allah's mercy. That position was rejected by the overwhelming majority and should be cited only as a minority view, not as the Islamic teaching.

What should a reader *do* with a verse like this? Two things, and they belong together. The first is to take the warning seriously enough to act: the existence of a real consequence is the reason the message is urgent, and a faith without any sense of accountability is not the faith of the Qur'an. The second is never to use this verse as a weapon against another human being. No one can declare a specific person an inhabitant of the Fire, because the final state of a heart is unseen and known only to Allah. The verse is addressed to the one who has rejected the message after the evidence has been made clear; it is not a licence for believers to assign destinations. Held properly, the verse produces in a believer humility and gratitude rather than fear of other people: gratitude that he was shown the way out, and the plea that the early Muslims made constantly — "O Allah, we ask You for Paradise and seek refuge in You from the Fire."

## Verse 25

**"Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever."**

In simple terms, immediately after the warning of the Fire, Allah tells the Prophet ﷺ to give the believers the opposite news: those who believe and do righteous deeds will have gardens with rivers flowing beneath them, fruit that resembles what they knew but is better than anything they knew, purified companions, and permanence.

The placement of this verse is itself a teaching method. Verse 24 warns; verse 25 gives good news. The scholars call this pairing *al-targhib wa al-tarhib* — hope and fear — and the Qur'an uses it constantly, because a human being moved only by fear becomes brittle and a human being moved only by hope becomes complacent. The Prophet ﷺ is addressed personally: *wa bashshir* — "and give good news." The message reaches the believers through a messenger, which is a reminder that faith is transmitted through people, not downloaded from the sky.

Who receives the good news? *Alladhina amanu wa 'amilu al-salihat* — "those who believe and do righteous deeds." This is the most repeated description of the saved in the entire Qur'an, appearing dozens of times. Its significance for the question of faith and works is direct. The mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah holds that deeds are the necessary fruit and completion of faith — that genuine belief in the heart produces obedience in the limbs — while rejecting the Kharijite position that a major sin expels a person from faith, and rejecting the Murji'ite position that deeds are irrelevant to it. The constant coupling of belief and deeds in the Qur'an is the textual basis for the middle position.

*Anna lahum jannatin tajri min tahtiha al-anhar* — "that for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow." *Jannah* comes from a root meaning to conceal or cover, and it originally denoted a dense garden whose foliage hides the ground. The description here is of gardens with rivers running *beneath* them — beneath the trees and the dwellings, not merely through them. The commentators noted the implication: water is everywhere under you, so there is no labour in reaching it. For the original audience — desert Arabs for whom drought was the permanent background anxiety and a well was a matter of survival — no image of security and joy could have been more immediate. The Qur'an builds its promise of the next world out of the deepest longings of this one, which is one reason it lands so hard.

Then the remarkable detail: *kullama ruziqu minha min thamaratin rizqan qalu hadha alladhi ruziqna min qablu* — "whenever they are provided with fruit from it as provision, they say: this is what we were provided with before." The commentators differed over why they say this. The majority reading, which is the one your translation follows, is that the fruit of Paradise resembles the fruit of this world in appearance and name but tastes utterly different — so they recognise it and then discover it is not what they thought at all. This reading is supported by the next clause: *wa utu bihi mutashabihan*, "and they were given things that resemble it." A second reading is that on receiving a second helping they say "this is what we were given before," meaning earlier in Paradise. Both are transmitted; the first is stronger because the clause about resemblance is clearly there to explain the statement.

The combination is theologically precise, and it is worth holding both halves. There is *continuity*: the fruit is recognisable, your memory and identity are intact, you are still you, and the good things you knew are not abolished but fulfilled. And there is *discontinuity*: the taste is different, and it is different in the direction of better. The Qur'an and Sunnah are emphatic that the reality is not merely a magnified version of this world. In the hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, Allah says: "I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has not occurred to the heart of any human being." And 32:17 says the same: "No soul knows what has been hidden for them of comfort for the eyes, as a reward for what they used to do." So the resemblance is a pointer for our limited imaginations, not a description of the reality.

*Wa lahum fiha azwajun mutahharah* — "and they will have purified spouses therein." *Azwaj* is a general word for spouses or mates, and *mutahharah* means purified. The commentators explained the purification as covering physical impurities — what the body in this world is subject to — and, in the view of many, moral and relational defects as well: the jealousy, pettiness, harshness and disappointment that mar even the best companionship here. The promise is of companionship with nothing in it that spoils it.

Two points of accuracy matter here. First, because *azwaj* is general, the promise belongs to believing men and believing women alike; the Qur'an elsewhere speaks of the *hur 'in* (52:20, 55:56–58, 56:22–23) and also of husbands and wives with their families reunited (13:23, 40:8: "Admit them to the Gardens of Eden which You promised them, and whoever was righteous among their fathers, their spouses and their descendants"). The standard position of the scholars is that the believing women of this world will be in Paradise with their husbands, and that whatever else Allah grants is additional. Second, this dignifies marriage and family in the present life: the relationships you built for the sake of Allah are not discarded at death. They are among the things that are kept.

*Wa hum fiha khalidun* — "and they will abide therein forever." The decisive word. The joy is not a long holiday that ends. It is permanent, and its permanence is what makes it capable of settling the heart in a way that nothing temporary can. Every pleasure in this life is shadowed by the knowledge that it is passing — a meal ends, a day ends, a life ends. The Qur'an's answer to that shadow is *khalidun*.

Now, practically: this verse is the answer to a question that wears many disguises — "what is the point of living a good life when it costs me so much?" The Qur'an's answer is not an argument about duty alone; it is a description of what is being offered. And note how carefully the description is pitched. It speaks of gardens, rivers, fruit and companionship — things the audience could taste and long for — while simultaneously declaring through the Sunnah that the reality exceeds anything the imagination can construct. That balance is the signature of the Qur'an's way with the next world: near enough to be desired, far enough to remain godly.

## Verse 26

**"Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—"**

In simple terms, Allah declares that He does not hold back from teaching by any comparison, however small or seemingly insignificant the thing compared. The same parable then produces two opposite effects: believers recognise it as truth from their Lord and are guided by it, while those who reject it ask "what could Allah possibly mean by this?" and are left further astray — and the verse is careful to say that no one is left astray by it except those who were already rebellious.

*Inna Allaha la yastahyi an yadriba mathalan* — "Indeed Allah is not shy to strike a parable." *Haya'*, modesty or shyness, is attributed to Allah here in the negative, and the tradition treats it the way it treats all such attributes: it is affirmed in a manner befitting Him, without likening it to the modesty of creatures. There is support for affirming it positively as well — a narration reported by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi states that "Allah is modest and generous; He is shy to send away empty the hands of a servant who raises them to Him." So the verse is not saying Allah lacks an attribute; it is saying that nothing is beneath Him to use in teaching, because His purpose is truth rather than reputation.

The occasion behind the verse is well attested in the commentaries, though the details vary. When the Qur'an struck parables using small or unimpressive creatures — the mosquito here, the fly in 22:73, the spider in 29:41 — some of the opponents, and according to many reports some of the hypocrites, mocked it: what would Allah want with a mosquito? This verse came as the answer. The reports differ on which parable prompted it, and it is better to say that the verse answers the general objection than to tie it to one specific incident.

*Ma ba'udatan fa-ma fawqaha* — "a mosquito or what is above it." Here the commentators genuinely differed, and the difference is worth reporting because your translation has chosen one side of it. The phrase *ma fawqaha* literally means "what is above it." Many classical commentators understood this as "what is larger than it" — so the sense is: Allah is not shy to strike a parable with something as small as a mosquito or with something larger; nothing is too small and nothing too great. Others understood it as "what is smaller than it" — and this is the reading your translation follows ("a mosquito or what is even smaller") — on the reasoning that the objection was specifically about smallness, so the answer pushes further: not even the tiniest thing is beneath His mention. Both readings are transmitted from the early authorities, and both serve the same theological point.

And what a point it is. Nothing in creation is too small, too lowly or too undignified for Allah to mention. Ants, bees, spiders, flies, gnats — the Qur'an names them, and in doing so it confers a dignity on the small that no ancient literature had thought to confer. This is a consistent Qur'anic theme and it has consequences: no human being is too insignificant to be addressed by his Lord, and no part of creation is beneath a believer's attention or respect.

Now the heart of the verse: the same parable produces opposite results. *Fa-amma alladhina amanu fa-ya'lamuna annahu al-haqqu min rabbihim* — "as for those who believe, they know that it is the truth from their Lord." Their response is *knowledge*: because they have already accepted the Source, whatever He says is fitting by definition, and so they receive the benefit even from a parable they do not fully understand. *Wa amma alladhina kafaru fa-yaquluna madha arada Allahu bi-hadha mathalan* — "as for those who disbelieve, they say: what did Allah intend by this parable?" On the surface this is a question. In reality it is a refusal. The question is not asked in order to learn; it is asked in order to dismiss, and the asking is itself the mechanism by which the person closes himself off.

*Yudillu bihi kathiran wa yahdi bihi kathiran* — "He leaves many astray by it and guides many by it." Here is the crux, and it needs to be stated carefully because it is one of the most misused verses in the Qur'an. The instrument is one — the same revelation, the same parable — and the outcomes are opposite. The resolution the mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah gives is that the difference lies in the receiver, not in the source. Guidance is by Allah's grace; being left astray is the consequence of the receiver's own rejection. The Qur'an says exactly this elsewhere: "We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers, but it does not increase the wrongdoers except in loss" (17:82). And in 9:124–125: "As for those who believe, it has increased them in faith... but as for those in whose hearts is disease, it has increased them in filth upon their filth." The Mu'tazila, consistent with their position throughout this surah, referred the misguidance to the person rather than to Allah's act. The Sunni reading is stronger because of what comes next.

*Wa ma yudillu bihi illa al-fasiqin* — "and He leaves none astray by it except the rebellious." This is the governing restriction, and it is the single most important clause in the verse for preventing abuse. Misguidance is not random, not arbitrary, and not inflicted on the sincere. It is restricted to one category: *al-fasiqun*, those who have already walked out of obedience. *Fisq* comes from a root meaning to exit or go out — the *fasiq* is one who has departed from the command of Allah. So the causal order is: rebellion first, being left astray second. The rebel is not left astray because Allah picked him; he is left astray because he has already chosen to leave.

That has two practical consequences that pull in opposite directions, and both are needed. For the sincere seeker, the verse is enormously reassuring: if you are approaching the Qur'an wanting to know, you are not in the category this verse warns about. Doubts that arrive unbidden, confusion about a difficult verse, a heart that sometimes feels hard — none of that is *fisq*. For the complacent, the verse is a warning: the thing that gets a person left astray is not a bad argument but a rebellious posture.

Consider how this plays out now. The internet is full of people holding up a verse of the Qur'an and saying, "can you believe it says this?" — pulling a text out of its context in order to mock it. That is precisely the posture of *madha arada Allahu bi-hadha mathalan*. The verse predicts that such a person will get nothing from the Qur'an, and explains why: he was not reading to learn. Whereas the believer who meets a verse he cannot understand says "this is from my Lord, and my understanding is limited," and benefits from the very verse that ruins someone else.

## Verse 27

**"those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the ˹true˺ losers."**

In simple terms, this verse defines exactly who the "rebellious" of the previous verse are: those who break their covenant with Allah after having made it binding, who cut the relationships Allah commanded them to maintain, and who spread corruption in the world. These are the truly lost.

*Alladhina yanquduna 'ahda Allahi min ba'di mithaqih* — "those who break the covenant of Allah after its confirmation." The verbs are chosen with precision. *Naqda* means to unravel — it is the word used for unpicking a twisted rope, strand by strand. *Mithaq*, from the same root as *wathaqa* (to bind firmly), is a covenant that has been securely tied and solemnly confirmed. So the picture is of someone patiently unravelling a bond that had been made fast. And *min ba'di mithaqih*, "after its confirmation," is the aggravating circumstance: this is not a promise forgotten or a duty never understood. It is a binding commitment, knowingly entered into, deliberately undone.

What is this covenant? The commentators described it at several levels, and the safest reading includes them all. There is the primordial covenant witnessed by every human soul, referred to in 7:172 — "Am I not your Lord? They said: Yes, we testify" — though it should be noted that some scholars, particularly in modern times, have read that passage as a dramatic representation of the innate disposition (*fitrah*) rather than as a literal event in a pre-existent realm, and the majority read it as a real event. There is the covenant implanted in the human constitution, the *fitrah* that every child is born upon, as the well-known hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim states. And there is the covenant delivered through revelation and through the messengers — including the specific pledge taken from the People of the Book to believe in the messenger who would come confirming what they had, mentioned in 3:81 and in this surah at verse 40. The phrase covers all of these.

*Wa yaqta'una ma amara Allahu bihi an yusala* — "and they sever what Allah has commanded to be joined." Again the Arabic sets the two verbs against each other: *yaqta'una* (they cut) against *an yusala* (to be connected), from the root *w-s-l* that gives us *silah*, the maintenance of a bond. What is it that must be joined? The commentators gave a range that is best read as concentric. The innermost and most cited instance is the ties of kinship — and the hadith literature supports this emphatically. Al-Bukhari and Muslim record from Jubayr ibn Mut'im that the Prophet ﷺ said: "The one who severs [the ties of kinship] will not enter Paradise." And when a man asked the Prophet ﷺ for a single deed that would admit him to Paradise, he was told, in the narration of Abu Ayyub recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: "Worship Allah and do not associate anything with Him, establish the prayer, give zakah, and maintain the ties of kinship." Wider circles in the same phrase include the bond of faith between believers, the unity of the messengers — believing in some and rejecting others is severing what Allah joined, as 4:150–152 makes clear — and, generally, every relationship Allah has commanded to be sustained.

*Wa yufsiduna fi al-ard* — "and they spread corruption in the land." This is the third trait, and it is the same phrase that appeared in verse 11 for the hypocrites, which tells you that the two groups are being described by overlapping symptoms. *Fasad* is decay and disorder, the undoing of the soundness that *islah* builds, and the Qur'an treats it as a public harm that spreads rather than a private vice.

*Ula'ika hum al-khasirun* — "it is they who are the losers." Note the commercial vocabulary, which connects this verse directly back to verse 16, where the hypocrites' trade "did not profit." *Khasara* is to lose in a transaction. So the passage's final verdict on all three groups is stated in the language of the market: what they bought did not pay. And it is worth putting this beside Surah al-'Asr, which is the Qur'an's complete answer to the condition: "Indeed mankind is in loss, except those who believe, do righteous deeds, enjoin one another to truth, and enjoin one another to patience" (103:2–3). Note that the escape from loss includes *enjoining one another to truth*, which is the exact opposite of severing what should be joined — the opposite of the *fasiqun* described here.

Now, practically: these three traits are a diagnostic, and they operate at the level of individuals, families and whole societies. Breaking the covenant looks like the marriage vow discarded, the contract honoured only while it is convenient, the oath of office treated as a formality, the professional code set aside when nobody is looking. Severing what should be joined looks like the family that has not spoken in ten years over an inheritance, the community that splits over a dispute about a building, the believer who quietly drops everyone who is no longer useful to him, the child who cuts off his parents. And corruption is everything catalogued at verse 11.

But the deepest use of this verse is devotional, and it should be said plainly. This is the Qur'an's answer to the question that verse 26 raises in every serious reader: could that happen to me? The answer given here is that the people who end up ruined are not the doubters, not the ignorant, not the struggling — they are the ones who had a covenant, broke it; who had ties, cut them; and who had a world entrusted to them, corrupted it. That is a description of a settled orientation, not of a bad week. Which means the way to stay out of it is not to achieve sinlessness but to keep returning: to renew the covenant by repentance, to keep mending the ties, and to keep refusing the corruption that is normal all around you.

## Verse 28

**"How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned."**

In simple terms, the verse asks a question of stunned disbelief: how can any of you deny Allah, when you were nothing and He gave you life, when He will cause you to die, when He will bring you back to life again, and when your return is to Him?

*Kayfa takfuruna billahi* — "How can you deny Allah?" The question is *kayfa*, "how," not "why." That is deliberate. It is not a demand for a motive; it is an expression of amazement that anyone could hold this position while knowing what he knows. The address is direct, in the second person, after verses that had been describing people in the third person — the same rhetorical turn of address (*iltifat*) that we saw in al-Fatihah, and it has the same effect: the reader is suddenly being spoken to rather than spoken about.

*Wa kuntum amwatan fa-ahyakum* — "and you were dead and He gave you life." The commentators explained *amwat*, "dead," in two ways, and they converge. The majority held that it means you were non-existent — you were nothing at all, and He brought you into being. Others held that it refers to your earlier material states: dust and clay, or a drop of fluid in the loins of your fathers or in the womb before the spirit was breathed in, as described in 23:12–14. Both readings make the same point with equal force: your existence is a gift you did not negotiate, did not earn, and had no claim to. You did not contribute to your own creation, which is the ground of the whole argument. If you did not make yourself, you are not your own.

*Thumma yumitukum thumma yuhyikum* — "then He will cause you to die, then He will bring you to life." This is the four-stage structure that the Qur'an sets out in several places and that the people of the Fire themselves acknowledge in 40:11: "Our Lord, You caused us to die twice and gave us life twice." So: non-existence, then life in this world, then death at its end, then life again at the Resurrection. This verse is one of the clearest single statements of that sequence in the Book.

This was, of course, the precise doctrine that the Arab polytheists rejected. They did not, for the most part, deny that Allah was the Creator — as verse 22 noted, they affirmed it. What they denied was the second life. And the Qur'an's answer throughout is the argument from origination: the One who produced you when you were nothing has no difficulty producing you again. "He it is Who originates creation and then repeats it, and that is even easier for Him" (30:27). And in Surah Ya-Sin: "Who will give life to bones when they are decayed? Say: He will give them life Who produced them the first time, for He is, of all creation, Knowing" (36:78–79). The logic is not that repetition is mechanically easier; it is that anyone capable of the first act is self-evidently capable of the second, and the Qur'an adds that to Allah the second is, if anything, simpler.

*Thumma ilayhi turja'un* — "then to Him you will be returned." The destination is stated last, and it is the point of it all. You did not originate yourself, you do not control your death, you will be raised, and the return is *to Him* — to the same Lord who gave you life in the first place. Which means this life is a trust, and its end is an audience.

One clarification is necessary because this verse is sometimes misread. The second "bringing to life" is the Resurrection, not a return to this world, and there is no doctrine of reincarnation in Islam. The four stages run in one direction only.

Now, what does holding this verse in mind do to a person? It reframes the two questions that quietly organise most human anxiety: where did I come from, and where am I going? The Qur'an answers both with the same Name. You came from nothing by His giving, and you are going back to Him. Everything in between is a trust held for a limited term. That reframing has a name in the tradition — *taqwa*, the very thing verse 21 said worship was designed to produce — and it works on the ordinary decisions rather than only on the cosmic ones. A person who remembers that he was nothing and is returning to his Lord does not treat his wealth as though he created it, his body as though he owns it outright, or his time as though it were infinite.

And there is a gentler use for this verse as well. The stages it lists — nothingness, life, death, life again, return — are also the shape of a great deal of ordinary human experience. Things come alive and then go quiet: a career, a season of faith, a relationship, a community. The Qur'an's framing here is that the One who brought something to life once is not diminished when it goes dark, because He is the One who will bring it to life again. That is not a promise of worldly continuity; it is the reason the believer's hope is never finally hostage to circumstances.

## Verse 29

**"He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things."**

In simple terms, Allah made everything on earth for your benefit, then turned to the ordering of the sky and formed it into seven heavens, and He knows every single thing.

*Huwa alladhi khalaqa lakum ma fi al-ardi jami'an* — "He it is Who created for you all that is in the earth." The *lakum*, "for you," repeats the theme of verse 22 and of the wider Qur'anic doctrine of *taskhir*, subjugation: the world has been put at the service of human beings by the One who made it (31:20; 45:13). And *jami'an*, "all together," removes any exception — everything in the earth, without remainder.

A qualification is needed here, because this phrase is sometimes taken further than it goes. "Created for you" means the earth and its contents were made for your benefit and placed at your disposal. It does not mean that everything in it is *permitted* to you. That distinction is basic to the methodology of Islamic law: this verse establishes gift and subjugation, while what is lawful and unlawful is determined by the rest of revelation. So the verse is not a licence; it is the ground for gratitude, and gratitude to a Giver means using the gift on the Giver's terms. This matters because the verse is sometimes quoted as if it authorised unrestrained exploitation. It does the opposite: everything here is described as someone else's, given to you — and verse 30 will immediately name you a steward rather than an owner.

*Thumma istawa ila al-sama'i* — "then He turned towards the heaven." Here we come to one of the most discussed words in Islamic theology. *Istawa* is the verb used for Allah's "rising over" the Throne in several verses, and the schools differed on how to handle it. The position of the early generations (*salaf*), and the one held by the Athari tradition, is to affirm it as Allah affirmed it — a real attribute of action — without asking how (*bi-la kayf*), without likening it to the actions of creatures, without denying it, and without reinterpreting it away. The statement attributed to Imam Malik ibn Anas captures the method exactly: "The *istiwaa'* is known, the 'how' is unknown, believing in it is obligatory, and asking about it is an innovation." Later Ash'ari and Maturidi scholars commonly practised *ta'wil*, interpreting the word according to a meaning befitting Allah — most often as *istawla*, "He took dominion over it" — while al-Ash'ari himself, in his later works, inclined toward affirmation without asking how. The Mu'tazila took the interpretive route as a matter of principle.

Three clarifications keep this from being distorted in either direction. First, this is an intra-Sunni disagreement about method, and it should be described as such rather than as a split between orthodoxy and error. Second, no legitimate Islamic position holds that Allah is spatially located, contained by a place, or subject to direction; all parties agree that He is exalted above that. Third, and most helpful for reading *this* verse: the context does much of the work. *Istawa ila al-sama'* is followed immediately by *fa-sawwahunna*, "and He proportioned them," which makes the functional sense unmistakable — He directed His will and creative act to the ordering of the heaven. Whatever one's theological method, the verse is about Allah turning to the formation of the sky.

*Fa-sawwahunna sab'a samawat* — "and He proportioned them into seven heavens." *Sawwa* means to level, to proportion, to make complete and well-formed. So the emphasis is not merely on creating but on ordering: He proportioned them and set them right. The number seven is stated plainly in several places: "He created seven heavens, and of the earth the like of them" (65:12); "Who created seven heavens in layers" (67:3 and 71:15); "We built above you seven strong [heavens]" (78:12); and 23:17 speaks of "seven paths" above you.

What are the seven heavens? The classical commentators understood them as seven created strata, one above another, the lowest of which is the sky we see, adorned with stars — "We have adorned the lowest heaven with the adornment of the stars" (37:6). This is the position of the early authorities and of the tradition generally, and it comes with the honest corollary that their exact nature is known to Allah. Modern writers have at times tried to identify the seven heavens with layers of the atmosphere, with the seven planets of ancient astronomy, or with structures in contemporary cosmology. These are speculative identifications, and they should be labelled as such. The word *sama'* in Arabic covers everything above you — the sky, the atmosphere, the expanse of space — and the Qur'an's purpose in this verse is not to deliver a cosmological map. It is to say that the sky above you is a proportioned, ordered, deliberate construction, and that the Builder is the One who deserves your worship.

*Wa huwa bi-kulli shay'in 'alim* — "and He is, of all things, Knowing." The closing attribute is exactly the right one. He knows what He created, what it needs, what benefits you, and what you are doing with what you were given. The pattern of closing a verse with the attribute appropriate to its content is constant in the Qur'an — verse 20 closed with *qadir*, the All-Powerful; this one closes with *'alim*, the All-Knowing.

Practically, the verse sets up the relationship between human beings and the natural world in a single sentence, and it is a relationship with two sides. Because everything in the earth was created *for you*, the world is not an accident, a trap or an enemy; it is a gift, and the appropriate response is gratitude and use rather than either ascetic rejection or greedy hoarding. And because it was created *by* the One who knows everything and who ordered the heavens above you, it is not yours to destroy. That is why the Qur'an calls wasteful destruction *fasad* — the very word used in verse 27 for what the losers do — and why the next verse immediately names the human being a *khalifah*, a steward who will be asked about what he was entrusted with.

## Verse 30

**"˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”"**

In simple terms, Allah tells the angels that He is going to place a human being on earth as His steward and successor. The angels ask — not objecting but seeking understanding — whether He will place there a creature who will spread corruption and shed blood, when they themselves do nothing but glorify and sanctify Him. Allah answers: I know what you do not know.

*Wa idh qala rabbuka lil-mala'ikati* — "and when your Lord said to the angels." The formula *wa idh* ("and when") carries an implied "remember," and it is the Qur'an's standard way of opening a narrative that the reader is meant to hold in mind rather than merely receive as information. Note also that the address to the Prophet ﷺ is *rabbuka*, "your Lord," which keeps the narrative personal: this is your Lord's doing, and your community is the inheritor of it.

*Inni ja'ilun fi al-ardi khalifah* — "I am placing on the earth a *khalifah*." This is one of the most consequential words in the Qur'an. The root *kh-l-f* means to come after, to succeed, to stand behind and in place of another, and the commentators explained the term in three overlapping senses. The first is that Adam succeeds previous inhabitants of the earth — a report widely transmitted in the tafsir literature holds that the jinn dwelt on the earth before him and spread corruption there, which would explain how the angels knew what to ask about. That report is common in the commentaries but its chain is not firmly established to the Prophet ﷺ; it rests on early authorities and material of the kind the scholars caution about, so it should be cited with that qualification and never as though it were certain. The second sense, and the one that carries the most legal and theological weight, is that the human being is Allah's vicegerent — one charged with authority and responsibility on His behalf, entrusted with implementing His trust in a place that does not belong to him. The third is that human beings succeed one another, generation after generation. All three are transmitted, and the word can bear all three at once.

That last point deserves emphasis because of how the word was used later. *Khalifah* became the title of the political leader of the Muslim community after the Prophet ﷺ, and Islamic political thought grew out of this concept. But the Qur'an gives the title first and most fundamentally to Adam — that is, to the human being as such, not to a ruler. Every human being is a *khalifah* within his own sphere: over his own self, his family, his work, his influence. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ibn 'Umar: "Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock." That hadith is the practical unpacking of this verse.

*A-taj'alu fiha man yufsidu fiha wa yasfiku al-dima'* — "Will You place in it one who will spread corruption there and shed blood?" Before anything else, an important correction is needed, because this question is frequently misunderstood. This is not an objection, and it is not the angels challenging Allah's decision. The Qur'an describes the angels as creatures who "do not disobey Allah in what He commands them, and do what they are commanded" (66:6) and who "do not precede Him in speech" (21:27). The question is therefore a request for wisdom — *why*, given that such a creature will do these things? Al-Razi and others discussed how they knew; the answers offered include knowledge of the Preserved Tablet, inference from the nature of a creature made with desire and free will, and, per the report mentioned above, observation of earlier inhabitants. What matters is the posture: they ask in order to learn, and Allah answers in kind — "I know what you do not know" — which is the appropriate response to a question and would be a strange response to a protest.

Two further observations. The first is that the two evils the angels name — corruption and bloodshed — are the two great social crimes, the destruction of order and the destruction of life, and they echo the *fasad* of verses 11, 12 and 27. The second is that the Qur'an never denies the charge. It puts the accusation in the mouths of the angels and never refutes the fact that human beings do these things. Islam has no doctrine of human innocence and no utopian account of human nature.

But neither does it have a doctrine of original sin. That balance is essential and is one of the distinctive features of the Islamic view. Human beings really do corrupt and shed blood, and every human being except the prophets has gone wrong and returned, as the Prophet ﷺ said in a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi. But no child inherits guilt, Adam's repentance was accepted and his sin was not transmitted, and every human being is born on the *fitrah* — the natural disposition towards the truth — as the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim states. The human being is capable of great evil and great good, and that dual capacity is precisely what makes the trust meaningful.

*Wa nahnu nusabbihu bi-hamdika wa nuqaddisu laka* — "while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You." *Tasbih* declares Allah free of every imperfection; *taqdis* declares Him holy and beyond every defect. The angels' implicit argument is: we do nothing but worship, so why place a creature who will do the opposite? Their premise is true and is never rebuked — the worship of the angels is real and is honoured throughout the Qur'an. But the comparison misses the relevant difference, and that difference is the key to Allah's answer.

The angels obey by nature; they have no capacity to disobey. Human beings are given desire, forgetfulness, and the real ability to refuse — and the one who, possessing that ability, chooses obedience, achieves something the angels cannot achieve by their constitution. This is why the majority of Ahl al-Sunnah held that the righteous among human beings, and above all the prophets, are superior to the angels, though the question was genuinely disputed, with other scholars holding the angels superior and others specifying. The disagreement is a well-known one in the books of creed, and it turns on exactly this point about the nature of moral achievement.

*Qala inni a'lamu ma la ta'lamun* — "He said: I know what you do not know." Notice that the answer does not explain. It asserts knowledge and leaves the matter there — and the following verses will demonstrate rather than explain, by showing the angels the names they could not name. The commentators drew several wisdoms from this restraint. Some of the wisdom is beyond what the angels could grasp at that moment and would unfold across human history. Some of it could only be understood by witnessing it. And rhetorically, the answer teaches the proper etiquette of a creature before a decree he does not understand: to defer, and to say "Allah knows what I do not know."

That is a lesson with direct application. The angels, who have never sinned and who worship without cease, are taught that their knowledge has limits — and they accept it, as verse 32 will show them saying in their own words: "We have no knowledge except what You have taught us." If the angels need that lesson, no human being is exempt from it. Every period of human history produces people who decline to accept a divine command because they cannot see its wisdom, and this verse is the standing reply.

And there is a final, quieter gift in this verse, which is the one most needed by people reading it now. In a world where human beings are constantly reduced to economic units, to problems, to demographics, to content, the Qur'an opens the story of humanity by announcing that the human being is Allah's *khalifah* on His earth — the one to whom the trust was offered when the heavens and the mountains refused it (33:72). Human dignity in Islamic thought is not derived from the state, from utility or from anyone's recognition. It is conferred here, at the very beginning, by the One who placed you here.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Verses 21 to 30 shift the surah from analysis to invitation. After sorting humanity into three groups, Allah addresses all of them at once and makes the case for worship in four movements. Verses 21 and 22 argue from provision: the Lord who made you, made the earth a bed, raised the sky as a structure and sends down the rain that grows your food is the only One who deserves your worship, so set up no rivals to Him — and you know better. Verses 23 and 24 issue the standing challenge, the public test by which the Qur'an's claim can be checked, and follow it with the warning of a Fire whose fuel is human beings and the very stones they worshipped. Verse 25 answers that warning with the promise: gardens, rivers, fruit that is familiar and yet unrecognisably better, purified companionship, and permanence. Verses 26 and 27 then explain the strangest fact of all — that one revelation guides some and ruins others — and they locate the difference not in the text but in the receiver, restricting the ruin to those who broke a confirmed covenant, cut what they were commanded to join, and corrupted the earth. Verses 28 and 29 return to the evidence: you were nothing and He gave you life, you will die and be raised and returned to Him, and everything on the earth was made for you by the One who proportioned the seven heavens and knows all things. Verse 30 opens the story of Adam with the announcement that defines the human being: a *khalifah*, a steward placed on the earth by a decree whose wisdom exceeds even what the angels could see.

Three threads run through the whole passage. First, worship is grounded in gift, not in power: every argument here begins from something already given — life, earth, sky, rain, fruit, the Book itself. Second, the invitation comes with evidence attached and a test offered, which means faith in the Qur'an's presentation is not a leap in the dark but a response to a claim that can be examined. And third, the same message that heals one person ruins another, and the difference is posture: the one who asks "what does my Lord mean by this?" in order to learn receives light, while the one who asks it in order to dismiss receives only more darkness — and that is the most searching question any reader of these ten verses can ask himself.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where narrations are disputed, the dispute is noted. Where the commentators genuinely differ — whether *ma fawqaha* in verse 26 means larger or smaller, whether the *mithaq* is the primordial covenant or the revealed one, whether "you were dead" means non-existence or inanimate matter, and how *istiwaa'* should be handled — the disagreement has been represented rather than resolved by assertion. Three boundaries have been observed: the report that the jinn inhabited the earth before Adam is flagged as transmitted in the commentaries but not firmly established to the Prophet ﷺ; attempts to identify the seven heavens with modern cosmological structures are labelled as speculation; and scientific reflection on the "protected ceiling" is presented as reflection rather than as the meaning of the text. Western academic scholarship has been engaged with at verse 23, where hypotheses about the challenge's historical framing are explicitly identified as hypotheses and weighed against the traditional position rather than either accepted or dismissed on the basis of who proposed them.
