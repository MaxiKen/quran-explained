## Verse 1

Verse 1 says: *O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.*

In simple terms, the verse opens Surah An-Nisa with a direct address to all of humanity, not only to the believers: fear and remain mindful of your Lord, the One who created you from one soul, created its mate from it, and spread from the two of them countless men and women. You invoke Allah and make requests in His Name, so honour the family ties that bind you to one another. Allah is always watching over you.

The address itself is significant. The verse begins *yā ayyuhā al-nās* — "O humanity" — and this is the same universal address that opens the earliest portions of Surah Al-Baqarah (2:21). An-Nisa is a Madinan surah, and the classical sources confirm this. Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn 'Abbas, through Al-'Awfi, and also Ibn Az-Zubayr and Zayd bin Thabit, held that An-Nisa is Madinan. The reason matters: the surah was revealed while the Muslim community in Madinah was being built into a society with laws of inheritance, marriage, finance, and social justice, and its opening line frames all of those rulings inside a single theological claim — that the One who legislates for you is the One who created you. Ibn Kathir also reports Ibn Mas'ud's famous saying that there are five verses in Surat An-Nisa that he would prefer to the life of this world and everything in it: the verses on Allah not wronging anyone by the weight of an atom (4:40), on avoiding major sins (4:31), on Allah not forgiving shirk but forgiving whatever is lesser for whom He wills (4:48), on obeying the Messenger (4:64), and on seeking Allah's forgiveness (4:110). The opening verse belongs to the same register: everything that follows in the surah is anchored in the majesty and watchfulness of the Creator.

The central command is *ittaqū rabbakum* — "be mindful of your Lord." The word *taqwa*, from which the command comes, is built on the root *wa-qā-ya*, meaning to protect, to shield, or to guard oneself. A person with taqwa is not merely afraid; he is conscious of Allah in a way that shapes his conduct. Here the object of that consciousness is specified by what follows: the Lord who created you. Ibn Kathir notes that remembering the creation is itself the reason for taqwa — because the One who made you from nothing is the One whose watchfulness you cannot escape, and whose commands you cannot ignore without cost.

The description of creation is precise. Allah created humanity from *nafs wāhidah* — a single soul — and from it created its mate, then spread from the pair *rijālan kathīran wa nisā'* — many men and women. The classical commentators identify the single soul with Adam and the mate with Hawwa' (Eve). Ibn Kathir reports that Eve was created from Adam's left rib while he slept, that when Adam woke and saw her he was drawn to her and she to him, and he cites the authentic hadith recorded by Al-Bukhari and Muslim: "Woman was created from a rib. Verily, the most curved portion of the rib is its upper part, so if you try to straighten it, you will break it, but if you leave it as it is, it will remain crooked." There is a deep instruction in that image: the relationship between man and woman is not to be forced into a straightness that will break it. The creation of the mate "from" the first soul, rather than apart from it, also establishes a shared origin and a shared dignity.

The verse then anchors the second command in ordinary human practice. The phrase *tastaqsimūna bihi* appears in the translation as "in Whose Name you appeal to one another" — the usage reflected in the reading *tasā'alūna bihi*, where people said to one another, "I ask you by Allah," or, "For Allah's sake." Al-Jalalayn notes the variant readings *tatasā'alūna* and *tasā'alūna*, and explains that the verse refers to the custom of invoking Allah when making requests and pledging. The point is profound: the very Name you use to bind one another to promises is the Name of the One who created you — so be mindful of Him, and let that mindfulness show in how you treat the people for whose sake you invoke Him.

The command to honour family ties follows in the same breath. The Arabic reads *wa-al-arhām* — literally "and the wombs" or "the kinship ties" — and classical readers understood this either as an additional object of the command (fear Allah and fear kinship ties) or, on a variant reading, as an oath-like emphasis connected to the divine Name. Al-Muntakhab paraphrases it: "revere the wombs that bore you and regard them as having a divine and sacred character." The word *rahim* (womb) is the same root as *rahmah* (mercy) and *al-Rahmān* (the Most Merciful), and the classical scholars noted the connection between maintaining kinship and receiving mercy. The verse's closing word is *raqīb* — translated "ever Watchful" — meaning one who observes constantly and never overlooks. Allah is watchful over your creation, your speech, and your family bonds.

For the modern reader, the verse gathers four realities into a single opening statement. First, humanity is one family: every ethnic group, nation, and lineage descends from one soul, so racism and tribal superiority have no theological foundation. Second, the Creator is the basis of ethics: fear and mindfulness of Him precede the commands about family, money, and marriage that fill the rest of the surah. Third, kinship is sacred: the name of Allah is invoked in the very relationships of family, and severing those ties is a violation of the Name. Fourth, watchfulness is real: nothing in the home, the clan, or the community is outside the sight of Allah, and this awareness is what makes the law that follows meaningful rather than burdensome.

## Verse 2

Verse 2 says: *Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin.*

In simple terms, the verse commands guardians to hand over the property of orphans in their care once the orphans reach maturity, forbids substituting worthless goods for the orphans' valuable ones, and forbids blending orphan wealth with one's own in a way that consumes it. Doing so is declared a great sin.

The verse follows immediately after the opening command on family ties, and the connection is deliberate. The first verse spoke of the wombs and kinship; the second speaks of the orphan, the most vulnerable member of that kinship circle. Ibn Kathir titles his commentary on the passage "Protecting the Property of the Orphans" and explains that the guardian must surrender the orphans' property in full at adolescence. The phrase in the translation, "when they reach maturity," renders the Arabic condition understood from the command and from the fuller explanation in 4:6, which specifies that the wealth is returned when the orphan reaches marriageable age and demonstrates sound judgment.

The verse names two specific corrupt practices. The first is *wa-lā tabaddalū al-khabītha bi-al-tayyib* — "do not exchange the bad for the good." Ibn Kathir reports interpretations of this from the early authorities. Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib and Az-Zuhri said it means: do not substitute a weak animal of yours for a fat animal from the orphans' herd. Ibrahim An-Nakha'i and Ad-Dahhak said: do not give something of bad quality in exchange for something of good quality. As-Suddi gives the practice a vivid picture: the caretaker would take a fat sheep from the orphan's property, put a weak sheep of his own in its place saying "a sheep for a sheep," take a good dirham and replace it with a forged one saying "a dirham for a dirham." The Qur'an forbids the whole transaction, because the guardian is not buying from the orphan: he is holding property that belongs to someone else, and any substitution is theft dressed as exchange.

The second practice is *wa-lā ta'kulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum* — "do not consume their wealth into your wealth," which the translation renders "nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own." Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Muqatil bin Hayyan, As-Suddi, and Sufyan bin Husayn all explain that this refers to the guardian who mixes the orphans' money with his own and then consumes the whole — so that, as Ibn Kathir puts it, he eats up both. The prohibition is not against keeping the orphan's wealth in the same purse while fully accounting for it; it is against blurring the boundary so that the orphan's share is devoured unnoticed. This is why the classical exegetes stressed scrupulous separation and careful record-keeping for anyone entrusted with another's property.

The verse closes with *kāna ḥūban kabīran* — "this would indeed be a great sin." Ibn Kathir lists the authorities who described the sin as major and substantial: Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Ibn Sirin, Qatadah, Muqatil, Ad-Dahhak, Abu Malik, Zayd bin Aslam, and Abu Sinan. The word *ḥūb* is stronger than ordinary sin; it carries the sense of a heavy, accumulated offence, and its position here is a warning to the guardian who might regard the orphan's funds as a convenient, interest-free resource.

The ruling has broad application. Classical jurists extended the principle beyond literal orphans to anyone holding another person's property in trust — the executor of a will, the administrator of a waqf, the agent of a business partner, the treasurer of a community fund. The underlying test is always the same: is the trustee treating the property as an amanah (trust) to be preserved and delivered, or as a resource to be enjoyed? The verse also teaches the ethics of substitution: replacing something of good quality for the beneficiary while taking the benefit oneself is morally equivalent to theft, whatever paperwork or verbal agreement accompanies it.

There is a spiritual dimension as well. The orphan is mentioned repeatedly in the Qur'an with commands to kindness, and the one who protects the orphan's property in this life is promised what the next verses will develop — the fire for the devourer and a reckoning for everyone. The verse does not merely prohibit theft; it builds a mindset. The believer holding the property of another should remember that Allah sees both the mixing and the motive, and that the day of the hand-over is a day of accountability.

## Verse 3

Verse 3 says: *If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice.*

In simple terms, the verse addresses a man who is the guardian of an orphan girl: if he fears that marrying her would make him fail to give her a fair dowry and her due rights, then he should marry other women instead — up to four. But if he fears he cannot maintain justice among multiple wives, then he should confine himself to one wife, or to bondwomen he possesses. The rule is designed so that injustice becomes less likely.

The verse has a precise occasion of revelation, and understanding it prevents a serious misreading. 'A'ishah, as recorded by Al-Bukhari and cited by Ibn Kathir, explained that the verse was revealed about a man who took care of a female orphan and then married her — not because he desired her, but because he wanted her money — while failing to give her an appropriate dowry. In another narration, 'A'ishah through her nephew 'Urwah bin Az-Zubayr explains: "This is about the orphan girl who lives with her guardian and shares his property. Her wealth and beauty may tempt him to marry her without giving her an adequate dowry... So such guardians were forbidden to marry such orphan girls unless they treated them justly and gave them the most suitable dowry; otherwise they were ordered to marry women besides them." When the people again asked about marrying orphan girls, Allah revealed the fuller supplement in 4:127. Ibn Kathir notes that the phrase "yet whom you desire to marry" refers to the guardian who does not desire the orphan girl because she is neither wealthy nor beautiful — and who therefore might marry her off unfairly or neglect her rights.

The permission to marry "two, three, or four" is the Quranic license for polygyny, and the classical exegetes were careful to state that these are upper limits, not recommendations. Ibn Kathir records the decisive proof from Imam Ahmad: Salim reported from his father that Ghilan bin Salamah Ath-Thaqafi had ten wives when he embraced Islam, and the Prophet told him, "Choose any four of them," divorcing the rest. Ibn Kathir reasons that had more than four been permitted, the Prophet would have allowed Ghilan to keep more than four, since they all embraced Islam with him. The collectors of the Sunan and Al-Bayhaqi recorded the hadith, with variants in how much of the story (including 'Umar's later conversation with Ghilan) was included.

The condition attached to the permission is what most commentators emphasize: *in khiftum allā tuqsitū fī al-yatāmā* — if you fear you cannot do justice with respect to the orphans — then marry others. The condition is then sharpened at the verse's end: if you fear you cannot maintain justice between wives, then one wife only, or what your right hands possess. Ibn Kathir and the majority read the permission as specifying a maximum of four, and note that perfect equality between wives is something Allah has explicitly declared impossible in 4:129 — "You will never be able to do perfect justice between wives even if it is your ardent desire" — so the justice demanded here is the justice that can be delivered: fair marriage contracts, fair dowries, fair maintenance, and fair division of time.

The crucial phrase is *adnā allā ta'ūlū* — translated "less likely to commit injustice." The root *'awla* carries the sense of leaning, deviation, or oppression. Ibn 'Abbas, 'A'ishah, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Al-Hasan, Abu Malik, Abu Razin, An-Nakha'i, Ash-Sha'bi, Ad-Dahhak, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Qatadah, As-Suddi, and Muqatil bin Hayyan all explain *ta'ūlū* here as deviating from justice, as Ibn Kathir records. 'A'ishah's report is also recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Marduwyah, and Ibn Hibban's Sahih, with Ibn Abi Hatim noting that his father regarded the attribution to the Prophet as a mistake — it belongs to 'A'ishah herself. Al-Jalalayn paraphrases the verse in plain terms: "Marry such women as seem good to you, two, three, or four; each man may marry two, three or four but do not exceed this. And if you fear you will not be equitable in expenses and individual share, marry only one or restrict yourself to slavegirls, since these do not have the same rights as wives."

The verse is therefore not an endorsement of multiple marriage as an ideal; it is a regulated answer to a specific social problem. A man who can provide justice to one wife should not take a second; a man who cannot do justice to an orphan girl in his guardianship should not use guardianship as a route to her wealth. In contemporary application, the verse's logic is a floor of responsibility, not a ceiling of permission: polygyny is allowable only where genuine justice — financial, emotional, and legal — can be delivered, and the burden of proof rests on the man who wishes to marry more than once. The verse's closing line, meanwhile, is its lasting ethical standard: the law exists so that injustice becomes less likely, not more convenient.

## Verse 4

Verse 4 says: *Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience.*

In simple terms, the verse commands that every woman a man marries must be given her full dowry as a gracious, unconditional gift — her right, not a fee or a bride-price paid to her family. If she herself, with a willing heart, waives part or all of it, then the husband may take what she waives without any guilt.

The key Arabic word is *nihlah*. Ibn Kathir records the explanations of the early authorities: 'Ali bin Abi Talhah reports from Ibn 'Abbas that *nihlah* means the dowry; Muhammad bin Ishaq reports from Az-Zuhri, from 'Urwah, from 'A'ishah, that it means "obligatory"; Muqatil, Qatadah, and Ibn Jurayj say "obligatory," and Ibn Jurayj also says "specified." Ibn Zayd explains that *nihlah* in Arabic refers to what is necessary and due, and Ibn Kathir draws the conclusion: "Allah is commanding: do not marry unless you give your wife something that is her right. No person after the Prophet is allowed to marry a woman except with the required dowry, nor by giving false promises about the dowry." The word's root carries the sense of a gift given sincerely and fully, and this is why the verse says "graciously" — the dowry is not a price paid to purchase a person, but a gift that honours the woman and gives her financial security within the marriage.

The second half of the verse guards the wife's autonomy: *fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾin minhu nafsan* — "if they themselves, of their own pleasure, remit any part of it to you." Three conditions are implicit and were drawn out by the commentators: the waiver must be voluntary (not coerced or pressured), it must be from the wife herself (not her family or guardian), and it must be made with a contented heart (*ṭibna nafs* — "with a willing soul") rather than under duress, anger, or fear. Ibn Kathir notes that the man "must pay the dowry with a good heart just as he gives a gift," and then, if the wife remits part or all of it voluntarily, "take it, and enjoy it without fear of any harm." The verse's phrase "with a clear conscience" renders that *hanīʾan marīʾan* — enjoyment that is lawful, wholesome, and free of the bitterness that comes from taking what is not yours.

The verse establishes a set of lasting principles. First, the dowry is the wife's own property from the moment of marriage; it does not belong to her father, her brothers, or her husband, and the husbands in the pre-Islamic custom who treated the dowry as a payment to the family were legislated against. Second, the dowry must be real and specified at the time of the contract — a marriage conducted with vague promises of "later" or with no dowry at all is defective under the majority understanding, which is why the classical jurists spoke of a *mahr al-mithl* (the comparable dowry of women of similar standing) where no amount was specified. Third, the gift must be given graciously — the verse's *ʿātiyah* framing (a giving that is complete, not grudging) is a comment on attitude as much as on amount. A husband who gives a large dowry with resentment has missed the verse's spirit; one who gives a modest dowry with sincerity and honour has embodied it.

The verse also connects forward to 4:19–20, which forbid taking anything back from the dowry. The logical sequence within the surah is deliberate: first prescribe the dowry (4:4), then forbid its confiscation (4:19), then forbid reclaiming it on divorce or replacement (4:20). Money given to a wife is hers, and the only way it returns to the husband is through her own willing gift or, in specific legal circumstances, through the recognized procedure of *khul'* — the wife's own redemption of the marriage with a mutual agreement.

In modern life the verse has practical application in every direction: it protects wives in societies where the dowry is collected by the groom's family and spent on the wedding itself, leaving the bride with nothing; it protects women where marriage is treated as a commercial arrangement in which the bride price is a means to secure the match; and it sets a tone of generosity and voluntary giving within marriage — with the wife's choice at the centre. A fair financial foundation is not materialism; in the Quranic ethic it is the precondition for honour, and honour is the first thing a marriage must protect.

## Verse 5

Verse 5 says: *Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly.*

In simple terms, the verse warns against handing one's wealth over to people who are not capable of managing it — children, the mentally incapacitated, women under guardianship, or anyone who is financially irresponsible. The wealth is not theirs to destroy; it is a means of support placed in the hands of the capable guardian, whose duty is to feed and clothe the dependants from it and to speak to them kindly.

The word translated "the incapable" is *al-sufahāʾ*, the plural of *safīh*, which carries a range of meanings in the classical sources. Ibn Kathir notes that Ad-Dahhak and Ibn 'Abbas explain it as referring to children and women; Ibn Mas'ud, Al-Hakam bin 'Uyaynah, Al-Hasan, and Ad-Dahhak say "women and boys"; Sa'id bin Jubayr says the orphans; Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, and Qatadah say women. The second-century jurists extended the term to anyone whose reckless judgment would dissipate capital: a spendthrift adult, a debtor whose property needs to be held in escrow until creditors are settled, or an heir prone to waste. Al-Jalalayn notes the verse applies "because of youth (for young people cannot make wise decisions), insanity, erratic behaviour, weak intellect or religious practice, and bankruptcy when debtors ask that a bankrupt's property be put in escrow."

The phrase "which Allah has made a means of support for you" is the heart of the verse's theology. The Arabic *qiyāman* (or *qiwāman*) means a support, a stand, an established means of life. Ibn Kathir reports 'Ali bin Abi Talhah from Ibn 'Abbas: do not give your wealth, which Allah made your responsibility and means of sustenance, to your wife or children; hold on to it and be the one who spends on them for clothes, food, and provision. The wealth is thus described as a *qiwām* — the pillar that holds a household and a society upright. This reframes property ownership entirely: the guardian is not a possessor for his own pleasure but a steward for the community's survival, and the command is that the pillar should remain in the hands that can support the structure.

The verse then gives the duties of the steward. First, feed and clothe the dependants from the wealth: the guardian who holds a minor's money must actually spend it on the minor's maintenance — food, clothing, medical care, education — and this spending is itself part of the preservation the command demands. Second, "speak to them kindly": the Arabic *qawlan maʿrūfan* — a statement recognized as good, fitting, and honourable. Mujahid renders it "speak to them words of kindness and justice." This is not a small instruction. The Qur'an repeatedly joins the care of vulnerable dependants with gracious speech, and the pairing here is exact: the one who controls the purse must also control the tongue. Financial provision without dignity is not the full command; a child or a widow who is fed but despised, or lectured and humiliated, is not being treated as the verse requires. The dependants' own dignity is part of the wealth's purpose.

There is also a protective logic in the verse that modern guardians should grasp. The incapacity being addressed is not primarily age but judgment: the test is whether the dependant can manage capital without destroying it. This is why the next verse commands testing the orphans "until they reach a marriageable age" and returning the wealth upon evidence of sound judgment — the incapacity is a stage, not a status. The guardian's task is therefore developmental, not possessive: he is to feed, clothe, speak kindly, and prepare the dependant for the day of hand-over, and the verse's prohibition protects the dependant from a guardian who would either exploit the wealth or surrender it carelessly.

Applied to the modern world, the verse speaks to financial trusteeship in every form: the executor of a will for a minor beneficiary, the guardian of a person with intellectual disability, the administrator of a trust fund, the treasurers of community and family resources. In each case the rule is the same — the person who cannot manage capital must not control it, and the person who controls it must use it for the dependant's benefit with kindness. The verse also corrects a common domestic error: the withdrawal of control from dependants who are competent, or the granting of control to dependants who are not. The Quranic middle path is competence-based stewardship: wealth stays with those who can keep it a support, and it is spent on those it supports — with respect, provision, and honour.
