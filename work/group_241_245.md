## Verse 241

Verse 241 says: *"Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the provision for the divorced women: the reasonable gift at the divorce — the duty on the mindful, the godfearing.

"Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women" — the verse's *mut'ah* — the provision for the divorced women. Ibn Kathir: the divorced woman's gift — the gift at divorce — "a provision according to what is acceptable" — the gift that is fitting, that is known, that is good — the duty on the godfearing, the mindful. The verse is linked with 2:236 (the mut'ah for the divorced who had no fixed dowry) and with the final clause of 2:240 (the whole year's bequest; the rest of the year — seven months and twenty days — is the widow's additional provision). Ibn Kathir: the scholars who ruled that the mut'ah at divorce is required for every divorced woman — whether a dowry was appointed or not, whether the marriage was consummated or not — relied on this ayah. Al-Jalalayn: "an obligation" (haqqan) — accusative, because it is governed by an implied verb — on those who fear Allah.

The verse's measure: the provision "according to what is acceptable" — the reasonable scale — not the extravagance, not the stinginess. The verse's *al-mut'ah* — the gift — the farewell. Ibn Kathir (on the occasion): a man divorced his wife and did not give her a mut'ah, and said, "If I want, I will be excellent and if I do not, I will not." Thereafter Allah revealed this ayah. The verse's frame: the divorce is not the end of the kindness; the divorce itself carries the provision.

"A duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺" — the verse's close: the duty on the muttaqin — the godfearing — the mindful. The gift is not the favor of the generous; the gift is the obligation of the mindful. Ibn Kathir: the mut'ah — the reasonable gift — this is a duty on the pious. The verse's frame: the mindful — those who guard against the injustice; the one who fears Allah does not let the divorce be the woman's loss.

The verse's place: after the widow's verse (2:240) and beside the divorced woman's laws (2:241) — the family law is anchored in the mindfulness of Allah. The verse's *al-mut'ah* — the provision — the known good — the measure of the divorce's decency. And the reader's part: the divorce is not the end of the woman's dignity — the provision is the farewell kindness, and the farewell kindness is the duty of the godfearing.

## Verse 242

Verse 242 says: *"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will understand."*

In simple terms, the verse closes the block of rulings: this is how Allah clarifies His revelations — so that you may understand.

"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you" — the verse's framing: the clarification. Ibn Kathir: this ayah refers to what Allah has explained of the rulings of the divorce and the provision — the laws of the family, the limits of the divorce, the gift — Allah made them clear, made them plain. The verse's function: the rulings of the previous verses (the divorce, the iddah, the mut'ah, the widow's bequest) are not the open text only — they are the clarified, the explained, the made-distinct.

"So perhaps you will understand" — the verse's purpose: the understanding. The clear statements lead to the comprehension; the comprehension is the Qur'an's aim. Al-Jalalayn: "perhaps you will understand" — by means of these details of the religion and the rulings. The verse's frame: the verses are not recited for the ritual only — they are revealed for the understanding, the grasping, the application.

The verse's note: the sequence — after the many rulings of verses 221–241 (the marriage laws, the divorce, the maintenance, the gift) — the Qur'an says "this is how" — the reminder — the laws are not arbitrary; the laws are the clear signs of the Wise. Ibn Kathir: the ayah — the explanation of the rulings, the elaboration — "so that you may understand."

The verse's close: the rhyme of the benefit — the clarity is the mercy; the understanding is the fruit. And the reader's part: the Qur'an's laws are to be understood, not merely watched.

## Verse 243

Verse 243 says: *"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death? Allah said to them, "Die!" then He gave them life. Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful."*

In simple terms, the verse tells of those who fled their homes in their thousands, fearing death — and Allah said "Die!", then revived them — the lesson that no escape can avert destiny, and that Allah's bounty is met by the ingratitude.

"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death" — the verse's story. Ibn Kathir (from Waki' ibn Jarrah, from Ibn 'Abbas): they were four thousand persons who escaped the plague that broke out in their land — they said, "We should go to a land free of death!" When they reached a certain area, Allah said to them, "Die!" — and they all died. Afterwards, one of the Prophets passed by them, supplicated to Allah, and Allah brought them back to life. Several scholars of the Salaf: these people were the residents of a city in the time of the Children of Israel — an epidemic broke out; they fled to the wilderness; they came to a fertile valley and filled it; Allah sent two angels, one from the lower side, one from the upper — the angels screamed once and all the people died instantly, as the death of one man. Al-Jalalayn: the numbers varied — four thousand, eight, ten, thirty, forty, seventy thousand — a people from among the Children of Israel who fled their homeland after it was afflicted with plague — Allah said "Die!" and they did.

The verse's lesson: the flight from the death does not avert the death. Ibn Kathir: this story indicates that no caution can avert destiny — there is no refuge from Allah but to Allah Himself — these people departed fleeing the epidemic, seeking long life, and earned the opposite of what they sought — death came quickly and instantaneously and seized them all. Maududi: the story's lesson — the fear of death, the attempt to escape, and the divine decree.

"Allah said to them, "Die!" then He gave them life" — the verse's power: the Word — "Die!" — and they died; and the will — the life — and they lived. The verse's note: the resurrection of this community is the sign of the resurrection of all — the One who said "Die!" to the thousands is the One who will call all from the graves.

"Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful" — the verse's close. Ibn Kathir: "Allah is Bountiful" — in what He shows them of the manifest signs, the decisive proofs, the conclusive evidences; "but most people do not show gratitude" — they do not give thanks for what Allah has bestowed, in their religion and their worldly affairs. The verse's frame: the bounty of the revival — the mercy of the second life — and the ingratitude of the majority.

The verse's place: after the family law — the story of the fleeing thousands — the medicine of the heart: the fear of death, the trust in Allah, the gratitude. And the reader's part: the refuge from Allah is only to Allah; the death will come where you are; and the bounty of the life after the death deserves the thanks.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 244

Verse 244 says: *"Fight in the cause of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse commands the fighting in the cause of Allah — the striving against the enemies of the faith — with the knowledge that Allah hears and knows all.

"Fight in the cause of Allah" — the verse's command: the jihad in Allah's path. Ibn Kathir (with 2:243): the paragraph of the story of the dead people (2:243) indicates that no caution can avert destiny — and this ayah follows with the command to fight — the believer is not to flee the death; the believer is to advance. The verse's context: after the story of those who fled and died, the command to fight in the cause — the reverse of the flight: the advance. Maududi: the command of the fight — the jihad — after the story of the fleeing people — the reminder that the death is not avoided by the flight, and the cause of Allah is served by the advance.

"and know that Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — the verse's close: the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. Ibn Kathir: the All-Hearing of the sayings, the All-Knowing of the deeds — the One who hears the call for help, who knows the intention, who weighs the striving. The verse's frame: the fighting in the cause is the service of the One who hears and knows — no effort is lost, no intention hidden.

The verse's note: the command follows the lesson of the dead — the believer fights because the decree is in Allah's hand, and the fighting itself is the obedience. The verse's *qital* — the fighting — the cause of Allah — the aim: the Word of Allah, the defense of the oppressed, the repelling of corruption (cf. 2:251: had Allah not repelled a group by another, corruption would have dominated the earth).

The verse's frame: the fear of death is cured by the fight in the cause — the death is appointed, and the cause is the honor. And the reader's part: the fighting in the cause of Allah is the trusting stride — the hearing and the knowing are the watch over the struggle.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 245

Verse 245 says: *"Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned."*

In simple terms, the verse invites the good loan to Allah — the spending in His cause — which He multiplies many times; it is Allah who tightens and expands the provision; and to Him the return.

"Who will lend to Allah a good loan" — the verse's *qard hasan* — the beautiful loan. Ibn Kathir: "Who is he that will lend to Allah a goodly loan?" — the loan to the One who is neither poor nor unjust — the metaphor: the spending in the cause of Allah. The good loan: the spending with the search of the countenance of Allah, from the halal wealth, without the mention and without the harm. The verse's frame: the loan to Allah — the Owner is the Borrower — the mercy of the metaphor: the spender's hand is the lender's hand, and Allah's hand is the multiplyer.

"which Allah will multiply many times over" — the verse's reward. Ibn Kathir: it is like His statement (2:261): the likeness of those who spend in the cause of Allah is the grain that grew seven ears — in every ear a hundred grains — and Allah gives manifold increase to whom He wills. The multiplication: many times over — beyond the count — the doubling of the reward in the Hereafter.

"It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺" — the verse's means. Ibn Kathir: "And it is Allah that decreases or increases (your provisions)" — spend in Allah's cause and do not be anxious — Allah is the Sustainer, Who increases or decreases the provisions to whomever He wills among His servants — and His wisdom is perfect. The verse's frame: the hand that gives is the hand that receives; the provision is Allah's — the tightening and the expansion — the believer is not to fear the spending.

"And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned" — the verse's close: the return to Allah — on the Day of Resurrection. Ibn Kathir: "and unto Him you shall return" — on the Day of Resurrection — the return of the deeds and the reward. The verse's frame: the loan is made to the One to Whom the return is made — the spender and the wealth meet the Owner at the return.

The verse's place: after the command to fight (2:244) — the spending for the cause — the wealth and the life are both the trust. And the reader's part: the wealth is Allah's — the lending is the trusting — and the return is to Him.
