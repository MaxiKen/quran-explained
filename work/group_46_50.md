## Verse 46

Verse 46 says: *And he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy and adulthood and will be one of the righteous.”*

In simple terms, the verse continues the description of the Messiah: he will speak to people in the cradle and in adulthood, and he will be among the righteous.

The verse is the second half of the angelic good news of 3:45. Ibn Kathir's section on the verse is titled "Isa Spoke When He Was Still in the Cradle." The verse makes two claims: the speech in infancy and the speech in adulthood, and both are miracles of a kind — the first is the miracle of the cradle, the second is the ordinary course of prophethood.

"he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy" — *yukallimu al-nas fi al-mahd* — he will speak to people in the cradle. Ibn Kathir: "calling to the worship of Allah Alone without partners, while still in the cradle, as a miracle from Allah." The speech of the infant is a divine sign: the one who cannot yet speak is made to speak, and the first speech is the declaration of the worship of Allah alone — the same declaration the whole surah is preaching. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes place the cradle speech at the moment of the accusation against Maryam: when she returned with the child and the people accused her, the infant 'Isa spoke in defense of his mother and in declaration of his prophethood (the fuller narrative of 19:27-33). Ibn Kathir records the hadiths of the Prophet: "No infant spoke in the cradle except 'Isa and the companion of Jurayj" (Muhammad bin Ishaq from Abu Hurayrah), and "No infant spoke in the cradle except three: 'Isa, the boy during the time of Jurayj, and another boy" (Ibn Abi Hatim). The cradle speech is unique among the ordinary signs: it demonstrates the power of Allah over the earliest stage of the human life, and it vindicates the honor of the mother.

"and adulthood" — *wa kahlan* — and in manhood, in full adulthood. Al-Jalalayn and Ibn Kathir treat the second the same way: the speech in manhood is "by Allah's revelation to him" (Ibn Kathir) — the prophecy of the adult, the message of the messenger. The verse's pairing is deliberate: the same mouth that spoke in the cradle speaks in manhood; the miracle of the beginning and the mission of the maturity are the same prophecy. The word *kahl* — full adulthood, the prime of life — also has a christological force in the Qur'an: it is the age of the prophet's mission and the age to which the narratives of 3:49-51, 5:110, 61:6 belong.

"and will be one of the righteous" — *min al-salihin* — among the righteous. Ibn Kathir: "in his statements and actions, for he will possess pure knowledge and righteous works." The phrase is the same formula used for Zachariah's request of a son "from among the righteous" (3:46's parallel in the good news of Yahya, 3:39) and for the description of the good offspring (3:38: *dhurriyyah tayyibah*). The Messiah is the completion of the household's prayer: the good offspring asked for by the family is the righteous one whose speech begins in the cradle.

For the reader, the verse completes the portrait of the Messiah that the surah is painting: the Word from Him (3:45), the honor in both worlds (3:45), the nearness (3:45), the speech in the cradle (3:46), the speech in manhood (3:46), and the righteousness (3:46). Every element is high — and every element is the description of a servant. The cradle speech and the adult prophecy are the same voice, and the voice is the voice of the one who says, in the same passage (3:51), "Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him." The verse's portrait is the Qur'an's answer to the Christian inflation of the Messiah's station: the higher the description, the clearer the boundary — the one who is described is described as the servant who was born, spoke, and was righteous, not as the Lord who is eternal.

The verse also contains the Qur'an's deepest picture of the unity of the prophetic mission: the man speaks in the cradle to declare the worship of Allah alone, and the man speaks in adulthood to declare the same worship. There is no infancy-of-the-religion and maturity-of-the-religion; the message of the cradle is the message of the manhood. The first word of the Messiah and the last word of the Messiah are the same word: the Oneness of Allah, the worship of Him alone, the straight path.

## Verse 47

Verse 47 says: *Mary wondered, “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” An angel replied, “So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!*

In simple terms, the verse records Mary's question and the divine answer: how can I have a child when no man has touched me? The answer: so will it be — Allah creates what He wills; when He decrees a matter, He only says "Be," and it is.

"My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?" — Mary's question is the question of the whole surah's theology, and it is the question of the human reason confronted with the divine command. Ibn Kathir expands her meaning: "Mary said, 'How can I have a son while I did not marry, nor intend to marry, nor am I an indecent woman, may Allah forbid?'" The question is not a doubt of the message; it is the faithful servant's statement of the natural impossibilities: no marriage, no intention of marriage, no illicit contact — and yet the child is announced. The same structure appeared in Zachariah's question of 3:40, and the two questions are the surah's mirror: the old man and the barren wife; the virgin and no man — both are the "how" of the natural order facing the decree of the Lord.

"An angel replied, "So will it be."" — *kadhalik* — thus it will be, so it is. The answer to Mary is the exact answer given to Zachariah: the same formula. The classical exegetes note the symmetry: the one who answered "How can I have a son?" with "Thus Allah does what He wills" (3:40) is the same One who answers "How can I have a child?" with "Thus it is, Allah creates what He wills." The parallel is the surah's argumentative structure: if the old man is granted a child by the decree, the virgin is granted a child by the same decree — and the second is the greater of the two, because the second is the greater suspension of the natural order.

"Allah creates what He wills" — *Allah yakhluqu ma yasha'* — Allah creates what He wills. Ibn Kathir: "He is Mighty in power and nothing escapes His ability." The sentence is the doctrine in one line: creation is the effect of the will, not of the means; and the will is not constrained by the patterns it has established. Al-Jalalayn's reading: "the One with power to bring about something in other than its natural time is able to bring about a child in old age and with those of his family line all deceased" — and by the same power a child without a father.

"When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, 'Be!' And it is!" — *idha qada amran fa innama yaqulu lahu kun fa yakun* — when He decrees a matter, He only says to it, "Be," and it is. The phrase is the Qur'an's most concise expression of the divine creative power: *kun fa yakun* — Be! and it is. The command is not the work of tools, processes, or materials; the decree and the existence are one. For Mary, the child comes into being by the same command by which the heavens and the earth came into being: the command "Be" is sufficient. The Quran's usage of *kun fa-yakun* in the context of 'Isa (and Adam, 3:59) is deliberate: the birth of the Messiah and the creation of Adam are the two explicit cases where the ordinary means of human generation are suspended, and the command "Be" stands as the surah's argument: to create without a father is no harder for Allah than to create without a father and a mother, and to create without any means is no harder for Allah than to create with means.

For the reader, the verse resolves the birth of 'Isa without reducing it. The miracle is real: the virgin birth is the suspension of the natural order and the sign of the divine power. And the doctrine is intact: the virgin birth is not the evidence of the divinity of the child but the demonstration of the power of the Creator. The one who asks the "how" is being answered by the "He says Be, and it is" — the answer to Zachariah and the answer to Mary and the answer to the Christian's argument about 'Isa and to the modern reader's question about miracles: the means are not the boundary of the decree.

## Verse 48

Verse 48 says: *“And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel,*

In simple terms, the verse continues the good news: Allah will teach the child writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel.

The verse is the third element of the angelic announcement to Mary (the Word from Him, 3:45; the speech in the cradle and manhood, 3:46; the answer to the "how," 3:47; and now the teaching, 3:48). Ibn Kathir frames it as part of the good news: "the good news brought to Maryam about 'Isa was even better because Allah would teach him the Book and Al-Hikmah." The teaching is the equipment of the messenger: the one who will carry the message to the Children of Israel is taught the message's instruments.

"writing" — *al-kitab* — the Book, the writing, the Scripture. Ibn Kathir: "It appears that the 'Book' the Ayah mentions" — and the verse's ambiguity is resolved by the context: the *kitab* here is the writing of the Book, the literacy of the revelation, and the verses that follow make it precise. The "Book" is one of the two things the Qur'an says Allah will teach him: *al-kitab wa al-hikmah* — the Book and the wisdom — a formula used in the Qur'an for the divine teaching of the prophets (2:129, 2:151, 62:2: "He teaches them the Book and the Wisdom"). For 'Isa, the Book is the Scripture given to him — the Injil — and the Book that preceded him — the Torah — both named in the next phrase.

"and wisdom" — *al-hikmah* — the wisdom, the judgment, the insight of the prophetic understanding: the application of the Book to the life, the discernment of the truth, the ability to judge and to instruct.

"the Torah and the Gospel" — *al-Tawrah wa al-Injil* — the Torah and the Gospel. Ibn Kathir and the classical readings take the phrase as the two books of the revelation: the Torah that was revealed before him (which he confirms at 3:50) and the Gospel (the Injil) that is revealed to him. The verse's ordering is significant: the one who is coming as a messenger to the Children of Israel is taught the scripture of the people he is sent to — the Torah — and the scripture he will bring — the Gospel — so that his teaching would be continuous: the confirmers of the previous and the bearers of the new.

There is also a christological point in the verse's order, which the classical exegetes treat: the Messiah is taught the Torah and the Gospel. He is not the source of them but the receiver of them; the one who is taught is not the teacher of the teaching's source. The "Word from Him" (3:45) is a created one who is taught the Book, the wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel. The teaching is the divine preparation of the servant for the mission: the child who will speak in the cradle is the adult who will confirm the Torah and bring the Gospel.

For the reader, the verse completes the portrait of the Messiah's authority: the one whose name is high, whose speech begins in the cradle, whose birth is by the command "Be" — is the one who is taught. The teacher is Allah; the taught is 'Isa; the materials are the Book, the wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel. The verse is also the model of the believer's learning: the instruments of the message are learned before the message is carried; the one who speaks the Word is first taught the Word. The surah's own process — the revelation to Muhammad of the unseen reports (3:44), the teaching of the Book and the wisdom (in the Prophet's own case at 62:2) — is the same process: the messenger is the taught one, and the teaching is from the Lord.

## Verse 49

Verse 49 says: *and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life— by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe.*

In simple terms, the verse names the mission and the miracles of the Messiah: he is a messenger to the Children of Israel with signs from his Lord — the bird from clay that becomes a bird by Allah's permission, the healing of the blind and the leper, the raising of the dead by Allah's permission, and the knowledge of what people eat and store in their houses — a sign for those who believe.

"a messenger to the Children of Israel" — the verse states the mission's audience: *rasulan ila Bani Isra'il* — a messenger to the Children of Israel. Al-Jalalayn: "He will make him a messenger to the Children of Israel during his tender years or after puberty." The mission is to the people of the Torah: the one who was taught the Torah (3:48) is sent to the people of the Torah; the confirmation of 3:50 is the mission's announcement. Ibn Kathir frames the section "The Description of 'Isa and the Miracles He Performed": "Allah states that the good news brought to Maryam about 'Isa was even better because Allah would teach him the Book and Al-Hikmah."

"I have come to you with a sign from your Lord" — the miracles are introduced as *ayah* — a sign — from the Lord: the proof of the messenger's truthfulness. The sign is not the point of the mission; the sign is the credential of the mission. Al-Jalalayn: "a sign, an indication of my truthfulness from your Lord."

"I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah's Will" — the first miracle is the creation of the bird from clay: *akhluqu lakum min al-tin ka hay'at al-tayr* — I create for you from clay something like the form of a bird; *fa anfukhu fihi* — then I breathe into it; *fa yakun tayran* — and it becomes a bird — *bi idhn Allah* — by Allah's permission, by Allah's leave. Every act of the miracle is qualified: the creation is by His permission, the breath is the human breath, and the becoming is the divine command. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn note the reading "a bat" — the classical tradition says he created a bat, being the most perfectly-created of birds — and the point of the tradition: "they would watch it flying, but when it went out of sight it would fall dead — so that the work of a creature (Jesus) may be distinguished from the work of the Creator (God), and that he might know that perfection belongs to God alone" (Al-Jalalayn). The miracle is the demonstration of the power of Allah through the hands of the servant: the power is Allah's; the servant is the instrument; and the miracle's own structure betrays the boundary — the creature's creation is by permission, and the creature's life is by Allah's will.

"I will heal the blind and the leper" — the second miracle pair: *al-akmah* — the blind, the one blind from birth — and *al-abras* — the leper. Al-Jalalayn: "these two are singled out for mention because with both afflictions the person is completely helpless." The healing of the incurable diseases is the sign that the healer is from the Lord: the physicians of the age — "he was sent in an age characterized by medicinal science" — could not cure them; the messenger of Allah could, by Allah's leave. The tradition (recorded by Al-Jalalayn) adds that he cured through supplication fifty thousand in one day on the condition that each person would become a believer.

"and raise the dead to life— by Allah's Will" — the third miracle: the dead are raised — *uqihi al-mawta* — I give life to the dead — *bi idhn Allah*. The raising of the dead is the sign of the resurrection, which the whole surah has been preaching: the One who raises the dead in the Hereafter is the One who raises the dead through the hands of the Messiah in this life. The classical narratives (Al-Jalalayn) add the specific cases — his friend 'Azar, the son of an old woman, the daughter of a tithe-collector — each raised, living, producing offspring; and Shem, son of Noah, who died again immediately. The traditions preserve the detail; the Qur'an preserves the structure: by Allah's permission, the dead are raised, and the raising is the sign.

"And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses" — the fourth sign is the knowledge of the unseen in the ordinary: *unabbi'ukum bima ta'kuluna wa ma taddakhiruna* — I tell you what you eat and what you store. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn: the prophet would tell people what they had eaten and what they would eat, what they had stored and what they would store — knowledge of the unseen at the level of the household and the pantry. The sign is placed last and it is the smallest; the classical commentary notes the pattern: the miracles move from the nature of the clay and the breath, to the incurable diseases, to the dead, to the ordinary domestic unseen — the whole range of the human life: creation, illness, death, and daily provision. The miracles are the evidentiary ladder and each one is "by Allah's permission."

"Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe" — the verse closes with the condition: *in kunta mu'minin* — if you are believers, if you truly believe. The sign is the proof; the belief is the response; and the sign is enough. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes: "This should be enough divine evidence to bring you to acknowledge the truth of my mission if indeed your hearts have been impressed with the image of religious and spiritual virtues."

For the reader, the verse is the surah's catalogue of the Messiah's signs: the bird, the blind, the leper, the dead, the unseen of the houses — every one of the five is introduced by the messenger and performed by the permission of the Lord. The verse answers the Christian claim of the Messiah's divinity with the Messiah's own words: "I have come with a sign from your Lord"; "by Allah's Will"; "by Allah's Will" — the creed of the servant is written into the miracle. And the verse answers the modern reader's question about the miracles of the prophets: they are not the suspension of the divine law but the direct demonstration of the divine power, and they are the credential of the messenger, not the property of the messenger. The signs were given not to prove that 'Isa was divine but to prove that he was sent — and the sign's own clause "by Allah's permission" is the boundary that the whole passage maintains.

## Verse 50

Verse 50 says: *And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me.*

In simple terms, the verse states the mission's relation to the previous scripture: Jesus confirms the Torah that came before him and makes lawful some of what was forbidden to the Children of Israel; he comes with a sign from the Lord; so fear Allah and obey him.

"And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me" — *musaddiqan lima bayna yadayya min al-Tawrah* — confirming what was before me of the Torah. The same confirming relationship that the Qur'an has to the books before it (3:3) is the relationship of the Gospel to the Torah: the confirmation is not the replacement of the previous by the later but the attestation of the previous by the later. Ibn Kathir notes at 3:49 the confirmation is the mission's announcement: the one who was taught the Torah (3:48) confirms the Torah — the confirmers of the previous message and the messenger of the new are continuous. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings place the confirmation in the context of the Messiah's message: he testifies that the Torah is the word of Allah, that the covenant of the prophets continues, and that the previous scripture had announced him.

"and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you" — *li uhillu lakum ba'd alladhi hurrima 'alaykum* — to make lawful for you some of what had been forbidden to you. The verse's phrase is one of the distinctive features of the religion of 'Isa in the Qur'an: the Messiah's law relaxed some of the strictures of the previous dispensation. Ibn Kathir and the classical notes see it as the relief of the burden of the Torah's law for the Children of Israel: the Gospels themselves record the Messiah's loosening of the strict sabbath and food laws, and the Qur'an states the principle — the new messenger is the relief of the old law's burden. The verse's "some" is precise: not the whole law, not the Tawhid or the fundamentals, but the particular strictures that were imposed as a test on the previous people; the same pattern appears in 4:160-161, where the Qur'an says the strictures were a punishment for the wrong-doing of the Children of Israel, and 2:185, where the Qur'an itself says the easier is intended.

"I have come to you with a sign from your Lord" — the mission's credential is repeated from 3:49: *bi ayatin min Rabbikum* — with a sign from your Lord. The signs of 3:49 and the credential of 3:50 are the same thing: the messenger proves his mission by the miracle, and the miracle is from the Lord. The pattern of the Qur'an's messengers: the sign — the proof — the mission; and the response required — belief and obedience.

"so be mindful of Allah and obey me" — *fattaqu Allah wa ati'un* — so fear Allah, be mindful of Allah, and obey me. The two commands are the content of the response: the taqwa of the One who sent the sign and the obedience of the one who bears it. Al-Jalalayn and the classical reading: the obedience of the messenger is the form of the worship of the Lord: the fear of Allah and the obedience to the messenger are one response — as the surah states at 3:31–32 (follow me, obey Allah and the Messenger). The Messiah's own command is the same command as the final Messenger's: the one who fears Allah obeys the messenger whom Allah sent.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's clearest statement of the continuity between the religion of 'Isa and the religion of Muhammad: the same confirmation of the previous Scripture, the same invitation, the same command — fear Allah and obey the messenger. The verse also shows the principle of the law's adaptation: the morality is permanent, the law is erasable, the burden can be lifted. And the verse's closing command is the surah's call to the Christians of Najran in the era of the revelation: the Messiah himself said fear Allah and obey me; the Qur'an is saying: the one you cannot obey now is the one whose command you must still obey — and the command of the Messiah is the command of the final messenger, the same worship of the One, the same obedience of the sent one.
