## Verse 56

Verse 56 says: *“As for those who disbelieve, I will subject them to a severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the verdict on the disbelievers in the Messiah's story: they will be punished severely in this life and in the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers.

The verse is the first of the two verdicts that follow the divine declaration of 3:55 (the disbelievers and the believers). Ibn Kathir's framing: the verse is the completion of the promise to 'Isa — "As to those who disbelieve, I will punish them with severe torment in this world and in the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers."

"those who disbelieve" — in the context of the verse, the disbelievers are those who rejected the Messiah: the Jews who plotted against him (3:54), those who accused his mother, those who denied his prophethood. Al-Jalalayn's reading: "those who disbelieved in you (namely the Jews)." The disbelief is the disbelief in the messenger of Allah — the same disbelief the surah has been treating throughout: the denial of the signs (3:21), the killing of the prophets (3:21), the refusal to submit to the Book (3:23), the claim of the "few days" (3:24), and the plot against the Messiah (3:54).

"severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter" — *'adhaban shadidan fi al-dunya wa al-akhirah* — a severe torment in this world and in the Hereafter. The double punishment is the Qur'an's standard verdict on the persistent deniers: the world's punishment — the humiliation, the defeat, the consequences of the rejection in history (as at 3:12: "Soon you will be overpowered"; as the Jews' punishment at 3:181; as the defeat of Bani Qaynuqa' in the Prophet's era) — and the Hereafter's punishment — the severe torment of the account. The verse's severity is the severity of the persistent rejection of the clear signs: the Messiah's miracles (3:49) were the clearest of the signs, and the plot was the response; the verdict is the double punishment.

"and they will have no helpers" — *wa ma lahum min nasirin* — and they will have no helpers. The phrase repeats the verdict of 3:22 (the helpers of the killers of the prophets was "no helpers") and answers the question of the plot's support: the plotters had the king, the state, the mob, the power of the era — and the helpers are nothing. The only helper that matters is the Lord, and the Lord is the One they plotted against; the helpers of the disbelievers are the helpers they cannot keep — the same helpers that the surah already declared the world's wealth and children (3:10) cannot provide.

For the reader, the verse is the doctrine of the outcome of the rejection of the Messiah: the rejection is not a private opinion; it is the rejection of the clear sign of the Lord, and its outcome is the double punishment and the absence of the helpers. The verse also answers the apparent victory of the plotters: they won the moment — the arrest, the execution of the substitute, the silencing of the messenger — and they lost the outcome: the severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter, and the helpers that were none. The disbelievers' power in the short term is the reverse of their standing in the long term; the verse's order is the order of the judgment: the disbelief first, the punishment second, the no-helpers third. And the believer who faces the plotters of his own era reads the verse as the guarantee: the plot may have the moment; the verdict belongs to the Judge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 57

Verse 57 says: *And as for those who believe and do good, they will be rewarded in full. And Allah does not like the wrongdoers.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the second verdict of the Messiah's story: those who believe and do good will be given their full reward — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers.

"And as for those who believe and do good" — *alladhina amanu wa 'amilu al-salihat* — those who believe and do righteous deeds. The formula is the Qur'an's standard pairing: the belief and the deeds — the inward faith and the outward work — and the reward belongs to the pair, never to the faith alone nor the deeds alone. In the context of the Messiah's story, the believers and the doers of good are the followers of the Messiah who believed in his prophethood and followed him (3:52-53), the disciples, and the faithful of every generation who accept the message of the Messiah's genuine teaching — the worship of the One and the straight path.

"they will be rewarded in full" — *fa yu waffihim ujurahм* — He will give them their rewards in full. Ibn Kathir: "Allah will pay them their reward in full." The full reward is the reward of the complete account: the one who believes and does good is paid in full — the same fullness of the account that 3:25 declared for the gathering (every soul paid in full) and that 3:185 declares at the end of the surah ("you will be given your full rewards on the Day of Judgment"). The reward of the believers is not the reward of the worldly equivalent; it is the full reward of the Lord.

"And Allah does not like the wrongdoers" — *wa Allah la yuhibb al-zalimin* — and Allah does not like the wrongdoers. The negative of the love of the believers: the verse's closing clause is the boundary — the believers and the doers of good are the ones Allah loves; the wrongdoers are the ones He does not. The *zalimin* — the wrongdoers, the unjust — are the ones who wrong themselves and others: the plotters of 3:54, the deniers of the signs, the killers of the prophets; and the *zulm* — the wrong — is the wrong that the whole surah has been exposing: the rejection of the truth, the murder of the just, the fabrication of the religion, the claim of the "few days," the plot against the messenger. The verse's pairing is the Qur'an's justice: the full reward for the believers, the not-liking for the wrongdoers.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's answer to the question of the ultimate standing of the two parties of the Messiah's history: the believers and the doers of good are paid in full; the wrongdoers are not liked by the Lord. The verse also contains the Qur'an's doctrine of the reward-justice: the reward is the full reward, and the not-liking is the not-liking of the wrong. The believer's comfort: the reward of the believers is not the partial reward of the world but the full reward of the Lord. The wrongdoer's warning: the "not liking" is the standing of the one who is wrong in the sight of the Lord. And the verse's placement at the end of the Messiah's story is the surah's answer to the Christians' claim of the specialness of the believers of the Messiah: the specialness is the belief and the deeds — the faith of the Messiah's teaching and the works of his way — and the verdict is the same verdict that the whole surah has been spelling: the full reward for the believers and the doers of good, the not-liking for the wrongdoers.

## Verse 58

Verse 58 says: *We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺ as one of the signs and ˹as˺ a wise reminder.*

In simple terms, the verse steps back from the story and points to its purpose: We recite this to you, O Prophet, as one of the signs and as a wise reminder — the account is a sign of the revelation and a reminder of the wisdom.

"We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺" — *dhalika natluhu 'alayka* — this We recite to you. The verse's "this" gathers the whole passage — the story of Zachariah, Mary, and the Messiah, the miracles, the plot, the verdicts — and the verb *natlu* — We recite, We rehearse — is the Qur'an's verb for the recitation of the revelation: the account is the revealed recitation, the *qur'an* of the story. The verse is the surah's self-reference: the story has been told, and the telling is the recitation of Allah.

"as one of the signs" — *min al-ayat* — from the signs, from the verses, from the signs. The word *ayat* is the word for the verses of the Qur'an and the signs of Allah: the story of the Messiah is one of the signs — the sign of the power of Allah (the virgin birth, the miracles, the raising), the sign of the truthfulness of the messenger (the unseen reports of 3:44), and the sign of the revelation itself (the recitation of the unseen by the unlettered Prophet). The story is the sign: the account of the Messiah is the evidence of the One who recounts it.

"and ˹as˺ a wise reminder" — *wa al-dhikr al-hakim* — and the wise reminder, the reminder of the wisdom. The *dhikr* — the reminder — is the Qur'an's own name for the revelation (15:9: "We have sent down the Reminder, and We will surely preserve it"; 38:1: "the Qur'an, the Reminder"), and the *hakim* — the wise — is the reminder's quality: the reminder that is not merely the report of the past but the wisdom of the present. Ibn Kathir and the classical readings treat the verse as the transition from the narrative to the argument: the story is recited as a sign and a reminder — the sign of the truth and the reminder of the wisdom — and the next verse (3:59) draws the argument: the example of 'Isa is like the example of Adam.

There is also a perspective in the verse's double description. The story is a *sign* — the evidence of the divine power and the prophecy of the unseen — and a *reminder* — the wisdom for the present dispute. The sign answers the question of the *kufr* (disbelief): the reports of the unseen are the proof of the revelation. The reminder answers the question of the *huda* (guidance): the story is the instruction of the present dispute with the Christians of Najran. The same event is both: the sign of the truth and the reminder of the way.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's framing of the whole passage: the story of the Messiah is not a biographical digression but a sign and a reminder. The sign: the power of Allah demonstrated in the creation of Adam, the birth of 'Isa, and the recitation of the unseen. The reminder: the wisdom for the present — the same dispute of the Christians, the same doctrine of the Tawhid, the same test of the followers. The believer reading the surah is reading not the history of the ancient but the sign and the reminder of the present: the sign of the revelation and the reminder of the way.

## Verse 59

Verse 59 says: *Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, “Be!” And he was!*

In simple terms, the verse delivers the argument: the case of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like the case of Adam — He created him from dust, then said "Be," and he was. The same command that created Adam without father and mother created Jesus without a father.

The verse is the surah's decisive argument against the claim of the divinity of 'Isa. Ibn Kathir's section: "The Similarities Between the Creation of Adam and the Creation of 'Isa" — "Allah said: Verily, the likeness of 'Isa before Allah — regarding Allah's ability, since He created him without a father — is the likeness of Adam, for Allah created Adam without a father or a mother. Rather: He created him from dust, then said to him 'Be!' and he was. Therefore, He Who created Adam without a father or a mother is able to create 'Isa as well, without a father."

"the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam" — *mathal 'Isa 'inda Allah ka mathal Adam* — the likeness of 'Isa before Allah is like the likeness of Adam. The comparison is exact: the two unique births — Adam without any parents, 'Isa without a father — are the two cases the Qur'an uses to demonstrate the divine power. Al-Jalalayn: "This is a comparison of one remarkable thing with another more remarkable, so that it convinces the disputer and establishes itself in one's mind more effectively." The comparison's direction is important: the more remarkable case (Adam — no father, no mother, created from dust) is used to explain the less remarkable ( 'Isa — a mother, no father). If the Christian's argument is "he was born without a father, therefore he is divine," then Adam — born without father or mother — is even more divine by the same argument; and the absurdity of the claim about Adam establishes the absurdity of the same claim about 'Isa. Ibn Kathir states it plainly: "If the claim is made that 'Isa is Allah's son because he was created without a father, then the same claim befits Adam even more. However, since such a claim regarding Adam is obviously false, then making the same claim about 'Isa is even more false."

"He created him from dust, then said to him, "Be!" And he was!" — *khalaqahu min turabin thumma qala lahu kun fa yakun* — He created him from dust, then said to him, "Be," and he was. The two cases converge on the command *kun* — Be: the same command that created Adam from dust is the command that created 'Isa in the womb; the command is the divine method of creation, and the command is the same for the two. Ibn Kathir completes the argument with the threefold demonstration of the divine ability: "by mentioning these facts, Allah emphasizes His ability, by creating Adam without a male or female; Hawa' from a male without a female; and 'Isa from a mother without a father — compared to His creating the rest of creation from male and female. This is why Allah said in Surah Maryam: 'And We made him a sign for mankind' (19:21)." The three original cases of creation — the first man, the first woman, the Messiah — are the three deviations from the ordinary pattern, and the three are the demonstrations of the power of the Creator, not the evidence of divinity of the created.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's argument in one image. The Christian's claim: the miracle of the birth proves the divinity. The Qur'an's answer: the miracle proves the power of the Creator, and the Creator's power is demonstrated even more clearly in Adam — who was created from dust without any parents. The command "Be" is the thing that both cases share, and the command belongs to the One who says it, not to the one who comes into being by it. The verse also carries the doctrine of the *kun fa yakun* forward to the whole theology: the same command that created Adam and 'Isa is the command that creates the universe, that raises the dead, that gathers the Day; the "Be" is the signature of the Creator, and the created is the one who comes into being by the signature. The believer reads the verse and the doctrine is complete: the Messiah is the highest of the created — the Word from Him, the honored, the nearest — and the created is created by the command of the Creator, and the Creator is the One who says "Be."

## Verse 60

Verse 60 says: *This is the truth from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt.*

In simple terms, the verse seals the argument: this is the truth from your Lord — so do not be among those who doubt.

"This is the truth from your Lord" — *al-haqq min Rabbika* — the truth from your Lord. The verse's declaration is the doctrine's seal: the account of the Messiah — the Word from Him, the created by the command, the likeness of Adam, the servant of the Lord — is the truth, and it is from the Lord. Ibn Kathir: "This is the only true story about 'Isa, and what is beyond truth save falsehood?" The phrase answers the whole dispute: the arguments of the Christians and the Jews over the Messiah are the dispute; the truth is the account of the Qur'an; and the truth is from the Lord — not from the traditions of the people, not from the arguments of the scholars, but from the One who sent the Messiah.

"so do not be one of those who doubt" — *fa la takun min al-mumtarin* — so do not be among those who doubt, those who are in doubt, those who doubt the truth. The verse's address is to the Prophet — and through him to the reader: the truth has been declared; the doubt is the only remaining option; and the doubt is the option the verse forbids. Ibn Kathir's connection: "Allah next commands His Messenger to call those who defy the truth, regarding 'Isa, to the Mubahalah (the curse)" — the verse that follows (3:61) is the challenge to the disputers: after the knowledge has come, the dispute is settled by the invocation of the curse of Allah upon the liars. The verse's "do not be of those who doubt" is the surah's warning not to the deniers but to the believers: the truth has been stated, and the believer stands on the truth, not in the doubt.

There is a great subtlety in the verse's structure: the command "do not be one of those who doubt" comes after the truth's declaration and before the mubahalah's challenge. The order is the order of the surah's method: the narrative (3:35-58), the argument (3:59), the declaration of the truth (3:60), and the challenge (3:61). The truth has been established by the narrative and the argument; the doubt is the failure to accept the establishment; the challenge is the call to the disputers to invoke the curse if they persist. For the believers, the verse is the declaration of the certainty: the story of the Messiah is the truth from the Lord, and the believer's standing is the standing of the certainty — not the doubt of the disputers.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's answer to the question of the Christian's argument: the truth has been stated, and the truth is from the Lord. The verse also corrects a common misreading of the Muslims' engagement with the People of the Book: the engagement was not the doubt of the truth but the declaration of it — the truth of the Messiah's servanthood, the truth of the divine unity, the truth of the command "Be," and the challenge to the disputers. And the verse's personal address — "do not be one of those who doubt" — is the address to every reader who encounters the story of the Messiah: the truth has been stated; the doubt is a choice; and the certainty is the standing of the believer of the truth from the Lord.
