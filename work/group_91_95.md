## Verse 91

Verse 91 says: *"When it is said to them: "Believe in what Allah has revealed," they reply, "We only believe in what was sent down to us," and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures! Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, "Why then did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are ˹truly˺ believers?""*

In simple terms, the verse now records the standard reply of the people of the Book to the call of Islam: "We only believe in what was sent down to us." The Qur'an's answer has two parts. First, the denial of the new revelation is the denial of the old: the Qur'an is "the truth confirming their own Scriptures" — the one who truly believes in the Torah must believe in what confirms the Torah. Second, the claim of belief in their Scripture is tested against their history: why did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are believers?

The claim "We only believe in what was sent down to us" appears to be piety — fidelity to the original. The Qur'an replies that it is something else: selectivity dressed as loyalty. The new Book confirms the old; therefore the person who rejects the new has, in fact, rejected the old — because he has rejected what it pointed to and confirmed. One cannot claim to believe in a message while rejecting the messenger whom that message promised. The Qur'an's phrase is exact: the denial of the *afterwards* is not a separate denial; it is the denial of the confirmation.

The second part of the answer is a historical question with an unanswerable implication: "Why then did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are believers?" The question exposes the pattern, not merely the episodes. The people who claimed to believe in the revelation given to them had a history — recorded in their own scripture and known to all — of rejecting the prophets who came with demands they did not like. The Qur'an records the same pattern in the previous verse: "rejecting some and killing others." The question is devastating in its logic: your ancestors did to the prophets exactly what you are doing to this one; if that was faith, then faith is what you are doing now; and if it was not faith, then your claim of belief in "what was sent down to us" has always been false.

The verse also engages a subtlety: the people of the Book did not deny the principle of prophecy; they denied the particular prophets. They believed in a message in the past tense — a message that had already been completed, a line of prophets that had already ended with the prophets they approved. The Qur'an's reply is that the message is not a museum; it is a living sequence, and the person who believes in the sequence must accept the next messenger to arrive in it. The claim to believe in the prophets of the past while rejecting the prophet of the present is not faith in prophecy; it is the selection of a past that demands nothing.

In modern life, the verse is the mirror of every religion of the past tense. The person who honors the founders while ignoring the successors, who believes the first generation while rejecting the last, who is loyal to the Scripture he has not read while dismissing the message that confirms it — is in the position of the people of the verse. And the question of the verse is addressed to him as it was addressed to them: if your faith is true, what does it produce in the present?

## Verse 92

Verse 92 says: *"Indeed, Moses came to you with clear proofs, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully."*

In simple terms, the verse continues the argument against the claim of belief: Moses himself came with clear proofs, and the very people who claim to believe in the Torah worshipped the calf in his absence. The verse is a single historical fact presented as the answer: the people who say "we believe in what was sent down to us" are the people of the calf.

The force of the verse is in the phrase "in his absence." Moses had gone to the appointed meeting with Allah; the people had seen the sea parted, the manna descend, and the cloud of guidance; and within the period of his absence they made a calf. The absence of the prophet was the test, and they failed it. The verse's word order is itself the argument: "Moses came to you with clear proofs" — and then, "you worshipped the calf." The proofs and the worship are placed in the same sentence so that the contrast cannot be missed: the evidence was clear, and the response was idolatry.

The "clear proofs" — *bayyināt* — are the signs that accompanied Moses: the staff, the hand, the parting of the sea, the manna and quails. The people had not merely heard of them; they had witnessed them. And yet — the verse's *thumma* — "then" — records what followed: the worship of the calf. The Qur'an names the state precisely: "acting wrongfully" — *wa antum ẓālimūn* — "while you were wrongdoers." The worship was not an error of interpretation or a misunderstanding of Moses' teaching; it was a wrongdoing, done knowingly.

The classical commentators connect this to the same theme as the previous verses: the calf was the first great apostasy of the Children of Israel, and the Qur'an returns to it repeatedly as the proof that the claim of fidelity to Moses was false from the beginning. The people who now tell the Prophet "we only believe in what was sent down to us" are the descendants of the people who, in the presence of Moses' own proofs, turned to an idol. The faith they claim as inheritance was already broken by their ancestors — and the verse's point is that the break has continued to the present.

In modern life, the verse is about the distance between proofs and obedience. It is possible to have witnessed the clearest signs of truth and still to worship what one has made with one's own hands — the calf being the image of every idol we construct: the success, the pleasure, the self, the position, the desire that we place where the revealed command should be. The verse's test is the absence: the moment when the prophet is not present, when the teacher is away, when no one is watching — what is worshipped then? The people of the verse had the clearest proofs and the longest record, and in the absence of the witness they made a calf. The believer is asked the same question about his own absences.

## Verse 93

Verse 93 says: *"And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, "Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey," they answered, "We hear and disobey." The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!""*

In simple terms, the verse records the covenant scene a second time — the mountain raised above them, the command to hold firmly and obey — but now with the answer: "We hear and disobey." The Qur'an then names the condition of the heart: the love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. And the verse closes with the devastating question: "How evil is what your belief prompts you to do, if you actually believe!"

The answer "We hear and disobey" is the Qur'an's most condensed image of spiritual resistance. It is the exact opposite of the response that the same covenant commanded, and it is the exact opposite of the response that the believers gave to their covenant: the Qur'an records the believers' answer as "We hear and obey" (2:285). The two answers are the two ways of receiving revelation: the submission that accepts the command, and the resistance that acknowledges it in words and rejects it in action. The people of the verse heard the words "hold firmly" and "obey" and answered with the words of refusal. The refusal is not ignorance: it is the failure of the will that has heard everything.

"The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief" — the translation renders the Arabic *ushribū fī qulūbihim al-'ijl* — literally "they were made to drink the calf into their hearts." The classical commentators explain the image: Qatadah (via Ma'mar from 'Abdur-Razzaq) said their hearts absorbed the love of the calf until the love resided within them; Abu al-'Aliyah and al-Rabi' ibn Anas said the same. The image of drinking is the image of absorption — the love was not a surface preference but something ingested, internalized, settled into the deepest part of the person. The reason is then stated: "because of their disbelief" — the condition of the heart and the love of the calf were not two separate things; the disbelief was the ground, and the love was what grew from it.

The closing question — "How evil is what your belief prompts you to do, if you actually believe!" — is ironic in the sharpest way. The claim of the people was that they believed in the Torah; the verse says: if you believe, then look at what your belief produced — the broken covenant, the worship of the calf, the answer of disobedience. A belief that produces this is not belief; it is the name the person gives to his refusal. The same irony runs through the whole section: the people who claim faith in the previous prophets are the people whose historical record is the record of the opposite of faith.

In modern life, the verse names the condition of the divided heart: the person who has heard the command clearly and answers with the words of a person who has also heard it — but whose heart has already been absorbed by something else. The "calf" of every age is whatever has been drunk into the heart: the love of money, of status, of a person, of an ideology, of the self. The verse's diagnosis is precise: the refusal is not the cause of the love; the love is the cause of the refusal. And the verse's encouragement is hidden in the same structure: the heart that has been absorbed by the calf can be freed only by the same process in reverse — the love of what is true, drank into the heart, until it resides there.

## Verse 94

Verse 94 says: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death if what you say is true!""*

In simple terms, the verse now moves from history to a direct challenge. If the Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively yours — as the people of the Book claimed — then wish for death, if you are truthful. The challenge is one of the Qur'an's most effective arguments: it takes the claim of privilege and tests it against desire.

The argument works on the logic of the claim itself. If the Hereafter is exclusively for a people, and if that Home is the eternal bliss of Allah, then death is the door to it — and the people who truly believe in their exclusive claim should be the least afraid of death, indeed the most eager for it. The fact that they will not wish for it (as the next verse says) is the proof that the claim is false: the people who believe the Home is theirs would leave this world for it without regret. The Qur'an's test is not a riddle; it is a psychological experiment, and it was performed in front of everyone.

The classical commentators explain that the "wish for death" here means invoking Allah against the lying party — the practice known as *mubahalah* — the mutual invocation of Allah's curse upon the one who is lying. Ibn Kathir explains that the "wish" is so called because every just person who is certain of his evidence wishes that the unjust opponent who denies him be destroyed; the Prophet ﷺ made this offer to the Jews, and they declined it. The same challenge appears in Surah Al-Jumu'ah (62:6-8), where the claim is friendship with Allah, and the same logic is used: wish for death if you are truthful — and they never will.

The verse's deeper point is about the relationship between belief and the world. A person who truly believes in the Hereafter holds this world with an open hand, because the better thing is ahead. A person who claims belief in the Hereafter but clings to this world with both hands has the psychology of a person who does not believe. As the commentators note, this is why the challenge was a test of the claim: the person who is certain that the best is coming does not beg to stay. The one who begs to stay reveals that he does not think the best is coming.

In modern life, the verse is a diagnostic instrument that has not grown old. Anyone — individual, community, religion — who claims that the eternal Home is privileged for himself can be asked the question: then why are you so attached to this transient one? The claim of exclusive salvation has always coexisted with the most intense attachment to the world, and the coexistence is the contradiction. The verse is not asking a person to wish for death; it is asking him to examine what his life reveals about what he actually believes. And for the believer, the challenge is inverted into an instruction: let your life show that the Hereafter is what you are living for.

## Verse 95

Verse 95 says: *"But they will never wish for that because of what their hands have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers."*

In simple terms, the verse records the outcome of the challenge: they will never wish for death, and the reason is stated — because of what their hands have done. The verse then closes with the knowledge of Allah: He knows the wrongdoers.

"Because of what their hands have done" is the verdict. The people could not wish for death because their own deeds had already placed them in a position where death was not a door to bliss but a door to the accounting. They knew the content of their history — the broken covenants, the killing of prophets, the calf, the rejection of the messenger they recognized — and they knew what that history meant for the Home they claimed. Their refusal to wish for death was not a failure of the challenge; it was the confession the challenge was designed to extract. The man who will not demand the door knows that the house behind it is not his.

The verse's logic is worth stating precisely: the claim was "the Home is exclusively ours." The test was "wish for death." The outcome — they will never wish for it — is the falsification of the claim. A person whose hands are clean of the wrongs would have passed the test without fear; the person whose hands have sent ahead what they know to be wrong cannot even attempt it. The Qur'an does not need to describe their secrets; their refusal describes them.

"And Allah has perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers" — the clause is not a threat in the ordinary sense; it is the ground of the whole argument. The verse does not say "Allah knows" as a vague acknowledgment; it says that the One who knows everything has observed the deeds — the expulsions, the shedding of blood, the selective belief, the rejection of the messenger, the insistence on the claim. The knowledge is complete, and the accounting will be based on it: not on the claim, not on the lineage, not on the wish, but on the knowledge.

In modern life, the verse gives a quiet criterion of self-knowledge: the things a person avoids reveal what he knows. The person who cannot bring himself to face a question — the question of death, of the accounting, of the wrong he has done — has already answered it. The Jews of the verse would not say "death, if we are truthful" because their hands had answered before their tongues. The verse's invitation is to the opposite: to the life in which a person can face the door without fear — not because he is sinless, but because his hands have been turned toward the One who knows, and the wrong has been exchanged for the right.
