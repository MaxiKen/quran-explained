## Verse 16

Verse 16 says: *And the two among you who commit this sin—discipline them. If they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful.*

In simple terms, the verse continues the interim ruling of the previous verse: the two people who commit the offence are to be disciplined, but if they repent and reform their conduct, the punishment ends — because Allah accepts repentance and is most merciful.

The verse's first words are *wa-alladhāni yaʾtiyānihā minkum* — "and the two of you who commit it." The dual form indicates two persons, and the classical commentators debated the reference. Ibn Kathir reports that Mujahid said the verse was revealed about two men, "as if referring to the people of Lut" — the sin in the early Islamic period could include both heterosexual immorality and the same-sex acts of the people of Lut, which the Qur'an condemns elsewhere. Other authorities understand the verse as addressing the man and woman who commit the offence together, completing the case of 4:15. Ibn Kathir notes Ibn 'Abbas and Sa'id bin Jubayr held that the punishment at this stage included cursing, shaming, and beating with sandals — that is, a public, corrective discipline — and that this was the ruling until Allah abrogated it with the flogging or stoning of Surat An-Nur.

The word *faʾdhūhumā* — "discipline them" — is the command of the interim law. It does not mean judicial execution or imprisonment as an end in itself; the preceding verse prescribed confinement for the women, and this verse adds corrective punishment for the two offenders, with the goal of deterrence and reform. The classical discussion of *taʾdhīb* in this verse covers verbal rebuke, physical discipline within the limits the law allowed at the time, and isolation — all measures meant to restore the offender rather than to destroy him. This is why the verse's center of gravity is the next clause: *fa-in tābā wa-aṣlaḥā fa-aʿriḍū ʿanhumā* — "if they repent and mend their ways, then turn away from them."

The condition has two parts. *Tawba* — repentance — is the turning of the heart away from the sin and back to Allah, with remorse and the resolve not to return. *Iṣlāḥ* — "mending their ways" — is the outward, verifiable change: the conduct that follows the repentance, the restored behaviour in the community, the evidence that the sin is not merely regretted but abandoned. Ibn Kathir notes the verse's sequence: if the two repent and make good, leave them alone — that is, stop punishing them, stop shaming them, and let them rejoin the community. The relief is not a discount on punishment but the condition of the law: the Qur'an withholds punishment's continuation when the person who sinned has genuinely turned.

The verse closes with two of the most repeated divine names: *tawwāb* — Ever Accepting of Repentance — and *raḥīm* — Most Merciful. The pairing is consistent: the Qur'an repeatedly joins the two at the end of verses about sin and repentance, because the two names correspond to the two divine responses — the acceptance of the return and the mercy that covers the past. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn note the comfort in the verse: the repentance must be sincere and the way must change, but the door remains open, because Allah "is Ever-Accepting of repentance, Most Merciful."

For the modern reader, the verse establishes the Quranic philosophy of correction: punishment is remedial, not merely retributive; its end is the offender's reform; and the moment reform is real, the community's duty is to stop punishing. The verse also establishes a psychological and social principle: when a person repents and changes, the past is not to be endlessly wielded against him. Ibn Kathir notes the verse's mercy even amid the severity of the preceding verses — the law of the early community was stern enough to confine and discipline, yet the verse's conclusion is that the offender who returns is to be left alone. The verse also carries a warning to the self-righteous: the members of the community who punish are themselves standing where repentance is the only deliverance, and the names *tawwāb* and *raḥīm* belong to the One they are imitating in their own lives.

## Verse 17

Verse 17 says: *Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after—Allah will pardon them. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the conditions for accepted repentance: those who sin out of ignorance or recklessness and then turn back to Allah quickly — before death — are pardoned. The verse also teaches that every sin is committed in some form of ignorance, and that the "soon after" means all of life before the soul reaches the throat.

The first condition is the state of the sinner: *alladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sūʾa bi-jahālah* — "those who commit evil in ignorance." The classical commentators are careful here, because the verse could be misread as excusing only the truly ignorant. Mujahid and others explain: "Every person who disobeys Allah by mistake, or intentionally is ignorant, until he refrains from the sin." Abu Al-'Aliyah reports that the Companions used to say: "Every sin that the servant commits, he commits out of ignorance." Ibn Kathir records the same from Qatadah, from 'Abdur-Razzaq through Ma'mar, and from Ibn Jurayj through Mujahid and 'Ata' bin Abi Rabah. Abu Salih reports from Ibn 'Abbas: "It is because of one's ignorance that he commits the error." The point is not that deliberate sin is excused — deliberate sin is also a form of ignorance, because the sinner is ignorant of the consequence and of the gravity of the act, or he knows the fact and not the meaning. The verse's *jahālah* covers both the one who does not know and the one who knows but proceeds as though he did not know.

The second condition is the timing of the repentance: *thumma yatūbūna min qarīb* — "then repent soon after." The commentators interpret "soon" in relation to death. Ibn Kathir records Ibn 'Abbas on "repent soon afterwards": "Until just before he looks at the angel of death." Ad-Dahhak says: "Everything before death is 'soon afterwards.'" Al-Hasan Al-Basri says: "Just before his last breath leaves his throat." 'Ikrimah says: "All of this life is 'soon afterwards.'" The meaning is that repentance is accepted throughout life, and the only boundary is the approach of death itself. This is why Ibn Kathir's summary of the verse is: Allah accepts the repentance of the servant who commits an error in ignorance then repents, "even just before he sees the angel who captures the soul, before his soul reaches his throat."

The prophetic hadith confirms the boundary. Ibn Kathir records Imam Ahmad from Ibn 'Umar: the Prophet said, "Allah accepts the repentance of the servant as long as the soul does not reach the throat" — collected by At-Tirmidhi, who graded it Hasan Gharib, and by Ibn Majah, who mistakenly mentioned 'Abdullah bin 'Amr; Ibn Kathir notes the correct narrator is 'Abdullah bin 'Umar bin Al-Khattab. The image is of a person in the last moments: the soul rising to the throat, the vision of the world receding, and the door of repentance closing with it. Before that moment, the door is open.

The verse then declares the outcome: *fa-ulāʾika yatūbu Allāhu ʿalayhim* — "then Allah will pardon them." The verb *yatūbu* in this construction is Allah's turning toward the servant, and it answers the servant's turning. The verse ends with *ʿAlīm* (All-Knowing) and *Ḥakīm* (All-Wise): Allah knows the sincerity of the repentance and the condition of the heart that made it, and He is wise in both accepting and deferring — no repentance is lost, no sincerity is measured by a human scale, and no one but Allah can judge the moment of the soul's return.

For the modern reader, the verse has a liberating and a sobering message. Liberating: the past does not disqualify. Every human being who has sinned — whether in ignorance, recklessness, or deliberate error — can return, and the return is accepted as long as there is life. Sobering: the boundary is real and named — death — and nobody knows when the soul will be at the throat. The verse is therefore both the greatest encouragement in the Qur'an on the subject of repentance and the clearest reminder that the time to return is now, not "later," because "later" has no name. The All-Knowing, All-Wise Allah is the One who both opens the door and knows the hour at which it closes.

## Verse 18

Verse 18 says: *However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the two exceptions to the previous promise: the person who persists in sin until the moment of death and then repents at the brink, and the person who dies in unbelief. For both, repentance is not accepted, and a painful punishment is prepared.

The verse's first clause is *laysati al-tawbatu li-alladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāt ḥattā idhā ḥaḍara aḥadahum al-mawt* — "repentance is not for those who continue in evil until death comes to one of them" — and the second is "nor those who die as disbelievers." Ibn Kathir's framing: when the hope of continued living diminishes and the angel of death comes forth and the soul reaches the throat, there is no accepted repentance nor a way out. He cites the parallel verses the Qur'an uses to make the same point: 40:84 — "So when they saw Our punishment, they said: 'We believe in Allah Alone...'" — and 6:158 — "The day that some of the signs of your Lord do come, no good will it do to a person to believe then..." Ibn Kathir notes that Allah decided repentance is not accepted from the people of the earth when the sun rises from the west, the sign that brings the door closed for all.

The crucial distinction between 4:17 and 4:18 is the moment and the motive. In 4:17, the sinner repents "soon after" — while there is still life, still a future, still the possibility of proof that the turn is genuine. In 4:18, the person repents at the moment of death: no life remains to prove the turn, no future exists in which the repentance can take effect, and the confession of "Now I repent!" is not a return to Allah but a retreat from death. The classical commentators also note the element of choice: the dying person has no choice but to face the consequence, and the repentance that is forced by the approach of the inevitable is not the turning of a free heart. Al-Jalalayn explains that the repentance must come "before the last gasps of death" and that the one who repents only at death has no accepted repentance.

The second exception — "nor those who die as disbelievers" — is the verse's decisive line. Ibn Kathir notes that the person who dies upon kufr is not merely refused repentance; he is said to have died in a state that the Qur'an itself describes over and over as loss. The verse does not linger on the reasons — unbelief embraced, revelation denied, the mercy of Allah rejected — but states the result: *ulāʾika aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāban alīman* — "for them We have prepared a painful punishment." The verb *aʿtadnā* — "We have prepared" — suggests something readied, waiting, decreed. The punishment is *alīm* — painful, severe — the same word used repeatedly in the Qur'an for the consequence of unrepented sin.

The pair of verses teaches the theology of the last moment with complete clarity. Allah's mercy is not limited by the number of sins; it is limited by life. The Quranic principle, confirmed in the hadith of Ibn 'Umar, is that the door of repentance remains open while the soul is in the body, and the door closes at the moment the soul reaches the throat. The person who has spent a lifetime sinning and returns in the last year, the last month, or the last moment before the angel comes is accepted — because Allah is *tawwāb*. But the person who waits until the deathbed and then professes is not, because the profession at that moment is not repentance; it is a reaction to the end. The difference is not measured by how long the sin lasted. It is measured by whether the turn was a return to Allah or an escape from death.

For the modern reader, the verse is a call to a practical discipline: do not postpone. The believer is to return while life is still open — today, not at death. The verse also safeguards the truthfulness of repentance: it can never be a transaction made in panic at the end. And the verse's second clause — "nor those who die as disbelievers" — is the Qur'an's warning that the mercy of Allah is not a force that operates against a person's own final choice. The door is open, and it closes at a moment nobody knows; the believer's task is to be inside while there is still time.

## Verse 19

Verse 19 says: *O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery. Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing.*

In simple terms, the verse abolishes the pre-Islamic custom whereby the male relatives of a deceased man inherited his widow as if she were property, and it forbids the husband who wishes to divorce from pressuring his wife to give back her dowry. The one permissible exception is adultery. The verse then commands fair treatment of wives and teaches that a husband may dislike something in which Allah has placed great good.

The first prohibition addresses a real historical institution. Ibn Kathir records Al-Bukhari from Ibn 'Abbas: "Before, the practice was that when a man dies, his male relatives used to have the right to do whatever they wanted with his wife. If one of them wants, he would marry her, give her in marriage, or prevent her from marriage, for they had more right to her than her own family. Thereafter, this Ayah was revealed about this practice." Al-Jalalayn gives the details of the Jahiliyyah practice: they would inherit women from kin, marry a woman without giving her a dowry, or marry her off and take the dowry for themselves, or prevent her from marrying until she gave up what she inherited or died. The verse's phrase "inherit women against their will" is therefore literal — the widow was inherited along with the estate, and the verse's opening words, *lā yaḥillu lakum* — "it is not permissible for you" — are a categorical abolition. The Quranic law returns the widow to her own person: she inherits from the estate (4:12), she chooses her own future, and her family decides for her, not the deceased's kinsmen.

The second prohibition concerns the dowry. The verse forbids a husband from mistreating his wife *li-tadhhabū bi-baʿḍi mā ātaytumūhunna* — "in order that you may take away part of what you gave her." Ibn Kathir explains: "Do not treat the woman harshly so that she gives back all or part of the dowry that she was given, or forfeits one of her rights by means of coercion and oppression." The goal is pressure — withholding affection, constant criticism, threats, neglect — until the wife, exhausted, offers to relinquish her mahr in exchange for release. The Qur'an permits a wife to give up part of her dowry voluntarily (4:4), but it forbids the husband from engineering that surrender. The recognized procedure for a wife who cannot live with her husband is *khulʿ* — a mutual, negotiated separation in which the wife redeems herself — and the verse sets its limits: the redemption must be her genuine choice, not the product of mistreatment.

The exception is stated in the translation as "unless they are found guilty of adultery." The Arabic is *illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyina* — "unless they commit a clear immorality." Ibn Kathir lists the authorities who explain *fāḥishah* here as illicit sexual relations: Ibn Mas'ud, Ibn 'Abbas, Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib, Ash-Sha'bi, Al-Hasan Al-Basri, Muhammad bin Sirin, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Ad-Dahhak, Abu Qilabah, Abu Salih, As-Suddi, Zayd bin Aslam, and Sa'id bin Abi Hilal — in which case the husband may take back the dowry, and may also push for khul'. Other authorities — Ibn 'Abbas in one report, 'Ikrimah, and Ad-Dahhak — explain it as disobedience and defiance, and Ibn Jarir considers the general reading — covering adultery, disobedience, defiance, and rudeness — to be a good and reasonable interpretation. The verse's concern throughout is clear: a marriage is not a financial trap, and a woman's money and dignity are not forfeited because she is unhappy.

The verse then says *wa-ʿāshirūhunna bi-al-maʿrūf* — "and live with them honorably." Ibn Kathir explains the meaning as kind words, kindly treatment, and appealing appearance "as much as you can, just as you like the same from them," and he cites 2:228 — "they have rights similar over them to what is reasonable" — and the Prophet's words: "The best among you is he who is the best with his family. Verily, I am the best one among you with my family." The command is not sentimental; it is a full standard of marital conduct. And the verse ends with the famous counsel: *fa-in karihtumūhunna fa-ʿasā an takrahū shayʾan wa-yajʿala Allāhu fīhi khayran kathīran* — "if you dislike them, you may hate something in which Allah has placed much good." Al-Jalalayn suggests the good may be a righteous child granted through them; the broader meaning is that a husband's judgment of his wife is partial, the discomfort may conceal a blessing, and the believer is not to act on first emotions. For the modern reader, the verse is a complete charter: the wife is a person, not an inheritance; her property is hers; her release must be free; her treatment must be honourable; and the husband's feelings, however strong, are not the measure of the marriage's good.

## Verse 20

Verse 20 says: *If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully?*

In simple terms, the verse closes the dowry legislation: a man who decides to divorce a wife and marry another may not take back anything he gave her — even if the dowry was a stack of gold. The verse ends with a rebuke: would you take it back unjustly and sinfully?

The verse begins with the situation: *wa-in aradtumū istibdāla zawjin makāna zawjin* — "and if you desire to replace a wife with another" — the case of a man who prefers a new wife and wishes to remarry. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn explain that the former wife may have been given a *qintār* — a large, abundant sum, an enormous hoard of gold — as her dowry; the verse says: take not the least bit of it back, and certainly not the whole. The Qur'an's use of the extreme example makes the point absolute: whatever was given, and however great it was, belongs permanently to the wife. The *qintār* is the largest unit of wealth the Arabic tradition named — the verse deliberately sets the bound at the furthest possible limit, so that no husband can plead "it was only a little" or "surely so much entitles me to some."

The core legal principle is established in 4:4 (the wife may waive) and 4:19 (the husband may not pressure her into waiving): the dowry belongs to the wife by right of the marriage contract. The verse before us adds the specific case of remarriage — the desire for a replacement is not a ground for reclaiming the first wife's property. The charge is stated as a rhetorical question: *a-taʾkhudhūnahu buhtānan wa-ithmnan mubīnan* — "would you take it wrongfully without a right and with a manifest sin?" Al-Jalalayn notes the interrogative "is meant as a rebuke and as a disavowal"; the verse is not asking information but condemning the act before it happens. The two words are precise. *Buhtānan* — calumny, or the wrongful taking that has no basis in right — describes the moral character of the act: a false claim, an unjust accusation, a taking that defames; *ithm* — sin — describes its religious character: it is disobedience to Allah. Al-Jalalayn notes both are accusative circumstantial qualifiers: the taking is wrongful, and the taking is sinful.

The verse's placement is strategic. The preceding verses dealt with marriage, inheritance, and the protection of women's property; this verse converges on the practical case of a husband who has given a large dowry and now wants a new wife. The message is unmistakable: a man's wish for a new marriage does not create any new right over the funds already given. The classical jurists applied this principle in detail — the husband cannot deduct from the mahr for "unpaid maintenance," cannot use the dowry as a bargaining chip in the khulʿ negotiations beyond the wife's own free offer, and cannot use the desire to remarry as a reason to pressure the current wife. Ibn Kathir frames the verse with the hadith-based rule that the dowry, once given, is the wife's property, unreachable except through her consent.

For the modern reader, the verse teaches the dignity of money given in marriage. The dowry is not a deposit to be recovered or a bride price to be refunded when the transaction changes; it is a gift with right attached — the wife owns it, controls it, and may not be stripped of it by the husband's change of heart. The verse also teaches the Quranic method of moral instruction: the rebuke is delivered as a question, so that the reader who is tempted to take back what he gave is confronted with the absurdity of his intention in his own conscience. And the verse closes the marriage and inheritance section of the surah's opening sequence with a principle that governs every financial exchange in it: what is given to a person is theirs; taking it back unjustly is both calumny and sin, and the heaviest punishment belongs to the practice of despising a woman's rights in order to fund a new preference.
