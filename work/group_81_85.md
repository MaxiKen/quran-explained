## Verse 81

Verse 81 says: *˹Remember˺ when Allah made a covenant with the prophets, ˹saying,˺ “Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom, if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.” He added, “Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?” They said, “Yes, we do.” Allah said, “Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.”*

In simple terms, the verse reminds the People of the Book of the covenant Allah made with the prophets: after giving them the Book and wisdom, if a messenger came confirming what they had, they were to believe in him and support him; the prophets affirmed this and Allah called them to bear witness to it.

The verse returns to the theme of the surah's opening — the covenant of the prophets — and places it at the head of the warning passage that closes the second major section of Surah Ali 'Imran. Ibn Kathir frames the section: "Allah states that He took a pledge from every Prophet whom He sent from Adam until 'Isa, that when Allah gives them the Book and the Hikmah, thus acquiring whatever high grades they deserve, then a Messenger came afterwards, they would believe in and support him. Even though Allah has given the Prophets the knowledge and the prophethood, this fact should not make them refrain from following and supporting the Prophet who comes after them."

"when Allah made a covenant with the prophets" — *wa idh akhadha Allahu mithaqa al-nabiyyin* — when Allah took the covenant, the mithaq, of the prophets. The covenant is the same word used for the covenant of the Children of Israel (2:40, 2:83-84) and for the covenant taken from the believers (5:7). The address is to the prophets themselves, and through them, as Al-Jalalayn stresses, to their communities of descendants: "their communities of descendants follow them in what is incumbent upon them." The covenant is thus the charter of the succession of the prophets and their peoples.

"Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom" — *lama ataytukum min kitabin wa hikmah* — since I have given you of the Book and wisdom. The *lam* can be read as an oath-introducing particle or as the cause: "for what I have given you" — the point being that the gifts of the Book and the wisdom are the very reason the prophets must acknowledge the later messenger. The grandeur of the gift does not exempt them from the response to the next revelation; it obliges them to recognize the confirmation of what they were given. Ibn Kathir stresses this: the knowledge and prophethood already given should not make a prophet refrain from following and supporting the one who comes after.

"if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him" — the covenant's condition and its two obligations: *la tu'minunna bihi wa la tansurunnah* — you shall surely believe in him and surely help him. The confirming messenger is the one who comes with what agrees with the revelation already received — and in the surah's context, this is Muhammad: the Prophet whose description the People of the Book found in their scriptures, who confirmed the Tawrah and the Injil, who made no distinction among the prophets. Ibn Kathir: "'Ali bin Abi Talib and his cousin 'Abdullah bin 'Abbas said, 'Allah never sent a Prophet but after taking his pledge that if Muhammad were sent in his lifetime, he would believe in and support him.' Allah commanded each Prophet to take a pledge from his nation that if Muhammad were sent in their time, they would believe in and support him." The two obligations are belief and support — the inner acknowledgment and the active assistance, the faith and the help, which together make the acknowledgment real.

"He added, 'Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?'" — *qala a'aqartum wa akhazhtum 'ala dhalikum isri* — do you affirm, and have you taken upon that My *isr*? The word *isr* is the crux of the verse. Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, Ar-Rabi', Qatadah, and As-Suddi: "My covenant." Muhammad bin Ishaq: "the responsibility of My covenant that you took — the ratified pledge that you gave Me." The *isr* carries the sense of a burden, a load, an obligation assumed — the same root as the *isr* lifted from the Children of Israel (7:157, 2:286): the heavy commitment accepted. The covenant is not a polite agreement; it is a load carried.

"They said, 'Yes, we do.'" — *qalu aqrarna* — we have affirmed, we have confirmed. The affirmation is the prophetic answer to the divine question. Al-Jalalayn: "They said 'We affirm.'" Ibn 'Abbas's treatment adds the dimension of the transmission: the prophets affirmed, each explained this to his nation, and each made the members of his nation witnesses for each other, just as each prophet bore witness to it. The covenant is not only between Allah and the prophets; it is between Allah, the prophets, and their peoples — the affirmation of the prophet obliges the people, and the witness of the prophet is the witness against the people.

"Allah said, 'Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.'" — *fashhadu wa ana ma'akum mina al-shahidin* — then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses. Al-Jalalayn: "Bear witness to this before your own souls and those of your followers, and I shall be with you among the witnesses before you and them." The witness is the transfer of the testimony: Allah takes the covenant, the prophets affirm it, and the testimony is recorded — Allah as the primary Witness and the prophets as the witnesses to their peoples. The verse closes the chain of the proof: the truth of the final messenger was not an innovation but the content of the ancient covenant, witnessed by the prophets and guaranteed by Allah.

Ibn Kathir draws the consequences of the covenant: Muhammad is the Final Prophet until the Day of Resurrection, the greatest Imam, who if he existed in any time period deserves to be obeyed rather than all other prophets; this is why he led the prophets in prayer during the night of Isra' when they gathered in Bayt al-Maqdis; he is the intercessor on the Day of Gathering, the holder of the Maqam al-Mahmud (cf. 17:79), a responsibility the mighty prophets and messengers will decline to assume. The covenant of 3:81 is therefore the thread that runs through the whole prophetic history: every prophet prepared his people for the one who would come after, and the final one gathers the witness of them all.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's statement of the deep unity of the prophetic message and the primacy of the truth over the authority of the past. The prophets were not given the Book to become the final authorities against later revelation; they were given it to become the witnesses to later revelation. The scholar who has received the Book and the wisdom is the one most obligated to recognize the confirmation when it comes; the grandeur of the gift is the grandeur of the responsibility. The verse also teaches the believer the meaning of the covenant in his own life: the testimony of the prophets, the affirmation of the community, and the return of the witness to Allah — and the burden of knowledge is the burden of the acknowledgment.

## Verse 82

Verse 82 says: *Whoever turns back after this, they will be the rebellious.*

In simple terms, the verse warns: after this covenant, whoever turns away will be among the defiant, the rebellious.

The verse is the penalty clause of the covenant of 3:81. Ibn Kathir: "'then whoever turns away after this' — from fulfilling this pledge and covenant — 'they are the rebellious.'" The word *fasiqun* — the defiantly disobedient, the rebellious, the ones who deviate from the command — is the Qur'an's standard term for those who break the covenant of Allah (cf. 2:26-27, 5:47, 57:26-27). The covenant's breach is not a mere disagreement; it is a moral deviation, a departure from the path.

"Whoever turns back after this" — *fa man tawalla ba'da dhalik* — whoever turns away after that. The *tawalla* is the turning of the back, the deliberate withdrawal — the same verb used in 3:63 for those who turn away from the true narrative and in 3:64 for those who refuse the common word. The qualification "after this" is essential: the verse does not condemn the one who has not heard, but the one who has heard the covenant, affirmed it, and turned away from its consequence. The turning is the rejection of the acknowledged truth — the same pattern the surah has been exposing in the People of the Book.

"they will be the rebellious" — *fa ula'ika humu al-fasiqun* — then those are the fasiqun. The grammatical form — "they are the rebellious" — is the verdict of identity: not merely "they are rebellious" but "they are the rebellious ones," the category of the deviants. Ibn Kathir's context: the exegetes of the verse place it among the warnings of the surah's second section — the covenant, the rebellion, and the consequences that follow in the next verses (the seeking of another religion, the disbelief after the clear proofs, the condemnation, the punishment).

The verse's place in the surah's structure: 3:81-85 form a single argument. First the covenant (81); then the verdict on those who break it (82); then the impossibility of a religion other than the religion of Allah, to which all creation submits (83); then the creed of the believers in all the revelations (84); and then the rejection of any way other than Islam (85). The covenant of 81 is the foundation; the rebellion of 82 is the breach; and the verses that follow define the religious order the breach rejects.

For the reader, the verse establishes the seriousness of the religious commitment. The covenant is made with the prophets and their peoples; the affirmation is a commitment; and the withdrawal after the affirmation is a moral deviation, a *fisq* — not because the truth changed but because the affirmer betrayed it. The believer is taught that the acknowledgment of the covenant is a burden and that the turning from it is a rebellion — the *fisq* is the internal contradiction of the one who witnessed the truth and abandoned it. The verse also teaches the standard of the final judgment: what matters is not the identity of the covenanter but the faithfulness of the covenant — and the one who turns after the affirmation is the rebellious one, whatever his past honor.

The verse's brevity is its force. The Qur'an does not expand the penalty here into a detailed punishment; it names the category: *fasiqun*. The verse is the divine signature of the covenant's seriousness, and it works in the surah exactly as the similar formula in 2:26-27 works — the covenant with the believers, the breaking of it, and the *fasiqun* as the category of the breakers. The *fisq* of 3:82 is the breach of the mithaq — the same word used of the breaking of the covenant of the Children of Israel (2:27) and of the covenant of the prophets themselves. The surah's unifying thread: the series of the covenants — the one with the Children of Israel at the beginning of Surah 2, the one with the prophets here, the one with the believers in 3:81's completion — all framed by the same verdict: the turners-away are the rebellious.

The verse also has a historical resonance with the People of the Book's own scholars. The ones who bore the Book and wisdom, who were the descendants of the covenanting prophets, who were themselves the keepers of the prophetic descriptions — they were the ones whose turning after the covenant was the most consequential: the witnesses of the testimony who refused the testimony's completion. Ibn Kathir's context places the verse against the Jews of Madinah who had the description of the Prophet in their Books and rejected him — the *fisq* of the knowing, not of the ignorant. The verse's warning is therefore not a distant matter of the ancient covenant but a direct matter of the present controversy.

For the modern reader, the verse is the caution against the reverence of the past that becomes the obstacle to the truth. The scholars and the communities of the earlier revelation received the Book and the wisdom; they were the guardians of the description of the final Messenger; and the covenant required them to recognize the confirmation. The turning after the affirmation — the refusal of the one whose coming their own Books announced — was the *fisq*. The believer of today is being taught the same lesson in his own context: the authority of the past, the tradition, the community's inheritance — all of it is a trust that points to the truth, and the one who receives the truth and turns from it after he has affirmed it is the rebellious one. The duty of the knowledge is the duty of the acknowledgment; the acknowledgment is the covenant's content; and the turning after the acknowledgment is the breach.

## Verse 83

Verse 83 says: *Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned?*

In simple terms, the verse asks the rhetorical question: do they seek a religion other than Allah's, while everything in the heavens and the earth submits to Him, willingly or unwillingly, and all of it will return to Him?

The verse is the argument of the impossibility of an alternative religion. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "The Only Valid Religion To Allah is Islam" and frames it: "Allah rebukes those who prefer a religion other than the religion that He sent His Books and Messengers with, which is the worship of Allah Alone without partners." The rhetorical question of the verse is the Qur'an's way of declaring the absurdity of the alternative: the same reality that submits to Allah, and the same future that returns to Allah, leave no room for a religion of another authority.

"Do they desire a way other than Allah's" — *afaghyra dini Allahi yabghun* — do they seek other than the religion of Allah? The Al-Jalalayn notes the disavowing hamza: "the hamza at the beginning of the verse denotes a disavowal." The question is not an inquiry; it is the rebuke of the choice. The *din* — the religion, the way, the allegiance — is Allah's: the worship, the submission, the law of the One who created. The seeker of another *din* is seeking the contradiction of the reality.

"knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will" — *wa lahu aslama man fi al-samawati wa al-ard* — and to Him submitted whoever is in the heavens and the earth. Ibn Kathir's distinction is the heart of the verse: "the faithful believer submits to Allah in heart and body, while the disbeliever unwillingly submits to Him in body only, since he is under Allah's power, irresistible control and mighty kingship that cannot be repelled or resisted." The submission of all creation is not the voluntary choice of every creature; it is the ontological fact of the creation: everything, willing or not, is under the divine order — the angels in obedience, the beasts in the law of their nature, the disbeliever in the power from which he cannot escape. The believer's submission is the voluntary alignment with the order; the denier's submission is the involuntary participation in it.

"willingly or unwillingly" — *taw'an wa karhan* — voluntarily or by compulsion. Al-Jalalayn: the unwilling submission is "by the sword and by seeing what a refusal results in" — in the context of the Qur'anic account of the nations that fought the prophets, the refusal of the voluntary obedience is followed by the involuntary submission. Mujahid's parallel (cited by Ibn Kathir): the verse is similar to 39:38 — "if you ask them, Who created the heavens and the earth, surely they will say: Allah" — the disbelievers acknowledge the Creator in their own affirmation while refusing the worship. Ibn 'Abbas: "When He took the covenant from them" — the primal acknowledgment of the covenant binds them beyond their refusal.

"and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned" — *wa ilayhi yurja'un* — and to Him they will be returned. The return is the Day of Return, the resurrection and the reckoning. Al-Jalalayn adds the variant reading *turja'un* — "you shall be returned" — the direct address to the disbelievers. The verse's argument is complete and circular: the reality that submits and the future that returns leave no third option; the seeker of another religion is the seeker of the impossible — the one who wishes to belong to a reality that has no other master.

Ibn Kathir's parallel verses: 13:15 — "And unto Allah falls in prostration whoever is in the heavens and the earth, willingly or unwillingly"; 16:48–50 — "Have they not observed the things Allah has created: how their shadows incline to the right and the left, making prostration unto Allah, and they are lowly? And to Allah prostrate all that is in the heavens and all that is in the earth, of the moving creatures and the angels, and they are not proud." The submission of the shadows and the creatures is the visible witness to the invisible submission of all things: the *din* of Allah is the order of the creation, and the religion of the believers is the voluntary participation in that order.

For the reader, the verse is the cosmological argument for the unity of the religion. The oneness of Allah is not a theological preference; it is the structure of the universe — the submission of all things, the return of all things, the sovereignty over all things. The believer's *din* is the alignment with the reality; the seeker of another *din* is the seeker of a fiction within the reality. The verse also teaches the distinction between the voluntary and the involuntary submission: the disbeliever is not outside the divine order but in rebellion within it; and the believer is not merely a creature who submits by necessity but a servant who submits by choice. The verse's question — *do they desire a way other than Allah's?* — remains the question for every generation: the seeker of the alternative must answer what alternative there is, when everything submits and everything returns.

## Verse 84

Verse 84 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord—we make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we ˹fully˺ submit.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the creed of the believers: they believe in Allah, in their own revelation, and in all the revelations given to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and his descendants, and to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets — making no distinction among them and submitting to Allah alone.

The verse is the positive creed that answers the challenge of the People of the Book and the negation of 3:83. Ibn Kathir: "Say: 'We believe in Allah and in what has been sent down to us' — the Qur'an — 'and what was sent down to Ibrahim, Ismai'l, Ishaq, Ya'qub' — the scriptures and revelation — 'and the Asbat' — the twelve tribes who originated from the twelve children of Israel (Ya'qub) — 'and what was given to Musa, 'Isa' — the Tawrah and the Injil — 'and the Prophets from their Lord' — and this encompasses all of Allah's Prophets." The creed is the summary of the Islamic understanding of revelation: one source, many prophets, one message, no distinction.

"We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us" — the creed begins with the immediate revelation: the Qur'an, given to Muhammad. The believer's faith is anchored in the revelation of the present, the Book in hand. Ibn Kathir makes this the ground of the whole assertion: the Muslims believe in what was revealed to them, and this is the standard by which the earlier revelations are recognized — not the acceptance of the earlier books over the later, but the confirmation of the earlier by the later.

"and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants" — the list of the patriarchs: Ibrahim, Ismai'l, Ishaq, Ya'qub, and the Asbat — the twelve tribes. The list is the Qur'an's genealogy of the revelation: the patriarchs of the line of the promise, the fathers of the twelve tribes, the bearers of the covenant before the prophets of the law. The mention of Ishmael is decisive in the surah's context: the son of the first Arab line, the father of the Ka'bah's builders, the prophet whose descendants are the believers of the final revelation. Ibn Kathir: "the Asbat are the twelve tribes who originated from the twelve children of Israel (Ya'qub)."

"and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord" — the two great scriptures of the earlier communities — the Tawrah and the Injil — and, beyond them, all the prophets, each given what was given from their Lord. The verse's formula is comprehensive: it names the patriarchs, the tribes, the two law-bearing prophets, and then "the Prophets from their Lord" — the universal category. Ibn Kathir: "this encompasses all of Allah's Prophets." The creed refuses the sectarian narrowing: the Muslims believe in *every* prophet Allah sent and *every* book He revealed, and never disbelieve in any of them.

"we make no distinction between any of them" — *la nufarriqu bayna ahadin minhum* — we do not distinguish between any one of them. The clause is the creed's sharpest edge: the Muslims believe in all the prophets without preferring one over another as the object of faith. Ibn Kathir: "We believe in all of them." The Jews distinguished between the prophets (believing in some, rejecting others like 'Isa and Muhammad); the Christians distinguished in another way (elevating 'Isa and rejecting the final Messenger); the Muslims' creed is the unity of the prophetic message: all of them, from Adam to Muhammad, messengers of the one Allah, and faith in each is faith in all.

"and to Him we ˹fully˺ submit" — *wa nahnu lahu muslimun* — and to Him we are surrendered, Muslims. The creed ends with the act: the submission to Allah alone. The *islam* of the believers is the conclusion of the unity of the prophets: if all the prophets came with the one religion of the submission to the One, then the believers of the final revelation are the *muslimun* — the submitters — and their creed is the creed of all the prophets. Ibn Kathir: "Therefore, faithful Muslims believe in every Prophet whom Allah has sent and in every Book He revealed, and never disbelieve in any of them."

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's inclusive creed, and it is at the same time the sharpest boundary of the faith. The believers believe in Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the tribes, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets — no distinction; and they submit to Allah alone. The verse is the answer to the religious pluralism of the surah's debate: the truth of the earlier prophets is affirmed completely, but the affirmation is the affirmation of the doctrine that they all transmitted — the worship of Allah alone — and the believers' submission is the completion of theirs. The verse also teaches the believer the etiquette of faith toward the prophets: the reverence, the affirmation, the love of all of them — and the worship of none of them. The one who reads the verse is being taught: belief in the prophets is not the belief in the sectarian versions of them but in the authentic message of them — the message of the One.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 85

Verse 85 says: *Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them, and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers.*

In simple terms, the verse declares: whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will not be accepted, and they will be among the losers in the Hereafter.

The verse is the conclusion of the argument of 3:83–84. Ibn Kathir: "whoever seeks other than what Allah has legislated, it will not be accepted from him, and in the Hereafter he will be one of the losers." The verse's structure is the double verdict: the non-acceptance in the present and the loss in the future. The *din* of Allah is the worship of the One; the seeker of another way is the seeker of the rejected.

"Whoever seeks a way other than Islam" — *wa man yabtaghi ghayra al-islami dinan* — and whoever seeks other than al-Islam as a religion. The word *islam* here is the verbal noun of the submission: the surrender, the religion of the submission to Allah — the religion of all the prophets, the religion of the *muslimun* of 3:84. Al-Jalalayn gives the occasion: "the following was revealed regarding those who apostatized and became disbelievers." Ibn 'Abbas (Tanwir): "he will be among the duped because he will miss Paradise and all that is in it and dwell in hell with all that is in it." The verse is addressed in the first instance to the rejecters of the final revelation among the People of the Book and the apostates from the faith, but its principle is universal: the *islam* as the religion of the submission is the only religion that corresponds to the reality of 3:83.

"it will never be accepted from them" — *falan yuqbala minhu* — it will never be accepted from him. The rejection is categorical: *lan* — never. The acceptance of the religion is not measured by sincerity alone but by the correspondence with the truth: the sincere worship of a false object is not accepted, any more than the sincere action against the command is accepted. Ibn Kathir cites the Prophet's hadith: "Whoever commits an action that does not conform to our matter (religion), then it is rejected" (authentic). The verse is the Qur'an's statement of the criterion of the accepted religion: it must be the religion Allah sent, not merely the religion the seeker prefers.

"and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers" — *wa huwa fi al-akhirati mina al-khasirin* — and he, in the Hereafter, is of the losers. The *khasirun* — the losers — are the ones whose trade is loss (cf. 2:27, 3:64's context, 39:15): they have spent the life in the worship of the false, and the balance is the loss. Ibn Kathir: the loser's end is "the Fire made everlasting for him." The double verdict — the non-acceptance and the loss — is the Qur'an's severity at its exact point: the religion of the submission is the only way, and the way of the other is the way of the loss.

The theological context of the verse requires care and balance. The *islam* of the verse is not the historical label of the later community alone but the submission to Allah — the religion of the prophets, the religion of the *hanif* Abraham, the religion of the worship of the One. The verse's categorical statement is the Qur'an's answer to the claim that sincerity saves regardless of the truth: the truth is the criterion, and the truth is the submission. At the same time, the verse is not a license for the arrogance of the believers: the *islam* is the submission of the heart and the body, and the admission to it is by the mercy of Allah, not by the label of the community. The verse's force is the exclusivity of the truth; its grace is the openness of the submission.

For the reader, the verse is the theological foundation of the exclusivity of the religion of the submission, and one of the most discussed verses of the Qur'an in interfaith dialogue. The reader is taught: the criterion of the accepted religion is the correspondence with the truth revealed, and the seeker of the alternative — the worship of other than the One, the autonomy of the human legislation against the divine — is the seeker of the rejected. The verse is also the believer's warning: the *islam* is not a label but a submission; the one who bears the name without the surrender is as much at risk as the one who never bore it. And the verse's double verdict — the non-acceptance and the loss — is the seriousness of the religious choice: it is not a matter of preference but of truth, and the truth is the way of the submission.
