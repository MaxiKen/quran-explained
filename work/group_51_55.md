## Verse 51

Verse 51 says: *Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord. So worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.’”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the creed of the Messiah: Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him alone — this is the straight path.

The verse is the conclusion of 'Isa's address to the Children of Israel that began at 3:49. Ibn Kathir's framing of the section: the Messiah's message is the message of the prophets — the Oneness of the Lord and the worship of Him alone. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings treat the verse as the declaration at the center of the surah's argument: the one who is called the Word from Him, the one whose name is the Messiah, the one who speaks in the cradle and in manhood — his own creed is the Oneness.

"Allah is my Lord and your Lord" — *inna Allah Rabbi wa Rabbukum* — Allah is my Lord and your Lord. The verse's pronoun structure is central: the Lord is mine and yours — the one who is the Word from Him, the one honored in this world and the Hereafter, the one among the nearest, has a Lord; the one who has a Lord is the servant of the Lord. The phrase is the answer to the claim of the divinity of 'Isa: the Messiah himself declares the Lordship of Allah and his own servantship. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn hold the same line: the Messiah's own testimony — "Allah is my Lord and your Lord" — is the testimony of the one who is not the Lord but the one who says the Lord is both his and ours.

"So worship Him ˹alone˺" — *fa u'buduhu* — so worship Him. The command follows the identity: since Allah is my Lord and your Lord, worship Him — not me, not the angels, not the intermediaries. The worship here is the worship with the exclusive object: the worship of Him alone, the Tawhid that the Qur'an places at the center of the religion of every prophet. The verse is the Messiah's own command on the subject that the surah is disputing with the Christians: the worship belongs to the Lord; and the Lord is mine and yours.

"This is the Straight Path" — *hadha siratun mustaqim* — this is a straight path, the straight path. The phrase is the Qur'an's name for the religion of the Tawhid: the path of the worship of the One is the straight one; the worship of others with Him is the crooked. The Messiah's creed is the creed of the Prophet and the creed of the surah: the straight path is the path that runs from the declaration "Allah is my Lord and your Lord" to the worship of Him alone.

For the reader, the verse is the heart of the surah's answer to Christianity's central question. The Christians of Najran came arguing the divinity of the Messiah; the Qur'an's answer is not a counter-argument but the Messiah's own words: "Allah is my Lord and your Lord. So worship Him alone. This is the Straight Path." The one about whom the claim is made is the one who makes the counter-claim. The verse is also the highest expression of the unity of the prophets: the creed of 'Isa is the creed of Ibrahim and Musa and Muhammad — the one Lord, the one worship, the one straight path. And for the reader, the verse is the personal standard: the straight path is not a mere set of rituals but the confession of the singularity of the Lord in the worship — and the confession is made in the mouth of the highest of the created before the Lord who is mine and yours.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 52

Verse 52 says: *When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people, he asked, “Who will stand up with me for Allah?” The disciples replied, “We will stand up for Allah. We believe in Allah, so bear witness that we have submitted.”*

In simple terms, the verse records the moment of decision: when Jesus sensed the persistence of disbelief among his people, he asked who would be his helpers for Allah; the disciples answered — we are the helpers of Allah; we believe in Allah; bear witness that we have submitted to Him.

"When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people" — *fa lamma ahassa 'Isa minhum al-kufr* — when 'Isa sensed from them the disbelief, the denial. Ibn Kathir: "'Isa felt that they were adamant in disbelief and continuing in misguidance." The sensing is the experience of the messenger of the closing of the people's hearts: the miracles had been performed (3:49), the creed had been declared (3:51), and the response was the hardening. Al-Jalalayn: "he became aware of their disbelief, and they plotted to kill him."

"he asked, "Who will stand up with me for Allah?"" — *man ansari ila Allah* — who are my helpers to Allah, who will be my helpers in the cause of Allah. The question is the messenger's call for the supporters: the same call that the final Messenger would make in the era of the Hijrah. Ibn Kathir notes the parallel: "The Prophet said during the Hajj season, before the Hijrah: 'Who will give me asylum so that I can convey the Speech of my Lord, for the Quraysh have prevented me from conveying the Speech of my Lord?' — until he found the Ansar. The Ansar helped the Prophet and gave him refuge. He later migrated to them, they comforted the Prophet and protected him from all his enemies. This is similar to what happened with 'Isa: for some of the Children of Israel believed in him, gave him their aid and support, and followed the light that was sent with him." Mujahid's reading: "Who would follow me to Allah?" — the helpers are those who follow the messenger to the Lord; the most likely sense per Ibn Kathir is the help of conveying the message: "Who would help me convey the Message of Allah?"

"The disciples replied" — *al-hawariyyun* — the disciples, the helpers. Ibn Kathir: "Hawari in Arabic means 'support.'" Al-Jalalayn: "They were Jesus's intimates and the first to believe in him. They were twelve men of pure white complexion; but some say that they were called hawariyyun because they were bleachers of clothes." The Two Sahihs record the Prophet's hadith at the battle of al-Ahzab: "Every Prophet has a Hawari, and Az-Zubayr is my Hawari" — the discipleship of the helper is the station of the supporter of the prophet. The disciples are the first believers of the religion of 'Isa: the twelve who answered the call at the moment the people denied.

"We will stand up for Allah" — *nahnu ansar Allah* — we are the helpers of Allah. The response takes the call and redirects it: the assistance is not the assistance of 'Isa in a personal cause but the assistance of Allah — the support of the religion of Allah. The believers' profession: we are the helpers of the Lord.

"We believe in Allah" — *amanna bi Allah* — we have believed in Allah. The confession precedes the action: the helpers are the believers; the support of the cause is the form of the belief.

"so bear witness that we have submitted" — *wa ishhad bi anna muslimun* — and bear witness that we are Muslims, that we have submitted. The word *muslimun* is used of the disciples at the very moment the surah is addressing the Christians: the first followers of the Messiah are the ones who submitted, the ones who are called *muslimun* — the ones whose religion is the Islam of 3:19, the submission to the One. Al-Jalalayn: "We accept the truth of God; witness, O Jesus, that we have submitted." The verse is one of the Qur'an's most pointed uses of the word: the faithful of the Messiah are Muslims; and the Christians of Najran who are disputing the Messiah are being shown that the Messiah's own disciples bore witness to the submission.

For the reader, the verse gives the picture of the prophetic community at the moment of the people's denial: the messenger asks for the helpers, and the believers answer. The structure of the answer — the support, the belief, the witness — is the structure of the faith: the declaration of belief, the commitment of the support, the witness of the submission. The verse is also the model for every later community: the believers are those who stand with the prophet, who follow him to Allah, and who bear witness to their submission. And the disciples' own testimony — "we have submitted" — is the verse's quiet answer to the surah's central dispute: the true followers of 'Isa were not the ones who argued for his divinity but the ones who bore witness to their submission; the submission is the religion of every faithful of the Messiah, and the Islam of 3:19 is the name of the disciples' own testimony.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 53

Verse 53 says: *˹They prayed to Allah,˺ “Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations and follow the messenger, so count us among those who bear witness.”*

In simple terms, the verse records the prayer of the disciples: Our Lord, we believe in what You have sent down and we follow the messenger, so write us among the witnesses.

"Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations" — *rabbana amanna bima anzalta* — Our Lord, we believe in what You have sent down. Ibn Kathir: the disciples' prayer continues the confession of 3:52: the belief in what was revealed — the revelation that came before ('Isa confirming the Torah) and the revelation that came with him (the Gospel). The prayer's structure is the faith's structure: the address to the Lord, the belief in His revelation, and the following of His messenger.

"and follow the messenger" — *wa ittaba'na al-rasul* — and we follow the messenger. The following of 'Isa is the form of the belief of the disciples — the same following that the surah has commanded at 3:31 (if you love Allah, follow me) and that the disciples have just confessed at 3:52 (we have submitted). The prayer states the two elements of the faith: the belief in the revelation and the following of the messenger — the attestation of the Book and the submission to the messenger. The same pair recurs in the Qur'an's description of the early Muslims (2:285: "We believe in Allah and what has been sent down to us and what was sent down to Ibrahim... and we do not differentiate between any of His messengers, and we submit to Him").

"so count us among those who bear witness" — *fa uktubna ma'a al-shahidin* — so write us down among the witnesses. Ibn Kathir records the reading of Ibn 'Abbas: "meaning among the Ummah of Muhammad" — the disciples pray to be written among the witnesses; and the tradition's interpretation of the witnesses is the community of the final Messenger. The verse's phrase is the disciples' prayer for the community of the witnesses: the ones who bear witness to the truth of the message and the mission. Al-Jalalayn and the classical notes: the witnesses are those who testify to the Oneness and the message; the disciples' prayer is the request to be written in the register of the witnesses. The same phrase appears in the Qur'an of Muhammad's own community (2:143: "so that you may be witnesses over the people").

For the reader, the verse completes the picture of the disciples: the confession of 3:52 (we are the helpers; we believe; we have submitted) becomes the prayer of 3:53 (we believe in what You sent down; we follow the messenger; write us among the witnesses). The progression is the faith's progression: the declaration, the belief, the following, and the prayer for the register. The verse also contains the answer to the surah's dispute with the Christians over the followers of 'Isa: the true helpers and witnesses of the Messiah are those who believe in the revelation and follow the messenger — not those who dispute the messenger's nature, and not those who turn the disciples of the Messiah into the founders of a worship of the Messiah. The same prayer is the prayer of every believer of the community of the witnesses: the believers of the revelation, the followers of the messenger, and the witnesses of the truth.

There is also a structural point in the prayer that the classical exegetes preserve. The prayer of the disciples has three clauses, and the three clauses are the three movements of the believers' response: the belief of the heart ("we believe in Your revelations"), the following of the practice ("and we follow the messenger"), and the request of the standing ("so count us among those who bear witness"). The order is the order of the faith: the belief comes before the following, and the following comes before the witness. There is no witness without the belief, and no following of the messenger without the belief in the revelation. The disciples did not first bear witness and then believe; they first believed, then followed, then asked to be written among the witnesses.

The word *shahidin* — the witnesses — also carries the meaning of the martyrs of the tradition: the *shuhada'* are the ones who testify by their faith and their lives, and in the later vocabulary of the religion the witness is the one who bears testimony even at the cost of the life. The disciples pray to be written among the witnesses, and the prayer is answered in the history of the disciples: the ones who answered the call of the Messiah at the moment of the people's denial became the witnesses of the faith in the age of the persecution. The Qur'an's own account of the witnesses (in the story of the believers of the city, 36:13-14; and in the prayer of the believers at 3:146-148) treats the witnesses as the believers who stand beside the prophets and the helpers.

For the believers of the community of Muhammad, the prayer of the disciples is the prayer of every believer of the community of the witnesses: "Our Lord, we believe in what You have sent down, and we follow the messenger, so count us among the witnesses." The verse is also the bridge between the community of the Messiah and the community of the final Messenger: the disciples' prayer and the believers' prayer are the same prayer, because the religion is the same religion — the belief in the revelation, the following of the messenger, and the prayer for the register of the witnesses. The Christians of Najran who disputed the nature of the Messiah are being shown the prayer of the Messiah's own disciples: the belief, the following, and the witness — not the dispute, and not the worship of the one they followed, but the following of the one who said "Allah is my Lord and your Lord" (3:51).

## Verse 54

Verse 54 says: *And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners.*

In simple terms, the verse states the conflict: the disbelievers plotted against Jesus, and Allah also planned — and Allah is the best of planners.

"And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺" — *wa makaru* — they plotted, they schemed. Ibn Kathir's section title: "The Jews Plot to Kill 'Isa" — "Allah states that the Children of Israel tried to kill 'Isa by conspiring to defame him and crucify him. They complained about him to the king who was a disbeliever. They claimed that 'Isa was a man who misguided people, discouraged them from obeying the king, caused division, and separated between man and his own son. They also said other lies about 'Isa, which they will carry on their necks, including accusing him of being an illegitimate son. The king became furious and sent his men to capture 'Isa to torture and crucify him." The plot was the completed crime of the political and religious powers of the era: the accusation, the defamation, the mobilization of the state, the decree of execution. The disbelievers made a plan.

"but Allah also planned" — *wa makara Allah* — and Allah plotted, planned. The divine plan is the answer to the human plan: the counter-scheme that the human scheme cannot see. Ibn Kathir's narrative: "When they surrounded 'Isa's home and he thought that they would surely capture him, Allah saved him from them, raising him up from the house to heaven. Allah put the image of 'Isa on a man who was in the house; when the unjust people went in the house while it was still dark, they thought that he was 'Isa. They captured that man, humiliated and crucified him. They also placed thorns on his head. However, Allah deceived these people. He saved and raised His Prophet from them." The divine plan is the plan that used the plotters' own instrument — the appearance, the arrest, the execution — and turned it against them: the one they thought they had captured was not the one they captured; the crucifixion of the substitute became the humiliation of the plotters; and the Messiah was raised to the Lord. Al-Jalalayn cites the same narrative of the substitution (the man in the house — in the tradition, the one who was made to resemble 'Isa) and concludes: "Allah deceived these people."

"and Allah is the best of planners" — *wa Allah khayr al-makirin* — and Allah is the best of those who plan. The title of the best of the planners is the theology of the verse: the human plan is real and its instruments are real — the accusation, the king, the arrest, the cross — and the divine plan is the deeper one: the plan that uses the human plan for the divine outcome. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings hold the same: the plotters plotted what they thought was the defeat of the messenger; Allah planned the triumph of the messenger through the defeat of the plot.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's central doctrine of the divine governance of the plot. The believers of every era face the plotters: the accusation, the defamation, the mobilization of power, the decree of destruction; the Qur'an's answer is the verse: they planned, and Allah planned, and Allah is the best of planners. The doctrine has two sides. The comfort: the plot is not the last word; the plan of the plotters is enclosed within the plan of the Lord. And the warning: the plotters' plan becomes the instrument of their own humiliation — the same plan that destroyed the appearance of 'Isa becomes the sign of his elevation and the testimony against the plotters. The believers' response — as the disciples did at 3:52-53 — is not the counterplot but the faith and the following: the belief, the support, and the witness; the plan belongs to Allah, and the best of the planners is the One whose plan the plotters cannot see.

## Verse 55

Verse 55 says: *˹Remember˺ when Allah said, “O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself. I will deliver you from those who disbelieve, and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes.*

In simple terms, the verse announces the divine response to the plot: Allah said to Jesus — I will take you and raise you to Myself; I will purify you from the disbelievers; I will place your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment; then to Me is your return, and I will judge between you in your disputes.

"when Allah said, "O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself"" — *inni mutawaffika wa rafi'uka ilayya* — I will take you (in the sense of the taking of the soul at death) and raise you up to Myself. Ibn Kathir treats the *tawaffi* at length: "while you are asleep" — the taking of the soul; the same word used in 6:60 ("It is He who takes your souls by night") and 39:42 ("It is Allah who takes away the souls at the time of their death, and those that die not during their sleep"); the Prophet's supplication upon waking: "All the thanks are due to Allah who brought us back to life after He had caused us to die (sleep), and the Return is to Him." Ibn Abi Hatim recorded that Al-Hasan said the *tawaffi* of 'Isa is in reference to sleep: "Allah raised 'Isa while he was asleep." Al-Jalalayn: "I am gathering you, seizing you, and raising you to Me away from the world without death." The verse is the Qur'an's answer to the plot: the one they planned to kill was not killed by them; he was taken and raised by Allah — and the taking is the divine act, not the act of the plotters. The fuller denial of the crucifixion appears at 4:157: "they killed him not, nor crucified him, but it was made to appear to them."

"I will deliver you from those who disbelieve" — *wa mutahhiruka min alladhina kafaru* — and purify you, cleanse you, deliver you from those who disbelieve. Ibn Kathir: "by raising you to heaven." Al-Jalalayn: "I am cleansing you of... removing you far away from those who disbelieved." The purification of the Messiah from the disbelievers is the end of their power over him: the plot of 3:54 ends with the prophet's removal from their reach.

"and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment" — *wa ja'il alladhina ittaba'uka fawqa alladhina kafaru ila yawm al-qiyamah* — and I will make those who followed you above those who disbelieved until the Day of Resurrection. Al-Jalalayn: "those Christians and Muslims who believed in your prophethood above those who disbelieved in you (namely the Jews), becoming above them through definitive argument and the sword until the Day of Resurrection." Ibn Kathir's section "Altering the Religion of 'Isa" continues the analysis: the elevation of the followers of 'Isa above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment is the divine arrangement, and the followers of the Messiah — the ones who believed in his prophethood and submitted — are the ones who are elevated, not the ones who argued his divinity. The verse's "until the Day of Judgment" sets the treaty: the elevation has a term; the final settlement is the return to Allah.

"Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes" — *thumma ilayya marji'ukum fa ahkumu baynakum fima kuntum fihi takhtalifun* — then to Me is your return, and I will judge between you in what you used to differ. The verse's closing is the settlement clause: the disputes of the Christians and the Jews, of the followers and the deniers, of the communities of the Messiah — the argument about his divinity, his crucifixion, his sonship, his mission — all of it is deferred to the return; and the Judge of the return is Allah. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn: the final judgment of the disputes belongs to Allah; the verse is the answer to every claim and counter-claim of the history of the Messiah. The same doctrine appears at 3:58 ("We recite this to you as one of the signs and a wise reminder") and at the end of the surah's section on the Messiah (3:56-60).

For the reader, the verse is the surah's declaration of the outcome of the Messiah's history: the plot fails; the prophet is taken and raised; the followers are elevated until the Day; and the disputes are deferred to the Judge of the return. The verse's significance for the dispute with the Christians is twofold. First, the crucifixion: the verse denies the plot's success — the one they planned to kill was taken and raised by Allah, and the fuller denial at 4:157 is the surah's answer to the Christian doctrine of the atonement. Second, the elevation of the followers: the promise is not to a sect but to the followers of the prophethood — the ones who believe in the revelation and follow the messenger (3:53): the Muslim believers of 'Isa as much as the disciples. The verse is also the believer's doctrine of the deferred settlement: the disputes of the religion are not settled by the argument of the strongest or the power of the state; they are settled by the Judge of the return, and the return is to Him.

===VERSE-END===
