## Verse 101

Verse 101 says: *"Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them— confirming their own Scriptures—some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs as if they did not know."*

In simple terms, the verse continues the accusation of the previous section. A messenger had come to them from Allah, confirming their own Scriptures — and some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs, as if they did not know it. The image is of a person who takes a priceless document and throws it away; the addition "as if they did not know" is the verdict on the performance.

The word "cast" is the key. The Qur'an does not say they rejected the Book, or neglected it, or misunderstood it. It says they *nabadhū* — they flung it behind their backs. This is the gesture of a person who turns away from something deliberately, with contempt, and it is the same word used in the previous verse for casting the covenant aside. The covenant, the Scripture, and the messenger are one sequence: the people who throw the covenant aside are the same people who throw the Book behind them, and the same people who throw the messenger aside.

"As if they did not know" — the phrase is the completion of the irony. They did know. The previous verses said so explicitly: they recognized the Book (2:89); they recognized the messenger as they recognized their own children (2:146). The "as if" describes the performance: they behaved as though they were ignorant, though they were not. The Qur'an is not describing ignorance; it is describing the appearance of ignorance chosen after knowledge. This is the most serious form of rejection, because it was not a failure to find the truth but a refusal to acknowledge it.

The classical commentators note in this context that the Jews had, in their own scripture, the descriptions and the predictions of the coming prophet; and when the Prophet arrived, the scholars among them — as the previous verses showed — had two responses: some denied him openly, and some turned to the books of their tradition, particularly the lore of magic, which the next verse describes. The verse before us is the moment of the casting: the truth was present, and it was thrown away.

In modern life, the verse describes the most dangerous form of rejection of knowledge: the person who has the evidence before him and acts as if it were not there. This is not the person who has not encountered the truth; it is the person who encountered it and turned his back — who reads the message and tosses it behind him. The verse's phrase "as if they did not know" is the mirror in which such a person may recognize himself: the performance of ignorance that is actually the avoidance of acknowledgment.

## Verse 102

Verse 102 says: *"They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, "We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith." Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!"*

In simple terms, the verse now exposes what the People of the Book had turned to instead of the Book: they followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. The Qur'an then sets the record straight on three points: Solomon did not disbelieve — the devils disbelieved; the devils taught magic; and the account of the two angels in Babylon, Harut and Marut, with their warning — "We are only a test for you, so do not abandon your faith." The verse ends with the verdict on the buyers of magic: miserable was the price they sold their souls for.

The occasion is recorded in the classical tafsir. As-Suddi's account, transmitted by Ibn Kathir, describes how the devils had spread the claim that Solomon's power over the jinn and the wind was magic — that he had control over them by a book of sorcery buried and then found. The claim was repeated until people said Solomon was a sorcerer, and the books were adopted. When the Prophet came, some of the People of the Book argued against him with these books, as though the Qur'an were the same kind of thing. The verse clears the prophet Solomon: he was a prophet, not a sorcerer; the disbelievers were the devils, who taught the people magic.

The account of Harut and Marut is one of the most discussed passages in the tafsir, and it is important to present the differences of opinion honestly. The mainstream reading is that these were two angels sent to Babylon as a test, and whatever "was revealed" to them was accompanied by the warning: "We are only a test, so do not abandon your faith" — meaning that the knowledge they made available was a trial, not a license. Some early authorities, including a view reported from al-Tabari, understood the passage differently — arguing that Allah did not send magic down at all and that the devils fabricated the teaching; al-Tabari's specific explanation (that Harut and Marut were two men, with Gabriel and Michael being the "two angels" of the verse) is recorded by Ibn Kathir, who states plainly that he finds it implausible. Other authorities, such as al-Qurtubi and al-Rabi' bin Anas, held that nothing magical was sent down; the verse's phrase is understood as the devils' claim. The point that all the authorities share is the verse's central warning: the knowledge was a test, learning magic is disbelief, and the teachers warned before teaching.

The verse gives the social consequence of magic in one clause: it causes a rift between husband and wife. Magic is not a neutral curiosity; it is a weapon of alienation, and its deepest victims are the closest bonds. The Qur'an then makes the theological point that contains the whole matter: "their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will." The magician acts within the world Allah created; no power is independent of Allah. The people learned what harmed them and did not benefit them — the Qur'an's exact description of every transaction with the occult, and indeed of every trade of the soul for a power that turns against the one who sought it.

In modern life, the verse is a warning against the fascination with the occult in every age: the astrology, the charm, the curse, the manipulation of unseen forces — all sold as power and bought at the price of the soul. The Qur'an's verdict is the same in every generation: the person who buys into it has no share in the Hereafter; the price is miserable; and the buyer knew it all along.

## Verse 103

Verse 103 says: *"If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew!"*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the account of magic is the counterfactual: if only they were faithful and mindful of Allah, there would have been a better reward from Allah. The "if only" is the Qur'an's lament over a choice: the people who sold their souls for magic could have had the reward of Allah — had they believed and been mindful.

The verse is a single sentence with two "if only"s — *law annahum āmanū* and *law kānū ya'lamūn* — and the repetition carries the whole of the human tragedy. The first "if only" names the condition they did not meet: faith and mindfulness. The second names the failure behind it: "if only they knew" — they did not know what they were trading away. In the previous verse, the buyers of magic "already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter" — they knew the doctrine; what they did not know was the value of what they were losing. The second "if only" is not about information; it is about appreciation. A person can know the value of a thing with his mind and not know it with his heart — and the person who trades the heart's treasure for the hand's amusement has not "known" in the sense the verse means.

The verse also completes the evaluation of the trade. The previous verse said "miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls." This verse shows the alternative: faith and mindfulness would have brought "a better reward from Allah." The two verses are the two sides of the account: what they lost, and what they could have had. The "better reward" is not better than magic — it is better than everything, because it is with Allah: the reward that is not lost, not corrupted, and not taken away.

The word "mindful" here is *taqwā* — the God-consciousness that the surah has been teaching from its earliest verses. Faith without mindfulness is incomplete; mindfulness without faith is impossible. The verse names the two together because the believer is the person who believes and therefore lives in the awareness of Allah — the awareness that changes what he buys, what he trades, and what he values.

In modern life, the verse is the voice of regret before it happens. The person who spends his life on what passes while neglecting what remains will one day use this verse's words: if only I had been faithful and mindful, there would have been a better reward. The verse's purpose is to place that sentence before the reader now, while the "if only" can still be turned into "yes." The reward of Allah is available; the only question is whether the person will know its value at the time of the choice.

## Verse 104

Verse 104 says: *"O believers! Do not say, "Râ'ina." [Herd us!] But say, "Unẓurna," [Tend to us!] and listen ˹attentively˺. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment."*

In simple terms, the verse now addresses the believers directly with a command about speech: do not say "Ra'ina" — but say "Unzurna" — and listen attentively. The background is one of the most remarkable episodes of the early Madinan period, and it is a lesson in the etiquette of words.

The background is recorded in the tafsir. The word *ra'ina* in Arabic means "attend to us" or "hear us" — a polite request for attention. But in Hebrew, the same sound is an insult — a curse — and the Jews used the Arabic word deliberately, pronouncing it in a way that sounded identical while intending the Hebrew insult, as a device for mocking the Prophet without the Muslims realizing it. When they said "Ra'ina" they were saying something else; and they were delighted when some Muslims, hearing the Arabic meaning, began to use the same word themselves — because now the Muslims had been tricked into participating in the mockery of the Prophet. Ibn Abbas, as recorded by Ibn Kathir, said they used to say to the Prophet words that were insults; al-Suddi mentions a Jewish man from Banu Qaynuqa' who would come to the Prophet and say "hear, let you hear nothing" — words the Muslims took as greetings, not realizing the abuse they contained. The believers were therefore commanded to use a different word — "Unzurna," "tend to us" — a word with no double meaning, so that the mockery could not be performed under cover of piety.

The verse's command is thus a lesson in the ethics of language: not every word that is permissible in one's own language is safe from being used against the truth; and the believer is commanded to use words that are unambiguous, so that his speech cannot be turned against the message or the messenger. The broader command ends with "and listen attentively" — the verse is not only about the word used but also about the manner of receiving the message: the believer hears and gives attention, whereas the mockers pronounced words of sarcasm while refusing to listen.

The verse's final clause — "And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment" — connects the etiquette of speech with the verdict of the hereafter. Mockery of the prophet is not a small fault; it is a form of disbelief, and the Qur'an names it as such. The person who uses words as instruments of contempt, who twists language to insult the truth while appearing to speak respectfully, has done something that the verse does not classify as a minor sin.

In modern life, the verse is the foundation of the etiquette of speech in a world of double meanings, irony, and coded insults. It teaches that words are not neutral: the same sound can mean one thing to one hearer and another to another, and the person who speaks without attention to what his words may carry has abdicated the moral responsibility of his tongue. It also teaches the believers to choose words that cannot be twisted, and to refuse the game in which the truth is mocked under the cover of politeness.

## Verse 105

Verse 105 says: *"The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord, but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty."*

In simple terms, the verse states the psychological background of the entire section: the disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want the believers to receive any blessing from their Lord. The statement is in the negative form — "they would not want" — and the verse then gives the answer: but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy, for Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

The verse identifies the common ground of the two groups. The People of the Book and the polytheists had nothing else in common — one group had a scripture and the other worshipped idols — but they shared this: neither wanted the blessing to descend on the believers. The blessing in question is the revelation, the prophethood, and the guidance sent to the Prophet and his community. The previous verses have shown the envy of the People of the Book (2:89-90); this verse names the same attitude at the level of desire: they do not want the good to come to you.

"But Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy" — the verse locates the matter in the choice of Allah. The objection of the People of the Book was not finally an objection about who should receive revelation; it was an objection about the freedom of Allah. In their view, the prophecy belonged to them by right of lineage; the Qur'an's answer is that prophecy is not an inheritance but a choice, and the choice belongs to the One who gives the blessing. "And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty" — the final clause is the refutation at the level of the divine: the bounty is not scarce; it is not competed for; it flows from the One whose treasury is not diminished by the giving.

The verse also teaches the believers the correct response to the hostility of others: not to build the religion in reaction to the wishes of the people, not to believe that the truth depends on their approval — but to receive the blessing with gratitude and to leave the choice where it belongs, with Allah. The believers' task is not to convince the envious but to receive the gift.

In modern life, the verse speaks to every community that has received a blessing — an opportunity, a message, a mission — and finds itself surrounded by those who do not want it to succeed. The attitude of such people is real and can be measured, but the verse says it is not decisive: the blessing comes by the choice of Allah, not by the consent of the onlookers. The believer's posture is gratitude and perseverance, and the believer's security is the infinite bounty of the One who has already chosen to give.
