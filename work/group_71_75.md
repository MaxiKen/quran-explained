## Verse 71

Verse 71 says: *"He replied, "Allah says, 'It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish.'" They said, "Now you have come with the truth." Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly!"*

In simple terms, the second half of the story is the final specification. The cow should be neither old nor young, bright yellow, pleasant to see, and it should have been used neither for ploughing nor for watering — it should be wholesome and free from blemish. The people then said, "Now you have come with the truth" — but even then, they slaughtered it hesitantly, as though they were doing it reluctantly.

The final description completed the portrait of a cow of exceptional value: a mature cow in its prime, of striking colour, never worked in the fields, healthy and unblemished. In the desert economy of the Children of Israel, a cow that had never been used for labour would be a rare and precious animal — one that had been reserved, perhaps, for a purpose. The four specifications together describe a cow that is not merely fit for sacrifice but is *the* sacrifice — an animal that has been preserved and set apart. Classical commentators, notably Ibn Kathir, recount from the early tradition that the cow was ultimately found in the possession of a single man who refused to sell it except for its skin filled with gold — a statement that captures the perfection of the animal and the price the children of Israel paid for their resistance. The man was the nephew of the murdered man, and the payment for the cow, in some accounts, came from the inheritance in question. The details of the early reports vary and should be handled with care, but the pattern is consistent: an ordinary command was turned, by resistance, into an extraordinary difficulty.

"They said, 'Now you have come with the truth.'" The people recognize the truth of the description. This is an important moment: they were not unable to recognize the truth; they were able, and they said so. Their recognition, however, was not followed by immediate action. The phrase "yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly" — *wa mā kādū yaf'alūn* — literally "and they almost did not do it" — captures the final state: they did the command, but they did it as though they were barely doing it. The hesitation is the mark of a heart that obeys reluctantly, that submits after resistance, that performs the act without the spirit of submission.

The whole story is thus a portrait of the difference between obedience and compliance. Obedience is the movement of a heart that has accepted the command; compliance is the movement of a body that has run out of excuses. The Children of Israel complied because the specifications had left them no room; they did not obey because they wanted to. The verse ends with the story's conclusion — the slaughter of the cow — but the emphasis is not on the slaughter but on the *almost* — the hesitation, the reluctance.

The lesson for the believer is profound. There are two ways to do what Allah commands: with the whole heart, or with the last resistance. The believer is being trained away from the second. The person who obeys after five rounds of negotiation, who does the command only when there is no loophole left, has done the act but missed the submission. The same deed can be entirely different in value depending on the state of the heart that performs it. The cow story is a mirror: it asks the reader to examine the difference between the command being fulfilled and the heart being submitted.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the cost of delay in obedience. The people of the story could have sacrificed an ordinary cow at the beginning; by the end they were searching for a cow of extraordinary value. The same is true of every virtue postponed: the command that was easy becomes difficult, the act that was simple becomes complicated, and the believer pays the price of his own hesitation. The one who obeys early obeys cheaply; the one who obeys late pays with difficulty.

## Verse 72

Verse 72 says: *"˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed."*

In simple terms, the verse now returns to the original mystery: a man was killed, and the people disputed who had killed him. The cow had been sacrificed; the point of the sacrifice was now to be revealed. Allah had known the murderer from the beginning — the verse says "Allah revealed what you concealed" — and the whole drama of the cow was the means of bringing the hidden into the open.

The verse confirms the story's frame: the murder was the beginning, and the dispute was the middle, and the revelation was the end. The murderer had hidden the act; the people disputed in confusion and suspicion; and Allah, who knows what is concealed, designed the means by which the truth would become visible. The drama is one of the Qur'an's most striking examples of divine foreknowledge and divine intervention in human affairs. Allah does not merely know the hidden; He brings it forth, in His own time and through His own means.

"Why did Allah require a cow?" is the question the story implicitly answers. The command was not arbitrary; it was a lesson in the relationship between obedience and revelation. The people were required to do something — sacrifice a cow — and their obedience to the command was itself the condition for the revelation of the truth. The divine wisdom was hidden in the command, and the revelation of the wisdom came only after the obedience. The story is a lesson about the method of Allah: He often requires a step of obedience before the wisdom is apparent, and the step is itself the key to the wisdom. The person who obeys the command in faith discovers the purpose; the person who refuses the command never reaches the purpose.

The phrase "Allah revealed what you concealed" (*Allāhu mukhrijun mā kuntum taktumūn*) — "Allah is the One who will bring out what you were concealing" — carries a deeper theological truth. The murderer's concealment was not an escape; it was a postponement. The truth that was hidden was going to come out, because Allah is the One who brings out what is concealed. The believer is taught that concealment is not a real strategy: every hidden thing — every deed, every thought, every secret — has already been seen, and will eventually be revealed. The Qur'an says this in many places: the Day when all secrets are brought to light (86:9); He knows what you conceal and what you reveal (64:4); and the Day when all that is in the hearts is brought to light (100:10). The verse is a warning to the concealer and a comfort to the seeker of justice: nothing stays hidden from the One who knows.

In modern life, the verse is a profound statement about truth and justice. Every unresolved crime, every covered-up injustice, every secret wrongdoing has already been seen by Allah — and the verse says He will bring it out. The believer is not asked to despair when justice seems impossible; he is asked to trust the One who holds the key to every concealment. And the one who hides wrongdoing is warned: the concealment is not a fortress; it is a debt that will be collected. The story of the cow teaches that the truth can be concealed for a time, but it cannot be hidden from its revealer, and the One who reveals it is the Most Just.

## Verse 73

Verse 73 says: *"So We instructed, "Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow." This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand."*

In simple terms, the climax of the story: the dead man was struck with a piece of the cow, and he rose up, and the murderer was revealed. The verse states the point of the whole episode: this is how Allah brings the dead to life — and this is a sign shown to the people so that they may understand. The cow story is not only about a murder; it is about resurrection.

The sequence in the classical account: after the cow was slaughtered, the people were instructed to strike the body of the murdered man with a piece of the cow. When they did, the man rose, and he named the killer. The account of what happened when he rose, recorded in the tafsir (including the version reported by 'Ubaydah al-Salmani and the report that the murderer was the man's nephew and was therefore denied the inheritance), varies in detail, but the essential facts are consistent: the dead was revived, the killer was named, and the purpose of the command — the revelation of the concealed — was fulfilled.

The deeper message is in the phrase "This is how Allah brings the dead to life." The verse presents the resurrection of the murdered man as an *argument* for the resurrection of all humanity. It is one of the Qur'an's most powerful set-pieces: the God who revived a single body with a single piece of a cow — and who asked the people to take a step of obedience before the miracle occurred — is the God who will revive all bodies on the Day of Judgment. The same power shown in a small sign is the power that will be shown in the final event. The Qur'an returns to this logic repeatedly: the one who can create from nothing and revive one corpse can revive all; the first and the last are equally within His power.

The supplied translation renders the Arabic *hakadhā* — "this is how" — as "This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life," with "easily" in brackets as the translator's gloss. The gloss captures the force of the verse: the resurrection is effortless for Allah. The same point is made elsewhere: the One who originates creation is the One who repeats it, and the Qur'an says that repeating it is even easier for Him (30:27). The reader is asked to reason from the small miracle to the great one: if the resurrection of one dead man was as easy as striking him with a piece of a slaughtered cow, then the resurrection of all humanity is nothing for Allah.

The verse also teaches about the method of divine signs. Allah showed the Children of Israel the sign in order that they might understand — not merely witness. The sign was not a spectacle; it was a lesson, and the lesson was resurrection. The same pattern runs through the whole Qur'an: every miracle is a sign pointing beyond itself, and the miracle is wasted on the one who sees it without understanding. The verse's final phrase — "so that you may understand" — is the purpose clause: the sign is for understanding, and understanding is the beginning of faith.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the believer who has lost confidence in the power of Allah over the impossible. A dead man, a piece of a cow, a command — and life returns. The Qur'an is saying: the power that does this is not confined to that day; it is the power of the Lord of all, and it is present in every moment. The believer who faces what seems beyond repair — a dead relationship, a dead hope, a dead dream — is given the image of the story: Allah brings the dead to life, and He does it as easily as He does everything. The command, however, is to strike the body with the piece — to take the step of obedience — and then to wait for the One who revives.

## Verse 74

Verse 74 says: *"Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the resurrection is one of the most devastating sentences in the Qur'an. Even after a dead man was raised before their eyes, the people's hearts became hard. And not merely hard — the verse says their hearts became like stones, or even harder. Then it makes the comparison: some stones gush rivers, others split open and pour water, others crack and fall in awe of Allah. The stones respond to Allah more than the hearts do. And the verse ends: Allah is not unaware of what you do.

The comparison is a masterpiece. Stones, in the Qur'an's portrait of creation, are not the picture of hardness — they are the picture of responsiveness: some of them "gush rivers" (as in the miracle of Moses' rock), some "split and water flows out" (as in the earth that cracks for springs), and some "fall down in fear of Allah" (as in 2:74's own image, and in the verse that says "the mountains humble themselves in awe of the Qur'an," 59:21). The rocks, in other words, respond to the divine command with obedience, with flow, with falling. The Children of Israel's hearts, by contrast, received every sign — the sea, the manna, the dead revived — and responded with nothing. The heart that has become harder than a rock is a heart that has refused the natural response to the divine.

What makes a heart hard? The Qur'an gives the answer throughout this very passage: the repeated refusal of the evidence, the repeated rejection of the prophets, the repeated turning away after the covenant. The hardening is the cumulative effect of the refusal. Each time a person hears the truth and turns his back, a layer of hardness is added, and eventually the heart reaches the state described here: it sees a dead man rise, and it does not bend. The Prophet ﷺ described the same process in the hadith of the black spot on the heart (recorded by al-Tirmidhi and others): every sin leaves a mark, and repeated sin covers the heart until it can no longer perceive. The verse before us is the Qur'an's own description of that state.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you do." The final sentence is the warning: the hardness of the heart is not invisible. Allah sees the refusal, the resistance, and the state of the heart. And this is what makes the verse so serious: the hard heart is not a private psychological condition; it is a condition that has been observed, recorded, and will be answered.

The lesson for the believer is about the preservation of softness. The heart of faith is a heart that responds — to the recitation, to the sign, to the call. The believer is asked to protect that responsiveness: by remembering, by repentance, by humility, by the constant turning toward Allah. The person who lets himself become hardened through neglect — who reads the Qur'an without feeling, hears the warning without changing, sees the signs without responding — is walking the same path the verse describes. And the believer who feels his heart becoming hard has been given the cure: turn back, seek forgiveness, renew the remembrance, and let the softness of the heart be restored. The rocks of the verse are the pattern: the heart was created to respond, and the heart that no longer responds is the heart that has been covered by its own actions.

## Verse 75

Verse 75 says: *"Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?"*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now turns from the history of the Children of Israel to their present situation with the believers of Madinah. The question is direct: do you still expect them — the People of the Book — to be true to you? The reason is given: a group of them would hear the word of Allah, understand it, and then deliberately corrupt it. The verse is a warning against naivety in dealing with a people whose pattern of betrayal is documented in their own history.

The word *tuqāti'ūn* — "expect... to be true to you" — carries the sense of trusting and hoping for faithfulness. The believers had hoped, in the early period of Madinah, that the Jews and Christians would recognize the Prophet ﷺ and accept Islam, and the Qur'an describes the persistence of that hope. The verse asks: how can you expect faithfulness from a people whose own history shows that a group of them hears the word, understands it, and then deliberately corrupts it? The pattern is documented: in their own scripture, in the conduct of their scholars, in the history of the prophets they killed, in the distortion of the Torah. The Qur'an is saying: do not build your policy on an expectation that history has already contradicted.

The phrase "hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it" describes a specific method of corruption. The corruption is not accidental; it is *after* understanding (*ba'd mā 'aqalūhu*). The people heard the word, grasped its meaning, and then twisted it. This is the precise nature of the corruption: not ignorance but the deliberate distortion of known truth. The Qur'an has described the same pattern in the earlier verses — the changing of the words (2:59), the mixing of truth with falsehood (2:42), the concealment of what is known (2:146) — and here it names the psychological state: the corruption follows comprehension.

The verse also teaches the believers something about trust and prudence. In the early Madinan community, there were Muslims who continued to ask the People of the Book for religious knowledge, and who were willing to treat their claims as reliable. The Qur'an is not teaching hatred of the People of the Book — it repeatedly commands justice, kindness, and peaceful coexistence with those who do not fight the believers (60:8; 5:82-86, with its recognition of the Christians' humility). It is teaching *discernment*: know the history, verify the reports, and do not treat the claims of a community that has a documented pattern of distortion as though they were neutral. The believer is commanded to be just toward all people and wise in dealing with all people — the two are not in conflict.

The modern application is significant in an age of information and disinformation. The verse is a lesson in critical thinking grounded in knowledge of patterns: when a source has a history of distortion, verify its claims with independent evidence. It is also a lesson against naive trust and against the romanticism of the "honest opponent" — the person who assumes that because the other party is educated or religious, it is truthful. The believer's mindset is neither suspicion without evidence nor trust without evidence; it is verification based on knowledge. And the deepest lesson is about the believers themselves: the verse is a mirror for every community that hears the word and then corrupts it — for the religious leaders who twist the message for gain, and for every believer who knows the truth and chooses to conceal it.
