## Verse 126

Verse 126 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day." He answered, "As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!""*

In simple terms, the verse records the prayer of Abraham for the city: make this city secure and provide fruits to its people — those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day. The prayer is pointed: Abraham prays for the city, but he qualifies the people: the believers among them. And the divine answer extends the request, with a warning: as for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire.

The prayer of Abraham is the prayer of the builder of the city. He had left his family in a barren valley, and he asks Allah for what only Allah can give: security and provision. The city he prays for is the city of the Kaaba — the same city that the surah has described as the centre and the sanctuary. Abraham's prayer is concrete: peace — the absence of the fear that makes life impossible — and provision — the fruits of the earth in a land that produces nothing on its own. The commentators note that the prayer was answered: Mecca became the secure city, and the provision of the earth was brought to it — the caravans, the trade, and the fruits of every land.

The qualification in Abraham's prayer — "those among them who believe" — is the same principle the previous verse established: the covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers. Abraham the intercessor cannot include the disbelievers in his prayer, because the connection to Allah is not a matter of location but of faith. The city may be secure for the believers; the blessings of the city belong to the people of faith.

The divine answer contains the most important note of the verse: "As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire." Allah answers Abraham's prayer by extending the provision: even the disbeliever in the city will receive the fruits — the world is not denied to the rejecter. The Qur'an's message is that the worldly blessing is not the proof of the divine favour; the disbeliever receives the security and the fruits and uses them for a while, and the "a little while" is the measure of the worldly life. The final destination is then named with the words of the verse: "What an evil destination!"

In modern life, the verse corrects the most common error of prosperity theology: the belief that the blessing of this world proves the approval of Allah. The verse says the blessing is given to the disbeliever too — security and fruits, for a little while. The believer's security is the city he cannot lose: the Hereafter. And the verse is also the prayer of every founder and every parent: the prayer for the place, the security, the provision — qualified by the prayer that the people of the place be people of faith.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 127

Verse 127 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ "Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*"

In simple terms, the verse now gives one of the most moving scenes in the Qur'an: Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundation of the House together, and praying as they work: "Our Lord! Accept this from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing." The scene is not primarily architecture; it is worship. The building of the House of Allah is itself an act of worship, and the prayer that accompanies it is the worship's completion.

The classical commentators give the details of the scene. Ibn Kathir relates, from the account transmitted in the tafsir, that as the walls rose, Ishmael brought his father the stone — the Maqam — on which Abraham stood while laying the stones, and the two prayed together, father and son, at every stage: "Our Lord! Accept this from us." The verse's picture is of the partnership: the father raising the foundation and the son bringing the stones, and both making the same prayer. The House of Allah, in the Qur'an's record, was built by a father and a son in a state of submission, and it was built with the prayer for acceptance.

The prayer begins with "Our Lord! Accept this from us" — *taqabbal minnā*. The prophets, the ones closest to Allah, do not presume that the work is accepted; they pray for the acceptance. The verse is the Qur'an's teaching on the relation between the deed and the divine reception: nothing is accepted automatically; the acceptance is sought, and the seeker knows that the All-Hearing hears the prayer and the All-Knowing knows the intention. The finest building, done for Allah and left without the prayer, is incomplete; the humblest deed, done with the prayer, is complete.

The partnership is also a message: the worship that Allah built into the world is not the worship of the solitary worshipper. Abraham and Ishmael — the father of the Arabs and the father of the Jews, both prophets — raised the House together, and their prayer is the same prayer: "Our Lord! Accept this from us." The House is the inheritance of the two lines, and the lesson of the scene is that the religion of the House is the religion of both.

In modern life, the verse teaches the ethic of the work that is done for Allah: the work is not its own reward, and the doer prays for the acceptance. The verse is for every builder of a mosque, a school, a family, a life — the work is raised, and then the prayer: accept this from us. And the verse's final sentence teaches the believer to work in the company of others: the greatest works are the ones in which the hands of the many are raised, and the prayer of the many is the same prayer.

## Verse 128

Verse 128 says: *"Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse continues the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael: make us both fully submit to You; and from our descendants a nation that will submit to You; show us our rituals; and turn to us in grace. The prayer is the definition of Islam in the form of a petition: submission — of themselves and of the descendants — and the rites that the submission takes.

"Make us both fully submit to You" — the word for submission is the same root as *islām*: the complete surrender of the self to Allah. Abraham and Ishmael, already the builders of the House and the prophets of Allah, pray that the submission be made complete. The verse's language reveals the Qur'an's understanding of the religion: it is not a condition that is reached once; it is a state that is prayed for, a submission that deepens, a surrender that is requested. The prophets do not claim to have finished submitting; they ask that the submission be made.

"And from our descendants a nation that will submit to you" — the prayer extends beyond the self to the community. The builders of the House pray that the House's worshippers be a nation of submitters — not merely a nation associated with the House, but a nation whose relation to Allah is surrender. The verse is the Qur'an's answer to every claim of descent: the descendants of Abraham are those who submit — the nation of the prayer, not the line of the blood.

"Show us our rituals" — *manāsik* — the rites of the worship of the House: the hajj, the tawaf, the standing, the sacrifice. The prayer asks Allah to teach the rites — the rituals are not invented by the people; they are shown by the Lord. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of worship: the form of the worship comes from Allah, and the worshipper receives it. "And turn to us in grace" — the prayer of repentance: the builders ask that Allah turn to them with mercy, for the work is not done without the need of the turning.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the two dimensions of the religion: the inner submission and the outer rites. The person who has the rites without the submission has the form without the surrender; the person who claims the submission without the rites has the spirit without the form. Abraham and Ishmael prayed for both, and both are named in the prayer that is recited in the supplications of the pilgrims to this day: the submission of the self, the nation of the submitters, the rites shown, and the mercy that turns to the repentant.

## Verse 129

Verse 129 says: *"Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. Indeed, You ˹alone˺ are the Almighty, All-Wise."*"

In simple terms, the verse completes the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael with the greatest of their requests: raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. The prayer is the prayer for the final Messenger — and the classical commentators identify its fulfillment in the Prophet Muhammad with a confidence the whole Islamic tradition shares.

The prayer's vision of the messenger is precise. The messenger does four things: he recites the revelations — he delivers the message as it was revealed, aloud, so that the people hear it; he teaches the Book — the meaning, the understanding, the law; he teaches wisdom — the application, the discernment, the way the Book is lived; and he purifies them — the inward cleansing from the idols of the heart, the corruption of the soul, and the falsehood of the habits. The four verbs together are the mission of Islam: the recitation, the teaching, the wisdom, and the purification. The order matters: the message comes first, then the understanding, then the wisdom, and then the life that the whole process produces.

The fulfillment of the prayer is the theme of the surah's later verses: the Qur'an describes the Prophet in the same terms — sent to recite, teach, and purify (2:151) — and the tradition records a narration in which the Prophet said that he was the answer to the prayer of his father Abraham. The scholars note the significance: the messenger prayed for by the builders of the Kaaba came from among them — an Arab, from the descendants of Ishmael — and the verse's "from among them" is the answer to the objection of the People of the Book, who had claimed that the prophecy belonged only to their line. The prayer of the first builder of the House was for a messenger from the people of the House.

The verse ends with the divine names: "the Almighty, All-Wise." The prayer is answered by the One who is able to raise the messenger and wise in the choice of him. The "Almighty" is the capacity; the "All-Wise" is the selection: the mission is not only the power to send but the wisdom to choose the one who can bear it.

In modern life, the verse is the believer's prayer for the teachers, the guides, and the parents: raise from among them one who recites, teaches, and purifies. And it is the criterion by which every claimant to the mission of the messenger is measured: the one who recites the revelation, teaches the Book, teaches the wisdom, and purifies the people — this is the mission; anything less is a corruption of the prayer.

## Verse 130

Verse 130 says: *"And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous."*

In simple terms, the verse now turns from the prayer of Abraham to the verdict on his religion: who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool? The question is rhetorical, and its answer is the condemnation: the rejection of Abraham's way is the act of the fool — the one who has fooled himself. The verse then sets Abraham's standing: We chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous.

"The faith of Abraham" — *millat Ibrāhīm* — is the religion of the man who submitted, the religion that the Qur'an defines as the upright faith, free of polytheism. The verse's question is addressed to the claimants of Abraham: the Jews and the Christians who had each claimed him while rejecting the other, and who had now come to the Prophet offering their own paths. The Qur'an says: the one who rejects the faith of Abraham has rejected the religion itself — and the only person who does that is the fool, the one whose foolishness has turned against his own interest.

The verse's identification of the fool is pointed: *safiha nafsahu* — "he has fooled his own self." The fool is not the one who lacks intelligence; he is the one who has traded against himself. The rejection of Abraham's faith is not a failure of reasoning but a failure of self-interest: the person who rejects the religion of submission — the religion that his own ancestor lived and taught — has cheated himself, and the one he cheated is the only one who will pay.

"We certainly chose him in this life" — the divine choice of Abraham: the prophethood, the leadership, the friendship — the standing that the verse has already described. "And in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous" — the choice is not only the world; it is the hereafter. The verse is the Qur'an's answer to the dispute over Abraham: the two communities were arguing over his religion as though they owned him; the verse says he was chosen — not by them, but by Allah — and the proof of his standing is not the claim of his descendants but the choice of his Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the standard for every claim to a religious inheritance. The question of who belongs to Abraham, to Moses, to Jesus, to Muhammad — the question of which community owns the ancestor — is settled by the verse in one stroke: the faith of the ancestor is the submission to Allah, and the one who rejects it is the fool. The person who wants the ancestor must take the religion of the ancestor: not the name, but the surrender, the uprightness, and the refusal of the polytheism of his own age.
