## Verse 61

Verse 61 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when you said, "O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions." Moses scolded ˹them˺, "Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for." They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah's signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now records the complaint of the Children of Israel against the manna and quails. They said to Moses: we cannot endure the same meal every day. Ask your Lord to give us what the earth produces — herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions. Moses answered with sharp rebuke: do you exchange what is better for what is worse? Then he told them to go down to any village and they would find what they asked for. The verse then records the consequences: they were struck with disgrace and misery; they incurred Allah's displeasure for rejecting His signs and killing the prophets; and the whole outcome was the fair reward of their disobedience.

The food they asked for is striking for its ordinariness. Herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions — the staple vegetables of the earth — were the very things they had left in Egypt, where they were enslaved. The manna and quails, by contrast, were the miraculous, refined, effortless provision of Allah. The people were not asking for something better; they were asking for something *lower* — for the food of their captivity rather than the gift of their deliverance. Moses' question, "Do you exchange what is better for what is worse?" exposes the psychology of complaint: the people who already had the best were dissatisfied because they didn't have the familiar. The complaint was not about nutrition; it was about the imagination. They preferred the taste of what they knew, even at the cost of what was superior.

The deeper lesson is the one the Qur'an draws in the same verse: the exchange of "what is better for what is worse" is the fundamental pattern of human ingratitude and rebellion. The same exchange of truth for falsehood, guidance for desire, and the great for the small runs through this entire passage, and it is the essence of every sin that prefers the immediate, familiar, and pleasurable to the commanded, distant, and true. The person who trades his soul for a moment of satisfaction is doing what the Israelites did: exchanging what is better for what is worse.

The verse's final sentence is a compressed historical verdict: "They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah's signs and unjustly killing the prophets." The disgrace and misery are the consequences of their long history of rebellion — the wandering, the rejection of the prophets, the killing of those who were sent to them. The phrase "unjustly killing the prophets" refers to a pattern that runs through the history of the Children of Israel and is repeated elsewhere in the Qur'an (2:87, 2:91, 3:21, 3:112, 4:155). The killing of prophets is perhaps the most extreme example of the corruption of a people: they rejected the very messengers who came to save them. The verse ends with the phrase *dhālika bimā 'asaw wa kānū ya'tadūn* — "that is because they disobeyed and violated the limits." The punishment corresponds to the crime.

The modern application is powerful. So much of modern dissatisfaction is the same exchange: people reject what is better because it is unfamiliar and complain about what they have. The believer who has been given the gift of faith — and all the goods of the world — can find himself complaining for the same reason the Israelites complained: not because he lacks, but because he has lost the imagination of gratitude. Moses' question cuts through: are you exchanging what is better for what is worse? The verse is an invitation to measure what we have against what is truly good, and to thank the Giver rather than demand the familiar.

## Verse 62

Verse 62 says: *"Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, this verse is one of the most important statements of the Qur'an about salvation. It names four groups: the believers (the Muslims), the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians. And then it says that whoever among *any* of these truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good — will have his reward with his Lord, with no fear and no grief. The verse teaches that the criterion of salvation is not the label but the faith and the deeds.

The first thing to understand is the direction of the verse. This is *not* a verse that says every Jew, Christian, and Sabian will be saved regardless of what they believe. The verse says the opposite: it specifies the condition — *man āmana billāhi wal-yawm al-ākhir* — "whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day." A Jew who denies Allah is not saved by being a Jew. A Christian who denies the Last Day is not saved by being a Christian. A Muslim who does not do good is not saved by the label "believer." The groups are named not as categories of automatic salvation but as communities within which salvation is found by those who meet the conditions. And the conditions are explicitly stated: true belief in Allah, true belief in the Last Day, and the doing of good deeds.

The verse also contains a crucial historical qualification. The scholars (including Ibn Kathir, in his commentary which quotes the later verse 3:85) note that this verse speaks of the state of the followers of the earlier religions *before* the final message arrived — or, more precisely, it describes the criteria by which *any* person's salvation is judged: sincere monotheism, belief in accountability, and righteous deeds. After the coming of the final Prophet ﷺ, the standard is the same but the mode of reception is the message of Muhammad ﷺ. Ibn Kathir explicitly records that after this verse was revealed, Allah revealed the qualification: "Whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will never be accepted from him, and he will be one of the losers in the Hereafter" (3:85). This is not a contradiction: the Qur'an teaches that faith in Allah and the Last Day is the core, but that the *form* of that faith, in the age of the final Prophet, is Islam — the complete submission to the message of Muhammad ﷺ. The verse before us is, in its primary context, an acknowledgment of the People of the Book's genuine monotheism and a refutation of anyone who claimed that they were automatically damned for their lineage. It teaches that the judgment of individuals belongs to Allah, based on faith and deeds, not on the group label.

"Sabians" (*al-Ṣābi'ūn*) is the third group, and the identity of the Sabians is one of the many questions in the Qur'an. The classical commentators — al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir — disagree about their identity. Ibn Kathir discusses several views: some said they were a sect among the People of the Book who read the Psalms; some said they were star-worshippers; others said they were a group between the Magians and the Jews who had no specific revealed religion and lived according to their natural instinct. Mujahid's statement, reported by Ibn Kathir, is that they are neither Jews, nor Christians, nor Magians, nor polytheists. Modern scholarship commonly identifies them with the Mandaeans, a monotheistic group in Iraq associated with the prophet Yahya (John the Baptist) and the practice of baptism; other theories identify them with the star-worshippers of Harran. The truth is that the exact identity of the Sabians is uncertain, and an honest commentary should say so. What the Qur'an makes certain is their inclusion in the verse: they are a distinct religious community, and whoever among them truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good is subject to the same principle of divine justice.

The conclusion of the verse — "there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" — is the standard Qur'anic language for the people of genuine faith: no fear of the future (the Judgment), no grief over the past (the losses of this world), because they stand with the Lord who holds both. The verse is a message of hope — and a rebuke to both exclusive claims and careless assumptions: no one is saved by an identity, and no sincere truth-seeker is judged merely by the label of his community. The final judgment is Allah's, and the criteria are faith and good deeds.

## Verse 63

Verse 63 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, "Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now recounts one of the most dramatic covenant scenes in the history of the Children of Israel: the mountain was raised above them, and they were commanded to hold firmly to the Scripture that had been given to them. The scene combines fear, urgency, and obligation: the covenant was not taken verbally but visibly, with a mountain suspended over them as a witness and a warning.

The event is described in the Qur'an in several places (2:63, 2:93, 4:154, 7:171). In the biblical account, the covenant at Sinai is a grand announcement; in the Qur'anic retelling, the emphasis is different: the mountain was uprooted and suspended over the people like a canopy, and they were told: hold firmly to what We have given you, and remember what is in it. The classical commentators explain that the people had initially hesitated to accept the responsibilities of the Torah — they had seen the punishment of other nations, and the burden of the law seemed too heavy. Allah then lifted the mountain over their heads like a shadow: either accept the covenant, or the mountain will fall upon you. This was a scene of compulsion in the sense of divine urgency: the commandment was not a suggestion, and the people were to understand that their response to the revelation was not optional.

The command has two parts. First, "Hold firmly to what We have given you" — *khudhū mā ātaynakum bi-quwwah* — "take what We have given you with strength." The phrase "with strength" (*bi-quwwah*) is significant. It does not mean with physical force; it means with resolve, with seriousness, with commitment, with the whole heart and the whole will. The Qur'an uses the same phrase for the Quranic message given to the Muslims: "Take what We have given you with strength" (7:145, 7:171). The revelation is not to be received half-heartedly. It is to be grasped, mastered, and lived. The second part is "and remember what is in it" — *wa udhkurū mā fīhi* — which sums up the method: constant remembrance, constant recitation, constant application. The Scripture is not a book to be owned but a message to be lived.

The purpose is then named: *la'allakum tattaqūn* — "so perhaps you may become mindful." The Torah was taken with strength so that the people would develop taqwa — awareness of Allah, reverence, and obedience. This is the same purpose that is attached to the Qur'an and the same purpose attached to every revelation: not merely knowledge but transformation, not merely law but God-consciousness. The covenant at the mountain was not a legal paperwork; it was a formation project.

The scene also teaches a great lesson about the nature of the divine covenant. The mountain suspended over the people shows that the relationship between Allah and humanity is not a game. The obligations of revelation are real, and the consequences are real. The believer is called to take the message with strength, to remember it, and to let it produce the fear of Allah. The one who takes the Scripture loosely, who reads without living, who owns the Book without obeying it, has not kept the covenant in the way the mountain scene demands. In modern terms, the verse speaks to the seriousness of the religious life: the believer is not a consumer of spiritual comfort but a covenant-holder who has accepted a commitment that carries both weight and consequence.

## Verse 64

Verse 64 says: *"Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the covenant records the failure. After the mountain had been raised above them and they had given the covenant, they turned away. The Qur'an does not soften the betrayal: they turned away *afterwards* — after the covenant, after the command, after the mountain. And then the mercy: had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy upon them, they would have been among the losers.

The sequence is the same pattern that runs through the whole section: covenant, failure, mercy. The phrase *thumma tawallaytum* — "then you turned away" — is precise. It is not "you fell," or "you failed," or "you stumbled." The *tawallā* verb carries the sense of turning the back, going away, refusing. It is the same word used for people who turn away from the message and the warning. The Children of Israel did not merely fail to live up to the covenant; they *turned away* from it. This is a more serious description than weakness; it describes a choice to leave.

The scholars note that the verse refers to the repeated pattern of the Children of Israel's history: they accepted the covenant, then turned away — through the calf, through the repeated rebellions, through the rejection of prophets, through the corruption of the scripture. The whole history is the history of turning away. And the mercy follows: *lawlā fadl Allāhi 'alaykum wa rahmatuhu* — "had it not been for Allah's grace upon you and His mercy." The *fadl* (grace) and *rahmah* (mercy) are the two divine attributes that held them back from the final consequence: total loss. They did not dishonour Allah; Allah honoured them with patience, with delay, with repeated forgiveness, with the continuation of the prophetic line.

The verse contains a profound lesson about the difference between Allah's justice and Allah's mercy. On the strict terms of the covenant, the Children of Israel turned away, and the just consequence would have been the loss they had earned. What saved them was not their own merit, which was nonexistent, but the grace and mercy of Allah — the divine attributes that took the initiative. The same is true of every believer: nobody's salvation is earned; even the best of deeds are themselves gifts, and it is the mercy of Allah that completes what the deed begins. The believer's confidence is not in his own performance but in the infinite mercy of the One he addresses in every prayer and every prostration.

The modern application of this verse is a warning against the "second turn." A person who makes a covenant with Allah — the shahada, the commitment to prayer, the promise to leave a sin — and then turns away after the commitment is in the exact position of the Children of Israel. The verse's mercy, however, is the reason for hope: turning away is not the end, because Allah's grace and mercy are not exhausted. The believer who has broken his covenant, who has turned away from what he promised, is being told: the One who held the Children of Israel from total loss is the same One who can hold you. The verse is both a warning and a door.

## Verse 65

Verse 65 says: *"You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, "Be disgraced apes!""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now gives the most dramatic example of the Children of Israel's rebellion and its consequence: the Sabbath-breakers. The verse says: you already know about those among you who violated the Sabbath. We said to them, "Be apes, disgraced and rejected." The command was executed: the people who broke the Sabbath were transformed into apes.

The story is told in detail in the Qur'an itself, in Surah Al-A'raf, which recalls the city that was by the sea whose people transgressed the Sabbath, when their fish came to them openly on their Sabbath day but did not come on the day they had no Sabbath, and Allah made them a trial because of their rebellion (7:163). The town was a coastal settlement of the Children of Israel — classical commentators identify it as the town of Aylah (Eilat), on the shore of the Red Sea. The people had been commanded to observe the Sabbath as a day of rest and prohibition against work, including fishing. The fish, in a deliberate test, would crowd the waters on Saturday and disappear on other days. The people then resorted to a legalistic trick: they placed nets, ropes, and artificial pools before the Sabbath, so that the fish would enter on Saturday and be trapped; then they collected the fish on Sunday, claiming they had not fished on the Sabbath. The Qur'an describes it plainly: they used deceitful means to circumvent the command of Allah. The test itself was designed by Allah to expose the heart: would they obey the spirit of the Sabbath, or would they find a way around it?

The consequence is stated with terrible simplicity: "Be disgraced apes." Classical commentators (Ibn Abbas via al-'Awfi; Qatadah; al-Dahhak) explain that Allah changed their bodies into monkeys — an actual physical transformation, not merely a moral comparison. The young among them became monkeys; some accounts say the old became swine; the reports vary in detail. They lived only three days in that state and then perished, without progeny — which is why, as Ibn Kathir quotes Ibn Abbas, the apes and swine that exist now are not the descendants of those transformed; they are a different creation. The transformation was a fitting punishment: the people who had used cleverness to make sin look lawful were turned into the animal that most resembles humans in form but lacks the moral capacity that distinguishes humanity. The punishment matched the crime.

Why was the punishment so severe? Because the Sabbath was not a trivial ritual; it was the test of obedience itself. The people were not being asked to do something difficult; they were being asked to abstain from one activity on one day, and they could not even do that without finding a loophole. The lesson is that the test of faith is often in the small, specific, seemingly arbitrary command. A person who cannot obey the simple command will not obey the great ones. And the legalistic avoidance of the command is worse than open violation, because it pretends to obey while actually disobeying — it is the same sin described in verse 42 as mixing truth with falsehood.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the subtlety of self-deception in religion. The Sabbath-breakers did not say "we refuse to obey Allah"; they said "we will find a way to obey the letter while violating the spirit." This is the religious psychology of the loophole, and it is universal: the person who finds a way to indulge the sin while keeping the appearance of compliance has made his intellect a tool of his desires. The Qur'anic answer is that Allah is not deceived by loopholes, and that a religion of legalistic tricks is a religion of corruption. The verse warns the believer against treating divine commands as puzzles to be solved in one's favour rather than boundaries to be honoured.
