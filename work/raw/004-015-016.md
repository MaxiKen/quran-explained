<<<VERSE 4:15>>>
The verses that protected orphans and widows now turn to the women of the community themselves, and to the gravest charge that can be laid against one of them: "˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves. If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them". The ruling of this verse belonged to the first period of Islam and was later replaced. What remains permanently is its lesson about proof, protection and the veiling of sins.

### Phrase by phrase

**"˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse"** speaks of zinā, unlawful intercourse. One reading limits the verse to free, married women, another includes the unmarried as well. The case goes before the executive authority, and it is that authority, not a mob of relatives, which acts on it.

**"call four witnesses from among yourselves"** sets the bar of proof. The witnesses must be four, they must be men, and the testimony of women is not valid in this matter. Islam took a deliberately strict attitude because the guilt is grave: it violates honour and modesty and brings a bad name on a whole family. Testimony from fewer than four renders the evidence invalid, and the complainant and witnesses may all be charged as liars, with the ḥadd of qadhf, the punishment for false accusation, falling on them for falsely accusing a Muslim. Sūrat an-Nūr says plainly that those who cannot produce four witnesses are liars (24:2). The severity is a shield. It stands so that a husband, a mother-in-law, a co-wife or a sister cannot level accusations out of personal spite, and no ill-wisher gets a channel for private hostility. Some revered elders gave another reason for the number four: the case involves two individuals, the man and the woman, so one case carries the purview of two, and as each requires two witnesses, four are necessary.

**"If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes"** was the first ruling. At the beginning of Islam the woman whose guilt was proven by sufficient evidence was confined to her house, kept from leaving, prevented from mixing with people, and that until death. No release was foreseen except one: **"until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them"**. The way referred to is the abrogation that came later. Ibn ʿAbbās said that confinement was the early ruling, and that it stood until Sūrat an-Nūr came down and replaced it with flogging for fornication and stoning for adultery. The same explanation is reported from ʿIkrimah and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, from al-Ḥasan and ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, from Abū Ṣāliḥ and Zayd ibn Aslam, from Qatādah and ad-Daḥḥāk, and the matter is agreed upon. This hadith of abrogation is the settled understanding of the verse. Interpreting the word "way" itself, Ibn ʿAbbās said it means stoning for the married and lashing for the unmarried, which al-Bukhārī records in the Book of Tafsīr of his Ṣaḥīḥ.

### The way that was ordained

The promised way came from the Prophet's ﷺ own mouth. Imam Aḥmad recorded from ʿUbādah ibn aṣ-Ṣāmit that when revelation descended on the Messenger of Allah ﷺ it would weigh on him and his face would show the strain. One day revelation came down, and when he was relieved of its strain he said:

> Take from me: Allah has made some other way for them. The married with the married, the unmarried with the unmarried. The married gets a hundred lashes and stoning to death, while the unmarried gets a hundred lashes then banishment for a year.

Muslim and the collectors of the Sunan recorded from ʿUbādah ibn aṣ-Ṣāmit the same instruction, opening with the words: "Take from me, take from me. Allah has made some other way for them." At-Tirmidhī graded the hadith ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ.

The way was applied. The Prophet ﷺ pronounced the ḥadd of zinā on Māʿiz ibn Mālik and on a woman of the tribe of Azd. Both were married, and both were stoned. A couple among the Jews of Madinah were also stoned, on a judgement pronounced by the authority of an injunction of the Torah. For the unmarried, the Qur'an itself gives the rule in Sūrat an-Nūr: the fornicator, woman or man, receives a hundred lashes (24:2). As for stoning, a verse of the Qur'an was revealed to cover it whose recitation was later abrogated while the injunction itself remained operative. ʿUmar said:

> Surely, Allah sent Muḥammad ﷺ with the truth and sent down to him the Book. In what Allah revealed there was the verse of rajm. The Holy Prophet ﷺ stoned and we stoned after him, and the revealed injunction of rajm stands proved against everyone who commits zinā despite being married, whether man or woman.

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record it, as cited in the Mishkāt.

### The veil over sins

Read inwardly, the four witnesses are a mercy. To lower the veil of generosity over the sins of servants, Allah made the allowance of an excessive number of witnesses for proving lewdness. Following the witnessing procedure of the Law makes establishing proof nearly impossible. When Māʿiz came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: "O Messenger of God, may the blessings of God be upon you, I have committed fornication," he answered: maybe you only kissed. On another occasion he told them to smell the man's breath. In this is the strongest proof of how the Law prefers to keep the veil lowered over ugly acts.

### In short

- The charge of zinā against a woman requires four male witnesses of good standing, and any shortfall turns the accusation back on the accusers as the sin of qadhf.
- The house-confinement of this verse was the first-period ruling, and it was abrogated by the way Allah ordained: a hundred lashes and a year's banishment for the unmarried, a hundred lashes and stoning for the married.
- The cases of Māʿiz ibn Mālik and the woman of Azd applied the ruling, and ʿUmar testified that the injunction of stoning stands by revelation even though its recitation was abrogated.
- Read inwardly, the near-impossibility of proof is deliberate: the Law would rather veil a sin than broadcast it.

<<<VERSE 4:16>>>
The companion verse moves from the woman accused to the pair who commit the act: "And the two among you who commit this sin—discipline them. If they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful". Like the verse before it, its ruling was later replaced by the fixed punishments. What it adds is the frame around punishment: discipline, then relief, then the doors of repentance.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And the two among you who commit this sin"** uses a dual masculine pronoun, and the grammar opened a real question. One reading takes the two as an unmarried man and woman guilty of adultery, since masculine expressions in general usage include the feminine, and this is the view of al-Shāfī. Mujāhid said the verse was revealed about the case of two men who do it, the act of the people of Lūṭ, and judging by the dual masculine pronoun this is the more obvious sense: the two suffer the same punishment, both are called to repent, both are to be left alone afterward, all of which fits two men, given that for women the verse before has stipulated confinement. Qāḍī Thanāʾullāh Panīpatī is among those who hold the two-males reading. Both readings remain possible.

**"discipline them"** names the first-period penalty, and no fixed method is prescribed. Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said the punishment includes cursing and shaming them and beating them with sandals, verbal reproach together with physical disgrace. The statement is illustrative rather than exhaustive. The whole matter was left to the discretion of the authorities, and the ruling stood only until Allah abrogated it with flogging or stoning. If the sin meant is the act of the people of Lūṭ, the abrogation applies there too in the view of al-Shāfī, though he held that the one who is the object of the act is flogged and banished, not stoned, even if he is married.

**"If they repent and mend their ways"** comes after the punishment in the order of the verse, and the letter fa', "then", carries the sequence. So the repentance does not absolve them of the punishment. What it ends is the disgrace afterward. **"relieve them"** means: stop reproaching them. Whoever truly repents is like the one who has no sin. Where repentance is absent, reproach may continue even after punishment.

**"Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful"** is the seal. The two Ṣaḥīḥs record the Prophet's ﷺ words: "When the slave-girl of one of you commits illegal sexual intercourse, let him flog her and not chastise her afterwards," because the lashes she receives erase the sin she committed.

### The act of the people of Lūṭ

If the verse does concern two men, the material gathered around it belongs here. The prohibition is of the severest order. Abū Hurairah narrates that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> Allah has cursed seven types of His creatures from above the seven heavens. He has cursed one of them thrice, and cursed the others once, which is enough for them. Of the one cursed thrice He has said: Cursed is he who did what the people of Lūṭ did. Cursed is he who did what the people of Lūṭ did. Cursed is he who did what the people of Lūṭ did.

It is recorded in the At-Targhīb wat-Tarhīb. Abū Hurairah also reports the Prophet ﷺ saying that four types of people begin their day under the wrath of Allah and reach their evening under His rage: men who imitate women, women who imitate men, the one who goes unto animals, and the one who goes unto men. Ibn ʿAbbās narrates the ruling: if you find someone doing what the people of Lūṭ did, kill both the doer and the one to whom it is done, and the collectors of the Sunan record the same from him in the Prophet's ﷺ words.

The first generation treated it as a crime with no precedent except one. The event is related on the authority of Muḥammad ibn al-Munkadir: Khālid ibn al-Walīd wrote to Abū Bakr that in a certain Arabian locality there was a man with whom they were doing what is done with women. Abū Bakr called a meeting of the Companions, ʿAlī among them, who said that no human group had committed such a sin except one, and all knew how Allah had dealt with that people. He proposed that the man be condemned to the fire. The Companions concurred, and Abū Bakr decreed it. Four caliphs after him, ʿAlī, ʿAbdullāh ibn az-Zubayr and Hishām ibn ʿAbdul-Malik, condemned the guilty to fire. Muslim jurists differ on the fixed punishment, the details standing in the books of fiqh, but the punishments they report are of the utmost severity: burning, bashing under a felled wall, throwing down from a height, stoning, and beheading with the sword.

The reference is to the people to whom Lūṭ (Lot) was sent as prophet. Beyond their disbelief and their ascribing of partners to Allah, they were addicted to this unnatural practice, and when his teaching had no effect on them, the angels, as commanded by Allah, lifted their habitations off the ground, turned them upside down, and threw the whole thing back down.

### The same prohibition within marriage

Other narrations carry warnings against the unnatural act with women. Ibn ʿAbbās narrates that the Prophet ﷺ said that Allah does not look with mercy on the person who commits anal intercourse with a man or a woman. Khuzaimah ibn Thābit reports that the Prophet ﷺ said: Allah does not get embarrassed by stating the truth, said three times, and then: do not go unto women anally. Abū Hurairah narrates: cursed is he who goes unto women anally, recorded in the At-Targhīb wat-Tarhīb. He also narrates that whoever approaches a woman in her menses or in her anus, or consults a soothsayer and believes his talk of the unseen, has disbelieved in what was revealed to Muḥammad ﷺ.

### The spirit of the two verses

The deeper law under both verses is the rule of law itself. A man or woman who commits an act considered a sin under Islamic tenets is dealt with under rules, not arbitrarily. It is unjust to name anyone a criminal before the legal conditions are satisfied, and even a proven offender may not be dealt with by summary violence. Punishment exists to carry justice, and justice is never carried by tyranny. Where the sinner pleads guilty and reforms, a sympathetic and forgiving attitude becomes essential. No one should be measured by his past. When God Himself pardons the sincerely repentant and turns in benevolence to those who reform, mere mortals hold no right to taunt former delinquents, and the taunter may yet prove a transgressor himself. Even the command to punish is, read inwardly, the kindest form of counsel: it warns the servant off the act so that he stays on his guard and never becomes deserving of the greater punishment.

### In short

- The pair in the verse are read either as a fornicating man and woman or as two males, the act of the people of Lūṭ, and both readings are argued from the dual masculine pronoun.
- The discipline commanded, reproach, shaming and beating with sandals, was left to the authorities and later abrogated by the fixed punishments of flogging and stoning.
- Repentance after punishment ends the disgrace, not the penalty, and the one who truly repents is as one who has no sin. The flogged slave-girl is not to be chastised afterward.
- If the verse concerns the act of the people of Lūṭ, the prohibition around it is of the utmost severity, from the thrice-repeated curse to the burning decreed by Abū Bakr, and it covers the unnatural act within marriage as well.
- Under all readings, the standing law is proportion and process: no punishment without legal proof, and no taunting of the reformed.
