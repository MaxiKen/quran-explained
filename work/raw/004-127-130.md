<<<VERSE 4:127>>>
The question about women: "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding women. Say, “It is Allah Who instructs you regarding them. Instruction has ˹already˺ been revealed in the Book concerning the orphan women you deprive of their due rights but still wish to marry, also helpless children, as well as standing up for orphans’ rights”" (4:127). The verse answers a question the people put to the Prophet ﷺ directly, and behind the question stood the practices of the age of ignorance.

### The question and its occasion

At the beginning of this sūrah, injunctions relating to orphans and women were given, with stress on fulfilling their rights. In the days of jāhiliyyah some people refused women and children any part of the inheritance, others unlawfully ate up what they received, and still others married the women while withholding the full dower. When no abrogation came of the new rules, some who had waited for one decided to go to the Prophet ﷺ and ask him outright. This question, in the reports of Ibn Jarīr and Ibn al-Mundhir, was the very reason for the revelation of the verse, and the verses that followed carried additional rulings about women. One record names the questioner ʿUyaynah, and the report reaches ʿĀʾishah through Ibn Wahb, from Yūnus, from Ibn Shihāb, from ʿUrwah, with Muslim carrying it from Ḥarmalah from Ibn Wahb.

### Phrase by phrase

**"concerning the orphan women you deprive of their due rights but still wish to marry"** opens the answer, and ʿĀʾishah explained the case. The verse concerns the man taking care of a female orphan, her caretaker and inheritor, whose money is joined with his to the point that she shares with him even the branch of a date he owns. For the material gain he would rather marry her himself, and he cannot bear that another man should marry her and share in the money through her. So he refuses to let her marry anyone, and the verse came down. Al-Bukhārī recorded this, and Muslim also. She added that the words also cover the opposite desire of the guardian, who avoids marrying the orphan under his supervision when she lacks property or beauty. What was forbidden to the guardians was marrying the orphan girls of property and beauty without being just to them, as they generally refrained from marrying the plain and the poor. The recited instruction of the Book is the earlier word of this sūrah: "If you fear that you shall not be able to deal justly with the orphan girls, then marry other women of your choice" (4:3). The basis stands in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported from Ibn ʿAbbās the old custom. The guardian of a fatherless girl would throw his rope over her as a mark of possession, and once marked, she was unmarriageable. A beautiful one he married and took control of her wealth. A plain one he held back from every suitor until she died, and then he inherited her money. Allah prohibited and outlawed the practice. The reading of the phrase runs two ways, and both expose a wrong. Read as and they tend to marry them, it names those who, attracted by the beauty of the orphan girls, married them without giving them the due rights of a wife. Read as and you avoid marrying them, it names those who would not marry them for their unattractive features yet let them marry no one else, wanting the wealth to stay in their hands. One gloss names the wronged ones as the daughters of Umm Kuhhah. The command that follows covers both: when a caretaker wishes to marry the orphan in his care, he must give her the suitable dowry that other women of her status receive, and if he will not, let him marry other women, for Allah has made the matter easy. And when he does not desire her, he is forbidden to prevent her from marrying another man out of fear that the husband would share in the money.

**"also helpless children"** recalls the second practice of jāhiliyyah. Young children and females were denied a share of inheritance. The verse prohibits the practice and fixes the share of each, "To the male, a portion equal to that of two females" (4:11), with no regard to youth or age, as Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and others stated.

**"as well as standing up for orphans' rights"** closes the charge with the golden rule, in the words of Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: just as you would want to marry her and have her for yourself when she is beautiful and wealthy, so marry her and have her for yourself when she is neither wealthy nor beautiful.

**"And whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah"** encourages the good deeds and the fulfilment of the commandments. Allah is knowledgeable of all of it, and He rewards it in the best and most perfect manner.

The occasion teaches the condition of all law. The questions people brought to the Prophet ﷺ about the sharīʿah's social rules always drew answers weighted toward goodness, justice, fraternity and piety. A law serves its purpose only when the one who must enforce it is God-fearing and truly desirous of justice. Social reform takes hold only when the evil-doer realises he is ultimately accountable to Allah, who cannot be fooled even if people are. The doer of good carries on whether appreciated or not, in the belief that Allah is watching and will reward, deterred from injustice by the dread of the Fire, and given courage by the hope of Paradise to endure the losses of a truthful life.

The inward reading hears the threat inside the mercy. Allah prohibited coveting what leads to harm or wrong against oppressed women and orphans, explained that Allah is their avenger, and showed that whoever is heedful of Allah regarding them loses nothing on His account but finds a beautiful recompense, while whoever abuses them suffers a painful tribulation.

### In short

- The question about women's rights followed the waiting of those who expected the inheritance rules to be abrogated.
- The guardian who trapped the orphan's wealth, by marrying her or by preventing every other marriage, is the target of the instruction, and the rope of jāhiliyyah is cut.
- Children and females receive the fixed shares, and the standing rule is to want for the plain orphan what one would want for the beautiful one.
- Allah knows the good done to the weak, repays it perfectly, and avenges their abuse.

<<<VERSE 4:128>>>
The strained marriage: "If a woman fears indifference or neglect from her husband, there is no blame on either of them if they seek ˹fair˺ settlement, which is best" (4:128). The verse opens the Qur'ān's instruction for the hard part of married life, the part every couple meets at one stage or another.

### The situation and the occasions

Mutual displeasure and tension, left unchecked, bring the couple severe problems. At times the evil travels outward to families and tribes, into confrontation and even killing. The Qur'ān introduces a system of family life that keeps the whole range of feelings in view. Followed, it makes a home a paradise, love replacing whatever bitterness there was. Should separation become inevitable, the parting is smooth and painless, leaving no fallout of enmity or hurt. Verse 128 concerns the strain that comes for reasons beyond the couple's control. The husband lacks amorous feeling for the wife, and she has no control over the means that would change his heart. She may be plain or aged while he is handsome, and neither can be blamed for what each is.

The occasions show the verse at work. Abū Dāwūd aṭ-Ṭayālisī recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that Ṣawdah feared the Messenger of Allah ﷺ might divorce her, and said: do not divorce me, give my day to ʿĀʾishah. He did, and Allah sent down the verse. Ibn ʿAbbās added that whatever legal agreement the spouses mutually agree to is allowed, and at-Tirmidhī recorded it, calling it ḥasan gharīb. In the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, ʿĀʾishah tells of Ṣawdah bint Zamʿah growing old, forfeiting her day to ʿĀʾishah, while the Prophet ﷺ spent Ṣawdah's night with ʿĀʾishah. By keeping her among his wives, his ummah received the pattern of this kind of settlement, for what he kept, others may keep. Al-Bukhārī also recorded from ʿĀʾishah her reading of the verse over the case of a man married to an old woman he no longer desires and seeks to divorce. Her answer is to say that she forfeits her right on him, and the verse was revealed. The records add more cases, one through ʿAbd ar-Raḥīm ibn Sulaymān, from Hishām, from ʿUrwah, from ʿĀʾishah. One is the woman who bears no children while the husband seeks divorce. Another is the woman with friends and children she cannot leave, who offers her husband the exchange of keeping her while she forgoes every right due to her. Muslim carries the report from Abū Kurayb from Abū Usāmah. And Ibn al-Musayyab related the case of the daughter of Muḥammad ibn Maslamah, married to Rāfiʿ ibn Khadīj, who wanted to divorce her over something he disliked in her, arrogance or the like. The report came through Ibn al-Rabīʿ, from al-Shāfiʿī, from Ibn ʿUyaynah, from az-Zuhrī. Her answer was to tell him not to divorce her, leaving him free to apportion whatever he liked for her, and Allah revealed the verse. One school gloss even names the pair, ʿUmayrah the wife and Asʿad ibn ar-Rabīʿ the husband, with his young favourite beside the old wife.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If a woman fears indifference or neglect from her husband"** describes the state precisely. Indifference means his looking down on her, his refraining from intimacy, his keeping the maintenance short. The cause is his aversion and his eye set on someone more beautiful. Neglect means his turning from her altogether, no word to her, no sitting with her.

**"there is no blame on either of them if they seek ˺fair˺ settlement"** allows the concession. The wife may forfeit all or part of her rights, provisions, clothing, dwelling and so forth, and the husband may accept. The wording of no sin covers a deal that on the surface looks like a bribe, with the husband tempted by forgiven dower and claims while the bond stays intact. The statement of the Qur'ān makes clear that this is not bribery. It is expediency, wise consideration under complex circumstances, the parties surrendering initial claims and agreeing to a moderated mean, and it is permissible. The words between them carry their own hint, as al-Mazharī notes. The compromise is best made by the two alone. A third person may make the very compromise impossible, and even where it is reached, the weaknesses of the couple stand exposed before an outsider without need. Staying safe from that is expedient for both.

**"which is best"** ranks the settlement above divorce, and the wording is general. It certainly covers the husband-wife dispute of the context. It covers as well every kind of family difference and every mutual dispute, altercation and litigation of worldly life, for the words of the Qur'ān are general: the compromise is better. The parties fare better when they avoid stubbornness over the fulfilment of demands in full, forgo something from each side, and meet on middle ground. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> Every compromise is permissible between Muslims, except a compromise in which something unlawful has been made lawful or something lawful has been made unlawful. And Muslims must abide by accepted conditions, except a condition in which something lawful has been made unlawful.

It is not permissible, for example, to compromise with one's wife on the condition that he also marry her sister, since Islamic law prohibits combining two sisters in marriage. Nor may the condition be that he stop fulfilling the rights of the other wife, which turns a lawful thing unlawful. From the generality of the hadith, Imām Abū Ḥanīfah deduced that all kinds of compromise are permissible. One kind is made with an avowal, as when the defendant confesses a debt and the plaintiff surrenders part or takes something in lieu. Another comes through the defendant's silence, a wish to settle whatever the truth of the claim. A third comes despite a disclaimer, part being paid to end the dispute. On the last two kinds some jurists differ. One boundary matters in the marriage settlement itself. If the woman surrenders rights whose fulfilment was already incumbent, such as a dower due before the compromise, the right lapses wholly or partly and her claim is gone. But rights whose fulfilment falls due only in the future, such as future expenses or the right to privacy, do not lapse forever. She may declare at any time that she no longer forgoes them, and the husband then has the choice of releasing her, as al-Mazharī and others rule.

**"Humans are ever inclined to selfishness"** explains why the concession works. The word names extreme niggardliness, and souls are ever in its presence. The woman's greed lies in her desire to protect the future of her children, fearing that release would ruin it or that life elsewhere would prove more bitter. The husband is tempted by what she does, the dower forgiven in whole or part, the other claims stopped, and a wife kept in the bargain, so that getting along comes to seem easy. A woman would scarcely allow another to share her husband. A man would scarcely let her enjoy him if he fell in love with another. Between these two selfishnesses the compromise finds its room.

**"But if you are gracious and mindful of Allah"** opens the third way. The husband had the legal choice of release on grounds of incompatibility, and the licence of compromise where she offers to surrender claims. Beyond both stands the conduct of grace: keeping the fear of Allah in the heart, being benign and accommodating despite the incompatibility, and fulfilling her rights as due. The text stops at the words that Allah is All-Aware of what you do, without spelling out the return. The hint is that the reward is far more than one can ever imagine.

### The summary of the law

The whole map runs as follows. When the husband feels estranged and his rights unfulfilled, he should first correct what lies within the wife's range, by coldness, hard advice, or in the last resort soft disciplining, as the early verses of this sūrah have set out. If correction fails, or the fault is not in her control at all, the law gives him divorce, a release conducted decently and without altercation. But if he chooses to carry on under the same conditions, ignoring his own rights and fulfilling hers in full, that conduct is highly meritorious and deserving of a great reward. The reverse case mirrors it. Where the husband fails the wife's rights and she wishes release, the course is open if he is willing, and if he is not, she may reclaim her freedom through the Islamic court, or she may brave his cold and crooked ways with patience, surrendering her claims while fulfilling his rights, which is as highly meritorious in her as in him. The guideline in essence: the Qur'ān gives both parties the legal right to remove difficulty and receive their due, while prompting both toward high morals and patience with the loss of rights, so that the relationship is severed only at the farthest limit of possibility, the general command standing as it was revealed: "retain in an honourable manner or part amicably" (2:229).

### The inward reading

When the companionship of created beings disconnects from the discourse of the Real, alienation and blame threaten, and aversion and disgust mix into it. Whoever rejects, ʿaraḍa, Allah in his heart finds created beings rejecting consideration for his right. They come out against him together, finding his affair pathetic and holding his worth cheap. But whoever returns to Allah in his heart finds the affair brought back into balance, whole and in its parts. The chest expands until it can bear the bad traits found in others, the cloak of pardon is drawn over the defects of all, and reconciliation is preferred to his own portion, given up in favour of theirs. To ignore one's aversion to those who quarrel is more appropriate and more advantageous than arrogance with the foe, the wanting of revenge and the claim of privileges, and most people sit in the fetters of this tribulation. The greed of the lower self is the servant's concern for his worldly portion. Veiled from witnessing the Real, a person is inevitably returned to witnessing the lower self. Being gracious, iḥsān, is to worship Allah as if you see Him. The fear commanded is to stop looking at your own status and worth, and to look rather to your Lord, so that in seeing Him you are annihilated from every vision of your own worth. And once you have been annihilated from yourselves and your acts, Allah suffices as Knower (4:70), and He suffices as the Giver of existence following your effacement.

### In short

- Strain beyond control is the setting, and the verse answers it with three doors: settlement, grace, or release.
- Ṣawdah's forfeited day, the childless wife's offer, and Rāfiʿ ibn Khadīj's wife's apportionment are the standing occasions.
- The settlement is no bribe but expediency, best made between the two alone, better than divorce, and lawful in every form that does not trade lawful for unlawful.
- Rights already due lapse when surrendered, future rights do not, and the reward of gracious forbearance is left unspoken because it exceeds imagination.
- The heart that rejects Allah is rejected by all, and the heart that returns finds the cloak of pardon and the expansion of the chest.

<<<VERSE 4:129>>>
The limit of the heart: "You will never be able to maintain ˺emotional˺ justice between your wives" (4:129), "no matter how keen you are". The verse sets the boundary of obligation exactly where human power ends.

### Phrase by phrase

**"You will never be able to maintain ˹emotional˺ justice between your wives"** states the impossibility: perfect justice between wives in every respect is beyond anyone. Even with the nights divided justly, degrees remain in love, desire and intimacy, so stated Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿUbaydah as-Salmānī, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Muzāḥim, for love lies outside a person's control. The verse is a clarification of the earlier ruling of this sūrah. The words of verse 3, that a man holding more than one wife is duty-bound to maintain justice and equality, and that whoever fears failure should keep to one woman (4:3), could give the impression that the obligation covers even the heart's inclination. This verse clarifies the matter: in things outside one's control, equality is not obligatory. Equality is obligatory in what lies within one's power, the nights spent with them, the living standards and the cost of maintenance. ʿĀʾishah said that the Prophet ﷺ took the greatest care to treat his wives equally, and while doing so he prayed:

> O Allah, this is my division in what I own, so do not blame me for what You own and I do not own,

meaning his heart. Imām Aḥmad and the collectors of the Sunan recorded it, and this is the wording Abū Dāwūd collected, with a sound chain. Who could be more in control of his self than the Prophet ﷺ? Yet even he ruled the matter of emotional inclination to be outside his control and prayed to be excused on that count.

**"So do not totally incline towards one"** draws the practical line. Liking one wife more is given, but the liking must not be exaggerated into treatment, and though equality of inclination is not in one's power, one must not lean so far toward the favourite as to prefer her even in the matters that do lie within one's power and control.

**"leaving the other in suspense"** names the crime of the muʿallaqah, the suspended wife, and the glossers of the word run from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr through al-Ḥasan, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas to as-Suddī and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, all with one meaning: neither divorced nor married. She is like a prisoner, without a husband to fulfil her right and without the freedom to seek another. Abū Dāwūd aṭ-Ṭayālisī recorded from Abū Hurayrah the warning of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ:

> Whoever has two wives and inclines to one of them too much, will come on the Day of Resurrection with one of his sides dragging.

**"And if you do what is right and are mindful of Allah"** gives the promise: justice and equal division in what one has power over, with the fear of Allah in all conditions, and Allah forgives the favouritism of the heart that could not be helped.

### The verse cannot be used against polygamy

Some juxtapose this verse with verse 3 to argue that since equality between wives is impossible, keeping more than one wife must be impermissible. Allah has Himself phrased the two verses with the material that removes the misunderstanding. The present verse says only do not incline totally, and the earlier verse said: "But, if you fear that you will not maintain equity, then, keep to one woman" (4:3). The condition if you fear openly indicates that justice between wives is not beyond possibility or ability, else the extended wording would serve no purpose. Consider the comparison with the forbidden degrees. The verse of prohibitions lists the women forbidden for marriage (4:23) and then declares the combining of two sisters in marriage unlawful (4:23). Had the absolute prohibition of more than one wife been intended, one sentence would have sufficed, and the restriction of the sisters would have been redundant. Instead the Qur'ān avoids the lexical brevity and spends two full verses on the detail, which indicates that the restriction of the two sisters itself suggests the justification of combining more than one woman in marriage, permissible on the condition that they not be sisters.

### The wider teaching and the inward reading

The differences between husband and wife, and between any two persons, largely result from greed, one party wanting demands met without consideration for the other, which breeds distrust. The proper course is for each to weigh the other's problems and, with due allowances, reach a consensus. As Allah desires consideration between people, so He shows the maximum consideration to His servants. He does not seize a person for natural weaknesses, but takes to task the deliberate excesses, the acts of injustice out of haughtiness and insolence. Whoever fears Allah and carries the urge of reform in his heart finds his rightly intended acts pardonable in the Hereafter. Nor should anyone nurse the thought that he is behind the success of another, for Allah alone is the provider, and His help may come under diverse sets of circumstances.

The inward reading turns the same light on the heart. When the concern is for oneself, the state of the union together is overturned and what was sound becomes ruined. When the concern is Allah, the balance of the life together is restored and cleared of turmoil. If Allah has decreed some deficiency as a person's state, no competence of yours will set it right. The command do not turn altogether away is a command not to stray from the clear path. Stop where the stopping was ordered, and do what there is to do. The suspended one is harmed in two ways, nothing to be had from the husband and no way to be with another, and this harm is very grave. The allusion within it is that when the path to your share in worldly things closes, Allah opens to you the witness of His reality toward you and the finding of His kindness. Whoever's annihilation is in Allah, the Real is his substitute. And when what is between you and created beings is set right, with trust placed in what lies between you and the Real, then Allah is forgiving of your failings and merciful in pardoning your sins.

### In short

- Perfect equality of the heart is impossible and not owed, and the Prophet's ﷺ prayer divided what he owned from what Allah owns.
- The owed equality is the nights, the maintenance and the living standard, and the forbidden thing is the suspended wife.
- The one who inclines too far comes on the Day of Resurrection with his side dragging.
- The verse clarifies verse 3 rather than repealing it, and the two-sisters restriction shows the way the rule runs.
- Greed divides people, consideration mends them, and the heart concerned with Allah keeps its balance.

<<<VERSE 4:130>>>
The open door of parting: "But if they choose to separate, Allah will enrich both of them from His bounties" (4:130). The verse closes the passage by answering the fear that separation means ruin.

### The case behind the passage

One record names the couple of the occasion, Asʿad ibn al-Rabīʿ, who had beside his old wife another young wife whom he favoured. Allah forbade him the favouring and commanded that the young and the old be treated equally. The passage that closes here thus grew out of a real household, and the closing verse answers the fear of ruin that hangs over every parted couple.

### Phrase by phrase

**"But if they choose to separate"** names the third case between husband and wife, reached when every effort at setting things right has failed. The separation may come by divorce issued by the man or by khulʿ initiated by the woman.

**"Allah will enrich both of them from His bounties"** is the comfort. Allah will suffice them, giving him a better wife and her a better husband. Another home and a source of support await the woman, and another woman the man, each compensated out of His plenty. There is no cause for concern and no reason to lose hope. Let each think of the life before the marriage, two strangers with no bond between them, whom Almighty Allah then joined as husband and wife. The same can happen again, for the power of Allah is extensive.

**"And Allah is Ever-Bountiful, All-Wise"** seals it. His favour is tremendous and His bounty enormous, and He is Wise in all His actions, decisions and commandments. The dimensions of His capacity are the most extensive, and everything issuing from Him rests on wisdom. In the separation itself may lie the readiest solution of the problem, and the time after it may bless each with a mate by whom life grows good to live.

The inward reading carries the comfort to its root. The essential companionship is the companionship of the heart in perpetual need of Allah, since nothing separates a person from the Real. Everything other than the Real leaves human beings needing one another in the external sense alone, and that is the thinking of the people of separation. The people of realisation hold no doubt that the need of created beings runs entirely to Allah.

### In short

- When all mending fails, separation by divorce or khulʿ opens, and poverty is not among its results.
- Allah enriches both from His plenty, as He united them when they had been strangers.
- The separation itself may be the wisdom, and the real companionship, which is of the heart, never parts from the Real.
