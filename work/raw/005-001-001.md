<<<VERSE 5:1>>>
This verse opens Surah Al-Ma'idah with a command to the believers: "O believers! Honour your obligations." It then declares that all grazing livestock has been made lawful to eat, except what will be announced later in the surah and except hunting while on pilgrimage. It closes with the reminder that Allah commands what He wills. The verse therefore does three things at once: it calls the believers to keep their covenants, it opens the food laws of the surah, and it asserts that the permission and the prohibition both come from Allah's will.

The setting is Madinah, after the Hijrah, when the community had treaties with neighbouring tribes and was responsible for a public order. The verse speaks to people who already have obligations to keep: covenants with Allah, covenants with other people, and the limits Allah has set in the religion. 'Abdullah bin Mas'ud advised that when one hears "O you who believe," one should pay full attention, for it is either a righteous matter being ordained or an evil thing being forbidden. One report notes that the Tawrah addresses the same kind of call in other words, as a summons to those in need, so the verse is a call to attention as much as a command.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers!"** is the address that opens many of the legal passages of the Qur'an. It marks the verse as a command to those who have already accepted faith, not a general announcement to all mankind. The material notes that this address appears repeatedly in the surah and that each time it introduces either an obligation or a prohibition.

**"Honour your obligations"** translates the command to fulfil the covenants. Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and others read "obligations" here as referring to treaties. Ibn Jarir noted that scholars agree on this reading, and that it covers the alliances the people used to make with one another. Ibn Abbas, as reported through Ali bin Abi Talhah, understood the covenants as the rulings Allah has made lawful, unlawful, binding and bounded in the Qur'an, and said the believers must not betray them. Ad-Dahhak said that it refers to what Allah has permitted and what He has prohibited, and that Allah took the covenant from those who proclaim faith in the Prophet ﷺ and the Book to fulfil the obligations He ordered for them in the permissible and the impermissible. The verse also warns against those who break the covenant of Allah once it is confirmed and cut what He ordered to be kept joined, citing 13:25.

**"All grazing livestock has been made lawful to you"** names camels, cows and sheep, according to Al-Hasan, Qatadah and others. Ibn Jarir said that this explanation conforms to the meaning of "beasts of cattle" that the Arabs had. The permission is general and covers the animals the Arabs were familiar with as livestock.

**"except what is hereby announced to you"** refers to the specific circumstances that prohibit some of these animals, which are announced in the following verses. Ibn Abbas, through the same chain, took the exception to mean the flesh of dead animals, blood and swine. Qatadah gave a narrower reading: carrion, and animals slaughtered without the name of Allah spoken over them. It appears, and Allah knows best, that the verse refers to the detailed list given later in the surah, for although the animals mentioned are types of permissible cattle, they become impermissible under the circumstances specified there. The verse also mentions what is sacrificed on stone altars, which is not permissible because it can no longer be slaughtered properly.

**"and hunting while on pilgrimage"** prohibits hunting wild beasts while in the state of Ihram. Some scholars held that the broad meaning of "cattle" also takes in wild animals of the same kind, such as gazelles, wild oxen and wild donkeys, alongside the domestic animals. Allah made the exceptions mentioned above and prohibited hunting wild beasts while in Ihram. It was also reported that the meaning here is that all types of cattle were allowed in all circumstances except what was excluded for the one hunting game while in Ihram. The exception for one forced by necessity, without wilful disobedience and not transgressing, is given later, and Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful in such cases.

**"Indeed, Allah commands what He wills"** closes the verse by asserting that the permission and the prohibition both belong to Allah. The point is that while the cattle are lawful in every state, game is forbidden to the one in ihram, and this is the judgement of Allah, who is wise in what He commands and forbids.

### Why the verse opens the surah

The verse is placed first because the surah is a surah of obligations, and the first obligation is to keep the covenant. The food laws that follow are themselves part of that covenant: a believer's life is a regulated life, and even where he is free to act, he accepts the supremacy and Lordship of Allah and makes himself subject to regulation. The verse also connects to the previous surah, An-Nisa', which spoke of the People of the Book and of those given a share of the Book. Al-Ma'idah opens by demanding that the believers fulfil their obligations, as if to say that the law given before is now to be carried out in full.

### The ruling on the fetus in the womb

Ibn 'Umar and Ibn 'Abbas, among others, took this verse as the basis for allowing the meat of a fetus found dead in the belly of a slaughtered mother. A report in the Sunan collections of Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah, passed on through Abu Sa'id, describes people asking the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about such a fetus. He told them to eat it if they wished, since its slaughter is complete once its mother has been slaughtered. At-Tirmidhi graded this report Hasan. Abu Dawud also recorded a saying from Jabir bin 'Abdullah that the proper slaughter of the unborn young is accomplished by the slaughter of the mother. Only Abu Dawud collected that second report.

### The sanctity of the symbols and the Sacred Month

The next verse, 5:2, forbids the believers to violate the sanctity of the symbols of Allah, the Sacred Month, the animals brought as offerings, the garlands on them, and those travelling to the Sacred House. Ibn 'Abbas said that the symbols of Allah are the rituals of Hajj. Mujahid took the symbols of Allah to include Safa, Marwah and the sacrificial animals. It was also said that the symbols are what Allah prohibited. The Sacred Month is to be respected and honoured, and fighting during it is a great transgression [2:217]. Al-Bukhari recorded in his Sahih that Abu Bakrah reported the Prophet ﷺ speaking during the Farewell Hajj. He said that time has returned to the form it had when Allah created the heavens and the earth. The year has twelve months, and four of them are sacred. Three come one after another, and the fourth stands alone, a month of the tribe of Mudar. The material reads this report as proof that the sanctity of these months continues until the end of time.

### The Hady and the garlands

The command not to abandon the practice of bringing the Hady to the Sacred House is a form of honouring the symbols of Allah. The garlands mark the animals apart from other cattle, showing they are an offering for the Ka'bah, and so anyone who might harm them holds back. Those who see the offering may be moved to follow the rite, and whoever calls to good receives a reward like those who follow him, with nothing taken from theirs. On the night before setting out for Hajj, the Prophet ﷺ stayed at Dhul-Hulayfah, also known as Wadi Al-'Aqiq. In the morning he visited his wives, nine of them at that time, bathed, put on perfume, prayed two rak'ahs, garlanded the offering and openly declared his intention for Hajj and Umrah. The Prophet's Hady at that time consisted of plenty of camels, more than sixty, among the best and healthiest animals.

### Justice even toward enemies

The verse's context includes a command that hatred for some people, who once stopped the believers from the Sacred Mosque, must not push the believers into transgression. Believers are to help one another in Al-Birr and At-Taqwa, and not in sin and transgression. The material gives a setting for this. Zayd bin Aslam reported that the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions were near Al-Hudaybiyyah when idolators had blocked them from the House, which weighed heavily on them. Later, some idolators passed them heading out for 'Umrah, and some Companions wanted to block them in return. Then this verse came down. Ibn 'Abbas and others explained that "Shana'an" means enmity and hatred. The lesson drawn is that justice is owed to everyone in every situation, even to people one dislikes.

### In short

- The verse commands believers to honour their obligations, which Ibn 'Abbas and others explained as treaties and covenants.
- It declares all grazing livestock lawful except what is announced later and except hunting while on pilgrimage.
- It asserts that Allah commands what He wills, so both permission and prohibition come from Him.
- It is followed by commands about the symbols of Allah, the Sacred Month, the Hady and the garlands, and by the duty of justice even toward enemies.
