<<<VERSE 3:104>>>
> “Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful” (3:104)

This verse calls for a community that invites people to what is good, encourages right conduct, and discourages wrongdoing. It makes moral care for others part of the ummah’s responsibility, while success belongs to those who carry it out with knowledge and wisdom.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Let there be a group among you”** establishes the need for people who devote themselves to teaching, inviting, and community guidance. The wording allows for a group with particular knowledge and responsibility. It does not mean that the rest of the believers have no duty. Each person should encourage good and resist wrongdoing according to their knowledge, ability, and role. Some duties are carried by the community collectively, while basic concern for one another belongs to every believer.

The group’s work is not simply to criticize. It should teach the Qur’an and the Prophet’s Sunnah, clarify what is required, and make good action easier. The Qur’an describes the believers as a community that calls people to what is right (3:110). Surah al-Asr likewise describes people of faith as urging one another to truth and patience (103:2). It also says that some people should go forth to gain understanding of the religion and then warn and teach their community (9:122). Together these passages show the value of both broad responsibility and trained guidance.

**“who call ˹others˺ to goodness”** includes inviting people to faith and helping believers live well. Khayr, goodness, includes worship, justice, truthful speech, mercy, family care, and service to others. It can be conveyed through words, but also by conduct that demonstrates patience and integrity. Calling to goodness is an ongoing task, not something reserved for moments when a public wrong has already occurred. Teaching prayer before its time arrives or reminding people to prepare for Ramadan are examples of proactive guidance.

The call also has different audiences. Muslims should help one another understand their obligations and develop good character. They may also explain Islam to people who have not accepted it, doing so without coercion or deception. The goal is to communicate guidance clearly, not to force an outcome. The Qur’an praises those who establish prayer, give zakah, encourage what is right, and forbid wrong when they are given authority (22:41). This places moral responsibility within a broader commitment to worship and justice.

**“encourage what is good”** means promoting what Allah and His Messenger have taught as right. The Arabic term maʿruf refers to good that is recognized in the revealed law and in sound moral understanding. Guidance should be based on knowledge, not personal irritation or a desire to control others. Before advising someone, a person should know that the act is actually wrong or neglected, understand the circumstances, and consider whether their manner is likely to help.

**“and forbid what is evil”** means discouraging conduct clearly prohibited or harmful. The term munkar refers to what is rejected by the revealed law and sound conscience. It does not authorize a person to treat every difference of opinion as misconduct. Issues that qualified scholars have interpreted differently through ijtihad should not be turned into grounds for public blame. The duty is to address clear wrongs, not to make one’s own preferences a test of another person’s faith.

A well-known hadith from Abu Hurayrah describes three responses when a person witnesses wrongdoing: act to stop it if able, speak against it if unable to act, and at least reject it in the heart if speech is not possible. The order is important. It ties action to ability and does not give every individual a license to use force. Authority, safety, likely harm, and the rights of others must be considered. Correcting a wrong by creating a greater wrong would betray the purpose of the verse.

### Knowledge, ability, and good counsel

A person should learn what is right and wrong before correcting others. Without knowledge, an enthusiastic adviser may condemn something permitted or encourage something harmful. Differences in Islamic law may arise because a text allows more than one interpretation or because scholars apply it to different circumstances. A person should not use the command to promote maʿruf as a weapon against a recognized school of law or a sincere jurist.

The strength of the response should also match the situation. A gentle reminder may be appropriate for a recommended good, while a clear prohibition may require firmer counsel. But advice should begin with respect and concern. If speaking would create a serious danger or cause greater harm, restraint may be wiser. The hadith’s reference to the heart recognizes a person’s limits. It does not make humiliation, surveillance, or vigilantism a religious duty.

Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman relates a stern warning that the community must encourage right and forbid wrong or risk punishment. At-Tirmidhi records the report and describes its grade as hasan. Its warning emphasizes that communal silence has consequences. It should not be used to justify reckless intervention. The same duty must be carried out with knowledge, proportion, and regard for safety.

### What makes the group successful?

The group succeeds by calling to the Qur’an and Sunnah, teaching what people need, and correcting wrong in a way that protects the community. Its members should be knowledgeable, trustworthy, and aware of how to communicate. The Prophet’s Companions made this concern part of their lives. A report attributed to Ibn Jarir describes them as a community that considered calling others to goodness a personal responsibility, not merely the task of a few public speakers.

The verse does not define success as winning arguments or gaining authority. Its promise is for those whose work is directed toward goodness and who respect the limits Allah has set. The duty includes inviting, teaching, encouraging, and restraining harm. A community that performs these tasks with sincerity helps people return to Allah and protects its own moral life.

Ad-Dahhak describes the dedicated group as including scholars and those who strive in jihad. Striving here means sustained effort in Allah’s cause, including teaching, service, and defending people from harm. It is not an excuse for coercion. The group’s tasks should include worship and public care such as salah, zakah, hajj, and guidance for people visiting Makkah, while leaving legal rulings and public order to qualified authorities. The Prophet’s companions in Makkah and Madinah modelled this responsibility through their conduct as well as their words.

Reports from Abu Hurayrah and Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman illustrate both personal and communal duties. Muslim records Abu Hurayrah’s hadith about responding to wrongdoing according to one’s ability. Ahmad records Hudhayfah’s warning, and At-Tirmidhi also transmits it. Abu Dawud and Ibn Majah preserve other teachings about encouraging good and preventing harm. The reports do not make every believer responsible for every public problem. They ask each person to use the knowledge, authority, and means actually entrusted to them.

The section’s wider context includes the warning against division and the judgment described in the next verses. Abu Umamah is associated with a report about people who fought one another over religious claims. Ibn Abbas and Al-Hasan Al-Basri transmit different explanations of the bright and gloomy faces in that scene. These accounts reinforce why advice should be offered with taqwa and supplication for guidance, not with anger or a desire to win.

The word fard describes what is obligatory, while some forms of advice and service are recommended. A community needs to distinguish the two. Correcting someone who has abandoned a clear obligation is not the same as urging an optional act. Gentle speech is especially important when encouraging what is commendable. The community’s call to goodness should make the essentials of Islam clear, not burden people with constant criticism over details that scholars have interpreted differently.

### In short

- A group should be equipped to teach and guide, while every believer contributes according to ability.
- Goodness includes faith, worship, justice, and care for others.
- Correcting wrong requires knowledge, proportion, safety, and good counsel.
- Success belongs to those who call people to what is right sincerely.

<<<VERSE 3:105>>>
> “And do not be like those who split ˹into sects˺ and differed after clear proofs had come to them. It is they who will suffer a tremendous punishment” (3:105)

The verse warns believers not to repeat the divisions of earlier communities after clear guidance had reached them. Its focus is not every difference of interpretation. It condemns division that grows from rejecting evidence, following desire, and turning disagreement into hostility.

### Phrase by phrase

**“do not be like those who split ˹into sects˺”** warns against separating into rival groups that treat their own faction as more important than Allah’s guidance. The preceding verse called the community to work together for goodness. This verse shows how that work can be undone when people abandon a shared moral purpose and turn differences into lasting enmity.

**“and differed after clear proofs had come to them”** identifies the basis of blame. The dispute continued even after evidence had made the truth clear. People may disagree honestly when a question is complex or when a text allows more than one sound interpretation. The verse is not a command to erase scholarly reasoning. It criticizes stubborn division after clarity, especially when pride or self-interest drives people to reject what they know.

Islamic scholars have differed in subsidiary rulings while affirming the Qur’an and the Prophet’s teaching. A qualified jurist who strives to reach a ruling may be rewarded for sincere effort even if another view is stronger. This kind of ijtihad differs from rejecting clear evidence or insulting those who follow another recognized opinion. The problem is not the existence of every difference, but the arrogance and hostility that turn differences into schism.

The Qur’an elsewhere describes Allah’s straight path as one route and warns against following scattered paths that pull people away from it (6:153). These warnings invite self-examination. A believer should ask whether an argument is helping people understand the truth or merely strengthening group identity and resentment.

Reports warn the ummah against following desires and breaking apart. One report attributed to Muʿawiyah ibn Abi Sufyan urges Arabs to remain committed to what came from the Prophet ﷺ. A version is recorded by Ahmad and Abu Dawud. The purpose of such warnings is not to label every person who differs as doomed. They call the community back to revelation and caution against treating a faction as the measure of truth.

### What kind of disagreement is dangerous?

The dangerous disagreement is one that rejects clear principles, fuels contempt, or makes people treat a secondary question as the whole of religion. It can lead people to attack one another, neglect shared obligations, and fail to address harms that everyone recognizes. The verse calls for humility: one may explain why a view seems correct while admitting that Allah knows the full truth.

The Qur’an gives ease in the law and does not make hardship its purpose (2:185, 22:78). This helps believers approach differences with mercy and care. Where a matter is open to interpretation, people should learn from qualified scholars rather than condemn one another. Where a matter is clear, they should follow it without using it as an excuse to be cruel.

This distinction is illustrated by fiqh, the careful understanding of Islamic law. Abu Hanifah and other early scholars sometimes reached different conclusions on subsidiary rulings while remaining within the shared foundations of faith. A jurist who makes a sincere ijtihad from the Qur’an and Sunnah is not equivalent to someone who knowingly rejects clear revelation. A well-known hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim says that a judge who strives to reach a ruling receives reward for sincere effort, even if the conclusion is mistaken, while a correct judgment brings greater reward. This encourages humility among scholars and restraint among their followers.

Ibn Abd al-Barr describes early scholars as issuing different rulings without declaring one another doomed. Their example does not mean that every opinion is equally sound or that people should invent rulings without knowledge. It means that the limits of legitimate interpretation must be respected. A person who follows a recognized school, whether Hanafi or another, should not be accused of abandoning salah simply because another school reaches a different conclusion about reciting al-Fatihah behind an imam.

The community should therefore protect its unity without pretending that all questions have identical answers. Its shared worship, the Qur’an, and the Sunnah are the center. The Torah and Gospel belong to the history of revelation, but this passage addresses Muslims who have received clear guidance through the final message. The warning is to avoid the conduct of earlier groups that turned religious disagreement into a cause of contempt and punishment.

### In short

- The verse condemns division after clear evidence, not every sincere difference of interpretation.
- Pride, factional loyalty, and hostility can turn disagreement into a serious wrong.
- Believers should return to revelation and treat one another with humility.

<<<VERSE 3:106>>>
> “On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy. To the gloomy-faced it will be said, “Did you disbelieve after having believed? So taste the punishment for your disbelief.”” (3:106)

The verse turns to the Day of Judgment, when people’s faces will show the difference between joy and distress. Those whose faces are gloomy will be questioned about rejecting faith and will face the consequence of their disbelief.

### Phrase by phrase

**“On that Day”** refers to the Day of Resurrection, when people stand before Allah and the truth of their lives is made clear. The warning follows the preceding discussion of division after clear guidance. It reminds the community that religious disputes are not merely social contests. People remain accountable to Allah for what they believed and did.

**“some faces will be bright while others gloomy”** describes visible signs of the people’s final state. Brightness suggests joy, hope, and the light of faith, while gloom reflects fear and regret. The Qur’an uses similar images of radiant faces and faces darkened by sorrow (75:22, 80:38-41), and describes those who lied about Allah with darkened faces (39:60). These images communicate the seriousness of the Hereafter, not a license for people to judge one another’s final fate in this life.

Reports preserve different explanations of who is meant by the two groups. Ibn Abbas connects brightness with the people who follow the Prophet’s Sunnah and gloom with those who turn away from guidance. Other explanations mention the Muhajirun and Ansar in contrast with communities who rejected the Prophet after recognizing him. Al-Hasan Al-Basri also relates the warning to hypocrisy. A report from Abu Umamah, recorded by At-Tirmidhi and also transmitted by Ibn Majah and Ahmad, applies the scene to people involved in violent religious rebellion. These interpretations reflect the seriousness of division, but they should not be used to make reckless declarations about individuals or entire communities. Allah knows what evidence reached each person and what they chose in response.

**“Did you disbelieve after having believed?”** is a rebuke addressed to those whose final condition reflects rejection after knowledge and faith. The wording calls people to account for turning away from the truth. It should not be used to accuse ordinary believers who struggle, sin, or seek answers. The verse speaks about a grave final outcome known to Allah.

**“So taste the punishment for your disbelief”** states that the punishment is the consequence of rejecting faith. It is not an arbitrary wrong inflicted on a person without cause. The verse warns people to repent while they are alive and still able to change their course. The warning applies to people who deliberately reject faith, not to those who ask questions or struggle to practice. A Muslim’s acts of worship—including salah, hajj in Makkah, jihad in its lawful forms, and supplication—are means of obedience, but none allows a person to claim knowledge of another individual’s final standing. Those choices belong to Allah’s judgment.

A reflective interpretation says that the face expresses the inward state: faith and trust bring light, while a heart that conceals hypocrisy is covered by shame. The image of a veil or hijab in the heart is a metaphor for turning away from truth, not a judgment on a person’s clothing. The spiritual light is a ruh, a gift from Allah. This interpretation encourages each believer to seek mercy and correct their own conduct rather than scrutinize others.

### Who are the faces?

The Qur’an speaks of faces bright with hope and faces covered by sorrow. Al-Qurtubi’s discussion gathers several reported explanations, including believers and disbelievers, sincere Muslims and hypocrites, or those who upheld guidance and those who rejected it. The central lesson is shared across these explanations: the final distinction is made by Allah, who knows the truth of each person. It is not a label that people may impose on a rival group. The Qur’an’s images of brightness and darkness (39:60, 75:22, 80:38-41) express the exposure of deeds and the emotions of that Day.

### In short

- The Day of Resurrection will reveal people’s true state.
- Brightness and gloom portray joy and distress before Allah.
- The verse warns against rejecting faith and leaves judgment of individuals to Allah.

<<<VERSE 3:107>>>
> “As for the bright-faced, they will be in Allah’s mercy, where they will remain forever” (3:107)

The verse gives the other side of the scene: those whose faces are bright will dwell in Allah’s mercy permanently. Their reward is described as mercy because no one enters Paradise independently of Allah’s grace.

### Phrase by phrase

**“As for the bright-faced”** refers to those who meet Allah with faith and the signs of joy described in the previous verse. Their brightness is a sign of hope and honor, not a reason for believers in this world to boast about their own standing.

**“they will be in Allah’s mercy”** is understood as their dwelling in Paradise. The wording draws attention to the origin of their salvation. Worship and good deeds matter, but the ability to believe and act righteously is itself a gift from Allah. People ask for His mercy and hope in His forgiveness rather than claiming that their deeds make them independent of Him.

**“where they will remain forever”** assures that this mercy is not temporary. The people of Paradise will not be expelled from it or have its blessing taken away. This promise gives believers hope while the previous verse’s warning encourages them to remain sincere and avoid turning away from guidance.

### In short

- The bright-faced are welcomed into Allah’s mercy, understood as Paradise.
- Their reward is a gift of grace, not a claim of independence from Allah.
- Their dwelling in that mercy is lasting.

<<<VERSE 3:108>>>
> “These are Allah’s revelations We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. And Allah desires no injustice to ˹His˺ creation” (3:108)

The verse affirms that the preceding revelations are being recited truthfully and that Allah does not wrong any of His creation. His warnings and promises are grounded in justice.

### Phrase by phrase

**“These are Allah’s revelations”** points to the verses just recited about communal responsibility, division, accountability, and mercy. They are signs from Allah, not the Prophet’s personal invention. Their authority comes from the One who knows people’s inner states and judges them with full knowledge.

**“We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth”** means that the message is conveyed faithfully. The Qur’an clarifies what is right and warns against error. The Prophet ﷺ receives and communicates revelation, with Gabriel carrying it by Allah’s command. Believers should therefore respond to the message with trust, reflection, and obedience.

**“And Allah desires no injustice to ˹His˺ creation”** reassures people that Allah will not punish anyone without reason or deprive anyone of what is due. No one will be blamed for another person’s choice. The warning in the prior verses is just, and the mercy promised to the bright-faced is equally just. Allah knows the circumstances, knowledge, and intentions that human beings cannot fully see.

### In short

- The Qur’an is Allah’s truthful revelation, conveyed by His Messenger.
- Allah’s judgment is free of injustice.
- The warning and promise in the passage both reflect His perfect knowledge and justice.

<<<VERSE 3:109>>>
> “To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺” (3:109)

The verse closes this passage by affirming Allah’s complete ownership and authority. Everything in the heavens and earth belongs to Him, and every matter ultimately returns to Him for judgment.

### Phrase by phrase

**“To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth”** means that all creation is His possession and dependent on Him. People may hold wealth, status, or authority for a time, but none of it makes them independent of their Creator. The verse calls the powerful and the vulnerable alike to humility.

**“And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺”** reminds people that worldly disputes are not the final measure of truth. Allah will judge what people believed, how they used authority, whether they protected or harmed others, and whether they remained faithful to His guidance. Those who were treated unjustly will not be forgotten, and those who abused their power cannot evade accountability.

This closing statement places both the warnings and promises of the passage in perspective. The community is asked to encourage good, avoid divisive hostility, and remain united upon revelation because every action belongs to a life that will return to Allah. His ownership is complete, His knowledge encompasses every hidden matter, and His judgment is final.

### In short

- All creation belongs to Allah and depends on Him.
- Every dispute and deed will return to Him for judgment.
- Awareness of Allah’s final authority should encourage humility, justice, and faithful conduct.
