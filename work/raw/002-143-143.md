Verse 143 explains the change of the qiblah by setting it inside a larger gift. "And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you. We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith. It was certainly a difficult test except for those ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah. And Allah would never discount your ˹previous acts of˺ faith. Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity". Just as Allah gave the Muslims a qiblah superior to every other orientation, so He gave the Ummah of Islam the unmatched distinction of being moderate, balanced and just, the honour of the central place among all religious communities.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community"**. "And so": just as We honoured you with the religion of Ibrāhīm (Abraham) and his qiblah, and guided you to it, so We have made you, O community of Muḥammad ﷺ, a wasaṭ nation. The Arabic adjective means moderate, middle or central, and is normally applied to the best of a kind. Allah changed the qiblah to that of Ibrāhīm and chose it for this Ummah so as to make it the best nation ever. For that reason it will bear witness over the other nations on the Day of Resurrection, when all of them will agree about its excellence. To say that Quraysh is "wasaṭ" among the Arab tribes means the best of them. To say the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was wasaṭ in his people means he came from the best branch. The ʿAṣr prayer is called al-wusṭā, the middle prayer, meaning the best, as the authentic hadith collections report. Because Allah made this Ummah the wasaṭ, He endowed it with the most complete legislation, the best method and the clearest way, as He says: He "has chosen you, and laid upon you no hardship in the religion—the way of your forefather Abraham" (22:78). At-Tirmidhī relates from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī that the Prophet ﷺ himself explained the word as ʿadl, just, in the sense of the best, as al-Qurṭubī notes. The Ummah was chosen to be a good, just and balanced nation among all communities in its beliefs, its worship and its dealings, excellent and upright.

**"so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you."**. Witnesses on the Day of Resurrection that the messengers delivered the message to their peoples, with the Messenger ﷺ bearing witness that he delivered it to you and that you are upright and on the straight path. This distinction will appear in its full splendour on the Day of Judgement. Those among the earlier nations who denied their prophets will pretend on that day that they never received a Book from Allah and that no prophet ever guided them. The Ummah of Islam will then be called to testify that prophets came from Allah in every age and guided every people. The earlier nations will object. This Ummah did not exist at the time, they will say, and cannot know what happened before it came into being, so its testimony against them cannot stand. The Ummah will answer that although it did not witness those events, it received an authentic report from the most reliable source there could be: the Last Prophet ﷺ and the Last Book of Allah. The Prophet ﷺ will then be called as a witness. He will confirm his Ummah's testimony. Aḥmad relates from Abū Saʿīd that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: Nūḥ (Noah) will be called on the Day of Resurrection and asked whether he conveyed the message, and he will say yes. His people will be summoned and asked whether Nūḥ conveyed it to them, and they will say that no warner came to them and no one was sent. Nūḥ will be asked who testifies for him, and he will say, "Muḥammad and his Ummah." The Prophet ﷺ then said that "wasaṭ" means ʿadl, just: you will be summoned to testify that Nūḥ conveyed his message, and I will attest to your testimony. Al-Bukhārī, at-Tirmidhī, an-Nasāʾī and Ibn Mājah also recorded it. In another report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, also in Aḥmad, the Prophet ﷺ said that a prophet will come on the Day of Resurrection with only two or more followers. His people will be asked whether he conveyed the message and will say no. He will be asked whether he conveyed it and will say yes, and when asked who testifies for him he will name Muḥammad and his Ummah. They will be summoned and asked whether he conveyed it, and will say yes. Asked how they know, they will say that their Prophet came to them and told them that the messengers had conveyed their messages. Aḥmad also relates from Abū al-Aswad that he came to Madinah during an epidemic that was killing many people, and sat with ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb when a funeral passed and the people praised the dead man. ʿUmar said, "Wajabat," it has become binding. Another funeral passed and the people spoke ill of the dead man, and again ʿUmar said, "Wajabat." Abū al-Aswad asked what he meant. ʿUmar replied that he was saying what the Messenger of Allah ﷺ had said: any Muslim for whom four people testify that he was righteous, Allah admits to Paradise. They asked about three, and he said three as well. They asked about two, and he said two as well. They did not ask him about one. Al-Bukhārī, at-Tirmidhī and an-Nasāʾī recorded this too.

**"We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith."**. We prescribed for you, O Muḥammad ﷺ, facing Bayt al-Maqdis (Jerusalem) at first and then changed it to the Kaʿbah, in order to find out who would follow and obey you and face whatever you face, and who would turn on his heels, reverting to his religion. Allah of course knew already; "that We might know" means that the knowledge might become manifest and the distinction be seen. The change showed who accepted Allah's decree gladly, did what He instructed and followed the Messenger ﷺ, and who abandoned his religion to chase his own desires instead of Allah's sacred law. On one reading "the direction you were facing" is the Kaʿbah itself, which the Prophet ﷺ had faced in Makkah before being commanded, on emigrating, to face Jerusalem so as to win over the Jews' hearts. He prayed towards it for sixteen or seventeen months, or nineteen months in one account, before the direction changed. Some doubted the religion and thought the Prophet ﷺ confused about the matter, and a number of people apostatised because of it. History records that after this verse came down, those weak in faith, or merely hypocrites, left Islam, and even accused the Prophet ﷺ of going back to the ways of his own people, the idolaters.

**"It was certainly a difficult test except for those ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah."**. The Arabic particle is softened: "though it was indeed a grave thing". Changing the qiblah from Bayt al-Maqdis to the Kaʿbah weighed heavily on hearts, except those whose hearts Allah guided and protected. These believe with certainty in the truthfulness of the Messenger ﷺ and that whatever he brought is true beyond doubt. They believe that Allah does and decides whatever He wills, gives His servants whatever commands He wills, and abrogates whichever of them He wills, with perfect wisdom and conclusive proof in all of it. They were certain that whatever He decrees for His servants carries far-reaching wisdom and underlying reasons. Those with a disease in their hearts are different. Whatever happens breeds doubt in them, while the same event increases the believers in faith and certainty. Allah says: "Whenever a sûrah is revealed, some of them ask ˹mockingly˺, “Which of you has this increased in faith?” As for the believers, it has increased them in faith and they rejoice. But as for those with sickness in their hearts, it has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness" (9:124-125), and, "We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss" (17:82). Those who stayed faithful to the Messenger ﷺ, obeyed him and faced whatever Allah commanded, with neither doubt nor hesitation, were the foremost of the Companions. Some scholars say that the early Emigrants and the Anṣār were the ones who prayed towards both qiblahs.

**"And Allah would never discount your ˹previous acts of˺ faith."**. The reward of your earlier prayers towards Bayt al-Maqdis will not be lost with Allah. Abū Isḥāq as-Sabīʿī relates in the Ṣaḥīḥ from al-Barāʾ that people asked about those who had prayed towards Bayt al-Maqdis and died before the change, and this phrase came down. At-Tirmidhī recorded the same from Ibn ʿAbbās and graded it ṣaḥīḥ. In Ibn Isḥāq's report, Ibn ʿAbbās explained that the phrase covers both your prayer towards the first qiblah and your belief in your Prophet and obedience to him in facing the second: Allah will reward you for all of it. "Faith" here can be taken in its usual sense. When the qiblah changed, some foolish people thought the Muslims had abandoned Islam and that their faith was now null and void, and the verse assures the Muslims that Allah will not let their faith go to waste and tells them to ignore such senseless conjecture. On the basis of certain hadiths, some early commentators took "faith" to mean ṣalāh. On that reading Allah assures them that the change does not touch the validity of the prayers they offered towards Bayt al-Maqdis, which were valid and have been accepted. Al-Bukhārī relates from al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, and at-Tirmidhī from Ibn ʿAbbās, that when the Kaʿbah was appointed people grew anxious about Muslims who had prayed towards Bayt al-Maqdis and died before they could pray towards the Kaʿbah. Another explanation: Allah does not thwart your faith as it stood before the abrogation of legal rulings. His purpose is only to abrogate the rulings that govern your faith. He did not nullify your prayers towards Jerusalem, He abrogated the ruling that required them. Allah will not let your faith in Him go to waste, nor your good actions, those prayers included.

**"Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity"**. Gentle with the believers and merciful in not letting their deeds go to waste. The first word denotes intense mercy, and is placed first for the sake of the rhyme with the preceding verse. He is kind and compassionate with people, does not overburden them, and does not deprive them of the reward of their actions. The Ṣaḥīḥ reports that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saw a woman among some captives who had been separated from her child. Whenever she found an infant boy among the captives she would clasp him to her chest, searching for her own. When she found him she embraced him and gave him her breast. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ asked his Companions whether they thought this woman would willingly throw her son into the fire. They said no, and he said: by Allah, Allah is more merciful to His servants than this woman is to her son.

### The most moderate of all people

The quality that gives the Ummah of Islam its superiority, according to this verse, is being wasaṭ, variously rendered midmost, moderate, just, intermediary, middle, central or justly balanced. To explain it, commentators have usually used the adjective muʿtadil, moderate or temperate, and the noun iʿtidāl, being equal, both from the root ʿadl, to be equal or to make equal. Why should the superiority of a human group or individual depend on moderation? Begin with a tangible fact. Every system of medicine, old or new, agrees that the health of the body depends on the temperateness of the elements that compose it, and that illness comes from a disturbance of this balance. In the ancient Greek medicine that the Muslims developed further, these elements or humours are four, blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile, and they produce four states in the body, heat, cold, wetness and dryness. While the four are properly balanced the body is healthy. As soon as any one of them increases or decreases immoderately the body falls ill, and if the balance is not restored in time it may die. In the ethical and spiritual sphere, likewise, health depends on temperateness and inner equilibrium, and illness arises from intemperance and imbalance which, if allowed to grow, ends in spiritual death. Anyone with eyes to see will also discover for himself where the essence of being human lies. What sets man at the head of all created beings is not the physical states of his body, which he shares with the animals, but something higher and subtler: spiritual perfection. As the great Sufi poet Rūmī put it, manhood does not reside in the flesh, the fat or the skin, but only in seeking to please the Friend. Of those who ignore this essential attribute and let it be destroyed in themselves he said that the people one sees all around are not human. They are not men, they only wear the masks of men.

It follows that only one who has reached ethical and spiritual equilibrium along with physical equilibrium deserves the title al-Insān al-Kāmil, the Universal Man. All the prophets were specially granted this quality, and the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ received it in its most perfect form, which makes him the Universal Man par excellence. For humanity in general Allah created, on the one hand, a stable and complex system of medicines, instruments and physicians for physical well-being. On the other hand He sent His prophets with divine guidance, equipped also with a measure of physical force, so that they could establish this law of equilibrium and moderation in the world. The Qur'an defines the purpose of sending messengers and Books: "We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and with them We sent down the Scripture and the balance ˹of justice˺ so that people may administer justice. And We sent down iron with its great might" (57:25). The Book is meant to produce inner equilibrium and temperateness in people, and the Balance to produce equilibrium in their social conduct and economic dealings. The Balance may also stand for the Sharīʿah of each prophet, which defines what equilibrium really means as it applies to the various spheres of human life, and which serves to set up justice in the world.

The verse under discussion characterises the Ummah of Islam as wasaṭ, and the Qur'an says elsewhere, "And among those We created is a group that guides with the truth and establishes justice accordingly" (7:181). The Ummah displays its spiritual equilibrium in three ways. It abandons the pursuit of personal desires and interests so as to follow divine guidance. It tries to bring others to do the same. And it settles every kind of dispute in the light of divine law, without being swayed by the vested interests of any person or group. Another verse is more specific still: "You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah" (3:110). It is the best Ummah because it was given a unique Prophet ﷺ who taught it to respect all the other prophets, and a Book which is the most comprehensive and perfect of the divine Books. The Ummah itself is endowed with temperateness and balance to a degree no other enjoys. It was destined to receive the subtlest modes of knowledge, and to outshine others in every form of faith and practice, most of all in fearing Allah. Its field of action is not limited to one country or race. It extends across the world and into every sphere of human existence. "Raised for humanity" indicates that the very purpose of its existence is to work for people's good and help them find the way to salvation and Paradise. Its function, its very insignia as an Ummah, is to guide people towards good deeds and turn them from evil ones. A hadith sums up this role: religion is to have the good of others at heart, especially one's fellow Muslims. The good deeds it guides others towards are those the Sharīʿah defines as good. The evil it turns them from includes kufr (disbelief), shirk (associating partners with Allah), bidʿah (innovation in religion), sins of every kind, illegitimate customs, transgression of divine commands and immoral or indecent acts. Dissuading people from evil may take different forms, sometimes requiring the tongue, sometimes the hand, sometimes the pen and sometimes the sword, so that it includes every form of jihad. In the breadth and depth of this quality no other Ummah compares with the Ummah of Islam.

### Moderation seen in practice

How far do the facts bear out this moderation? A full comparison of the beliefs and practices of all the communities is not possible here, but a few examples will establish the point.

Take doctrine first. Among the earlier communities, some took their prophets to be sons of Allah and worshipped them: "The Jews say, “Ezra is the son of Allah,” while the Christians say, “The Messiah is the son of Allah.”" (9:30). Others, having recognised and acknowledged their prophet on the strength of his repeated miracles, refused to obey him when he called them to a holy war and told him bluntly, "So go—both you and your Lord—and fight; we are staying right here!" (5:24). There was even the spectacle of prophets tortured by their own followers. The Ummah of Islam, by contrast, loves the Prophet ﷺ so deeply that Muslims in every period of their history have counted it the greatest blessing to give up their lives at his call, and even those of their wives and children. Yet it has never crossed the limit. It has placed him in the station of a prophet, not the station of Allah. Though it knows him to be the most perfect of the prophets, it has gone on calling him "the servant of Allah and His messenger". The doctrinal position, as the famous Arabic poem the Qaṣīdah al-Burdah puts it, is that short of attributing "the sonship of Allah" to him, which the Christians do with Christ and which is disbelief, whatever one says in his praise will be correct. A Persian poet addressed him in a single line: in short, after God, you are the greatest.

Turn from doctrine to worship and rites, and the same excesses appear among the earlier communities. On one side their religious scholars misinterpreted or altered the injunctions of their Sharīʿah, going so far as to distort the Sacred Books for a handful of silver, and devised every kind of ruse to escape the rites Allah had ordained. On the other side people renounced the world altogether and shut themselves in monastic cells. They refused their share of the blessings of the physical world, which Allah had not only given to man but permitted him to enjoy. They believed that imposing hardship on oneself carried the highest merit and was worship par excellence. The history of the Ummah of Islam presents a wholly different picture. It never made monasticism the highest form of religious life, something Islam in fact forbids. At the same time, through its readiness to sacrifice property and life, children and all, for the commands of Allah and His Prophet ﷺ, it established its sway, politically too, over a considerable part of the world. It has demonstrated in practice, as no other community has, that religion is meant to be lived in the marketplace and the halls of power as much as in the mosque and the contemplative retreat. It is this Ummah that showed the world how those poor in spirit can walk about in kings' robes, and how kings in spirit can hide in beggars' garb, because king and beggar alike know that the greatest dignity lies in being Allah's servant.

In human and social relations too, the earlier communities fell into excess one way or another. On one side there was indifference to human rights, above all an utter disregard of the rights of women, and a general pursuit of individual interests and desires regardless of right and wrong. On the other side there was an exaggerated sentimentality that forbade eating animal flesh although Allah had made it lawful, and frowned on killing an insect even by accident. It was the Ummah of Islam and its Sharīʿah that established equilibrium and a just order in human relations. It laid down a clear code of human rights, extended them to women, and prescribed that enemies too, not only in peace but on the battlefield itself, enjoy certain inalienable rights. It demarcated every right and every duty and counted every falling short of the prescribed mark, and every overstepping of it, as a crime. It also taught that one should try to fulfil all one's obligations to others, but that when one's own rights suffer one should show patience and forgiveness.

In the economic sphere the other communities have likewise been prey to excesses. In our own age there is, on one side, the capitalist system, which ignores the distinction between lawful and unlawful, is blind to the welfare of the people and treats the piling up of wealth as the highest virtue. On the other side stand economic systems with no respect for personal property. In reality these two hostile systems share one essence: worldly things pursued as the whole purpose of human life. The Sharīʿah of Islam brings the conflicting elements into balance and gives each its proper place. It does not let the piling up of wealth become the final aim of human effort, nor does it make human dignity depend on money, rank or office. At the same time it lays down principles for distributing wealth in a balanced way, so that no member of society is deprived of the basic necessities of life and no individual or group appropriates all the available wealth. Things that all members of a society can share have been placed under public or joint control, while the right to private property is fully respected in certain specific things. It drew a clear line between ḥalāl (lawful) and ḥarām (unlawful) possessions, insisted on the spiritual merit of lawful possessions and laid down the rules for using them.

### Standing between Allah and the people

The prophets acted as intermediaries, receiving guidance from Allah and conveying it to the people. That process went on for thousands of years until the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, with whom prophethood ended. But even after prophethood ended, the conveying of Allah's message to people had to continue, and still does. Generation after generation, countless people are born, and before they die someone must tell them about the reality of life and about the divine commandments. After the Prophet's ﷺ death this duty fell to the Muslims. With the end of prophethood the whole Muslim community stands between Allah and the people, in the position the prophets once held. This is no reward for superiority but a massive responsibility, which means all the Muslims' social and communal planning must necessarily be oriented to daʿwah, the call. Muslims should be most eager to carry out this mission, make it their principal goal and be ready to sacrifice everything for it. The change of qiblah was a sign that Allah had removed the Children of Israel from spiritual leadership and appointed the followers of Muḥammad ﷺ in their place. From then until the end of time the Kaʿbah is to remain the rallying point for the call of the divine religion and a centre for all true believers in Allah, with the Muslims acting as a "middle nation", intermediaries between Allah and humanity. It is for them to communicate the word of Allah to their fellow human beings, as the Prophet ﷺ did in his time, a duty incumbent on the Muslim community for ever. Their success in this world and in the Hereafter depends on how effectively they discharge it.

### Witnesses, trustworthiness and consensus

Several rulings follow from the verse. First, Allah has made the Ummah equitable and just, and hence trustworthy, "so that" it may qualify to bear witness. From this the jurists infer that a person who is not ʿādil, trustworthy as the Sharīʿah defines the term, is not acceptable as a witness in court.

Second, according to al-Qurṭubī this verse establishes ijmāʿ, the consensus of the Ummah, as one of the four deciding authorities in legislation. Since Allah Himself has accepted this Ummah as a trustworthy witness over the other communities, the consensus of the Ummah is decisive in legal matters and acting on it is wājib (obligatory). So the consensus of the Companions must be accepted by their successors, and that of the successors by the generation after them. Al-Tafsīr al-Maẓharī draws from the verse the principle that whatever deeds and practices of this Ummah have been approved by consensus are all commendable, because if a consensus on error were possible the Ummah could not be called moderate and just. Al-Jaṣṣāṣ adds that the reliability of consensus is not confined to the age of the Prophet ﷺ or the Companions. The consensus of the Muslims in any age whatever is equally trustworthy, because the verse addresses the whole Ummah, which includes not only the Prophet's ﷺ contemporaries but every succeeding generation of Muslims until the Day of Judgement. Muslims living in any age therefore qualify as Allah's witnesses, whose agreement on a point becomes decisive in legislation, and who cannot reach a consensus on anything that would be an error or a deviation. It should not be forgotten, however, that consensus in this context does not mean a sort of referendum by adult franchise. It means the agreement of the majority of those scholars who fulfil the conditions for pronouncing judgement in matters of the Sharīʿah, those who possess the authority of ijtihād. Once such a consensus is reached, the bulk of the Ummah naturally accepts it and holds to it.

### Which qiblah came first?

The Companions and their Successors differed over whether it was the House of Allah at Makkah or Bayt al-Maqdis at Jerusalem that was appointed as the qiblah when the five daily prayers became obligatory in Makkah, before the Hijrah. According to ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās the first qiblah was Bayt al-Maqdis, and it remained so for some sixteen or seventeen months after the Hijrah, and only then did Allah command that the House of Allah be taken as the qiblah. The Prophet's ﷺ practice in Makkah, on this view, was to pray between al-Ḥajar al-Aswad (the Black Stone) and al-Rukn al-Yamānī (the Yemeni corner), so that his face was turned towards the House of Allah and Bayt al-Maqdis at the same time. That was no longer possible after the migration to Madinah, hence his keen desire that the House of Allah be made the new qiblah. Other Companions hold that when the five prayers were made obligatory in Makkah, the House of Allah served as the qiblah of the Muslims, as it had for Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl (Ishmael). The Prophet ﷺ kept this qiblah as long as he stayed in Makkah. After the Hijrah Allah ordained a change, and Bayt al-Maqdis became the qiblah for sixteen or seventeen months. Then a new command came and the House of Allah was restored as the qiblah. Al-Qurṭubī, relying on Abū ʿAmr, prefers this second view.

On the second view the reason for the changes is this. On coming to Madinah the Prophet ﷺ had to deal with the Jews, and to familiarise them with Islam he adopted their qiblah under divine command. Little by little it became clear that so stubborn a people would not easily give up their hostility, so Allah allowed him to return to the original qiblah, which as the qiblah of his forefathers Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl was naturally dearer to him. In fact the mosque of Ṣāliḥ was oriented towards the House of Allah, as an incident al-Qurṭubī reports from Abū al-ʿĀliyah al-Riyāḥī shows. He once debated with a Jew over the orientation adopted by Mūsā (Moses). The Jew insisted that Mūsā prayed towards the Ṣakhrah, the Rock in the sanctuary at Jerusalem, while Abū al-ʿĀliyah maintained that he stood near the Rock with his face turned towards the House of Allah. He finally suggested settling the dispute by looking at the mosque of Ṣāliḥ, on a hill below Bayt al-Maqdis, and when they visited it they found it oriented towards the House of Allah. Those who prefer the first view explain the matter differently. In Makkah it was necessary to distinguish the Muslims from the idol-worshippers and to stress the difference. So Bayt al-Maqdis was appointed the Muslims' qiblah instead of the House of Allah, which was then the qiblah of the mushrikīn. After the Hijrah a new need arose in Madinah, to highlight the distinction between the Muslims and the Jews, so the Jews' qiblah was given up and the House of Allah adopted.

Because of this difference, "your former direction of prayer" has been read in two ways. On the first view it can only be Bayt al-Maqdis, the first and earlier qiblah. On the second it can also be the Kaʿbah, the earliest and first qiblah. The real import is the same either way. The command changing the orientation was a test of the faith of those who claimed to follow the Prophet ﷺ. It openly demonstrated the difference between those who genuinely obey Allah and His Messenger ﷺ and those who follow their own opinion.

### Rulings drawn from the change

First, the verse shows that an injunction based on the Sunnah is sometimes abrogated by the Qur'an. As al-Jaṣṣāṣ points out in his Aḥkām al-Qur'ān, the Qur'an nowhere states that the Prophet ﷺ was ever commanded, before the Hijrah or after it, to face Bayt al-Maqdis. The evidence is found only in the hadith and the Sunnah. A practice established by the Sunnah was thus abrogated by this verse of the Qur'an appointing the House of Allah as the qiblah.

Second, the verse shows that the hadith of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ cannot, in a certain respect, be separated from the Qur'an, and that the Qur'an recognises the legitimacy of injunctions that find no mention in it but rest on hadith alone. For the verse states plainly at its end that the prayers offered towards Bayt al-Maqdis, as the Prophet ﷺ had commanded, are wholly valid and accepted by Allah.

Third, the verse helps to resolve a delicate question of jurisprudence about the khabar al-wāḥid, a hadith reported by only one or two persons. Can such a hadith abrogate an injunction laid down in the Qur'an or otherwise definitely established? The Ḥanafī school holds that it cannot, while the reports about this verse suggest that it can, provided strong indications establish the hadith's authenticity. Al-Bukhārī, Muslim and nearly all the authentic collections report from several Companions that when the command changing the qiblah came down, the Prophet ﷺ prayed ʿAṣr, or according to other reports Ẓuhr, facing the House of Allah. Some Companions leaving the mosque passed the mosque of the Banū Salamah and found them praying towards Bayt al-Maqdis. They called out that the qiblah had changed and that they had just prayed with the Prophet ﷺ facing the House of Allah. On hearing this, those people changed their orientation at once, in the middle of the prayer. Nuwaylah bint Muslim relates that the women in the back row came forward, so that after the turn the men were again in front and the women behind. The Banū Salamah thus adopted the new qiblah the same day, while the news reached Qubāʾ the next day during Fajr, as al-Bukhārī and Muslim report from Ibn ʿUmar, and the people of Qubāʾ too turned towards the House of Allah during their prayer. From these reports al-Jaṣṣāṣ draws a conclusion. Although this hadith is essentially a solitary report, its general acceptance and the strong indications of its authenticity have raised it to the status of a hadith related by many trustworthy reporters in unbroken succession, which always yields certainty. The Ḥanafī jurists agree. They then face a further question: the hadith gained general acceptance only much later, so how could the news conveyed to the Banū Salamah suffice at the time? Al-Jaṣṣāṣ answers that not only they but all the Companions already knew that the Prophet ﷺ wished the House of Allah to be made the qiblah and had been praying for it. They had come to regard it as quite probable that the command to keep Bayt al-Maqdis would not remain in force. This probability had made the continuation of Bayt al-Maqdis as the qiblah somewhat uncertain rather than definite, and in view of that uncertainty a solitary report was enough to abrogate the earlier command. Otherwise a solitary report cannot justifiably abrogate a definite and final injunction of the Qur'an.

Fourth, the verse helps settle a controversy about the imām using a microphone in leading ṣalāh. Is it legitimate for the congregation to follow his call in their movements, and if they hear nothing but the sound from the loudspeaker, are their prayers invalid? Al-Bukhārī's hadith from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar tells how the people of Qubāʾ turned towards the House of Allah in the course of their prayer as soon as they heard of the change. Commenting on it, the great Ḥanafī scholar al-ʿAynī says that this hadith establishes a rule: a person who is not praying may teach or instruct a person engaged in prayer. Elsewhere in his ʿUmdat al-Qārī he adds that al-Ṭaḥṭāwī derived a further rule from it. If someone engaged in prayer hears words spoken by someone not praying, his prayer is not invalidated. Ḥanafī jurists generally do hold that if a person at prayer obeys the call of someone outside the prayer, his prayer becomes invalid. What they mean is that obeying someone other than Allah during ṣalāh invalidates it. If one is in fact obeying a divine command and the other person is merely the means of conveying it, the prayer is not invalidated at all. An example makes this clear. If a man joining the congregation finds no room in the first row and would stand alone in the second, the jurists say he should pull someone back from the first row to join him. On the face of it the man who lets himself be pulled back is obeying someone other than Allah during prayer, which ought to invalidate it, but it does not. The most authoritative Ḥanafī text, al-Durr al-Mukhtār, rules that his prayer is perfectly valid, and al-Ṭaḥṭāwī explains why: he has not really obeyed the newcomer but followed a divine command conveyed to him by the Prophet ﷺ. There are, then, two ways in which a person at prayer may answer the call of someone outside it. He may wish to please that person and obey him, in which case the prayer becomes invalid. Or he may obey a command of the Sharīʿah which the other person conveys to him, in which case he is essentially obeying a divine command and his prayer remains valid. This resolves the microphone question. Technical experts hold that the sound from a loudspeaker is exactly the sound the imām produces, and if so there is no question of invalidity. Suppose, though, that the transmitted sound were not the imām's own voice but an imitation of it, or a report of what he said. Even then it would be wrong to suspect the worshippers of obeying the microphone. They are plainly obeying the Prophet's ﷺ command to follow the imām's movements. The microphone merely informs them that the imām has bowed or prostrated, and in acting on that information they obey the imām, not the instrument, and it is a divine command that requires following the imām in congregational prayer.

### The chosen of the chosen

In the devotional reading, it is the wise Lord and knowing King whose power brings beings into existence. The created things exist through His exaltedness and through His making them manifest, and the earth and heavens stand by His keeping. He created the newly arrived things and chose the animals from among them. From the animals He chose the children of Ādam (Adam), from them the faithful, from the faithful the prophets, and from the prophets Muḥammad ﷺ. And he chose his community above those before it. This is why Muṣṭafā ﷺ said that he had been brought forth from the best generation of Ādam's children, one generation after another, until he came to be in his own. He also said that Allah chose his Companions above all the world's people except the prophets and envoys, and from his Companions chose four and made them the best, though there is good in all of them: Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān and ʿAlī. He chose his community over the other communities and sent him forth in the best generation, after which come the second and the third in succession, then the fourth, one after another. From this report it is understood that Muṣṭafā ﷺ is the best of the children of Ādam and the chosen from among the world's people. He is the leader of creatures and adornment of the world, the weighty one of the age, the earth's lamp and heaven's full moon. He is the shelter of the disobedient and intercessor for sinners, the master and seal of the messengers. After him the best of creatures is Abū Bakr, the sincerely truthful, whose cushion of imamate the Lord of the Worlds set on the throne of Muṣṭafā's ﷺ Sharīʿah. He made self-purification and truthfulness the lodging of his servanthood, and set trust and certainty to guard the rank of his friendship. Next in excellence is ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, in whose capable hand the Lord of the Worlds placed the reins for lowering and raising the legal rulings, drawing the robe of his rulership over the creed's forehead. Through his sternness and awe the smoke of associationism was subdued by its own misfortune.

### In short

- As Allah gave the Muslims the best qiblah, He made them the wasaṭ, the just and best nation, witnesses on the Day of Judgement that every prophet delivered his message, with the Prophet ﷺ confirming their testimony (22:78). The hadiths of Nūḥ, of the prophet with two followers, and of ʿUmar's "wajabat" illustrate this.
- The first qiblah was a test that separated those who follow the Messenger ﷺ from those who turn back. It was heavy except for the guided, whose faith grows where the sick at heart find doubt (9:124-125, 17:82).
- No prayer offered towards Jerusalem was lost, for Allah is more merciful to His servants than a mother to her child.
- Wasaṭ means moderation and balance, the health of body and soul alike. The Ummah was raised to guide humanity (57:25, 7:181, 3:110), and in doctrine, worship, social relations and economics it holds the middle between the extremes into which earlier communities fell (9:30, 5:24).
- With prophethood ended, the Ummah stands between Allah and people as bearer of the call. The verse grounds the rule that only the trustworthy may testify and the authority of the Ummah's consensus.
- Scholars differ over whether the Kaʿbah or Jerusalem was the first qiblah. The change shows that the Qur'an can abrogate the Sunnah, that rulings based on hadith alone are valid, that an accepted solitary report may abrogate in conditions of uncertainty, and that a worshipper may act on information from outside the prayer, which settles the microphone question.
