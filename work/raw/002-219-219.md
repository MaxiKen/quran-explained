<<<VERSE 2:219>>>
This verse belongs to the series of questions the Companions put to the Prophet ﷺ in this surah. It answers two of them, on wine and gambling and on how much to give. "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling." The answer: "There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit." Then: "They ˹also˺ ask you ˹O Prophet˺ what they should donate." The answer is "Whatever you can spare." And the verse closes: "This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect".

### Phrase by phrase

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling."**. When Islam came, drinking was common practice, part of the general run of Jāhilī customs, and when the Prophet ﷺ emigrated to Madīnah he found its people accustomed to wine and gambling too. Ordinary people were infatuated with both for their obvious benefits and had no idea of the evils inherent in them. Yet it is Allah's way that in every nation there are sensible people who let reason govern temperament and will not go near an urge that reason condemns. The Prophet ﷺ stood far above all in this, with an inborn distaste for things that would later be declared ḥarām, and some Companions never touched wine even when it was openly drunk. After the arrival in Madīnah al-Ṭayyibah some Companions grew deeply concerned about the effects of these two habits, and ʿUmar al-Fārūq and Muʿādh ibn Jabal, with some of the Anṣār, came to the Prophet ﷺ and said that wine and gambling not only spoil a man's reason but cause financial loss, and asked his guidance. Imam Aḥmad records from Abū Maysarah that ʿUmar prayed for a clear ruling on al-khamr. Allah sent down this verse. ʿUmar was summoned and it was recited to him. But he prayed again for a clear ruling, and the verse of Sūrat al-Nisāʾ came down, "Do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you are aware of what you say" (4:43), after which a herald would announce on the Prophet's ﷺ behalf at prayer time that no drunk person should attend. ʿUmar heard this too and prayed a third time. The verses of Sūrat al-Māʾidah were revealed, and when the reciter reached "Will you not then abstain" (5:91) he said, "We abstain, we abstain." Abū Dāwūd, together with al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī also record this, and ʿAlī ibn al-Madīnī and al-Tirmidhī declared its chain sound. This verse marks the first, preliminary step towards ending Muslims’ use of wine and gambling. The question covers the drinking, sale and purchase of wine, which refers to anything that impairs or intoxicates the mind, for ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, as Leader of the Faithful, used to say that khamr includes everything that clouds the intellect, and gambling, which refers to wealth taken in a contest to which both sides contribute, including the divinatory arrows.

**"There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people"**. In the partaking of both there is great sin. A variant reading has "much" sin in place of "great", because of the fighting, cursing and swearing that result from them. The harm of wine and gambling touches religion, while their benefit is material: for the body, aiding digestion and the expulsion of waste, a sharpening of the mind and a feeling of joy, and the money made by selling wine, as well as the gambler's winnings spent on himself and his family, acquiring money effortlessly. One early reading relates the benefit to trade in them before they were made unlawful, and the sin to their state after. The word ithm, sin, includes everything that may become a preliminary to sin. Wine dulls the senses and weakens reason, the faculty basic to human excellence, which acts as a brake on indulgence in evil. Once reason is blocked the door is open to every kind of evil.

**"but the evil outweighs the benefit."**. The sin in them, the degenerate behaviour they produce, is greater than their usefulness. These benefits are outweighed by the clear harm to mind and religion. Therefore, a reasonable person avoids them. The verse was the beginning of the process of prohibiting wine, not explicit but implied, which is why ʿUmar still prayed for a clear ruling, and soon afterwards Allah sent the clear prohibition of Sūrat al-Māʾidah. Ibn ʿUmar, together with al-Shaʿbī, together with Mujāhid, together with Qatādah, together with al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Aslam stated that this was the first verse revealed about khamr, then the verse of al-Nisāʾ, then the verse of al-Māʾidah which forbade it. Drinking is not clearly declared unlawful here, but its evils are pointed out, which amounts to good counsel to abandon it. Some Companions took the counsel so seriously that they stopped drinking at once. Others reasoned that the verse had not declared wine ḥarām but had identified it as a cause of sin, so that if they could keep themselves from the resulting evils there was no harm in continuing. They went on drinking until the day ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf invited some Companions to his home. After dinner they drank as usual, the time of maghrib came, they stood for prayer and chose one of their number to lead. He, being drunk, recited Sūrat al-Kāfirūn all wrong. Then the second step was taken with the verse forbidding the approach to prayer while intoxicated, making wine absolutely unlawful at prayer times while other times remained open. Some Companions had already given it up after the first verse, reasoning that what keeps one from prayer can hold no good. Now that prayer was forbidden in intoxication they kept away from whatever deprived them of prayer. Others continued at other hours until a further incident. ʿItbān ibn Mālik invited some Companions, among them Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ. After dinner wine was served, and the party, following another Arab custom, began reciting poetry about their own achievements. Saʿd recited a qaṣīdah satirising the Anṣār of Madīnah and eulogising his own tribe, and an angry young man of the Anṣār struck him with a camel's jawbone, wounding his head severely. Saʿd complained to the Prophet ﷺ, who raised his hands and prayed for a clear and conclusive guidance on wine, and the third verse, that of Sūrat al-Māʾidah, came down declaring it absolutely unlawful: "O believers! Intoxicants, gambling, idols, and drawing lots for decisions are all evil of Satan's handiwork. So shun them so you may be successful" (5:90).

**"They ˹also˺ ask you ˹O Prophet˺ what they should donate."**. The second question concerns how much should be spent in charity. One early report says it was asked by ʿAmr ibn al-Jamūḥ, who wanted to know what they should spend of their wealth.

**"Whatever you can spare."**. The answer is al-ʿafw, the surplus, which may be read in the accusative or in the nominative with an implied "it is". Al-Ḥakam relates from Miqsam that Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as whatever you can spare above the needs of your family, which is also the view of Ibn ʿUmar, together with Mujāhid, together with ʿAṭāʾ, together with ʿIkrimah, together with Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, together with Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb, together with al-Ḥasan, together with Qatādah, together with al-Qāsim, together with Sālim, together with ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī and al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas. Spend comfortably from what exceeds your needs, and do not spend what you need and ruin yourselves. Ibn Jarīr relates from Abū Hurayrah. Muslim records in his Ṣaḥīḥ, that a man told the Prophet ﷺ he had a dinar, and was told to spend it on himself. He said he had another, and was told to spend it on his wife, and another, to spend it on his children, and when he mentioned yet another, the Prophet ﷺ told him that he knew best where it should go. Muslim records from Jābir that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ told a man to begin with himself and give himself some charity, then if anything remained to spend it on his family, then on his relatives, and then on charitable purposes in every direction. A hadith addresses the son of Adam: if you spend what you can spare it is better for you, if you keep it, it is worse for you. You will not be blamed for keeping what is barely sufficient. The meaning is that one should not fall into personal hardship in this world through such spending, nor into the pain of answerability in the next by wasting away someone's due right. Initially this was the law of charity, but later Allah established the obligation of zakāh, requiring a set amount on specified possessions held for a specified period. One early report says this verse was abrogated by the verses on the poor-due.

**"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect"**. Just as He has stated and explained these commandments, He explains the rest of His verses concerning His commands and prohibitions, His promises and warnings, and the insignificance of this world, so that you may reflect on this life and the Hereafter. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that the reflection concerns the imminent end and brevity of this life and the imminent coming and permanence of the Hereafter, and that this world is evanescent. The answers to these questions reveal fundamental principles: whatever does more harm than good is to be shunned, wealth beyond one's real needs is to be spent for Allah's cause. In dealings with others one should avoid whatever may lead to evil and engage only in what brings some positive benefit to society. Grant that wine elates and that gambling is an easy way to money without work. From the moral angle, both lead to spiritual dangers that far outweigh any benefit, and the same rule applies to every human activity. Social celebrations, political initiatives and personal enterprises should all be weighed by this standard before one engages in them.

### The gradual prohibition of wine

Allah alone knows the full wisdom of His commands, but a close look at the Islamic legal code shows that the Sharīʿah leaves ample room for human feeling in following its dictates. Therefore, as to cause the least possible inconvenience. This is because the Qur'an says, "Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford" (2:286). This mercy and wisdom made Islam go slowly on forbidding wine. Four verses deal with it. The first is this verse of al-Baqarah, which identifies wine as sin-prone and corrupting and leaves the matter there, suggesting that drinking is worth giving up without directly commanding it. The second is the verse of al-Nisāʾ, forbidding it during prayer hours while leaving other times open. The third and fourth belong to al-Māʾidah and forbid it clearly and absolutely. The gradual method was used because it would have been far too hard on human nature to cut away a lifelong habit, above all addiction to intoxicants. The scholars say that changing an established habit is harder for a man than weaning is for a child used to its mother's breast. So Islam first stressed the evil, then forbade wine at prayer times, and finally, after a certain time, forbade it absolutely. The same wisdom that required gradualness before the prohibition required full force once it was promulgated. The Prophet ﷺ warned early that wine invites divine punishment, that it is the root of evils and indecencies, and that the drinker may go on to the worst possible sins, and in a hadith he said that wine and faith cannot be combined, reports found in al-Nasāʾī. In the Jāmiʿ of al-Tirmidhī, Anas reports that the Prophet ﷺ cursed ten people in connection with wine: the one who presses it, the one who has it pressed, the drinker, the server, the carrier, the one to whom it is carried, the seller, the buyer, the one who gives it as a gift and the one who lives on its proceeds. He did not stop at verbal teaching but made a binding proclamation that whoever had wine in his possession must deposit it at an appointed place.

### The matchless obedience of the Companions

As soon as the first order reached them, the Companions brought out all the wine stored in their homes and poured it into the streets. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar reported that when the Prophet's ﷺ crier went through the streets of Madīnah announcing that wine was forbidden, whoever held a vessel threw it away where he stood. Whoever had cups, goblets or flasks at home brought them out and smashed them. Anas was at that moment happily serving wine to a gathering of friends that included Abū Ṭalḥah, together with Abū ʿUbaydah ibn al-Jarrāḥ, Ubayy ibn Kaʿb and Suhayl, and when the crier's voice reached them everyone present called for the wine to be poured on the ground and the cups, goblets, ewers and pitchers to be broken. Some narrations say that at the very moment of the announcement everyone whose cup was near his lips flung it away as if electrified. That day wine ran through the streets of Madīnah like a stream of rainwater. For a long time afterwards rain would bring up the smell and colour of the wine soaked into the ground. When people were ordered to deposit their wine, little remained but the stock of casks and skins held in the market for sale. The Companions deposited even these without hesitation. The Prophet ﷺ went in person, slit many of the skins with his own hands and had the rest slit by the Companions. Another Companion, a merchant who imported wine from Syria, was on a business trip there and had invested his whole capital in a stock of wine. On his return he learned, before entering the city, that wine had been declared ḥarām. He stacked his cargo on a hillock by the road, went to the Prophet ﷺ and asked what he should do, and was ordered, in accordance with the divine command, to tear open the skins and pour the wine out on the ground. His love for Allah and His Messenger left no room for hesitation, and with his own hands poured his entire capital onto the sand of the slope. This is one of the great miracles of Islam, a demonstration of almost unrivalled obedience. Consider how hard it is to shake off any habit, when these people had been chronic drinkers who could not stay away from wine for long, and yet a single command from their Lord proclaimed by His Prophet ﷺ changed them so instantly that they came to hate the very wine and gambling to which they had been addicted. The verses and the events around them present a model of active Muslim response to a prohibition. Whether one calls it a miracle of Islam, a unique outcome of prophetic training or the natural product of the Islamic method of social change, its effectiveness was phenomenal. Compare the attempt at prohibition in the United States, backed by experts, lobbies, law, media and a constitutional amendment, which failed in the face of increased drinking and had to be abandoned. The secret of the difference is that Islam did not make law its sole means of reforming society. Law not being sufficient, it first prepared and refined the minds of its people with the golden prescription of deep devotion to their Creator, moderation in worldly living and genuine concern for the life to come. The revolution worked in this way produced matchless men ready to sacrifice life, property and honour at one call from their Prophet ﷺ, a preparation that went on through the whole Makkan period by rigorous spiritual training. Only when such a group was ready did the law come. The Americans used the vast powers of the media. But they had everything except concern for the Hereafter, which was the very lifeblood of the Muslims. The prescription is still valid, waiting for the peaceless people of the world to use it.

### The good and evil of wine

Wine’s commonly claimed benefits are familiar: a certain taste and feeling of well-being, a supposed increase of temporary energy and a glow on the face. These transient benefits hardly stand against the array of evils it breeds. In the body, drinking disorders the stomach, reduces appetite, alters the features, swells the waistline, damages liver and kidneys and contributes to all kinds of disease. Reason does not function under hard drink, and the effect lasts beyond the point at which the drinker claims to be sober, with the hangover as bad. A tempting stimulant and much-romanticised poison, wine works slowly despite the spirited defence of its users, and harms not only them but their children. Socially it breeds malice and enmity, a great evil in the sight of Islamic law, which is why Sūrat al-Māʾidah specifically mentions it: "Satan's plan is to stir up hostility and hatred between you with intoxicants and gambling" (5:91). One of its most harmful effects is that a man under its influence lets out guarded secrets, which is disastrous if he is a state official holding vital secrets, since passing them to an enemy agent can cause a coup, a political loss or a threat to national defence, and clever spies always watch for such chances. So drinking is not only an evil in itself but the mother of other evils, making men ready to commit the worst crimes against their fellows. Its spiritual evils are equally plain. This is because the Qur'an says that it keeps you from remembering Allah and from prayer. Then asks whether you will not desist.

### Intoxicants in Sūrat al-Naḥl

To complete the frame of reference, another mention of intoxicants, in a different context, should be brought in: "And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision. Surely in this is a sign for those who understand" (16:67). The preceding verses describe the blessings by which Allah produces human food through unique mechanisms, as in, "We give you to drink of what is in their bellies, between filth and blood, pure milk, sweet to drinkers" (16:66), where "We give you to drink" implies that milk needs no human processing. The next verse says that man procures things to eat and makes things that benefit him, hinting that human processing plays a part. The account also states that through the human factor two kinds of product arise, an intoxicant and a good provision, dates and grapes eaten fresh or dried and stored. Allah in His perfect creativity gave man these fruits and the right to use them as food. What he makes of them is his choice, intoxicants that spoil his reason or food and energy. So the verse cannot be used to argue for the lawfulness of intoxicating wine. Its purpose is to describe the gifts of nature and the forms in which they may be used, all part of the one blessing, and the fact that someone uses a thing wrongfully does not stop it being a blessing. Therefore, the context needed no details of permissible and impermissible use. Yet there is a subtle indication: when sakar, intoxicant, is set against rizqan ḥasanan, a good provision, it follows that an intoxicant is not a good provision. The majority take sakar to mean what intoxicates, as Rūḥ al-Maʿānī, together with al-Qurṭubī and al-Jaṣṣāṣ record. The ummah agrees that these verses are Makkan while the prohibition of wine came later in Madīnah al-Ṭayyibah. Though wine was lawful when they were revealed and Muslims drank freely, even then the verse hinted that drinking is not good, before the clear and forceful command of prohibition came.

### The prohibition of gambling

The word maysir is a verbal noun that lexically means to distribute. One who distributes is a yāsir. In the Jāhiliyyah several games of chance were common in Arabia. In one of them a camel was slaughtered and shares of its meat were gambled for, some winning more than one share and others none, and the loser who won nothing paid for the whole camel. The meat went to the poor, not to the gamblers themselves. The catch of this game was that it benefited the poor while displaying the gamblers' generosity, so that it became a matter of pride and anyone who would not join in was derided as a miser and a wretch. Because of this element of distribution, qimār is called maysir. All the Companions and Successors unanimously hold that maysir includes every form of qimār and that all of it is ḥarām. The early authorities and al-Jaṣṣāṣ in Aḥkām al-Qurʾān report that ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās, together with Ibn ʿUmar, together with Qatādah, together with Muʿāwiyah ibn Ṣāliḥ, together with ʿAṭāʾ and Ṭāwūs said that qimār of every kind is maysir, even children's games with dice and walnuts. Ibn ʿAbbās said that mukhāṭarah, putting something at stake, falls under qimār. Ibn Sīrīn said that whatever involves stakes is included in maysir. A stake is a deal that hangs between profit and loss, where one may get a great deal or nothing, exactly like modern lotteries. All such forms are qimār and maysir and ḥarām. Gambling has therefore been defined in the chapter on wagering (al-khaṭar) as a deal in which ownership of a valuable thing depends upon a contingency with two equally possible outcomes, so that there are two equal possibilities of total profit or total loss: the penalty may fall on A or equally on B. The rule, in sum, is that all forms in vogue in the past, current today or likely to emerge in future are maysir, qimār and gambling. Prize crosswords for which participants pay a fee and commercial lotteries, among whatever other forms exist, are all gambling. But a prize offered by one side only, to be given to whoever performs a certain feat, does not matter, provided no fee is taken from the performer. This is because the deal then hangs not between benefit and harm but between benefit and no benefit. For this reason authentic hadiths declare chess, backgammon and the like unlawful where money or property is staked on winning or losing, and playing cards for money likewise fall under maysir. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records from Buraydah that the Prophet ﷺ likened playing backgammon to staining one’s hands with swine’s blood and flesh. ʿAlī said that chess is part of maysir. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar that chess is worse than backgammon. In the early days of Islam gambling, like drinking, was permissible. When the opening of Sūrat al-Rūm foretold that the Byzantines, defeated by the Persians, would prevail again within a few years, and the disbelievers of Makkah denied it, Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq challenged them to a bet, something like qimār, that they would pay a certain sum if the Byzantines won within so many years. When the prophecy came true he collected the wager and brought it to the Prophet ﷺ, who was pleased at the outcome but told him to give the money away in charity. This is because Allah had kept His Messenger ﷺ protected from what was then permissible but would later be forbidden, which is why he always kept aloof from wine and gambling, as did particular Companions. A narration says that Jibrīl told the Prophet ﷺ that Allah loved four traits in Jaʿfar al-Ṭayyār. When asked about them Jaʿfar said that he had never spoken of them to anyone but would now relate them: he saw that wine ruins reason and never went near it, he saw that idols neither benefit nor harm and never worshipped them even in the Jāhiliyyah, his keen sense of honour for his wife and daughters kept him from adultery. He recognised lying as unbridled meanness and disgrace and never lied even in the Jāhiliyyah.

### Social ill-effects of gambling

The Qur'an says of gambling what it says of wine: there is some benefit in it, but the harm is greater. Everyone knows that it can turn a pauper into a millionaire overnight, but few know how evil it is economically, socially and spiritually. In gambling the gain of one depends on the loss of the other. The winner's whole gain is the loser's whole loss. This is because the transaction adds nothing to national wealth or production. Wealth is simply sucked out of one man into another, so that qimār ruins a people comprehensively and destroys human morality. Man, who should be an agent of public good and a model of sympathy and sacrifice, sinks to the level of a beast at the gaming table, seeing his own life in his brother's death, delighting in his distress, seeking gain in his loss and bending every faculty to selfishness. Permissible trade is the opposite, for buyer and seller both profit, and wealth circulates and grows through the exchange of goods. Gambling is harmful first because the addict loses the very habit of earning a livelihood, dreaming of grabbing another's property in an instant by a bet without work, and some scholars derive maysir from yusr, ease. This is because it takes another's wealth so easily. Even when a game involves only two or four people these harms are plain. But in this modern age, called the age of progress by the short-sighted, wines of every kind bear new names and labels, ever-new forms of interest are given the safe name of banking. Gambling has found thousands of dubious forms. Some are so collective that a whole nation's contributions add up bit by bit, any loss is spread over everyone and passes unnoticed, and when a sum from the total reaches one person his gain is conspicuous while the collective loss is ignored, so that people are led to think these new forms valid though they contain every harm of small-scale gambling. From another angle the harm of such large-scale gambling is far worse, since its effects reach further and spell the ruin of a whole nation. Its natural result is that the wealth of the common people keeps shrinking while the capital of a few capitalists keeps growing, until the nation's wealth is concentrated in a few individuals and families, as can be seen daily in stock markets and other forms of qimār. Islamic economic strategy has a principle of its own here: every deal that siphons the wealth of a whole community into the coffers of a few is ḥarām, and the Qur'an's rule for distributing the spoils of war among different segments of society aims precisely to prevent wealth from concentrating in a few hands. Gambling, like wine, causes disputes that reach the point of hatred and animosity, fatal to civilised society, which is why 5:91 mentions both together, and its effect, like that of wine, is a reckless absorption that leaves no room for prayer or remembrance of Allah. Perhaps this is why the Qur'an names wine and gambling side by side in identical terms, since qimār has an intoxicating pull of its own that blinds a man to what is good or bad for him. Its basic evil is that one devours another's property without any reasonable compensation or brotherly consideration, which the Qur'an forbids: "Do not consume one another's wealth unjustly" (2:188). The destroyer of many a home, gambling does not confine its effects to the gambler but spreads them to his whole family and to the wider circle of people who have transactions, contracts and loans with him, all of whom suffer when he becomes insolvent. Finally, amid the rising addiction to new forms of qimār, it distorts the natural attitude to work and fixes it on imagined gains, so that instead of developing his physical and mental skills to increase his earnings steadily a man becomes obsessed with ways of usurping the earnings of others.

### Juristic rules

Two rules follow from the verse. First, the acknowledgement of some benefit in wine and gambling, combined with the instruction to desist, shows that the presence of material benefit in a thing or act does not prevent its being declared ḥarām. A medicine or food is called harmful when its harm far exceeds its benefit, for otherwise nothing in the world, not even the worst thing, would be wholly without benefit. Deadly poison, the snake, the scorpion and the wild beasts all have their uses, yet in general they are called harmful and people are warned against them. Likewise, things whose evils outweigh their benefits are declared ḥarām under the Sharīʿah. No crime, theft, robbery, adultery, kidnapping or fraud, is devoid of some benefit, for otherwise no sane person would go near them. The most accomplished criminals are known for their tact and intelligence. Some benefit appears in every crime, but since the harm is greater no sane person calls them useful and permissible, and on this principle the Sharīʿah forbade wine and gambling. Second, the elimination of evil takes precedence over the acquisition of benefit: where something yields a benefit along with harm, the benefit must be abandoned to stay safe from the harm. A benefit that comes with harm attached is ignored.

### The wine of recognition

In the devotional reading, some earthly servants of Allah drink recognition’s wine and become intoxicated with love’s cup. In this world there is no more than a whiff of that wine's reality and only a glimpse of that intoxication. The world is a prison. What can a prison bear? But tomorrow, in the assembly of "serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss" (56:89), the arena of union with the Beloved, the servants will gaze upon the Real, and if the hope of union adds so much to life, what must union itself be? A man in turmoil came into a wine merchant's shop with one dirham and asked for wine. When told there was none left, he said that he was already in turmoil and could not bear the reality of wine. Therefore, let the merchant show him a drop to smell and see how drunk he became and what turmoil he stirred up. To one servant Allah gives the wine of bewilderment from the cup of awe. He is drunk with bewilderment, asking the guide of the bewildered to take his hand. This is because the work is hard and the pain has no cure. He has no use for the stories of the drunkards. To another He gives the wine of recognition from the storeroom of hope. He stands at the top of the street of yearning saying that one day luck will enter his door, one day the sun of elation will rise over him, one day the Beloved will glance his way and his grief will end. To yet another He gives the wine of union from the cup of love, shows him the way to the carpet of bold expansiveness and gives him a place on the leaning-place of intimacy.

### In short

- Asked by ʿUmar, together with Muʿādh and others, the verse took the first of four gradual steps against wine: here its sin outweighs its benefit, then 4:43 forbade prayer in intoxication, then 5:90-91 forbade it absolutely, each step prompted by ʿUmar's prayer and by incidents at the houses of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf and ʿItbān ibn Mālik (Abū Maysarah in Aḥmad, together with Abū Dāwūd, together with al-Tirmidhī, together with al-Nasāʾī).
- Khamr is whatever clouds the mind (ʿUmar), and its material benefits, taste, energy, trade, are dwarfed by its harm to body, reason, religion, society and state secrets, so that the Prophet ﷺ cursed ten people connected with it (Anas in al-Tirmidhī).
- Gradual prohibition suited human weakness (2:286), but once given the command was obeyed instantly: wine ran in the streets of Madīnah, skins were slit, and a merchant poured out his whole Syrian cargo, a contrast with the failure of American prohibition, explained by concern for the Hereafter.
- Sūrat al-Naḥl's mention of intoxicants (16:66-67) describes a natural gift misused, not a licence. Maysir covers every stake between gain and loss (Ibn ʿAbbās, together with Ibn Sīrīn), from Jāhilī camel-lotteries to lotteries, prize puzzles with fees, backgammon (Buraydah) and chess (ʿAlī, Ibn ʿUmar), but not a one-sided prize. Abū Bakr's bet on the Byzantines predates the ban. Gambling transfers wealth without creating it, ruins homes and nations, breeds hatred (5:91) and devours others' wealth unjustly (2:188).
- Benefit does not prevent prohibition, averting harm outranks securing benefit. Charity is from the surplus beyond family needs (Ibn ʿAbbās and many others), beginning with oneself, wife, children and relatives (Abū Hurayrah, together with Jābir in Muslim), a rule later supplemented by zakāh. Allah explains His signs so that believers reflect on this fleeting world and the lasting Hereafter, where the wine of recognition and union is drunk in full (56:89).
