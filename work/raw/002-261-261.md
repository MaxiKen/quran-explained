<<<VERSE 2:261>>>
Giving for Allah's sake is pictured as planting rather than simply losing a possession: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains." A small seed becomes a great harvest. The reward is not limited to the initial amount, and the closing words leave room for generosity beyond the seven hundred illustrated here.

### Phrase by phrase

**"The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah"** The expenditure seeks Allah's pleasure and obeys Him. Sa'id bin Jubayr explains it generally as spending in obedience. Makhul particularly mentions jihad and its practical needs, including weapons and the keeping of horses. The broader explanation also includes hajj, relief for poor people, widows and orphans, and help for relatives and friends.

**"is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains."** One seed yields seven hundred grains. Likewise, a believer's expenditure receives a multiplied return in the Hereafter. The growing plant touches the heart more vividly than a bare statement of the number. Allah causes deeds to grow for the person doing them, just as He grows the planted seed.

**"And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills."** Seven hundred is not an upper limit on His gift. The multiplication is connected with sincerity, acceptance and whether the person deserves it. A deed cannot be judged solely by the visible amount spent, for Allah knows its intention.

**"For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing"** His bounty extends beyond the needs of His creatures. He is neither impoverished by rewarding them nor unaware of what each deserves. He knows both the expenditure and the heart from which it comes.

### What kinds of giving are included?

Several words describe spending for Allah: infaq means expenditure, it'am feeding and sadaqah charity. They can embrace different forms of giving intended to please Him. Some are fard or wajib, obligatory. Others are nafl or mustahabb, voluntary or recommended. The recurring general word infaq indicates that the instruction is not restricted to one narrow category.

Zakah has its own distinct designation and particular requirements governing who gives and who receives it. Recognising the broad meaning of charitable expenditure does not dissolve those special rules. The encouragement here reaches obligatory and voluntary gifts without making their legal conditions identical.

Ibn 'Abbas says that a dirham spent in jihad or hajj receives the reward of seven hundred dirhams. The report is given with reference to Ahmad's Musnad. It applies the seed's multiplication to actual expenditure while keeping the return as reward with Allah, not a guarantee of immediate financial profit in this life.

### What does the camel report show?

Ahmad records from Abu Mas'ud that a man gave a camel with its bridle for Allah's cause. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ told him that his return at the Resurrection would be seven hundred camels, also with bridles. Muslim and An-Nasa'i record the hadith as well.

In Muslim's wording the man explicitly offers the camel for Allah's sake. The reply gives concrete shape to the verse's promise: the offered animal is not forgotten, and its return exceeds the original gift many times. The bridle too is included in the description.

This is encouragement to give with trust, not a reason to treat the recipient as a debtor to the giver. The gift is directed towards Allah's pleasure, and its lasting return comes from Him.

### Why is fasting mentioned with multiplied deeds?

Ahmad records from Abu Hurayrah a saying of the Prophet ﷺ about good deeds receiving ten times their value, seven hundred times and further multiplication as Allah wills. Muslim also records it. Fasting is singled out as belonging to Allah, who grants its reward, because the fasting person leaves food and desire for His sake.

The account describes two joys: one when breaking the fast, the other when meeting the Lord. It says the fasting person's breath is more pleasing to Allah than musk. Fasting is also described as a shield, protecting against sin.

The report broadens the view beyond money. A person's restraint and worship can also receive a reward exceeding ordinary calculation. It reinforces both the greatness of Allah's generosity and the central place of acting for Him rather than for an audience.

### Why does the grain image include conditions?

A farmer cannot expect the same result from any seed placed carelessly anywhere. Good seed, suitable ground and knowledgeable cultivation matter. The picture therefore teaches more than the size of the harvest. It also directs attention to the quality, intention and placement of a charitable gift.

First, the property should be halal, lawfully acquired and pure. A hadith states that Allah accepts only what is pure and lawful. Giving does not make unlawful earnings acceptable merely by moving them into another person's hands.

Second, the giver needs sound intention and righteous conduct. Someone seeking display resembles a person wasting seed through ignorant planting. The outward act may be recognisable as expenditure, yet the purpose directs it towards the wrong harvest.

Third, the recipient should deserve the assistance. Care is needed to find those entitled to it, rather than scattering resources without regard to need or proper use. The example calls for deliberate generosity, not indifference disguised as liberality.

The method of giving should likewise conform to the sunnah. The next instruction particularly guards against advertising favours or causing pain to recipients. Such restraint preserves the gift's value instead of allowing the giver's conduct to spoil it. Merely emptying a treasury does not by itself establish the promised merit.

### Where does the giver plant the deed?

Every act can be understood as a seed. If its aim is people's admiration, it is planted in this temporary world's soil. If its aim is Allah's pleasure, its harvest belongs to the enduring Hereafter.

The reflection uses the immense increase of grain as a familiar illustration of a return beyond the initial seed. It even mentions a thousand grains in ordinary growth. The point is abundance, not replacing the verse's seven ears and hundred grains with a different formula. A small-looking act can have a lasting fruit beyond what an observer can count.

### How does this begin the financial guidance?

The financial discussion introduces a sequence concerned with charity, riba and loans. It describes four sections running from verse 262 to verse 283 and calls them 21 verses. Those are the boundaries and count supplied in that discussion.

Its first concern is how to use resources beyond one's needs to support poor and needy people, through sadaqah and khayrat, charity and beneficence. Its second is avoiding the taking and giving of riba, interest or usury, which it calls haram, unlawful. The discussion proceeds from encouragement and conditions of giving to prohibited dealings and permissible borrowing.

The economic reflection contrasts this guidance with conflict among capitalism, socialism and communism, including their readjustment after the Soviet collapse. Its claim is that rightly practised financial guidance provides an answer to destructive rivalry. The emphasis here is not a detailed comparison of those systems but the positive duty to share and the prohibition of exploitative financial conduct.

This opening instruction supplies the motive for the coming rules. The discussion distinguishes the merit of giving, the conditions preserving it and attitudes that destroy it. It then contrasts acceptable and unacceptable gifts through two illustrations. The seed's growth belongs to the acceptable side of that contrast.

### What is the inward form of expenditure?

The allusive reading distinguishes giving wealth from giving one's states and spirit. Wealth given as alms receives the Garden. A heart surrendering its self-centred claims seeks nearness to Allah Himself. The distinction concerns the object of longing, not a rejection of practical charity.

The inner gift requires sidq, constant sincerity. It relinquishes worldly portions that compete with devotion and accepts the course of Allah's decree without inward resentment. A poetic image says that the lover wishes to remain while the beloved wishes departure, so the lover leaves personal preference for the other's wish.

Worshippers, called ʿābidūn, are distinguished from ecstatics, wājidūn. The former receive multiplication beyond reckoning, described in this reflection through seventyfold increase. The latter are pictured as finding no deed sufficient to present before the Beloved and no harm easily dismissed. Their language conveys humility and exacting love rather than a new calculation replacing the verse.

### In short

- Giving for Allah's sake resembles seed growing into a multiplied harvest.
- The promised reward can exceed seven hundredfold, according to Allah's bounty and knowledge.
- Lawful property, sincere intention, appropriate recipients and a sound method matter.
- The instruction embraces different kinds of charity while preserving zakah's particular rules.
- The inward reading adds surrender of self-centred desire to the outward gift of wealth.
