<<<VERSE 3:7>>>
The verse begins, “He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book,” then describes its clear and elusive passages. Coming after the affirmation of Allah’s oneness, it answers doubts about revelation and gives readers both a way to approach it and a warning about what can go wrong. Clear verses form the foundation. Passages whose full meaning is uncertain should be read in light of those foundations, not used to force a preferred claim. Those grounded in knowledge accept the whole Book as coming from Allah, while people who chase ambiguity to create doubt risk leading themselves and others astray.

### Phrase by phrase

**“He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book”** introduces the Qur’an as revelation from Allah to Muhammad ﷺ, brought by Gabriel. It is the shared origin of the clear verses and the verses whose meaning is not immediately apparent. Its parts are not in conflict with one another.

**“of which some verses are precise”** describes the muhkam verses, those whose meaning is established and clear. **“they are the foundation of the Book”** explains their role. They are Umm al-Kitab, the Mother of the Book, because they provide the basic principles to which other passages are referred. They include teachings that are clear enough to be acted on: obligations, limits, what is allowed and prohibited, and rules that are to be believed and followed. One explanation calls these the majority of the Qur’an and the reference point when a passage is disputed. Another says their clear meanings can be understood by a reader who knows the rules of Arabic grammar. 

The clear verses are also described as containing proof of Allah, protection for His servants, and answers to false claims. Muḥammad bin Ishaq bin Yasar describes them as proof of the Lord, protection for servants, and a rebuttal of opponents. Their meaning is not to be distorted. One explanation gives the command to recite what Allah has made binding (6:151) as an example. The Qur’an can be called clear in the sense that it has no imperfection, and its parts can also be described as resembling one another in beauty and truthfulness. This is why readers are asked to reflect on it as a whole: a revelation from Allah would not contain contradictions (4:82).

**“while others are elusive”** refers to the mutashabih verses, whose meaning is not clear to everyone and may appear to allow more than one interpretation. Some explanations include passages that describe unseen matters, such as Allah’s attributes and the realities of the next life, which cannot be fully grasped through ordinary language in this world. For example, “a human hand” can be understood directly, while “Allah’s hand” is an allegorical expression whose full reality exceeds human perception. Another classifies among them abrogated passages, parables, oaths, and matters to be believed in but not directly put into practice. Some say the opening letter combinations of surahs are examples. A separate explanation describes the people well-grounded in knowledge as those with a sound understanding of the Torah, naming ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām and his companions. A reading of those letters by numerical values, called al-Jumal, is also mentioned as something some people have pursued.

**“Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations”** describes people who leave the clear foundations and build arguments from passages with several possible readings. They seek Al-Fitnah, meaning confusion and division, and try to make the unclear wording support their own claims. One explanation says that such arguments can be used to mislead followers and make an innovation look as if it were proved by the Qur’an. The verse therefore warns against turning an uncertain phrase into a weapon against a clear teaching.

The example given is the claim that ʿĪsā (Jesus) is divine because he is described as Allah’s spirit and word given to Mary. That claim sets those expressions against clear verses identifying ʿĪsā as a servant blessed by Allah (43:59) and comparing his creation with Adam’s (3:59). Those clear passages establish that ʿĪsā is created and a messenger, not divine. The same method applies elsewhere: an interpretation of an unclear verse must not contradict a clear one.

**“but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah”** raises a question about where to pause in the reading. Some understand the verse to mean that Allah alone knows the true reality of the elusive passages. Others connect the next words to this phrase and hold that people firmly grounded in knowledge can understand their explanation, though not the unseen reality behind them. Both readings distinguish what people can explain from what remains known only to Allah.

The word ta’wil can mean either the true reality to which something ultimately leads or its explanation. If it means the ultimate reality, only Allah knows it. For example, the full reality of the promised Resurrection is beyond present experience (7:53). If it means explanation, scholars can understand some of what is meant. The word is used for explaining a dream (12:36), and for the later realization of a dream (12:100). A statement about Allah coming with the angels in rows (89:22) is also explained in a straightforward way as describing what will happen. These different senses help explain why scholars differ over the stopping point in the verse.

One report from Ibn ʿAbbās divides explanation into four kinds: what Arabs know through their language, what no one may claim ignorance of, what scholars know, and what only Allah knows. Ibn ʿAbbās also said he was among those grounded in knowledge who knew the explanation. The Prophet ﷺ prayed that he be given knowledge of the religion and taught the interpretation. These reports are consistent with the view that some meanings can be understood while ultimate realities remain beyond human reach.

**“As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say”** introduces their response. **“We believe in this ˹Quran˺”** and **“it is all from our Lord”** express their humility and trust. Those grounded in knowledge accept both the clear and the elusive verses as true and do not force the latter to contradict the former. They follow interpretations transmitted through the Companions and early generations, and hold the Qur’an and Sunnah together. They may explain what can be understood, but acknowledge the limits of their knowledge. They do not boast about what they know or belittle others. Ibn al-Mundhir records Nafiʿ ibn Yazid describing them as humble before Allah, seeking His pleasure and not exaggerating about those above them or looking down on those below them.

**“But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason”** means that reflection and sound understanding are needed to keep the whole Book in view. A person must not isolate an uncertain phrase from the clear teachings that explain it.

### What are the clear and elusive verses?

The clear verses are the foundation for belief and practice. They include established rulings and teachings that can be understood and acted upon. The elusive verses are those whose definite meaning is not apparent, whether because they concern realities beyond present human experience or because their wording can carry several readings. Explanations differ over whether abrogated rulings, parables, oaths, and the opening letters belong in this category. The practical rule offered across these explanations is consistent: read the less clear in light of the clear, and do not use an uncertain reading to overturn an established teaching.

There is also a distinction between the Qur’an being clear as a whole and containing passages that are elusive to some readers. The whole Book is described as free of defect and internally consistent. Its parts resemble one another in truth and beauty. That does not mean every reader can immediately determine the full meaning of every passage.

### Why do people pursue ambiguity?

The verse criticizes not careful study but a heart set on distortion. People who seek confusion take an expression with several possible meanings and choose the one that serves their agenda. The example of ʿĪsā shows why this is dangerous. A reader cannot isolate descriptions such as “spirit” or “word” while ignoring the clear statements that identify him as a created servant. Clear verses govern interpretation. They are not discarded whenever an ambiguous phrase seems to offer a desired conclusion.

The material gives historical examples of people who pursued hidden or numerical readings. Some Jewish opponents, including Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf, Ḥuyayy ibn Akhtab, and Judayy ibn Akhtab, are said to have treated the opening letters as numbers through al-Jumal and tried to predict how long the community would last. Another explanation names particular people who sought discord by fixing a future meaning to the letters. These accounts are presented as examples of the very danger the verse names: turning what is uncertain into a claim that serves one’s own purpose.

### Where should the reader pause?

The verse can be read with a pause after “except Allah.” On that reading, only Allah knows the full reality of the elusive verses, and those grounded in knowledge continue by saying they believe in all of them. Another reading joins “those well-grounded in knowledge” to the preceding phrase. They know the explainable meanings, while accepting that the final reality belongs to Allah. Both readings are reported, and the distinction in ta’wil helps explain them. If ta’wil means the final reality, the pause after Allah is fitting. If it means explanation, knowledgeable people may share in understanding it. A pause at this point is reported from ʿĀʾishah, ʿUrwah, Abū al-Shaʿthāʾ, and Abū Nahik. Others pause after the words about those firmly grounded in knowledge, taking them as part of the statement about who understands the explanation.

A hadith reported from ʿĀʾishah warns against following the unclear passages to sow discord. The Prophet ﷺ recited this verse and said that when people are seen pursuing its ambiguous parts, they are the people Allah has described, so believers should beware of them. Aḥmad recorded the report, and similar wording is recorded by al-Bukhārī, Muslim, and Abū Dāwūd.

Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī reported another warning from the Prophet ﷺ about three dangers for his ummah, the community of believers. One is that people become wealthy, envy one another, and fall into division and bloodshed. Another is that people treat the Book carelessly, as unqualified readers claim complete understanding from translations and make the verses whose meaning is not meant to be fully grasped the focus of argument. The third is that people stop increasing their knowledge after gaining some. The report is attributed to al-Ṭabarānī. It warns both against overconfidence and against allowing learning to stagnate.

### What does an inward reading add?

An allusive reading begins with the word “He.” It treats this not as a name or an attribute but as a pointer to Allah’s being, beyond location and beyond the limits of created attributes. It also reflects on the pronoun’s two letters, h and w: they begin in the throat at different points, suggesting that all things begin through Allah’s power and return by His decree.

A story is told of Shurayḥ ʿĀbid seeing a dervish in the sanctuary calling on Allah as “He, other than whom there is no he.” A voice from the unseen tells him that the reward for his first utterance will be recorded until the Resurrection. Another story tells of a person asked his name, origin, and wish, who answered “He” each time. When the name Allah was spoken, he was overcome and died. These stories express the inward reading’s focus on turning wholly toward Allah. A poetic reflection says that people may picture the divine name in a hundred ways, yet loving remembrance leaves them aware of their inability to describe Him fully.

This reading also distinguishes the clear and difficult passages as the outward law, or shariah, and the inward reality, or haqiqah. The clear guides ordinary people toward blessing through understanding and practice. The difficult points toward realities that cannot be fully grasped. This allusive reflection does not cancel the practical rule given above: the clear foundation remains the guide for reading what is less clear.

### In short

- The clear verses are the foundation for belief and practice. Less clear passages must be understood in their light.
- People go astray when they use ambiguity to create division or to force a preferred claim, as in arguments about ʿĪsā.
- Scholars differ over whether only Allah knows the ta’wil or whether knowledgeable people can explain some of it. The full reality remains with Allah.
- The Prophet ﷺ warned against chasing ambiguous passages to cause discord and against neglecting sound, continuing knowledge.
- The people of understanding affirm the whole Qur’an, remain humble about its limits, and ask Allah for steadfast guidance.

<<<VERSE 3:8>>>
Those grounded in knowledge answer the warnings of the previous verse with a prayer: “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us.” They know that understanding and faith are gifts that can be lost, so they ask Allah to keep them firm and grant them mercy. The prayer does not claim that a person’s learning or good deeds make steadfastness an entitlement. It asks the Giver for help.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us”** asks Allah to keep the heart from turning away from truth after being shown the right path. The prayer follows the warning about people who pursue unclear verses to support false readings. The worshippers ask not to become like them. One explanation says guidance and deviation are in Allah’s power: He inclines a heart toward good or allows it to turn away. For that reason, people concerned about faith ask Him for firmness rather than relying on themselves.

The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have prayed, “O Turner of hearts, make our hearts firm upon the faith You have chosen.” Umm Salamah reported that he used to make this supplication, and Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Ibn Jarīr recorded it. The prayer asks that Allah not allow confusion or straying to take hold, but keep the heart steady in service and in the Sunnah.

**“Grant us Your mercy”** asks for more than a reward earned by deeds. The prayer seeks the mercy that strengthens the heart, increases faith and certainty, and protects it from misguidance. An inward explanation stresses that mercy is Allah’s generosity, not payment for acts of obedience. Human actions are not a claim on Allah. The servant asks Him to overlook shortcomings and give from His bounty.

**“You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺”** recognizes Allah as the One who gives guidance, mercy, and firmness. Another explanation reads the title as the One who gave prophethood and Islam to Muhammad ﷺ. The prayer therefore looks to Allah as the giver of both the message and the ability to remain faithful to it.

### What does the prayer teach about steadfastness?

The prayer follows the account of two kinds of readers: those who stay with the clear foundations and those whose hearts turn toward confusion. Even a person grounded in knowledge needs Allah’s help. The Prophet ﷺ himself often asked for firmness. The prayer also gives a way to meet uncertainty: acknowledge human limits, avoid arguments that lead away from guidance, and ask Allah for mercy and steadiness.

An inward reading describes supplication as reaching its aim when the heart is clear, the present moment is given to remembrance, and the tongue is sincere. Its deeper request is for a life of service shaped by the Sunnah. Mercy is sought as a gift, not as a wage. A spiritual teacher from a Tariqah, a path of spiritual discipline, reflected on the verse about deeds becoming scattered dust (25:23) and found comfort in the thought that a person may be freed from relying on inadequate actions and attach the heart instead to Allah’s mercy.

### In short

- The people of knowledge ask Allah to keep their hearts steady after guidance.
- They seek mercy that strengthens faith, not a payment earned by their deeds.
- The Prophet ﷺ also prayed for firmness, and the verse teaches believers to depend on Allah for it.

<<<VERSE 3:9>>>
The prayer continues with confidence in the promised gathering: “Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity”. Allah will bring people together for judgment and will not fail to keep His promise. The believers ask for steadfastness because they are concerned with the final account, not only with the ease or success of this life.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity”** affirms that all people will be brought back after death. They will stand before Allah, who will judge between them and settle what they disputed. The reckoning, the crossing over the Bridge, the Scale, Paradise, and Hell are also named among the realities of that promised Day. Their deeds will be answered with reward or punishment.

**“for the ˹promised˺ Day”** identifies the Day of Resurrection. **“about which there is no doubt”** states that it is certain, not a possibility or a figure of speech. One inward reading distinguishes two gatherings. In this life, the hearts of those who love Allah gather in nearness and remembrance. In the next, all people gather for reward and punishment and face its terrors.

**“Surely Allah does not break His promise”** means that the promised return, judgment, and recompense will take place. The prayer’s mention of the promise shows why the worshippers ask to remain guided: they seek to meet that Day on the path of guidance.

Reports about the preceding warning against chasing ambiguous verses clarify why steadfastness matters. In a hadith, ʿĀʾishah says the Prophet ﷺ recited the warning and told believers to beware of those who pursue ambiguity. Bukhari and Muslim recorded this report. Al-Tabarani recorded Abu Musa al-Ashʿari reporting the Prophet’s warning about three dangers for the ummah: wealth that breeds envy and bloodshed, careless treatment of the Holy Qur’an, and abandoning the effort to increase knowledge. These reports urge believers to say they accept the whole revelation and to remain humble about its limits.

The discussion of the preceding prayer includes Ibn Abbas’s account of different kinds of ta’wil and the report about where to pause. A'ishah, Urwah, Abu Ash-Sha'tha, and Abu Nahik are among those named in that discussion. Nafiʿ bin Yazid describes the people grounded in knowledge as humble and moderate. The prayer in this verse is a supplication for guidance before the final gathering. An inward reading contrasts the gathering of lovers in nearness today with the gathering for reward and punishment tomorrow, including ʿiqāb, punishment, and the terrors or aḥwāl of that Day.

### In short

- All humanity will be gathered after death for judgment.
- The Day is certain, and Allah will fulfill His promise of return and recompense.
- The prayer for guidance is made with the final meeting with Allah in view.

<<<VERSE 3:10>>>
The verse warns that “neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah.” Possessions and family cannot protect a person from judgment or punishment. The verse says the disbelievers will be fuel for the Fire. These verses prepare for the coming declaration that the opponents will be overcome. The warning is not an argument against having wealth or children. An inward reading says possessions can help a believer when used rightly, but they become a danger when a person exceeds what is needed and lets them lead to rebellion.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Indeed, neither the wealth nor children”** names two forms of worldly security people may rely on. Abundance in property or descendants cannot shield a person from Allah’s judgment. Other passages warn that wealth and children can be part of a test in this life (9:55), and that a brief period of worldly prosperity should not deceive the disbeliever (3:196, 3:197).

**“of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah”** means these things cannot save those who reject Allah, His messengers, and His revelation from punishment. The warning is also expressed by saying that excuses will not help the wrongdoer on the Day when the curse and evil home are his (40:52). Another passage calls those worshipped besides Allah, together with their worshippers, fuel for Hell (21:98).

Some explanations identify the disbelievers in the immediate setting with Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf and his companions. Another identifies them as Abū Jahl and his companions. The verse’s warning is broader than either identification: wealth and children do not avail those who disbelieve.

**“and they will be the fuel for the Fire”** means that they themselves become what feeds the Fire. A grammatical note distinguishes the word for fuel from a similar word for the act of burning. Their possessions and children do not rescue them from this end.

### Can worldly possessions serve a good purpose?

An inward explanation distinguishes the disbeliever’s reliance on possessions from the believer’s responsible use of them. Wealth can help a believer when its obligations are met and it is used to seek the lasting good of the next life. Muṣṭafā (Muhammad ﷺ) is reported to have praised wholesome possessions in the hands of a wholesome person and described them as a help toward mindfulness of Allah. The Qur’an’s instruction to use what Allah has given to seek the next life is cited in this connection (28:77).

The same reading traces how material provision can support worship and tawhid, the oneness of Allah: food, drink, and clothing sustain the body. The body can then perform acts of obedience, and those acts can serve the heart’s recognition of Allah, the Lord of the Worlds. But wealth should remain within the measure of sufficiency. When people exceed that measure and become self-satisfied, possessions can lead to rebellion, as the Qur’an warns (96:6-7). The Prophet ﷺ prayed that his family’s food be kept at a sufficient level. A lawful estate belonging to Abu'l-Qāsim Kurragānī is given as an example of having enough without making abundance the goal.

Thus, the verse does not make possessions inherently evil. It warns that neither wealth nor family can substitute for faith or protect a person from judgment. Used with gratitude and restraint, material provision can support a life directed toward Allah. Treated as an ultimate source of security, it fails and may become a cause of disobedience.

### In short

- Wealth and children cannot protect disbelievers from Allah’s judgment.
- The verse describes those who reject faith as fuel for the Fire.
- Possessions can support a believer’s worship when used responsibly and kept within sufficiency, but they cannot replace faith or become a guarantee of safety.

<<<VERSE 3:11>>>
The verse compares the fate of the disbelievers with “the people of Pharaoh and those before them.” They rejected Allah’s signs, so He seized them for their sins. Their history is a warning that rejecting messengers and revelation has consequences, and that no one can escape Allah’s power.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them”** compares the current opponents with earlier communities who rejected the messengers sent to them. One explanation takes the phrase to mean their conduct and practice resembled Pharaoh’s people. Ad-Dahhak, reporting Ibn ʿAbbās, explains it this way. Another reads it as the same outcome and fate. The people of ʿĀd and Thamūd are named among the earlier communities that denied Allah’s signs.

One interpretation connects the warning to the conflict at Badr. It says that the people opposing the Prophet ﷺ had lied about and abused him as Pharaoh’s people had done to Mūsā (Moses), and that their defeat at Badr would resemble the destruction of Pharaoh’s people by drowning. This is presented as a specific application of the comparison, alongside its broader warning about every community that rejects revelation.

**“they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins”** explains why those communities were destroyed. They denied the scripture and messenger sent to them. Another explanation stresses that Allah seized them because they persisted in denial and insolence. Their wealth and children could not protect them, as the preceding verse said.

**“And Allah is severe in punishment”** means His punishment is painful and cannot be escaped. Nothing is outside His knowledge or grasp. An inward reading says that insolence became their way, or sunna, and that they did not strive to do good, expressed by the word Ṭamiʿū in the report. They persisted until regret came ʿalayhim, upon them, and the door was closed. Their repentance was then rejected.

### In short

- Pharaoh’s people and earlier communities are examples of the fate of those who reject Allah’s signs.
- One interpretation applies the warning to the opponents at Badr, while the verse also states a general lesson from past communities.
- Allah’s punishment is severe, and no one can escape His knowledge or power.
