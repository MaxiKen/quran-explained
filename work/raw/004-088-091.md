<<<VERSE 4:88>>>
The censure of a divided community: "Why are you ˹believers˺ divided into two groups regarding the hypocrites while Allah allowed them to regress ˹to disbelief˺ because of their misdeeds?" (4:88). The verse answers a real argument that broke out among the Companions, and it settles the status of people whose Islam had come undone.

### The occasion reports

Imām Aḥmad recorded from Zayd ibn Thābit that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ marched towards Uḥud, and some who set out with him went back to al-Madinah. The Companions of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ divided into two groups over them, one saying they should be killed and the other objecting, and the verse came down. al-Bukhārī and Muslim both carry the report, Ghundar and Ibn Muʿādh's father relating it from Shuʿbah on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn Yazīd ibn Thābit. The same occasion carries the Prophet's words about the city:

> She is Ṭaybah, and she expels filth, just as the bellows expels rust from iron.

A second report, carried by ʿAbdur-Raḥmān ibn Ḥamdān, tells of a group of Arabs who came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and embraced Islam, then contracted the illness and fever going round in Madinah, renounced Islam and left. Met by a group of Companions and asked why they had left, they answered that they had caught the illness of Madinah and disliked remaining there, whereupon the Companions asked whether they had no good example in the person of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. Some judged them hypocrites and others still Muslims, and because of the difference of opinion the verse was revealed.

A third report comes from Mujāhid, relayed by ʿAbdullāh ibn Ḥāmid. Some disbelievers of Makkah came to Madinah pretending to have become Muslims, claiming to have arrived as emigrants, then turned apostates. They told the Prophet ﷺ of a plan to go to Makkah for merchandise, made their false excuse, departed and never returned. The Muslims of Madinah differed over them, some calling them believers, until the verse declared them disbelievers whose killing was due. Mawlānā Ashraf ʿAlī Thānāvī explains the word munāfiq here. Killing was not the lot of the hypocrites as such, since their disbelief stayed hidden within. These people stood elsewhere, for their apostasy had come out into the open. Those who took them for Muslims had perhaps taken a benign view under some interpretation, but that interpretation rested on sheer opinion, unsupported by any proof of the Sharīʿah, so no reliance was placed on it.

Al-ʿAwfī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās a Makkan version. Some people in Makkah said they embraced Islam yet gave their support to the idolaters. Going out of Makkah one day to fulfil some needs, they said to each other that if they met the Companions of Muḥammad, no harm would come to them from that side. When the believers learned of their outing, one group said to march against these cowards and kill them, for they supported the enemy against the Muslims. The other group said: glory be to Allah, will you kill a people who say what you say, only because they did not perform the emigration or leave their land? Is their blood then lawful and their money to be confiscated? So the believers split into two groups while the Messenger ﷺ was present among them, and he did not prohibit either group from pressing its argument, until the verse came down. Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded this hadith. One gloss counts the people concerned as ten hypocrites who left Islam and Madinah for Makkah, the two parties disputing whether their wealth and blood had become lawful.

Ibn Abī Shaybah narrated from Ḥasan a different occasion. Suraqah ibn Mālik al-Mudlajī visited the Prophet ﷺ after the events of Badr and Uḥud and requested peace for his tribe, Banū Mudlaj, whereupon he sent Khālid to conclude the treaty on the terms:

> We will not support anyone against the Prophet ﷺ. If the Quraysh become Muslims, we too shall become Muslims. All tribes who enter into alliance with us shall become party with us in this treaty.

A further narration from Ibn ʿAbbās identifies the people of the fourth verse in this passage as the tribes of Asad and Ghaṭafān, while aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk ascribes the like conduct to the tribe of ʿAbd ad-Dār. The first two narrations appear in Rūḥ al-Maʿānī and the third in Maʿālim.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Why are you ˹believers˺ divided into two groups regarding the hypocrites"** asks in the tone of disapproval, and the question returns later in the verse in the same tone. The believers had split over the status of the returners, one party for the sword and the other for sparing them, over people who had not performed the hijrah, the emigration.

**"while Allah allowed them to regress ˹to disbelief˺ because of their misdeeds"** settles the matter from the divine side. The word of the verse means cast them back, as Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it, and what they earned was their defiance and disobedience to the Messenger ﷺ, their following of falsehood, their hypocrisy and their evil intentions.

**"Do you wish to guide those left by Allah to stray?"** exposes the futility of the sparing party's hope, for the question means: will you count them among the guided? Of him whom Allah has sent astray from His religion, no religion or argument can be found in favour.

**"And whoever Allah leaves to stray, you will never find for them a way"** states the rule outright. No path to guidance opens for the one He has left astray.

### The three groups and the two injunctions

The passage as a whole describes three groups of people under two injunctions. The first are the open apostates, whose case follows the ruling on disbelievers in general. The second are those who enter a no-war pact with the Muslims themselves, or who join hands with people holding such a pact. The third make peace only to buy time, and join the enemy camp at the first occasion of war, throwing the treaty obligations to the winds. The first group is fought in the absence of peace, the second is exempted from arrest and killing, and the third receives the same punishment as the first. The mapping runs through the following verses: the second verse carries the arrest and killing, the third gives the exemption in the event of peace, and the fourth returns to those who refuse peace, from which it follows that no fighting is due to them if they make peace, as the Bayān al-Qur'ān sets out.

The inward hearing of the verse is sterner still. Hold to the covenant regarding them, for they are My enemies. Neither in this world nor the next will they receive My good pleasure. No anxiety felt for them can rescue them from what the decree has fixed, and the decrees do not circle around the worries of people.

### In short

- The Companions split over the returners from Uḥud and over pretending emigrants who turned apostates, and Allah censured the division itself.
- The reports vary in setting: the Uḥud withdrawal, the fever that drove Arabs from Madinah, the merchandise excuse, the Banū Mudlaj treaty, and the Asad and Ghaṭafān peace-seekers.
- Allah had cast them back for what they earned, and no way exists to guide those He has left astray.
- Three groups stand in the passage, open apostates, pact-holders and double dealers, under two rules: fight in the absence of peace, and stand down in the event of peace.

<<<VERSE 4:89>>>
The wish and the command: "They wish you would disbelieve as they have disbelieved, so you may all be alike. So do not take them as allies unless they emigrate in the cause of Allah" (4:89). The verse exposes what the deserters want, then draws the line of association.

### Phrase by phrase

**"They wish you would disbelieve as they have disbelieved, so you may all be alike"** means they wish the believers to fall into misguidance, so that both stand equal in it, and the wish comes of their extreme enmity and hatred. They want company in unbelief, a level field with no side standing higher.

**"So do not take them as allies unless they emigrate in the cause of Allah"** forbids taking friends from among them in religion or for assistance and backing, even if they outwardly manifest belief, until the hijrah, the migration in the way of Allah, confirms it, an emigration in obedience to Allah. If they abandon it, as al-ʿAwfī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, the alliance stays closed, and as-Suddī read the turning away as making their disbelief public.

### The emigration that ended and the emigration that continues

The emigration here has its own history. In the early period, migration from the homeland of disbelief, the dār al-kufr, was enjoined on all Muslims, which is why whoever failed to carry out the hijrah was not treated as a Muslim. When Makkah was conquered, the Prophet ﷺ declared that there is no emigration after the Victory, for the Conquest had made Makkah an abode of Islam, and the duty lapsed. The later rule, as Muḥammad Taqī ʿUthmānī notes, is that a Muslim living in a non-Muslim country where he cannot fulfil his religious obligations must emigrate if he has the means.

Another emigration continues. The abandonment of sins lasts as long as the time to repent remains, in the report of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. Al-ʿAynī, who wrote the commentary on al-Bukhārī, glossed it exactly so, and a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ teaches that the muhājir, the emigrant, is one who leaves everything Allah has prohibited, a saying recorded in al-Mirqāt.

The word therefore carries a dual sense. One leaves one's homeland to save one's faith, as the Companions did in moving from Makkah to Madinah and Ethiopia. The other leaves one's sins.

**"But if they turn away, then seize them and kill them wherever you find them"** gives the penalty for the turning back, meaning capture as prisoners and death in the Sacred Precinct or anywhere else.

**"and do not take any of them as allies or helpers"** closes the door a second time. From this the rule is drawn that seeking help from disbelievers is forbidden, ḥarām. When the Anṣār of Madinah sought the Prophet's permission to seek help from the Jews to offset the disbelievers, he answered:

> The bad ones! We do not need them.

So al-Mazharī relates the instruction.

### In short

- Their wish is equality in unbelief, born of pure enmity, and it is not granted.
- Alliance waits on a true emigration in the way of Allah, the kind that confirms belief, while open desertion forfeits protection.
- Emigration as a precondition of faith ended with the Conquest of Makkah, though the emigration from sin continues, and the duty stands for anyone who cannot practise his religion where he is.
- Seizing and killing follow the turn away, and help from disbelievers is ḥarām, as the Prophet's answer to the Anṣār shows.

<<<VERSE 4:90>>>
The exemption: "except those who are allies of a people you are bound with in a treaty or those wholeheartedly opposed to fighting either you or their own people. If Allah had willed, He would have empowered them to fight you" (4:90). Between the apostate and the double dealer stands a middle kind, and the verse protects them.

### Phrase by phrase

**"except those who are allies of a people you are bound with in a treaty"** excepts those who join and take refuge with a people with whom the Muslims hold a pact of peace, or with people of covenant status, and they are treated as the pact people are treated. So read as-Suddī, Ibn Zayd and Ibn Jarīr together. The story of the treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyyah, recorded in al-Bukhārī's Ṣaḥīḥ, set the pattern: whoever wished for peace with Quraysh could conclude a pact with them, and whoever wished for peace with Muḥammad ﷺ and his Companions could enter a pact on that side. Ibn ʿAbbās held that this verse was later abrogated by the words "Then when the Sacred Months have passed, kill the idolators wherever you find them" (9:5).

**"or those wholeheartedly opposed to fighting either you or their own people"** covers a second type under the exclusion. These approach the Muslims with hesitation in their breasts, having no heart to fight the Muslims, and no heart to fight their own people on the Muslim side, so that they stand neither with nor against. One reading names them the people of Ḥilāl ibn ʿUwaymir al-Aslamī. The Prophet ﷺ held a covenant with them, a pledge of security covering whoever attached himself to them, and they were annoyed at the great cost the pact exacted and held back from war by the ties of kinship. Among the ten who fled to Makkah, whoever reached the refuge of this covenant was excepted.

**"If Allah had willed, He would have empowered them to fight you"** names the restraint for what it is, a mercy. Had He willed to give them sway, He would have strengthened their hearts, and they would assuredly have fought, but He did not will it, and He cast terror into their hearts instead.

**"So if they refrain from fighting you and offer you peace, then Allah does not permit you to harm them"** states the limit plainly. No way stands open against them, no excuse for killing, no means of taking them captive or slaying them, as long as they hold this position. The schools note that this statement and what follows were abrogated by the verse of the sword. The position itself was lived by Banū Hāshim, the tribe of the Prophet ﷺ, at Badr, where al-ʿAbbās accompanied the idolaters into battle with great hesitation, and the Prophet ﷺ commanded that he not be killed, but only captured.

### The test of the hard demands

The deeper lesson runs beneath the law. After a person accepts the religion of Allah, life keeps presenting situations that test the sincerity of the decision, and migration is one such test. When worldly gains and convenience stand as obstacles in the path of religion, they are to be thrust aside. The movement then goes forward towards Allah, abandoning relatives and home if the situation demands. Whoever moves ahead in the path of truth in the moment of trial, ignoring personal gain and expediency, consolidates the attachment to truth and grows more sensitive to it. Whoever does not, diminishes in receptiveness. The hard demands of religion divide people into simple people, opponents and hypocrites, and Muslims must deal with each kind accordingly. They stay firm in eradicating evil through peaceful means, lenient in the discharge of moral responsibilities, and considerate to the weak. They busy themselves influencing others rather than being swayed, and they never challenge a group that wants to make peace with them.

### In short

- Refuge under a valid treaty shields a person, and covenant status extends to whoever joins the covenant people, as in the pact with Ḥilāl ibn ʿUwaymir al-Aslamī.
- The hesitant, who cannot fight either side, stand protected, for their restraint is a mercy Allah placed in their hearts.
- Withdrawal plus peace closes every avenue of harm, and al-ʿAbbās at Badr, captured rather than killed, is the pattern of the exemption.
- Migration remains the standing test of sincerity, and peace-seekers are never to be challenged.

<<<VERSE 4:91>>>
The last group: "You will find others who wish to be safe from you and their own people. Yet they cannot resist the temptation ˹of disbelief or hostility˺" (4:91). On the surface these resemble the hesitant of the previous verse, but the intention of each type differs. These are the hypocrites of both camps.

### Phrase by phrase

**"You will find others who wish to be safe from you and their own people"** describes people who pretend Islam before the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions, so that their blood, property and families stay safe with the Muslims. In secret they support the idolaters and worship what they worship, so as to be at peace with that side too. The tribes of Asad and Ghaṭafān are named in the reports. Ibn ʿAbbās tells that they professed Islam outwardly on coming to Madinah. To their own people they confided that they had really believed in monkeys and scorpions, while before the Muslims they piously declared themselves followers of the faith. Their condition is the one the Qur'ān had already drawn: "But when they are alone with their devils, they say: truly, we are with you" (2:14).

**"Yet they cannot resist the temptation ˹of disbelief or hostility˺"** means that every time they are sent back to fitnah, they yield to it, dwelling in it, and whenever they are summoned to idolatry they are overwhelmed, falling into it in the worst of ways. As-Suddī said the fitnah here is shirk. Ibn Jarīr recorded from Mujāhid that the verse was revealed about a group from Makkah who used to come to the Prophet ﷺ in al-Madinah pretending to be Muslims, then go back to Quraysh and revert to worshipping idols, wanting peace with both sides, so Allah commanded that they be fought unless they withdrew from combat and resorted to peace.

**"If they do not keep away, offer you peace, or refrain from attacking you, then seize them and kill them wherever you find them"** is the command of the last resort. Withdrawal means reverting to peaceful and complacent behaviour, restraining the hands means refraining from fighting, and only failing these does the seizing come, capture and death wherever they are come upon, the word of the verse, Thaqiftumūhum, meaning wherever you find them, in the Sacred Precinct or anywhere else.

**"We have given you full permission over such people"** closes the file. The warrant is unequivocal and plain, a clear and manifest proof to slay them and capture them, on account of their treachery.

### The inner reading

Whoever sets out to combine two opposites finds the effort ruined and the resolve stunted. An individual cannot be at once a hypocrite and one who submits wholeheartedly. Equally, no one can long for the Real while living by the precepts of the people of habit, since spiritual desire and habit stand opposed. Abandon whatever stance contradicts its fellow, and keep apart from what has no relation to you.

### In short

- The last group plays both camps, Muslim by day before the Muslims, idolater at home among idolaters, safe with everyone and true to no one.
- Every recall to fitnah, which is shirk, sweeps them back in, so the verse names them unable to resist.
- Only withdrawal, peace or restraint protects them. Failing all three, the warrant against them is full, plain and earned by treachery.
- Two opposites cannot be combined, in religion or in the life of the spirit, and the double-hearted ruin their own resolve.
