<<<VERSE 4:24>>>
This verse adds one more group to the list of forbidden women, then opens the door wide for everything else, and it attaches the conditions of dowry and chastity: "Also ˹forbidden are˺ married women—except ˹female˺ captives in your possession. This is Allah’s commandment to you. Lawful to you are all beyond these—as long as you seek them with your wealth in a legal marriage, not in fornication. Give those you have consummated marriage with their due dowries. It is permissible to be mutually gracious regarding the set dowry. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise."

The earlier verse fixed the permanent prohibitions. The hadiths add a few more, and the verse does not list them. As in the earlier verse, the point behind the rules is that the natural desire between man and woman is allowed only inside a solemn, recognised agreement, in an atmosphere of chastity.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Also ˹forbidden are˺ married women"**. As long as a woman is married to one man, no other man may marry her. The rule covers women who have spouses, whether they are free Muslim women or not. It follows that a woman cannot have more than one husband at the same time. The Sharīʿah is so firm on this that even when a husband divorces his wife or dies, she cannot marry another man until her waiting period, the ʿiddah, has ended.

**"except ˹female˺ captives in your possession"**. The exception covers women acquired in a lawful war. They are permitted after it is certain that they are not pregnant, which means after one menstrual period. This holds even if their husbands are in the enemy camp, in Dār al-Ḥarb, the territory of those at war with the Muslims. When such women were brought to the Islamic state, their marriage to non-Muslim husbands who stayed behind ended with their entry. A Muslim could then marry her, if she was a Christian, a Jew or had accepted Islam. If the head of the Islamic state made her a bondwoman and gave her to a fighter as part of his share of the spoils, he could be with her. In both cases, she had first to go through at least one menstrual period after entering the Islamic state. If she was pregnant by her former husband, the wait lasted until she gave birth.

Two rulings follow. A disbelieving woman who accepts Islam in Dār al-Ḥarb while her husband stays a disbeliever is released from her marriage after three menstrual periods. If a disbelieving woman accepts Islam in Dār al-Islām, the Islamic territory, and her husband remains a disbeliever, the authority should invite the husband to think of accepting Islam. If he refuses, the Muslim judge separates the two. The separation counts as a divorce. She then completes her ʿiddah and is free to marry any Muslim she chooses.

**"This is Allah’s commandment to you"**. The prohibition is one that Allah ordained, so one should hold to His Book, not cross His limits, and stay with His laws and decrees. The unlawfulness of marrying the women He has named is fixed by Him.

**"Lawful to you are all beyond these"**. Women other than the forbidden types named are lawful, as ʿAṭāʾ and others said. Examples are the daughter of a paternal or maternal uncle, the wife of an uncle after his death or divorce, and the wife of an adopted son once he has divorced her or died. If one's wife dies, one may marry her sister. The generality of the words does not mean that marriage is unrestricted. A man may not have more than four wives at the same time, as the opening of the surah has already explained. The verse leaves out some other prohibitions that appear in the hadiths, and the verses themselves hint at them.

**"as long as you seek them with your wealth in a legal marriage, not in fornication"**. The reason the forbidden women are listed is to send people looking for lawful women with their wealth, and to marry them. Abū Bakr al-Jaṣṣāṣ points out two things from this. First, a marriage cannot exist without a dower, and even if the parties agree to marry with none, a dower is still required. Second, the dower must be something that counts as property, māl. According to the Ḥanafī school, the mahr must not be less than ten dirhams. Some explanations give other senses of "seek with your wealth", one being marrying up to four wives, another being buying female captives with your money, and a third being marrying women for a stated period, zawāj al-mutʿah. The lawfulness of that last practice was later abrogated, as the next sections explain.

The words "in a legal marriage" mean that a man seeks lawful women with his wealth in order to guard his modesty and chastity. This is the crucial purpose of marriage, and it must be achieved only through marriage. Fornicators spend money too, but that spending is plainly unlawful, and enjoying a woman obtained through it is never lawful. The words "not in fornication" do two things. They forbid zinā, and they point out that its only purpose is to chase lust and waste seed on an unlawful pleasure. Its aim is not to have children or preserve the human race. Muslims must stay chaste and use their strength where it belongs, for the good of human procreation, by having a wife in marriage, or a bondwoman if one happens to own one. Another explanation puts it as being with them as legitimate husbands, and not indulging in adultery without proper marriage.

**"Give those you have consummated marriage with their due dowries"**. The Arabic word is istamtaʿtum, from a root meaning to derive benefit. In this verse it means sexual relations. The full dower becomes due when the husband has had that benefit. If, after the contract, the wife never came to live with him and he divorced her before that, the dower he owes drops to half. Once the benefit has been received, payment of the dower is obligatory in every case. Falling short is against the letter and spirit of Muslim law. The universal human sense of honour also says there should be no failure or delay in paying the wife once the marriage has achieved its purpose. The Sharīʿah also gives the woman an extra right. If the dower is to be paid promptly, she may refuse to go to her husband until it has been paid to her. Other verses say the same thing. Allah says "And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately" (4:21), and tells husbands to give women their dowries (4:4). Elsewhere He forbids taking back anything of what was given (2:229).

**"It is permissible to be mutually gracious regarding the set dowry"**. The fixed dower is not an absolute figure to which nothing can be added or taken away. A husband can add to it of his own accord. The wife, if she wishes, can freely and gladly give up part or all of it. The words also allow a woman to agree freely to put off a dower that was meant to be paid at once. According to Ibn Jarīr's report from al-Ḥaḍramī, some men set a dowry but then fell into financial difficulty, and Allah said that there is no harm concerning mutual agreement after the requirement. If she gives up a part of it, the men may accept it. Short explanations say the same: you are not at fault in agreeing together after the obligation is waived, lowered or raised.

**"Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise"**. Two points stand out. First, Allah knows everything. His commands are to be obeyed. If someone acts against them, and even if no judge or ruler or other person finds out, Allah knows all about it, so one must fear Him at all times. Second, His rulings are all based on wisdom. His wisdom is too deep for everyone to grasp. The rules on what is lawful and unlawful must be believed, accepted and obeyed, whether or not one understands the reason. The Creator and Master of the command knows the reason in any case. The words fit well here, after a list of prohibitions. One explanation says that Allah knows the reason for allowing marriage for a fixed term and is wise in making it unlawful later. Another says that He knows what drove people to want it and is wise in forbidding it.

### Why one woman cannot have several husbands

Some people say that if men may have more than one wife, women should be allowed more than one husband. This goes against the verse. Plurality of wives has been recognised in all faiths and societies as a blessing. For a woman, several husbands at one time would be a burden for her and a disgrace for the men involved. It also leaves no way for children to have a sound line of descent. When many men have access to one woman, a child's fatherhood cannot be attributed to any one of them. When lineage cannot be established, there is no one who is made responsible for the mutual rights and duties.

Nature and reason give three more arguments. First, the basic purpose of marriage is to have children. Several women can become pregnant by one man, but one woman cannot become pregnant by several men. She conceives by one only, so the procreative ability of all her husbands but one is wasted. All that is left for them is the satisfaction of desire. Second, a woman is more delicate than a man. For a large part of the year she is not physically able to be intimate. There are times in married life when she cannot give one husband his rights, let alone several. Third, a man is stronger and healthier. If his strength is above average and one wife cannot satisfy him, he should have the chance, by lawful means, to marry two or three. If he is not allowed this, he will go to forbidden ways and ruin society. The likelihood that a woman would cause such ruin is remote.

### The captive women of Awṭās

Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī gave the circumstances in which the exception for captives was revealed. Aḥmad recorded his words: "We took some women captive near Awṭās who already had husbands, and we did not like being with them for that reason. We put the question to the Prophet ﷺ, and this verse came down. After that we lived with these women." At-Tirmidhī, an-Nasāʾī, Ibn Jarīr and Muslim, in his Ṣaḥīḥ, recorded this wording.

Other reports of the same event add details. In one, the Companions asked, "O Prophet of Allah, how can we be with women when we know their families and their husbands so well?" and the verse came down. In a third, narrated by Abū Saʿīd in a version that mentions Ḥunayn, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ sent an army to Awṭās on the day of Ḥunayn. The army met the enemy, defeated them and took many women captive. Some of the Companions were uncomfortable with the thought of relations with these captives, since their husbands were idolaters. Allah revealed the verse on this occasion.

### Temporary marriage (mutʿah)

The word in the verse for "have benefited" is from the same root as mutʿah, and this raises the question of temporary marriage. Mutʿah means a marriage that ends on a set date. Mujāhid said that the words "so with those among them whom you have enjoyed, give them their required due" were revealed about mutʿah marriage. The weight of the evidence is against this reading.

The two Ṣaḥīḥs record that ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib said that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ forbade mutʿah marriage and the eating of the meat of domestic donkeys on the day of Khaybar. Muslim recorded in his Ṣaḥīḥ that ar-Rabīʿ ibn Sabrah ibn Maʿbad al-Juhanī said that his father went with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ at the conquest of Makkah, and that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> "O people, I had allowed you mutʿah with women before. Now Allah has forbidden it until the Day of Resurrection. So whoever has any woman in mutʿah, let him release her, and let him not take back anything of what he gave her."

The root of istimtāʿ, to derive benefit, applies to any benefit drawn from a person or from property. The simple meaning in this verse, as the whole Muslim ummah has understood it from the early elders to their successors, is the one given above: sexual relations in a lawful marriage. A group among Muslims, the Shīʿah, holds that the word means conventional mutʿah and that the verse proves it lawful. This is a far-fetched argument that rests only on the shared root, and the verse itself refutes it.

Before Islam, mutʿah was a short-term arrangement in which a man and a woman had a sexual relationship for a fixed period, in return for money or something else. It was never meant to create the lasting rights and duties of marriage, and the Qur'an and Sunnah forbade it clearly. Just before the word in question, the verse says "in a legal marriage, not in fornication". The Qur'an's word here for lust, taken literally, means "flowing water", which points to seeking release out of desire. These words show that the only relationship the Qur'an approves is one aimed at chastity through the lasting bond of marriage, not one that satisfies lust for a short time. A mutʿah contract does not create lasting rights and duties, does not set up a family, and does not aim at children and chastity. It only satisfies desire for a short time. The woman is not even treated as a wife who could inherit, and the man does not count her among his recognised wives. Where the purpose is only gratification, men and women are driven to keep hunting for new partners in a temporary arrangement. So mutʿah can never guarantee modesty and chastity. It is their enemy. For these reasons the words are enough to rule out mutʿah as the verse's meaning.

The author of al-Hidāyah attributed to Imām Mālik the view that mutʿah is permissible. This is wrong, as the commentator on al-Hidāyah and other respected scholars have said. He attributed it by mistake.

Some claim that Ibn ʿAbbās held mutʿah lawful until his last years. This is not so. At-Tirmidhī gave a chapter to mutʿah, with two hadiths. The first is that of ʿAlī about Khaybar, which is also in al-Bukhārī and Muslim. In the second, Ibn ʿAbbās says that mutʿah belonged only to Islam's first period, ending when the verse came down. He added that any private parts other than a wife's or a bondwoman's are unlawful. Ibn ʿAbbās did hold mutʿah permissible for a time. Then, on the good advice of ʿAlī, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, and under the force of that verse, he gave up his earlier position, as the narration in at-Tirmidhī shows. It has been remarked that the group which holds mutʿah lawful claims to love and follow ʿAlī, and yet on this very question it goes against him, the narrator of the prohibition.

Rūḥ al-Maʿānī reports from Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ that mutʿah was lawful before the battle of Khaybar, that it was made unlawful during it, that it was allowed again at the conquest of Makkah, and that after three days it was declared unlawful forever. The Qur'an also states that those who guard their private parts, except from their wives and their bondwomen, are not blameworthy. That statement is so explicit that it leaves no room for another reading, and it shows plainly that mutʿah is unlawful. Looking for flimsy support in some rare and unreliable recitations is entirely wrong.

To sum up, there is no decisive proof that "you have benefited" refers to conventional mutʿah. It is a remote possibility, and it cannot override the decisive proof just cited. A settled principle of Islamic jurisprudence adds weight here. Where two readings are equally possible, the one that supports the prohibition is preferred.

A time-bound marriage, called nikāḥ muwaqqat, is also unlawful. The difference is only in the wording. Mutʿah is made with the word mutʿah, while a time-bound marriage is made with the word nikāḥ, which is used for ordinary marriage, and then limited to a set period.

### Reason is not the measure of Allah's laws

There are many educated but ignorant people today, among Muslims and non-Muslims, who go around digging for the causes of divine rulings. When they fail to find any, they sidestep the need to comply by saying that the word of Allah, Allah forbid, does not fit the needs of the modern age, or is unsuitable. The closing words of the verse have silenced such people for good. They say: you are ignorant, and your Creator is All-Knowing. You lack understanding, and Allah is All-Wise. Your own reason must never be made the test of what is true.

### The inner reading

When you stay mindful of Allah's limits, the ḥudūd, respect your agreements, the ʿuhūd, and obtain the mutual consent of women in the manner the law lays down, there will be no hostility in it among people. No punishment will follow from the Real, because it is lawful and carries no further restriction.

### In short

- Married women are forbidden, except captive women after a waiting period of one menstrual cycle, as the verse was revealed about the captive women of Awṭās. A woman cannot have two husbands.
- All other women are lawful, up to four wives, if a man seeks them with his wealth in marriage for chastity and not for fornication. A dower is always required, it must be property, and the Ḥanafī school sets its minimum at ten dirhams.
- The full dower is due after consummation and half if the couple divorce before. The couple may agree to add to the dower or to forgo part of it.
- Temporary marriage (mutʿah) was allowed early on and then forbidden for ever, as ʿAlī and others reported. The verse itself, with its words "in a legal marriage", does not support it. A time-bound nikāḥ is also unlawful.
- Allah knows everything and is wise in all He decrees, and human reason is not the measure of His laws.
