<<<VERSE 1:2>>>
The surah opens with the words "All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds". It is the first thing a Muslim says in every unit of prayer, and it is the first of three verses of praise before the surah turns, in its last three verses, into a request. This verse is a statement, not a request. It is also a lesson. Allah praises Himself here, and in doing so He teaches His servants how to praise Him, as if He had said, "Say: all praise is for Allah."

The verse makes two claims. First, that every kind of praise belongs to Allah, for who He is and for what He does. Second, that He is the Lord, the owner and nurturer, of every world there is. The second claim is the proof of the first. If He alone sustains everything that exists, then He alone deserves the praise that anything in existence can earn.

### Phrase by phrase

**"All praise"** translates al-ḥamd. Ḥamd is praise for someone's qualities as well as for their deeds, which makes it wider than thanks, since thanks is only for something done. The "al" in front of it (the Arabic "the") gathers up every kind of praise and thanks there is and gives all of it to Allah. A supplication of the Prophet ﷺ makes the same point:

> "O Allah, all praise is Yours, all dominion is Yours, all good is in Your hand, and to You all matters return."

**"is for Allah"** means it belongs to Him purely and alone, not to any of the things people worship instead of Him and not to any creature. Ibn Jarīr explained that it is His for favours too many to count. He gave people the bodies and faculties with which they are able to obey Him. He gives them their provision in this life and a comfortable living, though nothing obliges Him to. And He warned them and showed them the way to earn a home of everlasting happiness. From first to last, all of it is His.

**"Lord"** translates Rabb. A rabb is an owner with full authority over what he owns, a master, the one with the right to lead. In its root sense the word means the one who nurtures: who brings a thing along by gradual stages, in the way that is good for it, until it is complete. On its own, Rabb is used only for Allah. For anyone else it has to be qualified, as in rabb ad-dār, the master of the house, because a created being needs nurture itself and cannot truly nurture anything. It is also reported that ar-Rabb is Allah's greatest name.

**"of all worlds"** translates al-ʿālamīn, the plural of ʿālam. An ʿālam is everything in existence other than Allah, and the word is itself a plural with no singular, so "worlds" is a plural of a plural. Every kind of creation, in the heavens and the earth, on land and at sea, is a world of its own, and so is every generation. The word comes from ʿalāmah, a sign, because every created thing is a sign that points to its Creator and to His oneness.

### Why praise belongs to Allah alone

Whoever praises anything, anywhere, is in reality praising Allah. The world is full of things that draw admiration for their beauty or usefulness, but behind each of them is the same creative power. Admiring a work of craft is admiration of the craftsman. This short sentence pulls a person out of the maze of many things and shows how they are all knit into one. Only ignorance or inattention makes anyone give the praise to someone else.

That has a consequence. If one Being alone deserves all praise, then that Being alone deserves worship. So although the phrase is worded as praise, it cuts the root of worshipping created things and states, as something self-evident, the first principle of the faith: the oneness of Allah. Praise is His for His essence, His attributes and His actions. He created everything, everything belongs to Him, and He manages all of it. He gives blessings to individuals in particular and to humankind in general.

Ibn ʿAbbās put the reasons simply: praise is Allah's because He is kind to His creatures, so they praise Him. It was also said that it is His because of His endless blessings on the servants He guided to believe in Him. And it was also said that praise, pure oneness and divinity all belong to Allah, who has no child, no partner, no helper and no assistant.

### A statement Allah loves

ʿUmar once said to ʿAlī: we know "there is no god but Allah", "glory be to Allah" and "Allah is greatest", but what about "all praise is for Allah"? ʿAlī answered that it is a statement Allah liked for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to hear repeated. Ibn ʿAbbās called it the statement of gratitude, and said that when a servant says al-ḥamdu lillāh, Allah says, "My servant has praised Me." Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded this.

The Prophet ﷺ said the same in many ways. When al-Aswad ibn Sarīʿ offered to recite to him some words of praise he had composed for his Lord, the Prophet ﷺ told him, "Your Lord loves praise." Aḥmad and an-Nasāʾī recorded it. Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh reported him saying that the best remembrance of Allah is "there is no god but Allah" and the best supplication is "all praise is for Allah". At-Tirmidhī, an-Nasāʾī and Ibn Mājah recorded it, and at-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan gharīb (sound, with a rare chain). Anas ibn Mālik reported, in a hadith recorded by Ibn Mājah, that whenever Allah gives a servant a blessing and the servant says "all praise is for Allah", what he has given back is better than what he received.

Ibn ʿUmar reported, in another hadith recorded by Ibn Mājah, that a servant once said, "My Lord, Yours is the praise that befits the majesty of Your face and the greatness of Your authority." The two recording angels did not know how to write it down. They went up and told Allah that a servant had said something they could not record. Allah knew exactly what he had said, but He asked them what it was. When they repeated it, He said, "Write it as My servant said it, until he meets Me and I reward him for it."

### What Allah says back when you say it

Abū Hurayrah reported, in a hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, that the Prophet ﷺ said that Allah said: "The prayer is divided between Me and My servant, and My servant will have what he asks for." When the servant says "All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds," Allah says, "My servant has paid Me homage." The dialogue continues through the rest of the surah: the next verse is answered with "My servant has praised Me", the one after with "My servant has proclaimed My greatness", the fifth verse is shared between Allah and His servant, and the request for guidance is answered with "This is for My servant, and My servant will have what he asked for."

### How many worlds are there?

Scholars drew the boundary of "the worlds" in different places. Al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿUbayd said it covers everything with a mind, meaning jinn, humans, angels and devils, but not animals. Zayd ibn Aslam and Abū Muḥaysin said it covers everything Allah created with a soul. Qatādah said every kind of creation is a world. Az-Zajjāj said it covers everything Allah created in this life and the next, and al-Qurṭubī held that this is the correct view. The Qur'an itself supports it: when Pharaoh asked, "And what is the Lord of all worlds?", Mūsā (Moses) answered, "The Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything between them, if you would only be sure" (26:23-24).

On this reading the worlds include the sky, the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, wind and rain, angels, jinn, animals, plants, minerals and people, and whatever other universes may lie beyond ours. Imam ar-Rāzī argued that reason can show there is unlimited space beyond our universe, and that Allah, who is able to do all things, could have created millions of other universes in it. It is reported from the Companion Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī that there are forty thousand worlds, and ours, from east to west, is only one of them. Muqātil put the number at eighty thousand. To the objection that nothing could live in outer space without our kind of air, ar-Rāzī replied that beings elsewhere need not be built the way we are, and may be made of different stuff with different needs. He suggested this some eight hundred years before anyone travelled into space, and the guesses of modern science point the same way.

Ibn ʿAbbās explained "Lord of all worlds" as Lord of everything with a spirit that walks the earth and of everyone who lives in heaven. It was also said to mean the Master of the jinn and of humankind. And it was also said to mean the One who created everything that exists, provides for all of it, and moves it from one state to the next.

### What the order of the universe is telling you

Seen through this phrase, the universe is an immensely complex but perfectly joined order. From the heavens down to a speck of dust, everything is linked in one chain and doing the task assigned to it. A single mouthful of food reaches a person only because a thousand forces of sky and earth have worked together to produce it. That order is there to be thought about. If Allah has put millions of His creatures to work for human beings, then human beings cannot be pointless. The Qur'an insists that the universe is not absurd: "We have not created the heavens and the earth and everything between them in vain. That is the assumption of the disbelievers" (38:27). And it names the purpose of the one the universe serves: "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me" (51:56).

This is also why praise comes first in the surah. The whole opening chapter puts into words the feelings that rise in a person who has found the truth. Anyone who looks at the world sees Allah's power and mercy everywhere, an astonishing order and care, everything fitted to human needs. Praise is the natural response. The same observation then leads to the rest of the surah. A machine this great cannot run for nothing. So there must be a day when the grateful and the ungrateful are repaid, and the request for guidance follows from that. The surah is a miniature of the faith, and the rest of the Qur'an enlarges the picture.

### Praise at seeing blessings and praise at seeing the Giver

There is also an inner reading of the verse. It is praise of the lovingly kind God, the Creator who provides. He is found without being sought, known without being grasped, and loved without being seen. He is powerful without effort and self-subsisting without change. His kingship never declines. He has no beginning and no end, and He is described by majesty and by beauty. He saw that His servants could never measure Him, as the Qur'an says: "They have not given Allah His due measure" (6:91). So in His generosity He appointed them His deputies in praising Him, taught them how, and gave them permission. Otherwise who would have dared to say "praise belongs to Allah" if He had not said it first?

On this reading praise is of two kinds. One comes from seeing blessings: it extols Him, puts His gifts to work in obedience, and gives thanks so that blessings increase today and lead to Paradise tomorrow. The Prophet ﷺ said that the first people called to the Garden will be those who praise Allah fully in every state. The other kind comes from seeing the Giver Himself, when the one who praises forgets everything else, including himself: he hears One, sees One and reaches One. As one of the early masters of the path said, the two worlds became lost in love, love became lost in the Beloved, and he could no longer say whether it was he or He.

"Lord of all worlds" is read in the same spirit as the Nurturer of everyone in the world, who assigns each one a portion. For one person the portion is nourishment of the body with blessings, for another nourishment of the heart with the Giver of blessings. Blessings go to those who keep working in His service, the secret of the Giver to those whose only hope is to see Him. The Prophet ﷺ pointed to that second nourishment when he said, "I spend the night with my Lord, and He feeds me and gives me drink." He did not eat fine food or drink fine drinks, and he warned others against indulging in blessings. When they asked him why he did not eat, he said he was already drunk on the wine of being together with his Lord. It is said that this drink was special to him alone, the one who was shown the greatest signs and whose eye "did not wander, nor did it overreach" (53:17). He said he had a moment with Allah that no angel brought near and no prophet sent could share, and even the prophets, all one hundred and twenty-four thousand of them, came hoping for a taste of it. Allah nurtures the souls of worshippers with confirmation, the hearts of the pure with greater strength, and the states of those who truly know Him with tawḥīd, the pure oneness of Allah. Someone nurtured on that has little use for ordinary food. When a snake bites someone, you give him the antidote, not sweets. A story about Yazīd Hārūn makes the point. He forbade his student ʿUtbah al-Ghulām to eat dates, yet ate them himself. When the student's mother objected, he said, "Your son wants dates, but dates want me. They are allowed for me and not for him." The people of the world want food, but food wants people like these. It is in that sense that the report says Paradise longs for Salmān.

### In short

- "All praise" means every kind of praise and thanks, and all of it belongs to Allah alone, for who He is and for what He gives.
- Because He alone deserves all praise, He alone deserves worship: the verse states the oneness of Allah as something self-evident.
- "Lord" means the owner and nurturer who brings everything to completion by stages, and the word belongs to Allah alone.
- "All worlds" is every kind of creation, in this life and the next, and the ordered universe they form is itself the proof that praise is His.
- Saying al-ḥamdu lillāh is a statement Allah loves, the best of supplications, and one He answers with "My servant has praised Me."

<<<VERSE 1:3>>>
After naming Allah as Lord of all worlds, the surah describes Him with the words "the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful". These are the same two names that stood in the basmalah, ar-Raḥmān and ar-Raḥīm, and their meaning is explained there. What matters here is their placement. Allah has just been called the Lord who owns and governs everything that exists, a title with weight and warning in it, and before the surah goes on to the Day of Judgment it pauses to say that this Lord is merciful, twice over.

### Phrase by phrase

**"the Most Compassionate"** is ar-Raḥmān, and **"Most Merciful"** is ar-Raḥīm. Both come from raḥmah, mercy, and both are intensive forms, the kind of Arabic word that expresses a quality in its highest degree. The first speaks of the sheer abundance of Allah's mercy, the second of its perfection. Together they say that His mercy covers His creation, and that the believers are the ones who receive the greatest share of it. Mercy, in the sense the word carries here, is to want good for those who deserve it, and both names describe Him as gentle.

### Why mercy follows "Lord of all worlds"

Al-Qurṭubī pointed out that Allah describes Himself as the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful immediately after calling Himself Lord of all worlds, so that the verse holds a warning followed by an encouragement. The Qur'an pairs the two in the same way elsewhere: "Tell My servants that I am the Forgiving, the Merciful, and that My punishment is the painful punishment" (15:49-50), and "Your Lord is swift in punishment, and He is Forgiving, Merciful" (6:165). "Lord" makes a person fear, and "the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful" makes him hope. A hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim shows how large both sides are: the Prophet ﷺ said that if the believer knew what punishment Allah has, no one would hope for His Paradise, and if the disbeliever knew what mercy Allah has, no one would despair of it.

There is a second reason for naming mercy at this point. The previous verse said that Allah is the Lord, the One who brings up and sustains all worlds. Naming His mercy straight afterwards is a reminder that He did not take on the nurturing of His whole creation under any outside compulsion or inner need or necessity of any kind, but in answer to the demand of His own quality of mercy. If this universe did not exist, He would lose nothing by its absence, and now that it does exist, it is no burden to Him.

### Two mercies, two kinds of servant

Read for its inner meaning, the pairing of the names describes two ways Allah deals with people. He is the Most Compassionate in that He gives life, and the Most Merciful in that He shows Himself: the first is done through acts of kindness, the second through lights. As the Most Compassionate He smooths the road of the wage-earner, the servant who toils in hope of the maidens and palaces of Paradise. As the Most Merciful He lights the candle of friendship on the road of His friends, who are flooded with light in the sea of seeing Him face to face, and who say that on the day they reach union with Him they will think nothing of the state of the people of Paradise. He gives the strivers success in their struggle and gives the finders the realisation of contemplation. The first is the state of the one who desires, the second the state of the one who is desired. The desirer sets out with the lamp of success and reaches contemplation, the desired sets out with the candle of realisation and reaches vision. Contemplation is what happens when the barriers between the servant and the Real are lifted. Vision is a seeing in which the servant is not absent for a moment: he looks on love with the eye of response, on the Present with the eye of presence, on the Solitary with the eye of solitude. By distance from himself he draws near to Allah's nearness, by losing himself he grows familiar with His manifestness, by absence from himself he becomes present before His generous presence. For Allah is not far from the strivers, not lost to the seekers, and not absent from those who desire Him. A prayer in this spirit asks Him to have mercy on the hearts of His creatures and come out from behind the veil, so that the seventy-two sects can end their quarrels.

### In short

- The two names of the basmalah return, now right after "Lord of all worlds": warning first, then encouragement, fear then hope.
- Allah's punishment is such that a believer who saw it would despair, and His mercy such that a disbeliever who saw it would hope. Both are real.
- Allah sustains the universe out of mercy, not need. Its existence costs Him nothing and its absence would take nothing from Him.
- The Most Compassionate eases the road of the one who works for reward, and the Most Merciful lights the road of the one who seeks Him for His own sake.

<<<VERSE 1:4>>>
The third description of Allah in the surah is "Master of the Day of Judgment". The previous two verses looked at the whole of creation, the "all worlds" that He brings up and the mercy He pours over them. This verse looks forward to a single day, the day on which every creature will be brought back to life, called to account and repaid for what it did, and it says that the mastery of that day is His. It is the verse that gives the surah its seriousness. Praise and mercy come first, but the one who prays is reminded, before he makes any request, that he is heading towards a reckoning and that the only authority on that day will be the One he is addressing.

Read in the natural order of human thought, the verse is where a person's reflection arrives on its own. Whoever looks at the world around him cannot miss the power and the mercy that are evident everywhere, and the extraordinary order and supervision in everything he sees, all of it fitted to human needs. A machine so vast and so exact cannot be in vain, and so there has to come a day when the grateful and the ungrateful are repaid for how they lived. The prayer that rises from that realisation is: Lord, You are the Master of the Day of Judgment.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Master"** translates mālik. The word comes from milk, ownership, which means possessing a thing in such a way that one has the right and the power to dispose of it as one likes, and this is the sense given in the Qāmūs. The verse does not say what is owned, and according to al-Kashshāf this silence is the point: the phrase covers everything without exception, so that on the Day of Judgment the ownership of all that exists belongs to Allah alone. The word has been read in two ways. Read as malik, it means the King, and says that His kingship alone will be visible on that day. Read as mālik, the Owner, it says that He holds the whole affair of the Day of Resurrection in His hand. In either reading the word can stand as an adjective of the definite noun before it, because He is described by it at all times, in the same way that He is called "Forgiver of sin".

**"of the Day of"** is yawm, and the verse ties His ownership to one day in particular, for reasons explained below. Another rendering of the whole phrase is the Arbitrator of the Day of Judgment, the day of reckoning and of the settling of every dispute between created beings, when people are judged by their deeds and there is no judge but Him.

**"Judgment"** translates ad-dīn, which here means recompense, requital, the settling of accounts, with reward or punishment. The Qur'an uses the word in that sense when it says "On that Day Allah will pay them their just due (dīnahum) in full" (24:25), and when the people of Paradise recall their doubt, "Shall we really be judged (madīnūn)?" (37:53). Ibn ʿAbbās said that the Day of Dīn is the day of recompense for all creatures, meaning the Day of Judgment, when Allah will call His creation to account for their deeds, evil for evil and good for good, except those He pardons. Many other Companions, Successors and scholars of the Salaf said the same, because this is the plain and obvious meaning of the verse.

### Why single out that day?

Allah's ownership of the Day of Judgment does not cancel His ownership of everything else. He has just been called Lord of all worlds, which includes this life and the next. The day is named because on that day no one but Him will be able to claim ownership of anything at all, and no one will even speak without His permission: "On the Day when the Spirit (ar-Rūḥ, Jibrīl or another angel) and the angels stand in rows, they will not speak except the one the Most Compassionate permits, and he will say what is right" (78:38), "All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate, and you will hear nothing but a faint shuffle of feet" (20:108), and "When that Day comes, no soul will speak except by His leave. Some of them will be wretched and some blessed" (11:105). Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk related that Ibn ʿAbbās explained the verse in a sentence: on that day no one owns anything of what they used to own in the world.

The fuller answer takes two steps. First, who is the real master? A moment's reflection shows that the true owner of every particle of dust in the universe can only be the One who created it and brings it up, whose mastery is complete, without beginning or end, covering the living and the dead, the visible and the hidden, the seen and the unseen. Man's mastery, by contrast, is fenced in by a "before", when it did not exist, and an "after", when it will exist no longer. It reaches the living and not the dead, the seen and not the unseen, the outside of things and not their inside. So the real Master of the whole universe, in this world as much as on the Day of Judgment, is none but Allah. Second, why then specify that day? Because Allah, in His generosity and wisdom, has given man a kind of imperfect, temporary and outward mastery too, and the Sharīʿah, in regulating worldly affairs, takes man's limited right of ownership into account. But man, holding land, money or power that was given to him as a trial, is always liable to become drunk with pride and conceit. "Master of the Day of Judgment" is the warning to a man reeling in forgetfulness and self-importance that all his possessions and all his relationships with things and people are short-lived, and that a day is coming when masters will no longer be masters and slaves no longer slaves, when nobody will hold even the appearance of owning anything, and when the ownership of the whole universe, outward as well as real, will be seen to belong to Allah and to no one else. Sūrat al-Muʾmin, also called Ghāfir, the fortieth surah, serves as a commentary on this phrase. On that day Allah will ask, "To whom belongs the kingdom today?" and no one, however high his station, will answer, so He will answer Himself: "To Allah, the One, the Overpowering" (40:16). "On the Day they come forth, nothing of theirs is hidden from Allah. Today every soul is recompensed for what it earned. No wrong is done today. Allah is swift in reckoning" (40:17).

### Allah is the true King

The hadiths press the same point. Allah is the true Owner, "the King, the Holy, the One free from every defect" (59:23). Abū Hurayrah reported, in a hadith in both Ṣaḥīḥs, that the Prophet ﷺ said that the most despicable name in Allah's sight is a man who calls himself the king of kings, when there is no owner but Allah. Both Ṣaḥīḥs also record that the Prophet ﷺ said that Allah will seize the earth and fold up the heavens in His Right Hand and proclaim, "I am the King. Where are the kings of the earth? Where are the tyrants? Where are the arrogant?" When the Qur'an calls a human being a king, it is a figure of speech, as when it says "Allah has appointed Ṭālūt (Saul) as king over you" (2:247), "there was a king behind them" (18:79) and, of the Children of Israel, "He made prophets among you and made you kings" (5:20), and as when the Prophet ﷺ described some of his community sailing to war "like kings on their thrones", in a hadith in both Ṣaḥīḥs.

The inner reading says the same thing in its own words. The verse points to the permanence of the kingship of the One and the unending compelling power of the Godhead. Every king's empire has its day, then it ends and vanishes, his kingship finishes and his state changes. Allah's kingship is permanent, today and tomorrow, and never comes to an end. In both worlds nothing and no one lies outside His kingship and rule, and no one has a kingship like His. He is Lord of the worlds today and will be Owner of the Day of Judgment tomorrow, which no creature can say of itself. Where, then, is the servant's power to act? Ownership and kingship in both worlds are Allah's, without partner, condition or need, and "your Lord creates what He wills and chooses. They have no choice" (28:68). The one who owns nothing rules nothing.

### Taking account of yourself before you are taken to account

Since dīn is reckoning, the verse is a call to begin the reckoning now. A hadith says that the wise person is the one who calls himself to account and works for what comes after death. ʿUmar said: "Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, weigh yourselves before you are weighed, and prepare for the great presentation before the One from whom nothing of your deeds is hidden," and he recited "That Day you will be presented, and no secret of yours will stay hidden" (69:18).

A gentler side of the reckoning is also noticed. Since Allah says, in effect, that He alone owns and takes charge of calling His servants to account, no one else will learn of their faults, and so no one will be shamed before others. The reckoning itself is a severity, but to keep the veil in place while it is done is pure generosity, and that is His way: whenever He strikes a blow of severity, He lays on it the balm of generosity. One of the masters of the Path said that if, tomorrow at the place of reckoning, he has anything to offer and is given leave to speak, he will say, "Lord God, of three things that I have, look at one: a prostration that never wanted anything from You but a heart, an assent by which I called true whatever You said, and a spirit and a heart that have wanted nothing but You since the wind of generosity first rose."

### Why the Day of Judgment is real, and reasonable

Two questions arise once the words are clear: what is this Day, and why does it exist? The Day of Judgment is the day Allah has appointed for repaying good and evil deeds. This world is the field of action, the place where a person is required to do his duty, not the place where he is paid. A man's being healthy, rich or powerful does not prove that he has won Allah's favour, and a man's being ill, poor, weak or miserable does not prove that he is under His anger. Even in worldly terms this is obvious: a man sweating in a factory or an office does not count his sweat a misfortune. Take his chance to sweat away from him and you have earned his deepest displeasure, because beyond the toil he can see the wages coming at the end of the month. The same principle explains why the greatest sufferings in this world fall to the prophets and, after them, to the people of Allah, who are nonetheless content and even happy. Physical well-being, worldly glory and comfort are no sure sign of virtue and truthfulness, and sorrow and suffering are no sure sign of wrongdoing and falsehood. It can happen that a person receives some punishment or reward for his deeds in this world, but it is never the full repayment, only a faint sample shown as a hint or a warning: "We will surely make them taste the nearer punishment before the greater punishment, so that they may return" (32:21), and "Such is the punishment, and the punishment of the Hereafter is far greater, if only they knew" (68:33). What this world brings, whether suffering or joy, is sometimes a test and sometimes a punishment, but it is never the full repayment, because the world itself is passing. What really counts is the joy or suffering that lasts forever, which a person will come to know in the world beyond this one.

Put the two facts together, that deeds are not fully repaid here, and that justice and reason both demand that good and evil, being unequal, should each be repaid according to its kind, and it follows that beyond this world there must be another in which every deed, large or small, good or evil, is judged and justly rewarded or punished. The Qur'an calls that world al-Ākhirah, the Hereafter, and its day al-Qiyāmah, the Resurrection, or Yawm ad-Dīn, the Day of Recompense. The Qur'an itself lays out the argument: "The blind and the seeing are not equal, nor are those who believe and do good and the wrongdoer. How little you reflect. The Hour is surely coming, there is no doubt about it, yet most people do not believe" (40:58-59).

### In short

- Mālik means the owner with full right to dispose of what he owns, and ad-dīn means recompense. On the Day of Judgment every creature is raised, called to account and repaid, and ownership of everything belongs openly to Allah alone.
- Allah owns everything now too. The day is singled out because on it no one will own anything, no one will speak without His leave, and the pride that possessions breed in man will be seen for what it was.
- "King of kings" is the most hateful title in Allah's sight. Human kingship is a figure of speech, and "Whose is the kingdom today? Allah's, the One, the Overpowering" is the last word on it.
- This world is the field of work, not the payroll. Health and wealth prove nothing, suffering proves nothing, and justice itself requires a day when every deed is fully repaid.
- Call yourself to account before you are called, and know that the One who takes the account keeps the veil over His servants' faults.

<<<VERSE 1:5>>>
With the words "You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help" the surah turns. The first three verses spoke about Allah, in the third person, and this verse speaks to Him. Having praised Him and remembered the day on which He alone will rule, the servant finds himself standing before Him and addresses Him directly, with the "You" of iyyāka. The sentence he says is the heart of the surah. Some of the Salaf said that al-Fātiḥah is the secret of the Qur'an and this verse is the secret of al-Fātiḥah, because the whole of religion is contained in its two halves: worship for Allah alone, and reliance on Allah alone.

The verse is where the praise of the first three verses naturally leads. A person's life runs through three states of time. The verses "All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds" and "the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful" told him that for his past and his present he owes everything to Allah alone, who made him out of nothing, gave him the finest form in creation along with reason and insight, and still sustains and nurtures him. "Master of the Day of Judgment" told him that in the future, too, he will have no one to depend on but Allah, since on the Day of Recompense there can be no helper other than Him. If a person depends wholly on Allah in all three states of his life, the conclusion is unavoidable: Allah alone deserves to be worshipped, and so "You alone we worship". And once it is understood that there is only one Being who can meet all one's needs, it is just as natural to turn to Him alone for help, and so "You alone we ask for help". In the words of the natural prayer that this reasoning produces: I have submitted to You and I humbly seek Your help, have mercy on me.

### Phrase by phrase

**"You alone we worship"** is iyyāka naʿbudu. In Arabic the object, "You", is placed before the verb, and putting it first in this way means "You and no one else". Worship, ʿibādah, comes from a root meaning to be subdued or made submissive: a road that has been beaten flat by use is called muʿabbadah, paved. In religious usage it means the utmost love, humility and fear brought together, the greatest humility and submissiveness a person can show, offered out of intense respect and love. An attitude of willing self-abasement like that cannot rightly be adopted towards anyone but Allah. So the phrase means: we reserve our worship for You alone in acknowledgment of Your Oneness, we turn to You as the only God and obey You, and we associate no one with You. This is tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah, the oneness of Allah as the only object of worship. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk related Ibn ʿAbbās's explanation: "It is You whom we single out, You whom we fear and in whom we hope, You alone, our Lord, and no one else."

**"and You alone we ask for help"** is wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn, with the same emphatic "You" placed first. It means that we ask help from You in worshipping You, from You we gain the confidence to obey You, and from You alone we ask help in all our affairs, since all good is in Your hand and there is no helper besides You. This is tawḥīd ar-rubūbiyyah, the oneness of Allah as Lord, the only one whose help avails. Ibn ʿAbbās completed his explanation: "and You we ask for help, to obey You and in all of our affairs." Qatādah said that the verse contains Allah's command to us to worship Him sincerely and to seek His aid in everything that concerns us. The verse does not say what the help is for, and most commentators take the silence as deliberate: the request is general, covering everything from acts of worship to every worldly and otherworldly concern.

### Two halves, the whole religion

The first half of the verse is a declaration of innocence from shirk, associating anything with Allah. The second half is a disowning of all power and strength of one's own, and an admission that every affair is in Allah's hands. The Qur'an pairs the two again and again: "So worship Him and rely on Him, and your Lord is not unaware of what you do" (11:123), "Say: He is the Most Compassionate, in Him we believe and in Him we rely" (67:29), and "Lord of the east and the west, there is no god but Him, so take Him as the One to rely on" (73:9).

The inner reading calls these two halves the two great pillars of the religion, around which the whole journey of the religious turns. The first pillar is adorning the soul, by worship and by purifying oneself, so that one's worship has no showing off in it and one's obedience no hypocrisy. The second is to purify the soul of associating anything with Allah and of relying on one's own power and strength. "Adornment" stands for everything in the Sharīʿah that ought to be, and "purification" for everything in it that ought not to be, so that a heart with familiarity and light in it can read the whole law out of these two short phrases. The Prophet ﷺ, al-Muṣṭafā, said, "I was given the all-comprehensive words, and my speech was made concise." The first half, it is said, is pure tawḥīd, the belief that nothing besides Allah deserves worship, the worshipper knowing that lordship belongs to Allah and that He is an object of worship without peer, unique and one. The second half is the recognition of those who truly recognise Him: that He alone acts in everything, and that the servant cannot manage on his own without His help. The root of this tawḥīd and this recognition is to recognise, first, that Allah is and that He is One, then His power, knowledge and loving-kindness, then His beautiful doing, His friendship and His nearness. The first recognition is the foundation of submission, the second the foundation of faith, and the third the foundation of purifying the self. The road to the first is to see the governance of the Maker in the loosening and tying of what He has made. The road to the second is to see the Maker's wisdom in oneself and recognise the correspondences. The road to the third is to see the gentleness of the Patron in His doing of good deeds and His setting aside of sins.

### Why worship comes before asking for help

"You alone we worship" is placed before "You alone we ask for help" because worship is the goal and Allah's help is the means of reaching it, and the more important thing is put first. There is also a logic in the surah's order as a whole. Praise of the One whose help is sought comes first, and only then the request. That is the best way to ask for anything: first praise the one you are asking, then ask for his help, for yourself and for your fellow Muslims, which is what the surah does next with "Guide us along the Straight Path". This method is the most fitting and the most effective in bringing an answer, which is why Allah taught it. In the hadith Abū Hurayrah reported in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in which Allah says that He has divided the prayer between Himself and His servant, the first three verses are answered with "My servant has praised Me" and "My servant has glorified Me", and when the servant reaches this verse Allah says, "This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for." The verse belongs to both of them: the worship is the servant's side, and the help is Allah's.

Asking for help can take several forms. One can describe one's own condition, as Mūsā (Moses) did: "My Lord, I am in need of whatever good You send down to me" (28:24). One can name the attributes of the One being asked, as Dhun-Nūn, Yūnus (Jonah), did: "There is no god but You, glory be to You, I have been one of the wrongdoers" (21:87). And one can simply praise Him without stating the need at all.

### Being a servant is an honour

Allah called His Messenger ﷺ "His servant" at the three noblest moments of his mission: when He mentioned sending down the Book, "All praise is for Allah, who has sent down the Book to His servant" (18:1), when He mentioned his calling people to Allah, "When the servant of Allah stood up calling on Him" (72:19), and when He mentioned the Isrāʾ, the night journey from Makkah to Jerusalem and then to heaven, "Glory be to Him who took His servant by night" (17:1). Because the surah opens with Allah praising Himself by His most beautiful attributes, and so teaching His servants to praise Him the same way, the prayer is not valid without it for anyone able to recite it: ʿUbādah ibn aṣ-Ṣāmit reported, in a hadith in both Ṣaḥīḥs, that the Prophet ﷺ said, "There is no prayer for the one who does not recite the Opening of the Book."

He also directed His Prophet ﷺ to worship as the remedy for the distress the deniers caused him: "We know that your breast is tightened by what they say. So glorify your Lord with praise, be among those who prostrate, and worship your Lord until the certainty comes to you" (15:97-99).

### What worship includes, and what shirk excludes

Worship is not confined to the prescribed prayers and fasting. In his Arbaʿīn, al-Ghazzālī counted ten forms it takes: the prayers, the prescribed alms, fasting, the pilgrimage to Makkah, reciting the Qur'an, remembering Allah in every situation, earning one's livelihood within the rules of the Sharīʿah, fulfilling one's duties to companions and neighbours, urging people to good and dissuading them from what is wrong and forbidden, and following the Sunnah of the Prophet ﷺ.

Not associating anyone with Allah in worship means, in practice, that one should not love, fear or depend on anyone as one loves, fears and depends on Allah, nor place one's hope in anyone as one places it in Him, nor regard obedience, submission or service to anyone as binding in the way His worship is binding, nor make a votive offering, consecrate or dedicate anything, or take a vow in anyone's name in the way these are done for Allah, nor abase and humble oneself completely before anyone in the way one is required to before Allah, nor perform for anyone other than Allah the particular acts of worship that belong to Him, the acts that mark the furthest limit of self-abasement, such as rukūʿ and sajdah, the bowing and prostration of the prayer. Beyond its two aspects of praise and prayer, then, the verse is a teaching: do not worship anyone but Allah, do not regard anyone else as truly able to meet your needs, and do not beg anyone else to meet them. It is not against this principle for a person praying to Allah to mention the name of a prophet or a righteous servant as a means, a wasīlah, of drawing Allah's mercy upon himself.

### In short

- The surah shifts here from speaking about Allah to speaking to Him. Having praised Him, the servant stands in front of Him and says "You".
- "You alone we worship" renounces shirk, and "You alone we ask for help" renounces reliance on one's own strength. Between them they hold the whole religion, which is why this verse was called the secret of al-Fātiḥah.
- Worship is the goal and help is the means, so worship is named first. Praise before request is the best way to ask, and Allah answers this verse with "This is between Me and My servant".
- Worship means the deepest love, humility and fear, and it includes prayer, alms, fasting, pilgrimage, recitation, remembrance, honest earning, duties to neighbours, enjoining good and following the Sunnah.
- Allah called His Prophet ﷺ "His servant" at the proudest moments of his mission. Servanthood is the highest rank a human being can hold.
