<<<VERSE 3:181>>>
> “Indeed, Allah has heard those ˹among the Jews˺ who said, “Allah is poor; we are rich!” We have certainly recorded their slurs and their killing of prophets unjustly. Then We will say, “Taste the torment of burning” (3:181)

This verse answers the blasphemous claim that Allah is poor because He invites people to give for His cause. Allah has heard the insult and records it, along with the unjust killing of prophets. The demand to “taste the torment of burning” warns that such words and deeds carry consequences.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Allah has heard those among the Jews who said, ‘Allah is poor; we are rich!’”** refers to a group who mocked the Qur’anic call to give a goodly loan to Allah (2:245). They twisted a metaphor of charity and reward into the claim that Allah needed their wealth. Allah is free of need. The “loan” is an invitation to give for good causes, with Allah promising a multiplied reward to the giver.

A report from Ibn Abbas, transmitted through Saʿid bin Jubayr, connects this statement to the revelation of the call to give. Ibn Abbas said that some Jews addressed Muḥammad ﷺ with the taunt that his Lord must be poor if He asked His servants for a loan. Ibn Marduwyah and Ibn Abi Hatim transmit the report. Al-Hasan is also reported among those who explained the demand for a fire-consumed offering discussed in the following verses.

Another account places the exchange in Madinah. Abu Bakr al-Siddiq visited a Jewish place of study and urged Finhas ibn Azura to accept the Prophet, pointing out that Muḥammad ﷺ was mentioned in the Torah. Finhas replied with the same taunt about Allah asking for a loan. Abu Bakr became angry, but the account says the Prophet ﷺ asked him what had happened and heard his explanation. A report through Shibl says the episode concerned the Jews and identifies Finhas as the speaker. A similar accusation that Allah’s hand is restrained appears in another passage (5:64). The shared point is that the claim was recorded as an insult, not accepted as a true description of Allah.

The verse identifies what was said by particular people. It must not be turned into an accusation against every Jew across time. The Qur’an criticizes the individuals who made this claim and connects their words with a history of rejecting messengers. The same Qur’an recognizes that people within religious communities are not all alike.

**“We have certainly recorded their slurs”** means that no insult is lost or forgotten. Allah’s recording is not because He needs evidence to learn what happened. It signifies that every word is known and will be brought to account. A person may mock revelation and then forget what was said, but Allah’s knowledge and justice do not forget.

**“and their killing of prophets unjustly”** adds a grave action to the blasphemous statement. The insult about Allah and the violence against prophets reflect the same defiance of divine guidance. The Qur’an recalls that some earlier communities killed prophets without right, including the prophets Zakariyya and Yahya in the historical explanation given here. Earlier messengers had brought al-Bayinat, clear proofs, but some were still rejected. The verse does not claim that every member of a later generation personally committed those murders. The responsibility belongs to those who acted and to anyone who endorses injustice or kufr.

**“Then We will say, ‘Taste the torment of burning’”** describes the final judgment of those who persisted in such wrongdoing. The command is a form of rebuke and humiliation. It is not a statement that believers may inflict punishment on people themselves. Judgment belongs to Allah, who knows what each person said, did, understood, and repented from.

### Words and actions both count

The verse brings speech and conduct together. A contemptuous statement about Allah is not a harmless joke, and killing a messenger is not a political disagreement. At the same time, the verse is careful to identify a group and specific crimes. Believers should not transfer guilt to people who did not commit those acts.

The account of a “loan” also teaches how Qur’anic language can be deliberately distorted. Allah uses a familiar human expression to encourage generosity and promise reward. The expression does not imply need in Allah. People are the ones who benefit from giving, while Allah remains completely self-sufficient.

One report says that Abu Bakr struck Finhas in anger after hearing the insult. Finhas then complained to the Prophet ﷺ, who asked Abu Bakr what had led him to act. The account presents the Prophet as investigating the dispute and hearing the explanation. It should not be used as permission for individuals to attack people who offend them. The Qur’anic response is that Allah has heard and recorded the words and will judge them. The story places the exchange in its historical setting, while the general lesson for believers remains to respond within justice and lawful authority.

The reference to the Torah in the account also shows the speaker’s contradiction: he was addressed with a call to recognize a messenger known in earlier revelation, yet answered with mockery. The insult did not make Allah dependent, and the call to charity did not add anything to Allah’s wealth. Zakah and charity purify the giver and help people in need. Their reward is an act of Allah’s generosity, not repayment that satisfies a need in Him.

### In short

- The “loan” to Allah is a metaphor for giving that Allah rewards, not evidence that He needs wealth.
- Allah records blasphemous speech and unjust violence against prophets.
- The accusation concerns particular wrongdoers and should not be generalized to all Jews.

<<<VERSE 3:182>>>
> “This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.” (3:182)

This verse explains that punishment answers to a person’s own deeds. Allah does not wrong His creation. The statement closes the warning that began with the insult against Allah and the unjust killing of prophets.

### Phrase by phrase

**“This is the reward for what your hands have done”** means that people face the consequences of their own choices. “Hands” represents actions, including harmful deeds carried out by a person and words spoken with contempt. The punishment is not arbitrary or imposed without cause.

The verse does not suggest that every hardship in this life is a direct punishment for one particular sin. It speaks here about the final reckoning of people who committed the acts just described. Believers should not use it to accuse someone who is ill or suffering of deserving that hardship. Only Allah knows the full account of a person’s life.

The people addressed in Madinah were not necessarily the people who had physically killed earlier prophets. Some explanations connect the history of such killings with Zakariyya and Yahya. The Qur’anic warning also challenges those who approve of an unjust act or remain satisfied with kufr. This is a warning about moral complicity, not a declaration that descendants inherit another person’s guilt merely through ancestry. A person who rejects wrongdoing is not the same as one who celebrates it.

The distinction matters for understanding “what your hands have done.” The phrase refers to responsibility for one’s own choices, including participation and deliberate approval. It does not authorize a crowd to punish people based on family history. Allah alone knows each person’s belief, intention, and response to wrongdoing.

**“And Allah is never unjust to His creation”** assures every person that Allah’s judgment is fair. He does not punish an innocent person for another’s crime, nor does He overlook a wrong that has not been repented from or forgiven. His knowledge encompasses intention, circumstance, and opportunity, things that human courts and observers cannot fully see.

### Justice and accountability

The verses just before this one mention a public insult against Allah and the killing of prophets. Verse 182 answers anyone who might see the announced punishment as arbitrary: it is connected to the deeds “your hands” sent ahead. The people being addressed had been warned, and their own actions account for the judgment.

The Qur’an’s account of earlier prophets includes Zakariyya and Yahya. They were killed by wrongdoers in an earlier generation, while the people addressed in Madinah lived later. Some explanations say that a person who is satisfied with an act of kufr shares moral complicity in it, even if they did not perform it. The principle concerns deliberate approval of wrongdoing. It does not make every later generation guilty by birth, and it does not remove the need to judge people individually.

A person can repent, reject an inherited wrong, or make amends for their own actions. Allah’s justice takes all of this into account. The verse is not a blanket explanation for illness, bereavement, or other suffering in this life. It concerns the final reckoning described in the passage, where Allah knows exactly what each person did and intended.

### In short

- The warning concerns consequences for the deeds just described.
- Allah’s judgment is never unjust.
- The verse does not authorize people to treat another person’s hardship as proof of guilt. The broader exchange includes clear proofs, al-Bayinat and al-Baiyyinat, and the rejected guidance of earlier prophets. This context explains why the punishment is tied to deliberate choices rather than imposed without warning.

<<<VERSE 3:183>>>
> “Those ˹are the same people˺ who say, “Allah has commanded us not to believe in any messenger unless he brings us an offering to be consumed by fire ˹from the sky˺.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Other prophets did in fact come to you before me with clear proofs and ˹even˺ what you demanded—why then did you kill them, if what you say is true?”” (3:183)

The verse answers people who claimed that they could not believe in a messenger unless he brought an offering consumed by fire from heaven. It points out that earlier messengers had brought clear proofs and the very sign being demanded, yet some of those prophets were killed. The claim was therefore an excuse, not a sincere condition for belief.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Those are the same people who say”** begins with alladhīna, “those who,” and links the argument to the group criticized in the previous verses. The issue is not a sincere question about evidence. It is a shifting demand made by people who had already rejected messengers.

**“Allah has commanded us not to believe in any messenger unless he brings us an offering to be consumed by fire from the sky”** describes a claim about a supposed divine covenant. In some earlier religious contexts, a sign of acceptance was said to be fire descending and consuming an offering. The people invoking this condition insisted that a messenger must reproduce that sign before they would believe.

The Qur’an does not accept their description as a valid excuse. A genuine search for truth remains open to evidence. A person who sets a condition and then rejects the messenger when it is met has not been prevented from believing by lack of proof. The supposed pledge was claimed from the Torah and applied by the speakers to messengers before Muḥammad ﷺ. The account does not make the fire sign a universal condition for every prophet, including Jesus. Allah Almighty sent each messenger with the signs and guidance appropriate to his mission.

**“Other prophets did in fact come to you before me with clear proofs”** means that earlier messengers brought al-bayyināt, the evident proofs also transliterated al-Bayinat or al-Baiyyinat. These were signs confirming their truth. Their message was not unsupported. Their communities received guidance and had the opportunity to recognize it.

**“and even what you demanded”** confronts the condition directly. Earlier prophets brought the requested sign of an offering consumed by fire. The evidence they demanded was therefore not absent. The question is whether they accepted the message when proof came.

**“why then did you kill them, if what you say is true?”** exposes the contradiction. If the demanded sign was truly the only obstacle, the people should have believed when it appeared. Instead, some responded by rejecting and killing prophets. Their actions revealed that they did not intend to follow the truth even when it was made clear.

The verse speaks of those who committed or endorsed the killing. It does not assign guilt indiscriminately to every descendant or every person in the Children of Israel. The Qur’an records wrongdoing in order to warn and guide, not to license collective hatred.

### A demand that keeps moving

The account illustrates a familiar form of evasion. A person asks for a specific sign, but when the sign is provided, they invent another objection. The original demand was never the real barrier. Pride, attachment to custom, or fear of losing status can keep a person from accepting truth even after clear evidence.

The verse also teaches that miracles do not compel faith in someone who is determined to reject. Evidence matters, but the willingness to respond honestly matters too. A messenger’s task is to convey the truth. The listener remains responsible for what they do with it.

One account attributed to al-Kalbi names Finhas ibn Azura and Huyayy ibn Akhtab among those who presented the demand. Kaʿb al-Ashraf is also identified as part of the group. Another version names Malik ibn al-Dayf, Wahb ibn Yahudha, and Zayd ibn Tabuh. These reports situate the claim within a particular debate. They should not be turned into a charge against every member of the Children of Israel.

A spiritual reflection uses the word ʿirfān for deep recognition of truth and observes that a person committed to doubt may not accept even an extraordinary sign. This is an inward lesson about readiness to recognize guidance. It does not replace the verse’s direct argument: the earlier messengers had clear proofs and the requested offering, yet some were still rejected or killed.

The Qur’anic reference to fire consuming an offering is not a general requirement that every prophet perform the same miracle for every audience. It is an answer to the particular claim made in this passage. Believers should not turn one group’s demand into a condition for recognizing every messenger.

### In short

- Some people claimed that a fire-consumed offering was a required sign for belief.
- Earlier messengers brought clear proofs and the demanded sign.
- Killing those messengers exposed the claim as an excuse rather than sincere openness.

<<<VERSE 3:184>>>
> “If you are rejected by them, so too were messengers before you who came with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures” (3:184)

Allah comforts Muḥammad ﷺ by reminding him that earlier messengers were also rejected. Their rejection did not invalidate their message. They came with clear proofs and revelation that guided their communities.

### Phrase by phrase

**“If you are rejected by them”** acknowledges the Prophet’s experience without blaming him for it. People may refuse a messenger even when the message is clear. Rejection is not proof that the messenger has failed.

**“so too were messengers before you”** places Muḥammad ﷺ in the company of earlier prophets. Their histories show that opposition is not new and that a messenger’s worth is not measured by how many people accept immediately.

**“who came with clear proofs”** means their message was supported by signs and evidence. The term al-Bayinat, also rendered al-Baiyyinat, refers to these clear signs. They did not ask people to believe without guidance.

**“divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”** refers to revealed writings that taught people what Allah required and clarified the path of truth. The plural descriptions emphasize that Allah sent guidance through successive messengers and scriptures. The Scriptures include the Psalms and the scrolls associated with Abraham, while the illuminating Book refers to revelation such as the Torah and Gospel. These books conveyed guidance and the sacred law for their communities. The Prophet can say with confidence that his rejection does not erase this history: earlier messengers also faced denial despite the signs and books they brought.

### In short

- Earlier messengers faced rejection despite bringing clear proof.
- Opposition does not invalidate a messenger’s mission.
- Revelation provides guidance and clarification for those willing to receive it. The Prophet is told to remain patient.

<<<VERSE 3:185>>>
> “Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment. Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment” (3:185)

Every living soul will die, and complete recompense comes on the Day of Judgment. True success is to be saved from the Fire and admitted into Paradise. The pleasures of this world are temporary and can deceive people if they mistake them for the final measure of success.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Every soul will taste death”** is a universal statement. Prophets, rulers, the poor, the powerful, and ordinary people all leave this life. No person’s status makes them exempt from death. Allah alone is everlasting, while all created beings pass away when their appointed term ends.

This truth comforts people who fear that death is an injustice reserved for them alone. It also prevents arrogance. Wealth, family, and social rank cannot keep death away. The Qur’an reminds believers that death is part of the created order and that every soul will return to Allah.

**“And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment”** means that the complete account of every deed is not settled by worldly outcomes. Some people suffer while doing good, and some enjoy comfort while doing wrong. Their circumstances in this life do not tell the whole story. Allah will recompense every person fully and justly on the Day of Resurrection.

The verse does not deny that Allah may grant reward or punishment in this world. It says the complete reward and final judgment come later. A believer can endure an apparent loss without assuming their good deeds have been wasted, and should not envy someone whose present prosperity hides an unsettled account.

**“Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will indeed triumph”** defines real success. It is not merely avoiding embarrassment, gaining wealth, or winning a temporary contest. Salvation from punishment and entry into Paradise are the lasting victory.

The verse describes a great triumph, al-kubrā, rather than a merely worldly success. A hadith reported from Abu Hurayrah compares a place in Paradise as small as the space occupied by a whip with the entire world and all it contains. The comparison shows how little worldly advantage is worth beside even a small share of the lasting reward. Al-Bukhari, Al-Hakim, and Ibn Hibban transmit reports about this teaching, with differing chains and wordings. The report appears in the Two Sahihs in a related form. Abu Hatim Ibn Hibban also records it in his collection. The point is not to measure Paradise by worldly objects, but to help people see the scale of the Hereafter. Every nafs, or soul among the children of Adam, must face this final return to Allah.

**“whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment”** means worldly pleasures are brief and can mislead when treated as ultimate. Food, wealth, status, and family can be blessings, but they cannot provide permanent security. They deceive when people pursue them while forgetting death and accountability.

The Qur’an elsewhere says that what is on earth will perish while Allah remains (55:26-27). It contrasts the preference for worldly life with the better and more lasting Hereafter (87:16-17), and says that worldly enjoyment and adornment are less than what is with Allah (28:60). These references do not call worldly life worthless in every respect. They put it in perspective as a temporary place of action, not the final home.

### What success means

The verse reframes the way believers measure their lives. A person may lose wealth, reputation, or health but still be successful if they remain faithful and receive Allah’s mercy. Conversely, someone may appear triumphant in public yet lose the lasting reward through injustice and rejection.

Qatadah reminds people that life’s pleasures soon fade. The believer should use what they have been given for obedience while it remains available. Enjoyment is not condemned simply because it is worldly. The danger is allowing it to distract from Allah and the Hereafter.

Death also makes life’s moral choices urgent. People do not know how long they will have to repent, repair harm, or fulfil obligations. The verse calls for action now, before the opportunity ends, while leaving the final reward to Allah’s justice and generosity.

This teaching brings together the mortality of each person and the oneness of Allah, who alone remains when creation passes away. The children of Adam share this limit, whether their lives are brief or long. The Qur’an also describes fear, hunger, and the loss of property and lives as tests (2:155). A test is not the final judgment. The soul’s full recompense waits until the Day when all deeds are weighed, and those who are saved will recognize that Paradise is a greater victory than any worldly comfort.

### In short

- Death comes to every soul, and full recompense belongs to the Day of Judgment.
- Real triumph is salvation from the Fire and admission to Paradise.
- Worldly enjoyment is temporary and should be used without forgetting the Hereafter.

<<<VERSE 3:186>>>
> “You ˹believers˺ will surely be tested in your wealth and yourselves, and you will certainly hear many hurtful words from those who were given the Scripture before you and ˹from˺ the polytheists. But if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺—surely this is a resolve to aspire to” (3:186)

Allah tells believers to expect tests in their wealth and persons, as well as painful words from some people of earlier scriptures and from polytheists. Patience and taqwa are the resolve that enables them to meet these trials without abandoning what is right.

### Phrase by phrase

**“You believers will surely be tested in your wealth and yourselves”** includes loss of property, difficulty earning, physical injury, illness, fear, and other hardships that touch a person’s life. Tests differ from one person to another. They are not all punishments, and a person cannot infer Allah’s anger simply from the fact of suffering.

The Qur’an elsewhere mentions fear, hunger, loss of wealth, lives, and produce as tests (2:155). In Surah al-Baqarah, this description is followed by glad tidings for those who meet hardship with patience. A believer may be tested according to their circumstances and capacity. The response is to seek help, take lawful measures, and remain patient without claiming that the trial is easy.

Hardship does not suspend the sacred law or make every reaction permissible. A person may defend their rights, ask for protection, and speak honestly, while still avoiding revenge, false accusation, or cruelty. Taqwa helps the believer keep those boundaries when hurtful words provoke anger.

**“and you will certainly hear many hurtful words”** prepares the believers for verbal hostility as well as material loss. Some among those given earlier scripture and some polytheists insulted or provoked the Muslims. The verse identifies the groups from whom such words may come in the context of the early community. It does not accuse every Jew, Christian, or polytheist of the same conduct.

The companions faced hostility in Madinah before Badr. A report from Usamah bin Zayd records an encounter on the Prophet’s way to visit Saʿd bin ʿUbadah of the Khazraj. The details show how patience and wise restraint worked in a tense gathering.

The account shows the kind of hurtful speech the believers might hear and the Prophet’s response to it. Saʿd bin ʿUbadah later asked him to pardon Abdullah bin Ubayy, explaining that the people of Madinah had once considered making him their leader before Islam changed their direction. The Prophet ﷺ forgave him. The story illustrates restraint and patience, not approval of the insult.

A further report says that the Prophet ﷺ and his companions continued to pardon and overlook the People of the Scripture until Allah gave further commands. The Qur’an had already told believers to forgive and overlook until Allah’s command came (2:109). This context does not make patience a prohibition on every form of self-defence. It shows that the Prophet chose a measured response to a particular dispute and situation.

**“But if you are patient and mindful of Allah”** joins sabr with taqwa. Patience is not silent approval of injustice or a refusal to seek help. It is steadiness that keeps a person from answering harm with wrongdoing. Taqwa guides the response so that anger does not lead to slander, cruelty, or betrayal of principle.

The believers are not asked to seek insults or hardship. They are told how to respond when those things occur. Patient perseverance can include protecting oneself, speaking truthfully, asking for justice, and choosing forgiveness when it is wise. The verse praises restraint that remains within Allah’s guidance.

**“surely this is a resolve to aspire to”** describes the strength required to be patient and mindful. It is a firm decision, not a passive temperament. A person must choose repeatedly to remain just while provoked and faithful while tested.

### Trials and the community

The account of Abdullah bin Ubayy also shows why the Prophet did not respond to every insult with punishment. Saʿd bin ʿUbadah understood the rivalries and wounds in Madinah and asked the Prophet to pardon the man. The Prophet’s forbearance helped prevent a quarrel among people who would have to live together. Later, after Badr, Abdullah bin Ubayy and those with him entered the Muslim community. The report should not obscure the harm of his earlier words, but it shows that people and situations can change.

The names in this account locate the event in the social life of Madinah. Usamah bin Zayd was with the Prophet. Saʿd bin ʿUbadah belonged to the Khazraj, and Abdullah bin Rawahah spoke in defence of the Prophet. Abdullah bin Ubayy, also called Abu Hubbab in the report, had influence among the people before the Prophet’s arrival. The story is about a particular exchange, not a judgment on the entire community or tribe. The report is recorded by Al-Bukhari and places the event in a mixed gathering in Madinah, before Badr. The Prophet was visiting Saʿd bin ʿUbadah among Bani al-Harith of the Khazraj. Abdullah bin Rawahah welcomed the Prophet ﷺ and encouraged him to address the gathering. Abdullah bin Ubayy objected and asked him not to disturb the meeting. The Prophet greeted those present, called them to Allah, and recited to them. The exchange became heated among Muslims, idol worshippers, and Jews, but the Prophet calmed them rather than turning an insult into a fight.

Afterward, Saʿd bin ʿUbadah, a leader of the Khazraj, asked the Prophet to pardon Abdullah bin Ubayy. Saʿd explained that before the Prophet’s arrival, people in Madinah had considered making Abdullah their leader. The new community and its message had changed those expectations, and Abdullah’s resentment contributed to his conduct. The Prophet accepted Saʿd’s request and forgave him. The report calls Abdullah bin Ubayy Abu Hubbab, a name by which he was known in that account.

The report further says that the Prophet and his companions continued to pardon and overlook the People of the Scripture and the polytheists until Allah gave further instruction. The Qur’an had already directed believers to forgive and overlook until Allah’s command came (2:109). After Badr, when Quraysh’s leaders were defeated, Abdullah and some of those with him entered the Muslim community. This account illustrates that a person’s behaviour can change, and that a measured response may preserve peace. It does not make the earlier insult harmless or require people to remain in an unsafe situation.

The verse’s promise also reaches beyond verbal insults. Believers may face pressure over wealth, family, health, and safety. The Qur’an describes fear, hunger, loss of wealth, lives, and produce as tests (2:155). Patience and taqwa help people keep their obligations while seeking appropriate means of relief. The Prophet ﷺ and the companions experienced fear and loss, but they did not conclude that hardship meant Allah had abandoned them.

Patience here is not mere silence. It can mean speaking truth without abusive retaliation, protecting the vulnerable, seeking a just remedy, and deciding when forgiveness will prevent a greater harm. Taqwa keeps these responses within Allah’s limits. A person who faces cruel words may feel anger or grief. The verse does not deny those feelings. It calls for a firm resolve not to let them make one unjust.

The phrase “a resolve to aspire to” praises a demanding form of character. Anyone can remain courteous when unprovoked. The test is to preserve fairness when insulted, maintain faith when pressured, and avoid revenge that violates justice. This is why patience and mindfulness are joined: patience steadies the person, while taqwa directs that steadiness toward what pleases Allah.

### In short

- Believers will face material and personal trials as well as hurtful speech.
- The verse warns about particular hostile conduct, not entire religious communities.
- Patience joined with taqwa means steadfastness, justice, and wise restraint under pressure.
