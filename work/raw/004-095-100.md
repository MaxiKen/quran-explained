<<<VERSE 4:95>>>
The rank of the striver: "are not equal to those who strive in the cause of Allah with their wealth and their lives. Allah has elevated in rank those who strive with their wealth and their lives above those who stay behind" (4:95). The verse sets the mujāhid above the sitter, then guards the sitter who has a reason, and the balance of both duties runs through the whole discussion.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Those who stay at home"** are the believers who sit away from the struggle while suffering no disabling hurt, and the verse says plainly that they are not the equals of those who strive in the way of Allah with their possessions and their lives. The exception came down by revelation itself. Al-Barāʾ said that on the coming down of the verse, a summons went to Zayd from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ with the command to write it. Then Ibn Umm Maktūm arrived and spoke of his blindness, and the exception came down by revelation. Sahl ibn Saʿd as-Saʿʿadī told the fuller form, from Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, from Zayd ibn Thābit, who dictated the verse for the Prophet ﷺ and was leaning his thigh on his when Ibn Umm Maktūm came, saying that if he had power he would surely take part in jihād, for he was blind. Revelation descended on the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and the weight on Zayd's thigh grew so heavy that he feared it would be crushed, until the exception came down and the weight lifted. Al-Bukhārī recorded both forms, and Muslim carries the second from Bundār and Ghundar, both relating it from Shuʿbah, with az-Zuhrī in the chain of the fuller version. At-Tirmidhī recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās the identification of the two parties as those kept from the battle of Badr and those who went to it. As Badr drew near, the question had come from Abū Aḥmad ibn Jahsh and Ibn Umm Maktūm: blind men both, they asked what excuse could cover them. The school gloss names them ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm and ʿAbdullāh ibn Jahsh al-Asdī, and the answer was the exception. Whoever has a disability, blindness, a limp or an illness preventing him from joining, is not compared with the mujāhidīn as the able sitter is.

**"Allah has elevated in rank those who strive with their wealth and their lives above those who stay behind"** gives the preference its measure: a degree, since both may hold the same intention, and the extra degree goes to the one who carried the struggle through. Allah made those who fight stand above those who sit at home, not hindered by any disability.

**"Allah has promised each a fine reward"** keeps the sitter from despair. Unto each, the fighters and the sitters alike, Allah has promised good, meaning Paradise and tremendous rewards. In his Ṣaḥīḥ, al-Bukhārī records from Anas the word of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ:

> There are people who remained in al-Madinah who were with you in every march you marched and every valley you crossed.

Asked how that could be while they stayed in al-Madinah, he said that only their disability had hindered them. The verse thus indicates that jihād is not a duty on each and every individual, but a duty of sufficiency.

**"those who strive will receive a far better reward"** states the difference in kind: a huge reward above the common promise.

### The duty of sufficiency

According to the leading exegetes, this verse teaches that jihād is fard ʿalā al-kifāyah under normal conditions. As opposed to fard ʿalā al-ʿayn, the obligation of every individual, the duty of sufficiency works this way: should some people fulfil it, the rest of the Muslims stand relieved, subject to the condition that those engaged are sufficient for that jihād. If the situation is otherwise, jihād becomes an absolute obligation on all Muslims living in the neighbouring areas, who must come forward to help.

In the terminology of Islamic law, the duty of sufficiency covers obligations the fulfilment of which is not mandatory on every individual. Most activities of a collective nature fall under the category. The teaching of the religious sciences and their wider dissemination is one such obligation: if enough people devote themselves to it, the rest are absolved, but if nobody discharges it where the need stands, everyone becomes a sinner. The ṣalāh for the deceased, with the washing and the shroud, is likewise a collective obligation and a requirement of Islamic law, the right of a Muslim brother that a brother discharges. The building of mosques and religious schools and the running of public welfare come under the same rule. Injunctions of collective need are classed this way of necessity, so that all duties are discharged under the principle of distribution of work: some engaged in jihād, others in education and daʿwah, and others attending to the remaining Islamic and human needs. The escalation returns when defence is short: if the fighters need support, jihād becomes an absolute obligation first on the neighbouring Muslims, then on those nearby, and in the last resort on every Muslim of the East and the West.

The wider teaching reads the same scale. Muslims fall broadly into two categories by their actions. One adopts the Islamic way of life within the confines of the obligatory injunctions, worshipping Allah and living discreetly, heeding the lawful and the unlawful. The other lives at the level of sacrifice, exerting itself to carry the message to people, willingly enduring the difficulties, placing life and possessions in jeopardy for the cause. Both are sincere, and both receive their shares of grace, but the status of the second differs. Their offerings in the path of Allah were not measurable, so neither will the requital of their efforts be. They joined the mission without consideration of expediency, and accordingly the grace and mercy returned to them will be beyond measure. In the inward reading, the favours of the Real gather all His friends together, yet the degrees keep their distinctions. Rich or servant, anyone finds someone richer. Greatness always meets a greater. The stars glisten, and the moon stands above them, but the sun, once risen, dims every light with its own.

### In short

- The sitter without excuse is not the equal of the striver, and the exception for disability was revealed while Zayd ibn Thābit's thigh bore the weight of it.
- Both parties are promised good, the sitters of al-Madinah counted present at every march through their hindrance alone.
- Jihād is a duty of sufficiency in normal conditions, relieving the community when enough respond, and pressing outward neighbour by neighbour when they do not.
- The two levels of religion, obligation and sacrifice, both receive grace, and the sacrifice that cannot be measured is requited beyond measure.

<<<VERSE 4:96>>>
The reward itemised: "far superior ranks, forgiveness, and mercy from Him. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" (4:96). The great reward of the previous verse opens into its parts.

### Phrase by phrase

**"far superior ranks"** are degrees, stations one higher than the other in honour, rooms in Paradise that Allah gives the strivers along with His forgiveness and the descent of mercy and blessing, as a favour and honour from Him. In the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī narrates that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that there are a hundred grades in Paradise prepared for the mujāhidīn in His cause, the distance between each two grades being as the distance between heaven and Earth. Forgiveness covers the sins, and mercy is what saves from torment. One reading of the grammar folds the parts together, the forgiveness and the mercy standing as the object of the preferring, so that the degrees and the pardon come in one gift. **"forgiveness, and mercy from Him"** divide the gift further, forgiveness covering the sins and mercy saving from torment, and the grammar of the passage folds them into one act of preferring.

**"And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** names the Giver: Forgiving toward those who repent of staying back and go out, Merciful toward him who dies in a state of repentance. The ruling stands alongside: no obligation of jihād falls on the lame, the crippled, the blind or the sick, nor on others the Islamic law holds excusable.

### In short

- The huge reward divides into degrees, one station above another, and the highest reach a hundred grades for the mujāhidīn.
- Forgiveness and mercy ride with the ranks, as one gift of the preferring.
- The disabled and the sick stand excused from the obligation entirely.

<<<VERSE 4:97>>>
The death of the wronged soul: "When the angels seize the souls of those who have wronged themselves" (4:97). The verse turns from the one who stayed behind with excuse to the one who stayed where staying was sin.

### The occasion of the warning

Al-Bukhārī recorded from Muḥammad ibn ʿAbdur-Raḥmān, Abū al-Aswad, that the people of al-Madinah were forced to prepare an army against the people of ash-Shām during the khilāfah of ʿAbdullāh ibn az-Zubayr at Makkah, and he was enlisted. Meeting ʿIkrimah, the freed slave of Ibn ʿAbbās, he told him of it, and ʿIkrimah forbade him strongly, then related that Ibn ʿAbbās had told him that some Muslims used to go out with the idolaters, increasing the size of their army against the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. An arrow would hit one of them and kill him, or a sword would strike his neck, and Allah sent down this verse. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk stated that it came down about the hypocrites who stayed behind from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, kept to Makkah, marched out with the idolaters to Badr, and fell among the fallen there. One report counts them among fifty who had left Islam and were all killed at Badr, and another describes them as people who professed Islam outwardly but were hypocrites inwardly, joined the idolaters against the Muslims, and had their faces and backs struck by the angels as they were taken. The verse thus concerns residence among the idolaters while able to emigrate and unable to practise the faith, an injustice against the self and a prohibition by consensus.

### Phrase by phrase

**"scolding them, 'What do you think you were doing?'"** is the angels' question at the taking. It asks after their condition, why they stayed and never made the hijrah, what their religion stood in at the end.

**"they will reply, 'We were oppressed in the land.'"** is the excuse offered: we were weak and oppressed, unable to leave the land or move about in the earth, unable to establish religion in the land of Makkah.

**"The angels will respond, 'Was Allah's earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate?'"** rejects the excuse, for emigration from the land of unbelief to another land stood open, as others had done. The rule behind the rebuke was stated by the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, in the record of Abū Dāwūd from Samurah ibn Jundub:

> Whoever mingles with the idolater and resides with him, he is just like him.

**"It is they who will have Hell as their home"** gives the end of the matter, an evil destination and an evil journey's end.

The Prophet ﷺ knew the trap and prayed against it. Al-Bukhārī records from Abū Hurayrah that while he was leading the ʿIshāʾ prayer, he said before prostrating:

> O Allah! Save ʿAyyāsh ibn Abī Rabīʿah. O Allah! Save Salamah ibn Hishām. O Allah! Save al-Walīd ibn al-Walīd. O Allah! Save the weak Muslims. O Allah! Be very hard on the Mudar tribe. O Allah! Afflict them with years of famine similar to the famine years of the time of the Prophet Yūsuf.

The lesson beneath the law reaches every soul. A believer naturally wants a free environment where his believing personality finds room to express itself, and if the environment turns unfavourable, he had better change his surroundings, which is the reality of hijrah. Take a person attached to an organisation dominated by certain personalities, where his room shrinks to glorifying the personalities, with no room left for glorifying Allah. If he compromises with that atmosphere for the sake of self-interest, fails to proclaim the truth as he sees it, and breathes his last in that state, then he has perpetrated an injustice against his own soul.

The inward reading sharpens it further. The verse alludes to the person whose hour arrives with him still penned in the lower self and enslaved to its desires, and he has no excuse, for he never sought the shelter of his Lord's nearness, the shelter that would have delivered him from the self's contemptible nature. There is no veil between a person and this talk except his own whims.

### In short

- Muslims who marched with the idolaters against the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and died at Badr are the occasion, and residence among idolaters while able to leave stands prohibited by consensus.
- The angels' question dismantles the excuse of oppression, for Allah's earth was spacious.
- Whoever settles with the idolater becomes as he is, and the Prophet ﷺ prayed by name for those trapped, weak Muslims.
- The trap is inward as much as outward, for the soul that never left its whims died wronged.

<<<VERSE 4:98>>>
The exception: "Except helpless men, women, and children who cannot afford a way out" (4:98). After the warning comes the mercy.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Except helpless men, women, and children"** names the weak ones, the elderly and the infirm among men, and the women and the children, in Makkah unable to plan a way out and not shown one.

**"who cannot afford a way out"** explains the inability: they have no strength to emigrate and no substance, and they do not find the way to it, as Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah and as-Suddī stated. Allah gives this excuse because such people are unable to free themselves from the idolaters, and even if they managed it, they would not know which way to go.

Ibn ʿAbbās put himself inside the verse, in the report that came through Ayyūb and Ibn Abī Mulaykah, saying that he and his mother were among those weak ones whom Allah excused.

### In short

- The helpless of men, women and children stand outside the warning.
- Their excuse is real inability, no plan, no way, no strength, no substance.
- Even Ibn ʿAbbās and his mother counted themselves among those Allah excused.

<<<VERSE 4:99>>>
The hope: "it is right to hope that Allah will pardon them. For Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving" (4:99). The exception ends not in bare acquittal but in expectation.

The word of likelihood here means certainty: Allah shall pardon them for not migrating, for He is Ever-Pardoning, Oft-Forgiving, clement toward the likes of these and forgiving of those among them who repent.

### Phrase by phrase

**"it is right to hope that Allah will pardon them"** rests the hope on the exemption. Those truly helpless and incapable of support are spared the condition laid on the able. When a call of truth is raised and others refuse to leave their cocoons of self-interest, failing to join and support the caller, dying in that state is the injustice to their own lives, and it is from this that the helpless are exempted.

**"For Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving"** seals the hope with two names of pardon.

The wider lesson of the set runs on. The inward reading of the exception reaches the life of the spirit.

It alludes to those overwhelmed and annihilated by meanings, who then continue on as those who act on His behalf. Of themselves they own neither force nor strength, and what reaches them is only what He lets flow over them of the decrees. Once the self has returned through the reality of the Real, the ḥaqq of the ḥaqq, they are effaced from themselves. No way guides them to anything but Him, and no breath of theirs goes to anything but Him. On the outward aspect of the verse, the reading of the scholars is that the Real's pardon shall surely reach those whose excuses held them back from choosing to emigrate.

### In short

- The likelihood of pardon is a promise in the language of hope.
- The truly helpless receive the exemption, while the able who merely chose comfort stand under the warning.
- In the life of the spirit, the overwhelmed and effaced have their own portion of the exception.

<<<VERSE 4:100>>>
The promise to the emigrant: "Whoever emigrates in the cause of Allah will find many safe havens and bountiful resources throughout the earth" (4:100). The verse closes the set with the widest promise of all, held out to whoever leaves home for Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Whoever emigrates in the cause of Allah"** names the act first, and the act has a definition. In the lexicon, hijrah with its sisters ḥijrān and hajr names displeasure with a thing and the leaving of it. Common speech applies it to leaving one's home country. The Sharīʿah gives it the technical sense of quitting Dār al-Kufr, the homeland of disbelief, for Dār al-Islam, in the definition of Rūḥ al-Maʿānī. Mullā ʿAlī al-Qārī notes in Sharḥ al-Mishkah that leaving a home country for religious reasons also comes under it. The verse "Those who have been driven away from their homes" (59:8), revealed about the emigrating Companions, adds a further case: the expulsion of Muslims from a country of disbelievers, on account of their faith, counts as hijrah with them. The Muslims who left the subcontinent for Pakistan out of disgust with the abode of disbelief, or were driven out for their faith, were muhājirs in the legal sense, while those who moved for business or employment are not entitled to the name in that sense. Then there is the hadith of al-Bukhārī and Muslim in which the Prophet ﷺ says that the true muhājir abandons all that Allah and His Messenger have prohibited. Its first sentence sets the frame: a Muslim is the one from whom all Muslims remain safe, from his tongue and from his hands, as at-Tirmidhī records in the Book of Īmān. A true and successful emigrant does not treat migration from his homeland as the ultimate obligation. He leaves everything else the Sharīʿah has declared unlawful, for, as the poets of the path put it, when you change into the iḥrām garment, change your heart too.

### The merits of hijrah

The merits fill the Qur'ān. Sūrat al-Baqarah says: "As for those who believed and those who migrated and carried out jihād in the way of Allah, they do hope for Allah's mercy" (2:218). Sūrat at-Taubah says: "And those who believed and emigrated and took up jihād in the way of Allah with their wealth and lives are greater in rank in the sight of Allah" (9:20). And this very verse promises the many places of refuge and the wide resources. Some narrations tie the verse to Khālid ibn Hizām, who set out from Makkah with the hijrah to Ethiopia in view. A snake bit him on the road and brought on his death. In a hadith, the Prophet ﷺ says that hijrah undoes all the sins committed before it.

### The blessings of hijrah

The blessings have their verse in sūrat an-Naḥl: "And those who migrated for the sake of Allah after they were subjected to injustice, to them We shall give a good place to be in the world" (16:41). The word muragham in the promise is a verbal noun meaning the moving from one land to another, and it also names the place to which one moves and settles. The good place has been read variously, Mujāhid explaining it as lawful sustenance, Ḥasan al-Baṣrī as a good home, and others as superiority in excellence, honour and power over antagonists. The truth is that all these elements are included in the sense of the verse. History bears witness. Ibrāhīm migrated to Syria from his homeland in Iraq, and Allah gave him all those things. Mūsā (Moses) and Banū Isrāʾīl migrated from Egypt for the sake of Allah, and He gave them the land of Syria, a better homeland, and then Egypt too. When the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions left Makkah for Allah and His Messenger, Madinah received the blessed emigrants with the best of shelter, better far than what Makkah had held, and honour and power and peace and prosperity followed, apart from the transitory hardship of the early period.

The events related to the poverty and hunger of the noble Companions belong to that early period, or to a volitional acceptance of poverty, a dignified readiness to live with less. They did not care for worldly wealth, spending what they received in the way of Allah without delay, and the Prophet ﷺ lived in that state, his lack of means and his patience in hunger voluntary, for he simply did not choose to be rich. In the sixth year after Khayber, sufficient means were available to him and his family, and the same held for the righteous caliphs. When Islam needed support, Abū Bakr came forward and donated everything in his house. Zaynab, the Mother of the Faithful, gave her whole stipend to the poor and lived contentedly in want, earning the name Mother of the Needy. On the other side, many rich ones among the Companions left wealth and property behind, and many poor ones, like Abū Hurairah, were made rich after the hijrah. When he was appointed governor of a province, he would address his own past with relish:

> O Abū Hurairah, you are the same man, the servant of a tribe. Your salary was what you could eat, your duty was to walk with those who rode, and to collect their firewood when they broke journey. Today, because of Islam, you are here, so high from so low, and they call you the Commander of the Faithful.

The promise nonetheless carries a condition, that they be true emigrants for the sake of Allah, not migrating for worldly wealth, office, power, recognition, honour or influence. In a hadith of al-Bukhārī, the Prophet ﷺ said that the migration of those who migrate for Allah and His Messenger is precisely for Allah and His Messenger, while those who migrate to make money or to marry a woman receive exactly what they migrated for. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs, with the Musnad and the compilers of the Sunan, record from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb the foundation of it all, that actions go by intentions, so that each emigrant receives according to what he set out for. Groups of emigrants in distress today are either passing through the transitory hardship that marks early hijrah, or they are not muhājirs in the real sense. Their course is to correct the intention and take charge of their circumstances, and the truth of Allah's promise will then show itself to their own eyes.

### The dying emigrant

The closing words secure the reward even at the roadside: whoever leaves his home as an emigrant to Allah and His Messenger, and death overtakes him, his reward is incumbent upon Allah. The case stands in the records. Jundaʿ, or Jundab, ibn Damura al-Laythī, an old man of Makkah, set out for Madinah and died on the road at aṭ-Ṭanʿīm. His reward matched the reward of those who completed the journey, and he died with his endeavour highly praised. Another report ties the verse to Aktham ibn Sayfī likewise. Whoever starts emigrating and dies on the way acquires the reward of those who emigrate for Allah.

### The three sorts of emigrants

In this verse the Lord of the Worlds sets a mark of His mercy before the servants, the knowing and lovingly kind inviting the faithful to emigrate. Three sorts set out. The first goes for the sake of this world, taking up trade or the hunt for livelihood. The Sharīʿah allows it, yet no one can say where such a journey will finally lay its head. Muṣṭafā said that love for this world is the beginning of every offense, and he also said not to take a landed estate, lest eagerness for this world grow. Such an emigrant stays in suffering and hardship, caught by thieves and on the verge of destruction, hoping to gain something allowed while leaving aside something obligatory, and in the end he loses the basis of both, as the word says: "You desire the chance goods of this world" (8:67).

The second sort are the renunciants, whose emigration serves the afterworld, their travelling a journey through meanings. On the feet of aspiration they cross the way stations of obedient acts and the stages of worship, making the hajj, going out to battle, striving, performing the prayers, remembering the name, meditating on the blessings. Of them the hadith says:

> Concerning them God's Messenger said: Travel! The solitary will be the preceders. They said: O Messenger of God, who are the solitary? He said: The engrossed, those engrossed by the remembrance of God. Remembrance has lifted away their loads, and they come forth unburdened on the Day of Resurrection.

Of them the Exalted Lord says: "Whosoever desires the afterworld and strives after it with due striving" (17:19).

The third sort are the recognizers, and the Patron himself is the object of their emigration. Their journey runs within their own makeup. Curtain by curtain they pass through the soul until the heart, through the heart until the spirit, and through the spirit until union with the Beloved.

### In short

- Hijrah means leaving what displeases, the homeland of disbelief, or every prohibited thing, and the forced expulsion for faith counts within it.
- The verses of merit run from al-Baqarah through at-Taubah to this very passage, and hijrah undoes what came before it.
- The good place of the world and the great reward of the Hereafter are proved by Ibrāhīm, by Mūsā and Banū Isrāʾīl, and by the emigrants of Madinah, with intention the one condition.
- The emigrant who dies on the road, like Jundaʿ ibn Damra al-Laythī at aṭ-Ṭanʿīm, arrives all the same.
- Of the three sorts of emigrants, the worldly traveller risks losing both worlds, the renunciant travels by meanings, and the recognizer emigrates through the soul to union with the Beloved.
