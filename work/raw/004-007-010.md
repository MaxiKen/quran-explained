<<<VERSE 4:7>>>
The surah now arrives at inheritance, and its first sentence overturns a whole social order: "For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares". Before this verse, women and children in Arabia did not inherit at all. After it, every relative named by the law holds a fixed, compulsory share that no one may enlarge or shrink.

### Phrase by phrase

**"For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave"** states the right of inheritance in general. Two words carry the system. "Parents" points to the bond of birth between children and their father and mother. "Close relatives" covers every kind of family tie: birth, the wider kinship, and the bond created by marriage. Parents are named separately only because of their importance. The same word also fixes the operating principle of the whole law: mere kinship is not enough, the heir must be the nearest in kinship. If nearness were not the standard, the estate of every deceased person would have to be split across the entire human race, since everyone descends from one father and mother, Ādam (Adam) and Ḥawwā (Eve). Each person would receive a particle worth nothing. So when choosing among relatives, the nearer excludes the farther. And where several relatives are equally near, though in different ways, they share together: the father, the mother, the children and the wife are all nearest, each in their own mode.

**"and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave"** repeats the right in a second, independent sentence. The repetition is deliberate. The two entitlements could have been merged into one sentence, but the Qur'an states each on its own, to emphasize that a woman's right in inheritance stands on its own and matters as much as a man's. Kinship is the same in a boy and a girl. Both are born to their parents. If inheritance hangs on kinship alone, nothing justifies cutting out a small child or a girl.

**"—whether it is little or much"** corrects another old custom. Some people would assign particular items, a horse or a sword, to young males alone as a matter of right and deprive everyone else of them. The verse sweeps that away: in everything the deceased owned, big or small, every inheritor holds a standing right, and no inheritor may set anything aside for himself before the estate has been formally distributed.

**"˹These are˺ obligatory shares"** closes the foundation. The shares are determined, apportioned by Allah Himself. Nobody may add to them, delete from them, change them or transpose them by personal opinion or analogical deduction. The word also makes the transfer of ownership automatic and compulsory. It does not depend on the heir's acceptance, consent or satisfaction. Even if he declares that he will not take his share, he is its owner in the sight of the sharīʿah. Once he owns it, he may gift it, sell it or give it away under the rules of the sharīʿah if he wishes.

### The world before this verse

The pre-Islamic Arabs lived by a plain principle: the one who deserves inheritance is the one who rides a horse, fights the enemy and collects the battle spoils. Women and children self-evidently could not live by that principle. So only a grown young man could inherit. A girl was out of the question, minor or grown, and a boy too young to fight was passed over as well. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Qatādah said exactly this: the idolaters used to give adult men a share and deprive women and children of it, and this verse came down against them. Nor was the injustice local. The nations around the world left these two weaker members of society without their natural rights, and none of their laws recognized the rights of orphans and women as such. Even where a right was conceded in words, no power existed to wrest it from the strong and hold it safe. Islam championed their cause, legalized their rights and built the safeguards around them.

### The occasion of the verse

The reports centre on the family of Aws ibn Thābit al-Anṣārī. He died leaving his wife, Umm Kujjah in one report and Umm Kuhhah in another, and children: two daughters, or three, and in one account a young son as well. Two paternal cousins, named in one report as Suwayd and ʿArfajah, came and took the whole estate into their possession, following the old custom that women and children do not inherit. They gave nothing to the widow or the children. Ibn Marduwyah reported from Jābir that Umm Kujjah came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and said: "O Messenger of Allah! I have two daughters whose father died, and they do not own anything." In the fuller report she said that their father had left a considerable fortune now held by the two cousins, who fed her nothing and cared for the children not at all. The cousins were summoned and defended themselves: her children cannot ride a horse, bear responsibility or harm an enemy. The Prophet ﷺ told them to leave for now, that he would wait for what Allah related to him concerning the matter. He did not yet have a verse. In one account the widow even suggested that the cousins marry the two orphaned daughters, to relieve her of the burden of providing for them, and they refused that too.

Then this verse came down, and after it the verses that detail the shares. The Prophet ﷺ divided the estate by the Qur'an. The wife received her one-eighth. Of the rest, half went to the son and half was shared between the two daughters. The cousins, farther in kinship than the children, received nothing.

### Not need, but nearness

The verse teaches that distribution is not based on need but on nearness of kinship. The nearest relative outranks a poorer, needier one. This is not hardness but law. Need is a shifting thing: conditions change, levels change, and a rule built on need or usefulness could never settle into solid law, for the claimants would multiply and the settlement would never end. Nearness is fixed. One hard case is answered directly: the orphaned grandson. If his father died before the grandfather, the grandson may be poorer and needier than anyone, but in the presence of a son of the deceased he is not the nearest, and he does not inherit. The entire Muslim ummah has held this, clarified by the Qur'an and the hadith, and only some contemporary westernized modernists have argued otherwise. The need of such a grandson is not forgotten. It is met by the very next verse, which opens the estate to those excluded from it.

### The inner reading

Read inwardly, the fixing of inheritance carries a lesson about gifts. Inheritance does not vary with virtue or good qualities, nor is it reduced by fault, defect or sin. A man dies leaving two sons: their rights are equal even where one son is God-fearing and the other disobedient and wicked. The good one receives nothing extra for his fear of Allah, and the bad one loses nothing for his badness. The deeper meaning is that inheritance is a gift from Allah that comes before any action of the heir, so the pious and the wicked share it alike. The same is true of faith itself, which is a gift given before any action or virtue. Allah said: "Then We bequeathed the Book to those of Our servants whom We chose. Yet some of them are those who wrong themselves, and some of them are moderate, and some are those who take the lead in good works by the leave of God. That is the greatest favor" (35:32).

### In short

- Men and women both inherit from parents and near kin, in everything the deceased owned, whether little or much. The two rights are stated separately on purpose.
- Before Islam, only fighting adult men inherited in Arabia, and much of the world was no better. The verse came down after the cousins of Aws ibn Thābit seized his whole estate, leaving his widow and daughters nothing.
- Inheritance follows nearness of kinship, not need. The nearer excludes the farther, and equally near relatives of different kinds share together.
- The shares are fixed by Allah. No one may change them, and ownership passes automatically, whether or not the heir consents.
- Read inwardly, inheritance is a gift that precedes action, like faith itself, shared alike by the pious and the wicked.

<<<VERSE 4:8>>>
The rule of nearness excludes some people from every division of an estate: the distant relative, and sometimes the poor who happen to be standing there. This verse catches them on their way out: "If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly". The excluded are not to be shooed away from the table. They are to be given something from what is being divided, and spoken to well.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution"** paints the scene. The relatives meant are those of kinship who cannot inherit, and the orphans and the needy are those present before the division is effected. They watch each eligible person assume a share while they stand desperate, receiving nothing.

**"offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it"** is the command of kindness. Those who are receiving shares without any effort of their own, simply through the mercy of Allah, should let their hearts beat with the urge to give. What they hand over becomes charity, rewarded by Allah, and it compensates something of the sense of deprivation the onlookers carry. It also covers the orphaned grandson excluded under the rule of nearness: his uncles and aunts should be glad to give him something each from their own shares. One caution bounds the command. The gift must come from the shares of the adult inheritors present. Gifts from the shares of minors and of those absent are not allowed.

**"and speak to them kindly"** handles the harder case. If the excluded are not satisfied with what they receive and start demanding a share equal to everyone's, the demand is unjust and contrary to the sharīʿah, and there is no way to satisfy it. Even then, no rudeness. The rule itself is explained to them politely: they have no share under the law of inheritance, and what they were given was a gift. Where the heir is himself a minor, the guardian makes a goodly promise on his behalf: "when the child grows up, we will tell him to give you something."

### Command, counsel or abrogated?

Is this giving obligatory? The reports divide. Ibn Jarīr recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that the verse still applies and should be implemented. Ath-Thawrī narrated from Ibn Abī Najīḥ, from Mujāhid, that implementing it is required of those who have anything to inherit, paid in whatever portions their hearts are satisfied to give away. Among those reported with a similar explanation are Abū Mūsā and Ibn Masʿūd, ash-Shaʿbī with Abū al-ʿĀliyah, ʿAbdur-Raḥmān ibn Abī Bakr and al-Ḥasan. A second group went further and called the payment obligatory: az-Zuhrī and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar, Ibn Sīrīn with Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Makḥūl, ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ and Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī. Others said the verse refers to the bequest a person makes at the time of death. Others said it was abrogated, and others again that it was never prescribed, only encouraged, and that people simply neglected it because it was counsel rather than command. Weighed against these, the ruling given is that the instruction is commendatory, not obligatory: a moral obligation on those who receive, answered in the spirit of the Qur'an's own arrangement, which presses the rule of nearness with one hand and tends the disappointment of the excluded with the other. The Qur'an teaches the same generosity at harvest: "Eat of the fruit when it bears fruit, and give away the due of the poor on the day of harvest" (6:141).

### The inner reading

There is a subtle allusion in the verse for those who have sinned. At the division, those present with no legal portion are still granted something. So too, on the Last Day, when the Real forgives the obedient and hands them the rewards of their deeds, the sinners of the family of faith, ahl al-īmān, will not be turned away without pardon, if God wills. They were not there when the shares were fixed and hold no prior entitlement. His favor made them deserving anyway, though He knew beforehand the lapses their states would carry.

### In short

- Non-inheriting relatives, orphans and the needy present at a division of inheritance are to be given something from it and spoken to kindly.
- The gift comes from the shares of the adult inheritors present, never from the shares of minors or the absent.
- If they demand an equal share, the law is explained politely, and where the heir is a child, a promise is made on his behalf.
- The strongest reports make the giving obligatory or at least required in proportion to what the heart is satisfied to give. Others read it as bequest, or abrogation, or encouragement. The settled ruling is that it is a moral obligation, not a legal one.
- Read inwardly, the verse is a picture of pardon: sinners arrive at Allah's court with no entitlement, and His favor provides for them anyway.

<<<VERSE 4:9>>>
The verse turns from the estate to the deathbed: "Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably". It addresses everyone who stands near a man about to die, and everyone who administers what the dead leave behind, and it gives them a single test: treat other people's children the way you would want yours treated.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind"** builds the test on a natural fear. Every person who leaves behind weak offspring, young children unable to fend for themselves, is afraid for them, afraid they will be ruined. The verse says: let that same fear govern how you handle the children of others, and how you guard the orphans in your care. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that the verse refers to a man near death who dictates a will damaging the rights of his heirs. Whoever hears such a will must fear Allah, turn the dying man toward what is right and fair, and guard those heirs as jealously as he would guard his own children. A similar report comes from Mujāhid and several others.

**"So let them be mindful of Allah"** names the standing motive. The guardians of orphaned children must protect the property and hand it over in full at maturity, taking pains over the trust, never letting negligence touch the duty. What a Muslim should accumulate for his dependents is not wealth. It is taqwā (the fear of Allah) and ṣalāḥ (righteousness). The verse does not say: let them gather their wealth, multiply real estate for them, leave behind household goods. It says: let them fear Allah, for He looks after the righteous. Deal with the orphans of others as you would have others deal with your children after you, and Allah will bring it about that your own are treated well.

**"and speak equitably"** is the bedside duty. In the days before this verse, people would visit the dying and press them to bequeath their property to this person and that, until nothing was left for their own children. The practice is what the verse closes down. The one who sits with a dying man speaks the pertinent words to him: enjoin on him the voluntary almsgiving of no more than a third of the estate, and the remainder for the inheritors, so that they do not end up as dependants begging from others. Nor should the one making his will deprive himself of this good deed by leaving none.

### Saʿd's one-third

The living application is recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the two Ṣaḥīḥs. When the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came to visit Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqās during an illness, Saʿd said: "O Messenger of Allah! I am wealthy and have no inheritors except a daughter. Should I give two-thirds of my property in charity?" He said: "No." Saʿd asked: "Half?" He said: "No." Saʿd said: "One-third?" The Prophet ﷺ said: "One-third, and even one-third is too much," and added that it is better to leave one's inheritors wealthy than to leave them poor, begging from others. The ruling is recorded in the Mishkāt in its chapter on bequests, Bāb al-Waṣāyā. Had the whole or half been given away as ṣadaqah, the inheritors' share would have been consumed or curtailed, and that is what the verse and the Prophet's ﷺ answer both prevent.

### In short

- Whoever hears a will that harms the rightful heirs must fear Allah, intervene, and protect those heirs as if they were his own children.
- Guardians protect the orphan's property and hand it over whole at maturity, treating the orphans of others as they would want their own children treated after them.
- What to store up for one's dependents is not wealth but taqwā and righteousness, for Allah looks after the righteous.
- The dying are to be spoken to justly: no more than a third in voluntary almsgiving, as Saʿd was told, and the rest for the heirs, so no child is left begging.

<<<VERSE 4:10>>>
The section on orphans ends with its most terrible sentence: "Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans' wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell". One report ties the verse to Hanzalah ibn Shamardal. What it says is not a metaphor dressed as a threat. It is a description of what the eaten property already is.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans' wealth"** covers every form of taking. The verb is "eat", but the usage, as elsewhere in the Qur'an, embraces eating, drinking, consuming and utilizing. Using an orphan's property in whatever manner, without right, is totally unlawful and a sure cause of divine wrath.

**"˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies"** states the identity. Many have read this metaphorically: eating the orphan's property is like stuffing one's stomach with fire, because that is what awaits the person in the Hereafter. Others, with deeper insight, refuse the figure of speech. What is eaten from the orphan's property unlawfully is, in reality, fire, though it does not look like fire at the time. A man may call the matchbox in his hand a fire, and the piece of arsenic a killer. Handling the matchbox does not burn the hand, and holding arsenic in the mouth does not kill. But a little friction proves the first name right, and once the arsenic goes down the throat, the second. So with the wrongfully eaten wealth: its true form appears on the Day. The Qur'an's own generalizations support this. Whatever deeds a person does, good or evil, those very deeds are the trees, flowers and fruits of Paradise, or the embers of Hell, in a different form here, transformed into their true forms on the Day of Doom: "On the Day of Doom, they shall find what they did, present" (18:49).

**"And they will be burned in a blazing Hell"** completes the sentence. On the Day of Resurrection, the fire kindles in their stomachs. In some narrations, those who devour the property of orphans unjustly are raised with flames issuing from their bellies by way of the mouth, the nostrils, the ears and the eyes. The Prophet ﷺ said that a people will be raised on that Day with their mouths erupting with fire. His Companions asked who these could be. He said: "Have you not read it in the Qur'an? Those who eat up the property of the orphans unjustly." A detail of the closing word carries two readings, an active and a passive form, both ending in a blaze: a fierce fire in which they burn.

### The One who takes the orphan's side

Behind the warning stands a portrait of Allah: the majestic compeller and great Lord, the renowned keeper of servants, clement and generous, loyal and tremendous, the lord of all and the carrier of everything, the One who takes the weak by the hand and joins them to Himself in love. In this verse He caresses the weak and shows love to the orphans. Toward the wrongdoers, those who stab the orphan's liver and drink the blood of the destitute, He turns with a fearful punishment. He makes Himself the deputy of the helpless and takes up their dispute against their wrongers. He is the companion of the weak and the helper of the despairing. He answers the distressed when they call, and hears the voice of the grieved. And He loves a particular kind of servant: the one who, beaten down, helpless and destitute, breathes a cold sigh, lets warm tears fall, and lifts a pair of empty hands toward his Lord, asking once more to be excused.

The same passage presses the sinner toward exactly that excuse. In one tradition a man was praying:

> O Lord, O Lord! You have written, You have measured out, and You have decreed. All that was, is and shall be is what You want, what You bring about, and what You write for the creatures. None of it lies outside Your predestination.

A call came in his secret core: "That is tawḥīd. Where is servanthood?" What he had said was pure oneness, fitting for Godhood. What, then, was the mark of his servanthood? The man answered:

> O Lord, surely I have disobeyed, I have sinned, and I ask. I have broken the covenant and have no loyalty. Greed has made me all grasping claws. I am all war with the Apportioner's apportioning.

That is the confession the verse seeks from the one whose hand is in an orphan's property.

### The seven destructive sins

The consuming of an orphan's property stands among the gravest sins in Islam. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record Abū Hurayrah's report that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "Avoid the seven great destructive sins." The people asked what they were. He named them:

> To join others in worship along with Allah, magic, to kill the life which Allah has forbidden except for a just cause, to consume interest, to consume an orphan's property, to turn your back to the enemy and flee from the battlefield at the time of fighting, and to accuse chaste women, who never even think of anything harmful to their chastity, of wrongdoing.

And Abū Hurairah narrates that the Prophet ﷺ said: "I warn you to abstain from the property of the two weak ones, the woman and the orphan."

### Where it happens in ordinary homes

The warning is not for a vanished age. When a man dies, the right of every inheritor attaches to everything he owns, every part, big or small, and his minor children become orphans in that same hour. Whoever then assumes control, an uncle, an elder brother, the mother, a guardian or trustee, stands exactly where this section warns him. The pattern is familiar. The distribution is postponed for years while the household spends a little here and there on the children's food and clothes. Then the spending turns liberal, and the jointly held property goes on religiously unacceptable customs and wasteful heads of expenditure, on the guardian's own person, and at the limit into manipulated documents that enter the guardian's own children in place of the orphans. Hardly a home is untouched. Donations made for orphans in religious schools and orphanages must reach the orphans. Not spending them on the orphans is itself a form of eating up the orphan's property.

### Rules for what the dead leave

The verse yields concrete rulings for the estate before distribution.

Inheritance includes everything the deceased owned, down to the clothes on the body. People give these out in charity without realizing they belong to the estate, and in some places copper utensils are handed to the poor before the property is formally divided, though minors and absentees hold rights in all of it. The proper method is to distribute first, according to the shares fixed by Islamic law. After ownership has passed, the sharers may, if they willingly wish, give away part on behalf of the deceased. If they do it jointly, all must be adults, for a minor's permission is not valid, and nothing may be taken from the share of an absent inheritor without his permission.

The sheet spread over the bier on the way to the graveyard is not part of the required shroud, so it may not be bought from the estate, which is jointly held. Someone may cover its cost himself. In some places a prayer mat is cut from the shroud cloth for the imam who leads the funeral prayer and given to him afterward. That expense is extra to the needed shroud and may not come from the undivided inheritance.

In some areas new utensils are bought for bathing the deceased and broken after use. There is no need to buy new ones at all, since the bath can be given with what is in the house, and if they must be bought, breaking them is not permissible. It wastes property and causes loss to the orphans and absent inheritors whose rights run through the whole estate.

Expenses before distribution, such as entertaining guests or giving charity and alms, are impermissible. Such giving brings the deceased no merit. Done in the belief that it brings good returns for the departed soul, it is a far greater sin, because giving charity from a combined property that includes the orphan's share is like stealing from someone's property and giving it away on the deceased's behalf. Even the inheritors' permission does not make it right: an orphan among them cannot give valid permission in the first place, and an adult's yes may come with a heavy heart, only to ward off shame. The right order is: distribute first, then let each inheritor give from his own share for the good of the deceased, if he wishes.

A story of a pious elder seals the point. He went to visit a sick Muslim and had hardly sat down when the man died. The elder at once put out the lamp burning there and gave someone money to buy oil and relight it. Asked why, he said the lamp had belonged to the deceased while he lived, and its light was lawful to use. Now that he had died, his inheritors held the right over everything he owned, and since they were not all present to give permission, the lamp would be lit at his own cost.

### In short

- Whatever is taken from an orphan's property without right is, in reality, fire in the belly, revealed as fire on the Day of Doom, when flames are seen coming from the mouths, nostrils, ears and eyes of those who devoured it.
- Consuming an orphan's property is one of the seven great destructive sins, and the Prophet ﷺ warned against the wealth of the two weak ones, the woman and the orphan.
- Every use of an orphan's property without right, eating, drinking or utilizing, is unlawful, and the ordinary household patterns of postponing division and spending from the joint estate fall under the same warning.
- Before distribution, the estate is untouchable: no charity, no guest meals, no shroud extras, no broken utensils, nothing from the shares of minors or the absent. First distribute, then let each sharer give from his own share.
- Behind the warning stands Allah as the deputy of the helpless, who loves the servant who comes to Him with empty hands, and asks to be excused.
