<<<VERSE 4:7>>>
Here the surah turns from orphans' property to the law of inheritance, and it begins by ending an old injustice: "For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares." Before Islam, women and children were left out of the estate altogether. This verse declares that they have a right to a share, that Allah has fixed it, and that nobody may reduce it. The details of the individual shares come in the passage that follows.

The verse belongs to a run of rulings about the weak. Orphans and women had been the victims of every kind of injustice. Their rights were not even recognised, and even where a right was conceded, there was no power to take it from the men and keep it safe. Islam took up their cause, made their rights law and put safeguards around them, at a time when the nations of the world had left these two groups deprived of rights that were natural and due.

### Phrase by phrase

**"For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave"**. The Arabic says "what the two parents and the nearest relatives leave". Some explain "men" here as the young ones and the kin. Another explanation says the relatives include brothers and sisters, uncles and aunts. The verse does not give the amounts. One explanation says Allah stated the principle first, because people did not usually give women and children any share of an estate, and then He explained the exact shares afterward.

**"and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave"**. The Qur'an could have said in one sentence that men and women both have a share. It chose two separate sentences, even though this looks like repetition. The point is to stress that a woman's right to inherit stands on its own and is as important as a man's. In the time of ignorance before Islam, as one explanation says, women and children were simply left out.

**"whether it is little or much"**. This corrects another custom of ignorant people. Certain items were reserved for special heirs. A horse or a sword, for example, could be inherited only by young men, and others were shut out. The words make it plain that in everything the dead person owned, large or small, every heir has a standing right. No heir may set aside anything for himself before the whole estate has been formally divided by the rules.

**"˹These are˺ obligatory shares"**. The Arabic word is mafrūḍan, determined or made obligatory. The shares set out in the Qur'an have been fixed by Allah. Nobody has the right to add to them, take from them, change them or swap them by personal opinion or by analogy. The word also shows a further principle. Ownership passes to the heirs through the law of inheritance automatically and by force of law. It does not need the heir's acceptance, consent or satisfaction. Even if he declares that he will not take his share, he is still its owner in the eyes of the Sharīʿah (the revealed law). If he does not want to keep it, he may, once he has become the owner, give it away, sell it or share it out by the rules.

### The custom this verse ended

Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Qatādah said that the idolaters used to give a share of the estate to grown men and deny it to women and children. Allah then revealed this verse. The pre-Islamic Arabs seem to have lived by the rule that the one who deserves to inherit is the one who rides a horse, fights the enemy and gathers the spoils. This is recorded in Rūḥ al-Maʿānī. Women and children could not live by such a rule. By it, only an adult young man could inherit. A girl was out of consideration, whether she was grown or a minor, and a boy was out as well if he was young and immature.

### The widow who came to the Prophet ﷺ

Several reports tell of the case that led to the verse. One says that Aws ibn Thābit died and left behind two daughters, a young son and a wife. Two of his cousins on the father's side came and took the whole property and gave nothing to his wife or children. This fitted their custom, since a woman could not inherit at all, and a boy who was a minor did not count.

The widow still hoped the two cousins would at least marry the two daughters, so that she would be free of that worry. They refused. She went to the Prophet ﷺ, told him her sad story and explained her children's destitution. The verse of inheritance had not yet come down, so the Prophet ﷺ gave no answer. His heart was at rest, and he was sure that revelation would remove this injustice. Then this verse was revealed.

Another account gives other details. Aws ibn Thābit al-Anṣārī died, leaving a wife called Umm Kujjah, also written Umm Kuhhah, and three daughters. Two men, Suwayd and Arfajah, who were his cousins and the executors of his estate, took all his wealth. They gave his wife and daughters nothing. Umm Kujjah went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and said that she was the widow of Aws ibn Mālik, that he had left her with daughters she could not support, and that Suwayd and Arfajah, who held a large fortune, would not feed her or look after the girls. The Messenger ﷺ called the two men. They said in their defence, "O Messenger of Allah, her children cannot ride a horse, bear any burden, or hurt an enemy." He told them to go for now, as he would wait for what Allah would reveal about the matter. Then Allah revealed the verse. In another report from Jābir, recorded by Ibn Mardawayh, Umm Kujjah came to the Messenger ﷺ and said, "I have two daughters whose father has died, and they own nothing," and the verse came down in answer.

After this, the second verse of inheritance followed with the details of the shares. The Prophet ﷺ acted on the Qur'an's rulings. He gave the wife one-eighth of the whole. The rest he divided between the son and the daughters, so that the boy had half and the two girls shared the other half equally. The cousins were not the nearest relatives, in comparison with the children, and they were excluded. All of this is recorded in Rūḥ al-Maʿānī.

### Two principles in two words

The verse uses two words, "the parents" and "the nearest relatives", and they state two basic principles of inheritance. The first is the bond of birth between children and their mother and father, given by the word for the parents. The second is general kinship, given by the word for the nearest relatives. On the correct reading, that second word covers all kinds of family relationship: the tie of birth, other family relations, and relations made by marriage. The parents are named separately because of their importance.

The word "nearest" also sets down another principle. Kinship by itself is not enough to make a claim. The heir must be the nearest. If nearness were not the test, the estate of every dead person would have to be shared out among the whole human race, since everyone descends from one father and mother, Ādam (Adam) and Ḥawwāʾ (Eve). In theory that is impossible, and if it could be done, everyone would receive one worthless speck. So the rule is that when there are relatives of different degrees, the nearer are preferred to the farther, and in the presence of the nearest, the most distant get no share. When several relatives are all counted as nearest together, even if their kind of nearness differs, all of them are entitled, as with the father and mother alongside the children or a wife, since each is among the nearest in his own way.

The same word shows that women and children cannot be shut out of inheritance. Kinship is the same in a boy and a girl as far as the fact of being related goes. A boy comes into the world through his parents, and a girl does too. If the right to inherit rests on being related, there is no sense in denying it to a small child or a girl.

### Nearness, not need

The word "nearest" also tells us that the estate is not divided by need. It is divided by closeness of kinship. So the more needy relative is not automatically more deserving. The one who is nearest to the dead person is entitled to the share before one who is farther, even if the farther one is poorer. If need or benefit replaced nearness as the test, it could never become a settled rule or a firm law. Poverty, need and usefulness are not permanent, since conditions and levels change, and a crowd of claimants would arise and leave those in charge struggling to decide.

### The orphaned grandson

Once this Qur'anic principle is clear, the problem of an orphaned grandson's inheritance resolves itself. It has been made to look like a disputed question for no good reason. A grandson may be needier than the son, but under the rule of the nearest relative he has no claim on the estate while the son is alive, because he is not the nearest. Other arrangements take care of his needs, and one of them appears in the next verse. Only some modern, Westernised thinkers have opposed this position. The whole Muslim ummah has held, on the evidence of the Qur'an and the hadith, that a grandson does not inherit in the presence of a son of the dead man, whether the grandson's own father is dead or alive.

### Inheritance is a gift that comes before deeds

The fixing of inheritance does not depend on virtue or good character, and it does not change because of a fault, a defect or a sin. Suppose a man dies leaving two sons. Their rights are equal, even if one is devout and God-fearing and the other is wicked and disobedient. The God-fearing one does not receive more for his fear of Allah, and the wicked one does not receive less for his wickedness.

The deeper meaning is that inheritance is a gift from Allah that arrives before any action or virtue, so the pious and the wicked share in it alike. In the same way, faith is a gift for Muslims that arrives before any action. Allah said: "Then We granted the Book to those We have chosen from Our servants. Some of them wrong themselves, some follow a middle course, and some are foremost in good deeds by Allah’s Will. That is ˹truly˺ the greatest bounty" (35:32).

### In short

- The verse ends the pre-Islamic custom that only adult men who could fight were heirs. Men and women both receive a share of what parents and close relatives leave, large or small, and Allah has fixed those shares.
- Women's right is stated in its own sentence to show that it stands independently. Ownership passes to the heirs by law, with or without their consent, and no heir may set aside a part of the estate before it is divided.
- The nearest relative excludes the farther ones, and closeness of kinship decides, not need. This is why a grandson does not inherit alongside a son, although other provision is made for him.
- The verse is said to have come down after the widow of Aws ibn Thābit complained to the Prophet ﷺ that his cousins had taken everything.
- Inwardly, inheritance is a gift that does not follow virtue, just as faith is a gift that comes before deeds.

<<<VERSE 4:8>>>
The previous verse fixed who inherits, and the nearer relatives exclude the farther. This verse looks after the feelings of those left out: "If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly." The Qur'an gives a just rule on one hand, and on the other it shows care for the disappointment of the people the rule excludes. It devotes a whole verse to how they should be treated.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution"**. These are the relatives who have no share in the estate, along with orphans and poor people. In one explanation, the orphans are the orphans of believers and the needy are the needy among believers. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of al-ʿAwfī, described what such a scene is like. An estate is sometimes large. When poor relatives who cannot inherit, orphans and the poor stand by while the heirs take up their shares, their hearts long for a portion. They are desperate, and they are given nothing. Allah the Most Kind and Most Compassionate tells the heirs to give them a share, as an act of kindness, charity and mercy.

Relatives who cannot receive anything under the rules of the Sharīʿah are likely to feel disappointed when the estate is divided. Not everyone knows the details of how shares are worked out, and most relatives would like to have something. If they are there in person, and especially if there are orphans, poor and needy people among them, the sight of the others walking away with their shares while they simply watch is a terrible one.

**"offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it"**. Several explanations say that the heirs should give them something kindly out of the estate before it is divided, because they may be looking at it with desire. It came to the heirs without any work on their part. Those who receive a share have a moral duty to give some of it voluntarily. It becomes an act of charity for the recipients, and a means of reward from Allah for the givers.

At a moment when wealth is arriving without effort, only through Allah's mercy, hearts should want to give whatever they can in His way. A similar thought appears in another verse: "Eat of the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste" (6:141). The presence of distant kin, orphans and the needy at the division should not annoy anyone. A person should be grateful that Allah gave him something he did not work for, and give away part of it as a token of thanks. He should see it as a God-sent chance to make up in a small way for the sense of loss these people feel.

This covers the grandson who was left out of the inheritance. His uncles and aunts ought to be glad to give him something each from their own shares.

**"and speak to them kindly"**. If the heirs are young, one explanation says, the guardians should speak honourable words to the needy by apologising, and telling them that the estate is not theirs to divide as they please, but belongs to the young heirs. Another explanation says that the guardian should promise them that he will speak to the young heir about giving them something. The same idea appears in the explanation that when the legal heir is a minor, a goodly promise is made to the poor: "When the child grows up, we will tell him to give you something." A short explanation asks only that people speak to them in a kind way, without any meanness.

The words also tell us what to do when these people are not satisfied with what they get and start demanding a share equal to everyone else's. The demand is unjust and against the Sharīʿah, and there is no way to meet it. Even so, it should not be answered crudely, in a way that hurts their feelings. The actual rules should be explained politely. They can be told that under the rule of Islamic law they have no share in the inheritance, and that what they were given was a gift.

### Is giving to them required?

Scholars differ on whether this command is a duty. Ibn ʿAbbās said, in a report collected by al-Bukhārī, that the verse was not abrogated. Ibn Jarīr recorded that he said the verse still applies and must be carried out. Ath-Thawrī said that Ibn Abī Najīḥ reported from Mujāhid that carrying it out falls on those who inherit, and that they give from whatever portions their hearts are content to give away. Al-Ḥasan, ash-Shaʿbī, Abū al-ʿĀliyah, ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān ibn Abī Bakr, Abū Mūsā and Ibn Masʿūd gave the same explanation.

Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar, az-Zuhrī, ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ, Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī, Makḥūl, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Ibn Sīrīn said that this payment is obligatory. Others hold that the verse refers to a bequest made at the time of death, and still others that it was abrogated. A further explanation says that it was not abrogated, but that people neglect it too readily, because it is encouraged and not prescribed. According to this latter view, the command is commendable and not obligatory. Ibn ʿAbbās, however, held that it is a duty.

### How to give

The gift must not come out of the common estate. It must be made by the adult heirs who are present, from their own shares. Gifts taken from the shares of minors or of heirs who are absent are not allowed in the Sharīʿah.

### The inner reading

The verse has a hidden hint for sinners. When the people of faith come before Allah in the future, the Real will pardon the obedient and give them the reward of their deeds. He will not shut the poor Muslims among you out of that pardon, if Allah wills, since they too were among the family of faith, ahl al-īmān. In the same way, you were not present on the day of the division, so you have no prior claim. Whatever you know of Allah, He has made you worthy of by His favour, although He knows what slips will occur in your states.

### In short

- After the nearer relatives exclude the farther, the verse asks the heirs to give something from the estate to relatives who do not inherit, to orphans and to the needy who are there at the division.
- Speaking kindly means explaining politely, apologising to the needy if the heirs are minors, or making a goodly promise, and not scolding anyone who asks for more.
- Ibn ʿAbbās and many early scholars held that the gift is a duty and that the verse was not abrogated. Others take it as a bequest, as abrogated, or as encouraged but not required. The gift is made by adult heirs from their own shares.
- It should be seen as a God-sent chance to give thanks for wealth that came without effort.

<<<VERSE 4:9>>>
This verse speaks to everyone who stands near an estate or near a dying man's will, and asks them to feel for other people's children as they would for their own: "Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably." It tells Muslims in general to make sure that the inheritance of the dead reaches his children fully and fairly, and to avoid any action that may harm the children's share.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind"**. The early explanations take the verse in two ways. The first concerns people who are present at a will. Ibn ʿAbbās said, in the report of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, that it refers to a man who is near death and dictates a will that harms some of those who should inherit. Allah commands whoever hears such a will to fear Him, to steer the dying man toward what is right and fair, and to be as keen to protect the heirs of the dying man as he would be for his own. Mujāhid and several others said the same.

One explanation names the people involved. They are those who visit a sick person and urge him to bequeath more than a third of his estate. Such people used to go to men on their deathbeds and urge them to give their property to this person and that, until nothing was left for their own children. Allah forbade it. They should fear the ruin of the dying man's children, as they would fear ruin for their own if they died leaving them weak and unable to look after themselves. A short explanation says: urge the dying person to do what you would urge for yourself.

The second way concerns guardians of orphans. The verse asks them to treat the orphans as they would wish others to treat their own children after them, with no harm or injustice. Those who guard orphans' property should protect it and give it to them in full when they are mature. They must take pains over this trust, and never let the least carelessness spoil their duty. One explanation says that executors and guardians should feel the fear they themselves would feel if they were to die leaving small, weak children who might be ruined.

**"So let them be mindful of Allah"**. According to one explanation, this means that people should fear Allah when they look after the orphan's wealth. Ibn Jarīr recorded it from al-ʿAwfī, who reported it from Ibn ʿAbbās. It is a sound opinion, because it fits the warning that follows against consuming an orphan's wealth unjustly. The meaning becomes: just as you want your own children to be treated fairly after you, treat other people's children fairly when the care of them is entrusted to you. Another explanation says: let them fear Allah in the matter of bequeathing more than a third to those who have no right to inherit. Another adds that if guardians treat orphans well, Allah will bring others to treat their own children well after they die.

**"and speak equitably"**. In one explanation, the words mean to speak truthfully. According to others, they ask a person to speak justly to the sick man about his will. They ask him to tell the dying man to give no more than a third in voluntary charity, and to leave the rest to those who inherit, so that they do not end up dependent on others. Another explanation says that whoever is present when a will is made should speak up for what is fair to the children, so that no heir suffers wrong through the will. The giver, for his part, should not deprive himself of this good deed by failing to make a will.

### The hadith of Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ

The two Ṣaḥīḥs record that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ visited Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ when he was ill. Saʿd said, "O Messenger of Allah, I am wealthy and have no heir except a daughter. Should I give two-thirds of my property in charity?" The Prophet ﷺ said, "No." Saʿd asked, "Half?" He said, "No." Saʿd asked, "One-third?" The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "One-third, though even one-third is a great deal. It is better to leave your heirs rich than to leave them poor, begging from other people."

The Prophet ﷺ stopped Saʿd from giving all or half of his property in ṣadaqah (charity) and allowed him only a third. If the whole or half had been given away, the heirs' share would have been used up or cut down. This is recorded in Mishkāt, in the chapter on wills, Bāb al-Waṣāyā. The same principle applies when anyone sees a Muslim making a will or giving away property in a way that would damage his children and other heirs. He should be stopped.

### What a Muslim should store up for his family

The verse shows that what a Muslim should collect for his dependants is not wealth but fear of Allah, taqwā, and righteousness, ṣalāḥ. The verse does not say that they should gather their wealth, multiply their real estate or leave behind household goods. It says that they should fear Allah, for He looks after the righteous.

### In short

- The verse asks guardians, executors and people who attend a will to want for other people's children what they would want for their own.
- It has two applications. People present at a will should urge the dying man to be fair and not to leave away more than a third. Guardians should protect the orphans' property and hand it over in full.
- The Prophet ﷺ refused to let Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ give away two-thirds or half, and he allowed a third, saying that it is better to leave heirs rich than poor and begging.
- What a person should leave behind for his family is taqwā and righteousness more than money.

<<<VERSE 4:10>>>
After all the commands about orphans, the verse states the penalty: "Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell." It carries a severe warning for those who steal or dispose of an orphan's property unlawfully, and it makes plain how serious the earlier commands were.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth"**. In the Arabic the word is yaʾkulūna, "they eat". It means that using the orphan's property in any way at all is completely unlawful, whether by eating, drinking, spending or making use of it. It is a sure cause of Allah's anger. The punishment for eating up what belongs to someone else, wrongfully and without right, covers every kind of use. One short explanation reads "unjustly" as extortionately. Others say it means without any right, or spending it wrongly and unjustly. The verse is said to have been revealed about Ḥanẓalah ibn Shamardal.

**"˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies"**. In the Qur'an's wording, whatever they swallow of the orphan's wealth is nothing but fire. One explanation says it is whatever is unlawful. Another says that Allah will put fire in their bellies on the Day of Judgment. Another says the property is called fire because that is where such conduct ends. Whenever you consume an orphan's wealth without right, you are consuming only fire, and it will burn in your stomach on the Day of Resurrection.

**"And they will be burned in a blazing Hell"**. The Arabic verb is yaṣlawna, in the active form, or yuṣlawna, in the passive in another reading. It means that they will enter a blaze, an intense fire in which they will burn. One explanation says they will be exposed to blazing fire in the Hereafter, and another that on the Day of Judgment the flames of Hell will consume them.

### Is the fire only a figure of speech?

Many scholars understood the words as a figure of speech. Eating an orphan's property unjustly is like stuffing one's stomach with fire, because that is what it will lead to in the Hereafter. Others, who looked more deeply and gave their reasons, do not accept a figure of speech here. In their view, whatever is eaten of an orphan's property unlawfully is in reality nothing but fire, though it does not look like fire at the time.

They compare it to someone who says that the matchbox in his hand is fire, or that a piece of arsenic is a killer. Holding a matchbox does not burn the hand, and holding arsenic, even putting it in the mouth, does not kill a man. But a little friction shows that the one who called the matchbox fire was right, and when arsenic has been swallowed, it is clear that whoever called it a killer told the truth. The Qur'an's general statements support this view. Whatever deeds a person does, good or evil, those deeds themselves are the trees, flowers and fruits of Paradise, or the embers of Hell. Their form here is different, but on the Day of Judgment they will come forth in their true forms. The Qur'an says: "They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺" (18:49). The punishment and reward they see will be, in reality, the result of their own deeds.

According to some narrations, those who wrongly devour an orphan's property will be raised on the Day of Judgment with flames pouring from their bellies and out through their mouths, nostrils, ears and eyes. The Prophet ﷺ said that a group of people would be raised on that Day with fire erupting from their mouths. His Companions asked who they were, and he said, "Have you not read it in the Qur'an?" and recited the words about those who eat up the orphans' property unjustly.

### The two weak ones, and the seven destructive sins

Because the property of an orphan eaten unjustly is in reality the fire of Hell, though it is not felt as fire at the time, the Prophet ﷺ gave very clear instructions to take the strictest care. Abū Hurairah reported that he said:

> "I warn you against the property of the two weak ones, the woman and the orphan."

It is recorded in the two Ṣaḥīḥs that Abū Hurayrah said the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, "Avoid the seven great destructive sins." The people asked, "O Allah's Messenger, what are they?" He answered:

> "To join others in worship with Allah, magic, killing a life that Allah has forbidden except for a just cause, consuming interest, consuming an orphan's property, turning your back and fleeing from the battlefield at the time of fighting, and accusing chaste women, who never think of any harm to their chastity, believing women."

### How orphans are really wronged

The whole first section of this surah is about orphans. It orders the protection of their property, forbids treating it as one's own, and orders that the share they receive in inheritance be given to them. It forbids hastily spending their property out of fear that they will grow up and take control. It forbids marrying orphan girls for a lower dower, and forbids seizing their property. In the end, the surah says that eating up an orphan's property unjustly is filling the belly with fire, because such people will be repaid after death with fire in their bellies.

When someone dies, the right of every heir attaches to everything he owned, down to the smallest part. It is a tragedy when the young children of the dead become orphans. Mostly they meet some injustice and excess in every home. Whoever takes control of the property after the father's death, whether an uncle, an elder brother, the mother, a guardian or a trustee, falls into doing things this section forbids. To begin with, they put off the division of the property for years, and in the meantime spend a little here and there to feed and clothe the children. After that, they open up and spend this jointly held property with great freedom on customs and practices that religion does not approve and on wasteful items. They even spend it on themselves and go so far as to manipulate legal documents and put their own children's names in place of the orphans'. Hardly any home is untouched by these things.

Donations made for orphans in religious schools and orphanages must be spent on the orphans. Not spending them on the orphans is a form of "eating up" their property.

### Rulings about the estate of the dead

Four rulings follow from this.

First, the inheritance includes everything the dead person owned, even the clothes on his body. People tend to give these away in charity without realising that they belong to what he left. In some places, copper utensils are given to the poor well before the estate is formally divided, although minors and absent heirs have rights in all such things. The right method is to divide the property first, according to the shares of the children, wife, parents, sisters and anyone else under the Islamic law of inheritance. Once ownership has passed to those who receive shares, it is up to them to give away part of it in charity on behalf of the dead, if they choose to do so willingly. If they want to do this together, they must make sure they are all adults, since a minor's permission is not valid. Nothing may be taken from the share of an absent heir without his permission.

Second, the sheet spread over the janāzah (the bier) while the dead is carried to the graveyard is not part of the required shroud, the kafan. It is not permitted to buy it with money from the estate, since the estate is held jointly. If someone covers the cost himself, that is allowed. In some places a prayer mat is cut from the cloth bought for the shroud for the imām who leads the funeral prayer, and he keeps it. That expense is an extra and may not come from the undivided estate.

Third, in some areas new utensils are bought to bathe the dead and are broken after use. There is no need to buy new ones, because the bath can be given with utensils already in the house. If for some reason they must be bought, breaking them is not permitted. It wastes property, and it harms orphans and absent heirs, whose rights are attached to the whole estate.

Fourth, any spending before the estate is divided, such as feeding guests or giving charity and alms, is entirely impermissible. Giving charity in this way brings the dead person no reward. Giving it in the belief that it will help the departed soul is a much greater sin. The reason is that, after a person dies, all his property belongs to his heirs in proportion to their rights, and there are orphans among them. Giving things in charity from the joint property, which includes the orphan's share, is like stealing from someone and giving the stolen goods away on behalf of the dead. Divide the property first, and then the heirs may give in charity from their shares for the good of the dead if they wish.

It is better not to give charity from the joint estate even before the division, even with the heirs' permission. The permission of an orphan among the heirs is not valid at all. As for the adults, their permission may not come from a willing heart. They may be left with no choice but to agree, so as not to be shamed in front of others, and they may say yes with a heavy heart to ward off embarrassment.

A story told of a pious elder makes this clearer. He went to visit a sick Muslim, and the man died almost as soon as he sat down. The elder at once put out the lamp burning there and gave someone money to buy oil and light it again. When people asked why, he said that the lamp had belonged to this man while he lived, and it was right to use its light. Now that the man had left the world, his heirs had a right over everything he owned. We could use the lamp only with the permission of all the heirs, and since they were not all there, the lamp was lit at the elder's own cost.

### A lesson in plain speech

In this world a person may seize a weak man's right. But every unlawful acquisition is like filling his belly with fire. The fact that what he took is like fire may not be clear now, but it will become clear in the Hereafter. People have freedom of action in this world, but they cannot control the results of their actions. Whoever wishes to be saved from bad consequences should not wrong others. He should adopt a culture of kindness and be a giver to others as far as he is able. If he cannot do anyone any good, then by Islamic standards he should at least not hurt anyone's feelings. He should speak plainly and truthfully, or else keep quiet.

### The inner reading

Allah is the mighty Compeller, the great Lord, the renowned keeper of His servants, the gentle, the generous, the faithful, the tremendous. He is the Lord of everyone and the bearer of everything, who takes the weak by the hand and joins them in love. In this verse He caresses the weak and shows love to orphans. As for the wrongdoers who pierce the livers of orphans and drink the blood of the poor, He threatens them and warns them of a fearful punishment. He stands in for the helpless and argues with the wrongdoers on their behalf. He keeps company with the weak, supports the despairing, answers the distressed and hears the voice of the grieved. He loves a servant who is beaten down, helpless and destitute, and who sighs coldly, weeps warm tears, raises two empty hands to Him and asks once more for pardon.

A tradition tells of a man who was saying, "O Lord, O Lord! You have written, You have measured out, and You have decreed!" He added that all that was, is and will be is what Allah wants, brings about and writes for His creatures, and that none of it lies outside His predestination or happens without His decree. A call came from the man's inmost heart: "That is tawḥīd, the oneness of Allah. Where is servanthood?" What he said was only tawḥīd, and fit for Allah's Godhood. What, then, was the mark of his servanthood? The man said, "O Lord, O Lord! I have disobeyed, I have sinned, and I ask." What comes from me is fitting for me, he said. I have broken my pledge, I keep no faith, I am unfaithful and worse. What can I do about this story? I am all artifice and colour, and I am too crippled to walk the straight path. Greed has turned me into grasping claws, and I am at war with the way the Apportioner apportions.

The passage goes on to say that it is harsh to eat the wealth of orphans and to crave their possessions foolishly. It then begins a story of ʿĪsā (Jesus), pure and a marvel of his time, passing by a graveyard, but the telling breaks off there.

### In short

- Whoever consumes an orphan's wealth unjustly, in any way at all, swallows nothing but fire, and will be burned in a blazing Hell.
- Scholars differ on whether the "fire" is a figure of speech or the real nature of the wealth. On the second view, deeds will appear on the Last Day in their true forms, like a matchbox that is truly fire.
- The Prophet ﷺ warned against taking the money of the two weak groups, women and orphans, and counted consuming an orphan's property among the seven destructive sins.
- Everything the dead person owned belongs to the heirs. Charity, feasts and other spending out of the undivided estate are not allowed, because orphans and absent heirs have rights in it.
- Inwardly, Allah stands in for the weak and warns those who wrong them, and He loves the servant who comes to Him helpless and in tears.
