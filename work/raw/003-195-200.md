<<<VERSE 3:195>>>
> “So their Lord responded to them: “I will never deny any of you—male or female—the reward of your deeds. Both are equal in reward. Those who migrated or were expelled from their homes, and were persecuted for My sake and fought and ˹some˺ were martyred—I will certainly forgive their sins and admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, as a reward from Allah. And with Allah is the finest reward!” (3:195)

Allah answers the prayers of the people of understanding and assures them that no sincere deed is lost, whether performed by a man or a woman. He particularly promises forgiveness and Paradise to those who migrated, were driven from their homes, suffered for His sake, and struggled and died in His cause. Their supplication is part of the wider promise that Allah answers those who call upon Him (40:60).

### Phrase by phrase

**“So their Lord responded to them”** connects this answer to the prayers of the previous verses. The believers asked for forgiveness, a righteous end, and the fulfilment of Allah’s promise. Their Lord heard their supplication and answered with a promise of reward. Prayer is not ignored simply because the answer is not immediate or takes a form the supplicant did not expect.

The verse also connects remembrance and reflection with action. The believers who pondered the creation and asked Allah for help were willing to live by what they believed. Their faith appeared in worship, migration, patience, and sacrifice.

**“I will never deny any of you—male or female—the reward of your deeds”** makes clear that Allah’s justice and generosity are not restricted to men. A woman’s good deed is not erased because she is a woman, and a man does not receive a better standing simply by being male. Each person is rewarded according to faith and action.

**“Both are equal in reward”** means that men and women share in the promise of reward for sincere obedience. It does not mean that every person’s deeds or circumstances are identical. It means that Allah does not withhold the reward of a woman’s work or discount it on account of her sex.

A report recorded by Saʿid bin Mansur says Umm Salama asked the Prophet ﷺ why women were not mentioned in connection with hijrah. Al-Hakim also transmitted the account and considered its chain sound. Allah then revealed this verse, affirming that the work of women and men would both be rewarded. The Ansar remembered Umm Salama as the first woman to migrate to them. Her question gave voice to a concern about recognition, and the answer is direct: Allah does not lose the work of any of you.

**“Those who migrated”** describes people who left the land of shirk where they were persecuted and moved to a place where they could practise their faith. The early Muhajirun left Makkah for Madinah, often leaving family, homes, and livelihood behind. Migration was not a search for comfort. It carried personal and material costs. Muḥammad ﷺ led the community through the hijrah, and the Muhajirun joined the Ansar in Madinah.

The migration named here is tied to persecution for faith. It should not be treated as a general order for people to abandon their homes whenever life is difficult. The verse praises those who left under the conditions of the first Muslim community, when remaining in Makkah exposed them to coercion and danger.

**“or were expelled from their homes”** emphasizes that some did not leave freely. The disbelievers forced them out because they believed in Allah. Their loss of home and security was part of the cost of maintaining faith.

**“and were persecuted for My sake”** includes injury, intimidation, and other harm suffered because they worshipped Allah alone. Their only offence in the eyes of their persecutors was faith. The Qur’an elsewhere describes believers being driven out because they believed in Allah, their Lord (60:1), and people punished for believing in Allah, the Mighty and Praiseworthy (85:8).

**“and fought and some were martyred”** refers to those who struggled in Allah’s cause and some of whom were killed. The verse honours their sacrifice, but it does not glorify violence for its own sake or permit individuals to take up arms without lawful authority. The historical setting is the first Muslim community defending itself under the Prophet’s leadership. The text praises those who bore the danger in obedience to Allah.

**“I will certainly forgive their sins and admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow”** promises pardon and Paradise. The rivers and gardens describe lasting provision and joy. The reward is not limited to worldly victory or recognition. Allah’s forgiveness and acceptance are greater than any temporary benefit.

A hadith describes a man asking Muḥammad ﷺ whether he would be forgiven if he fought patiently and did not retreat. The Prophet answered that Allah would forgive him, then made an exception concerning unpaid debt. Jibril brought this reminder while the question was being asked. The report, recorded in the collections of Al-Bukhari and Muslim, reminds believers that martyrdom or shahadah does not erase the rights owed to other people. Debts and claims must be settled or forgiven by the person to whom they are owed, unless Allah grants a particular case a special mercy. Fighting in jihad is praised here in its revealed setting, not as an unrestricted permission to use force. The early Muslims had migrated from the land of shirk and suffered persecution for their faith.

**“as a reward from Allah. And with Allah is the finest reward!”** concludes by identifying the One who gives the promise. Allah gives the reward from His generosity, and what is with Him is better than anything people can obtain in the world.

### The reward of women and men

The verse came as reassurance that women who believed, migrated, and suffered were not overlooked. Umm Salama asked about the omission of women from a description of migration, and the revelation answered by naming both sexes. Her own migration illustrates the cost borne by women in the early Muslim community.

The promise is equal in dignity and divine regard. It does not require that men and women have identical roles or face exactly the same trials. It means that Allah recognizes sincere work in each person’s circumstances and does not waste it. An unseen act of patience, worship, or service is known to Him even if people do not praise it.

The verse also guards against using religious language to deny women’s agency or spiritual standing. Men and women are addressed as responsible believers, each capable of faith, good deeds, migration, patience, and sacrifice. Their reward rests with Allah, not with human recognition.

### Migration, suffering, and rights

The verse brings several experiences together: leaving home, being expelled, suffering harm, fighting, and being killed. Not every believer experiences all of them. The list describes the ordeals faced by a community and the reward promised to those who endured them.

Forgiveness of sin does not authorize neglecting the rights of other people. The report about debt makes that distinction clear. A person may receive Allah’s pardon for their own failings while still owing compensation or repayment to another person. Repentance therefore includes repairing harm where possible and seeking the other person’s pardon.

The promise also does not mean that believers should seek persecution or death. The faithful may protect themselves, seek safety, and work to prevent harm. The verse comforts those who endured trials for Allah’s sake. It does not tell people to create trials or ignore the value of life.

### In short

- Allah rewards women and men for their deeds and loses none of their sincere work.
- The verse honours migration, expulsion, persecution, and sacrifice for Allah’s sake.
- Divine forgiveness does not cancel the rights people owe to one another.

<<<VERSE 3:196>>>
> “Do not be deceived by the prosperity of the disbelievers throughout the land” (3:196)

The verse warns believers not to interpret the disbelievers’ travel, trade, and prosperity as proof that they are safe from accountability. Their success in the world is temporary and cannot tell the whole story of their final state.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Do not be deceived”** addresses the believer who sees an opponent’s apparent success and begins to doubt. A person may wonder why those who reject faith have wealth, influence, and freedom of movement while believers face hardship. The verse cautions against judging Allah’s favour only by what can be seen at the moment.

**“by the prosperity of the disbelievers throughout the land”** refers to their ability to travel, trade, and enjoy material comfort. Some Muslims saw the disbelievers of Makkah and other enemies of Allah living with ease while the believers suffered hunger and toil. The verse responds to that comparison: movement and prosperity are not proof that a person’s deeds are approved. Some believers said they were surprised by the contrast, and this verse answered their concern.

The warning does not mean that every disbeliever is prosperous or that every believer must be poor. It addresses a mistaken conclusion, not a fixed rule about wealth. Material provision may be a blessing, a test, or simply part of a person’s worldly circumstances. Only Allah knows the full meaning of a life and its end.

The verse continues the teaching that worldly success is not the final measure. A person may gain a great deal and still lose what matters most if they reject Allah and use their life in wrongdoing. Conversely, a believer may have little and still be honoured with a lasting reward.

The Qur’an repeats this warning in several ways. It says that the disbelievers’ movement through the land should not deceive the believers (40:4), that worldly enjoyment is brief before the return to Allah (10:69-70), and that respite is temporary (31:24, 86:17). It contrasts the person promised Paradise with one allowed to enjoy the world and then brought to judgment (28:61), and reminds the reader that delay may increase sin rather than prove approval (3:178). Ibn Jarir records Abu Ad-Darda using the description “Al-Abrar,” the righteous, to remind believers that what is with Allah is better. His statement is a contrast of lasting outcomes, not a call to seek death or to assume that any individual’s fate is known.

### In short

- Visible prosperity does not prove Allah’s approval.
- The believer should not envy opponents’ trade or travel as if it guarantees lasting success.
- Worldly circumstances are temporary, and the Hereafter is the final measure.

<<<VERSE 3:197>>>
> “It is only a brief enjoyment. Then Hell will be their home—what an evil place to rest” (3:197)

The prosperity described in the previous verse lasts only for a short time. For those who persist in disbelief, the final home is Hell. The verse contrasts temporary enjoyment with the lasting consequence of rejecting Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**“It is only a brief enjoyment”** means that worldly comfort has a limit. Wealth, trade, and freedom of movement may seem extensive, but they end with death. The verse reduces their apparent permanence to its true scale.

**“Then Hell will be their home”** describes the end of those who persist in rejection. Their worldly lodging and possessions cannot follow them as protection. What they chose to do with life determines the outcome they face before Allah.

**“what an evil place to rest”** conveys the horror of that destination. The phrase is not a taunt for believers to use against individual people. It is a warning about the consequence of disbelief and an invitation to turn back before life ends.

Ibn Jarir records Abu Ad-Darda contrasting the brief comfort of disbelievers with the reward of Al-Abrar, the righteous, with Allah. The Qur’an warns that disputing its signs does not give disbelievers a lasting advantage (40:4), that the temporary enjoyment ends and a severe return awaits liars (10:69-70), that worldly respite can mislead (31:24, 86:17), and that the person promised Paradise is unlike one who only enjoys the present life before judgment (28:61). The warning in 3:178 also clarifies that delay is not necessarily favour. Abu Ad-Darda’s statement should not be taken as a call to seek death. Its point is that a short worldly advantage cannot outweigh the lasting judgment of the Hereafter.

### In short

- Worldly comfort is brief, even when it appears secure.
- Those who persist in disbelief face accountability and a painful final home.
- The warning is meant to guide people, not to encourage contempt for individuals.

<<<VERSE 3:198>>>
> “But those who are mindful of their Lord will be in Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever—as an accommodation from Allah. And what is with Allah is best for the virtuous” (3:198)

In contrast to temporary worldly enjoyment, those who have taqwa will receive lasting Gardens from Allah. Their reward is an honourable welcome, and what Allah has prepared is better than the prosperity people may envy in this life.

### Phrase by phrase

**“those who are mindful of their Lord”** describes people who believe in Allah’s oneness and guard themselves from disobedience. Tawhid is not simply a statement of belief. Taqwa is awareness of Allah that shapes choices, including what a person earns, says, and does when no one else is watching.

**“will be in Gardens under which rivers flow”** describes the lasting reward of the righteous. The rivers signify abundant provision and beauty. The garden is not a temporary pause before loss. It is a home in which the faithful remain.

**“to stay there forever”** contrasts with the brief enjoyment of the previous verse. Worldly success ends, while the reward Allah grants to the righteous endures. The believer’s hope is not limited to gaining comfort now.

**“as an accommodation from Allah”** portrays the Gardens as a welcome prepared by the Lord. The Arabic word for hospitality evokes the provision offered to an honoured guest. The believers receive this not because they can compel Allah, but because He grants it from His generosity.

**“And what is with Allah is best for the virtuous”** states the comparison directly. The best outcome is not the prosperity of disbelievers but the lasting reward Allah has prepared for the people of taqwa.

The Qur’an contrasts this welcome with the temporary enjoyment of those who disbelieve. They may dispute Allah’s signs and travel widely (40:4), enjoy worldly comfort that passes (10:69-70, 31:24, 86:17), and appear secure, yet the one promised Paradise is not like the one who merely enjoys this world before judgment (28:61). The warning about delayed punishment (3:178) should not make believers envious. What Allah has prepared for the Al-Abrar is better than present prosperity.

### In short

- Taqwa guides a believer’s conduct and guards against disobedience.
- The Gardens are a lasting welcome from Allah, unlike temporary worldly comfort.
- What Allah has prepared for the virtuous is better than any worldly prosperity.

<<<VERSE 3:199>>>
> “Indeed, there are some among the People of the Book who truly believe in Allah and what has been revealed to you ˹believers˺ and what was revealed to them. They humble themselves before Allah—never trading Allah’s revelations for a fleeting gain. Their reward is with their Lord. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning” (3:199)

The verse makes clear that the People of the Book are not all alike. Some sincerely believe in Allah, accept the Qur’an and the revelation given before it, humble themselves before Allah, and do not conceal truth for worldly gain. Their reward is with their Lord.

### Phrase by phrase

**“there are some among the People of the Book who truly believe in Allah”** rejects sweeping generalizations. The verse praises a sincere group among Jews and Christians. It does not say that every member of those communities believes, nor that none do. People are to be judged by their own faith and actions.

**“and what has been revealed to you believers and what was revealed to them”** describes their acceptance of the Qur’an together with earlier revelation. They do not treat faith as a contest in which accepting one messenger requires rejecting the others. They honour the Torah and Gospel while recognizing the Qur’an as revelation. They recognize that Allah sent guidance across generations.

The Qur’an describes people who received scripture before as reciting it as it should be recited (2:121), and describes a community among the people of Moses that leads with truth and establishes justice (7:159). It says some of the People of the Book stand in prayer at night (3:113), and that those who hear the Qur’an may fall in humble prostration (17:107-109). These passages show that the verse’s phrase “some among them” reflects a broader distinction: sincere people respond to revelation with humility, while others may reject it or conceal it.

The Qur’an also acknowledges that some Christians show closeness to believers, while warning against shirk and praising those who believe and are rewarded (5:82, 5:85). In Surah al-Qasas, it describes people who received scripture before the Qur’an, believed in it when it was recited, and received their reward twice (28:52-54). This is the surah named Al-Qasas, and its account parallels the principle in the present verse: recognizing truth in both earlier revelation and the Qur’an is a virtue.

Some explanations identify Abdullah bin Salam and other people who accepted Islam after learning the truth. Others connect the verse with the Negus, the ruler of Ethiopia, who received the Prophet’s message and responded with humility. The reports describe different examples of the verse’s broad praise for sincere believers among the People of the Book. Ibn Jarir also records explanations that the verse includes believers who accepted the message, beyond one named individual.

**“They humble themselves before Allah”** means they are not arrogant about their learning, status, or religious inheritance. Their knowledge leads to reverence and worship. They are willing to accept truth even when it requires changing their previous position.

**“never trading Allah’s revelations for a fleeting gain”** connects their conduct with the earlier warning against hiding scripture for money or influence. These believers do not conceal what they know about revelation in order to protect their position. They do not sell religious truth for prestige or a small worldly benefit.

**“Their reward is with their Lord”** promises recompense from Allah. Their sincerity may not be recognized by people, but it is not overlooked by Allah.

**“Surely Allah is swift in reckoning”** means that Allah’s judgment is complete and does not require the delay or limitations of human courts. He knows every person’s belief, intention, and deed. The phrase encourages humility, not fear that Allah overlooks complexity.

### Examples of sincere belief

The Negus, known as An-Najashi, the ruler of Ethiopia, is one example in the reports connected with the verse. When Jaʿfar bin Abi Talib recited Surah Maryam, chapter 19, before the Negus and Christian leaders, they were moved to tears. Their response showed reverence for the revelation they recognized as coming from Allah. The story reflects a particular audience and should not be used to claim that every person in Ethiopia or every Christian responded in the same way.

When the Negus died in Ethiopia, Gabriel informed the Prophet ﷺ, who was in Madinah, and he led the companions in the funeral prayer. A report from Anas is transmitted through al-Muʿtamir ibn Sulayman. Some objected that the Negus was a Christian from another land whom they had never met. The Prophet’s action affirmed the faith of a sincere believer whose allegiance to Allah was known to Him. The report is transmitted in the Two Sahihs.

Another hadith from Abu Musa describes three people who receive a double reward, including a person from the People of the Book who believed in the messenger sent to them and then believed in Muḥammad ﷺ. Al-Bukhari and Muslim transmit this report. It emphasizes continuity in accepting Allah’s messengers. The believer does not reject earlier revelation, nor refuse the later messenger once the truth is clear.

Ibn Abi Najih transmits Mujahid’s explanation that the verse refers to people among the scriptures who accepted Islam. Qatadah, Jabir, Anas, and Ibn Abbas are also named in reports about its occasion. A statement from Al-Hasan al-Basri describes some earlier believers who recognized Muḥammad ﷺ before his mission and then accepted him after he was sent. Ibn Abi Hatim records these explanations. The accounts differ in the individuals they highlight, but they share the verse’s point that sincerity and humility matter more than inherited labels.

Reports also mention Abdullah bin Salam and other Jews who accepted Islam. Mujahid explains the verse as including those among the People of the Book who embraced the Prophet’s message. Al-Hasan al-Basri says that some had believed in Muḥammad ﷺ before his mission was publicly established, then received a further reward for believing once he was sent.

These examples do not mean that people should presume the faith of others without evidence. The verse itself uses “some,” leaving judgment of individuals to Allah. Its purpose is to praise sincere believers and prevent prejudice against an entire community.

### Humility and honesty

The verse contrasts these believers with those who concealed revelation for gain. The sincere group does not hide a teaching to maintain dominance or mislead others. They accept what they recognize as true, even when doing so costs them social advantage.

Their humility is not self-hatred or weakness. It is freedom from arrogance before Allah. They worship, seek the truth, and accept correction. They follow the Sunnah of the messenger they believe in and join the ummah of those who affirm Allah’s guidance. They do not claim that their ancestry or former religious identity guarantees salvation. Their devotion keeps them on the path of truth even when it costs them worldly standing.

Their example also reminds Muslims that faithfulness to revelation requires honesty. A believer should share knowledge responsibly and avoid manipulating scripture for status. The verse praises those who do not trade Allah’s words for a small price, whatever their community background.

### In short

- Some among the People of the Book sincerely believe in Allah and His revelations.
- Their humility and refusal to trade truth for worldly gain earn reward with Allah.
- The verse praises particular believers and rejects sweeping judgments about whole communities.

<<<VERSE 3:200>>>
> “O believers! Patiently endure, persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful” (3:200)

The final verse of Surah Āl ʿImrān gathers the believers’ responsibilities into four commands: be patient, outdo others in perseverance, remain steadfast or on guard, and maintain taqwa. Together, these qualities lead to success in this life and the Hereafter.

The preceding verse has just praised sincere believers among the People of Scripture. The Qur’an describes people who recite their revelation properly (2:121), a truthful community among the people of Moses (7:159), and those who listen to revelation with humility (3:113, 17:107-109). It also recognizes that some Christians respond with closeness and faith (5:82, 5:85), and describes earlier believers who accept the Qur’an (28:52-54). The final command to persevere applies to all who believe, regardless of their former community.

### Phrase by phrase

**“O believers!”** addresses those who have accepted Allah and His Messenger. The commands that follow are not abstract virtues. They are practices for a community facing worship, temptation, hardship, and opposition.

**“Patiently endure”** translates sabr: holding oneself steady in what Allah has commanded. Patience includes continuing in worship when it is difficult, restraining oneself from prohibited acts even when they are attractive, and meeting hardship without losing faith or acting unjustly.

Patience with duties means carrying out obligations such as prayer and charity even when they require effort. Patience against sin means refusing what Allah has forbidden, even when a person strongly desires it. Patience in distress means enduring illness, loss, fear, or injury without despair or excessive resentment. It does not mean denying pain or refusing lawful help.

Sabr is sometimes described as holding or restraining the self. A person controls an impulse that would pull them away from obedience. This can require more strength than a single dramatic act. The believer may need to renew patience many times over a long period. Al-Hasan al-Basri explains that believers should remain patient in the religion Allah chose for them, in ease and hardship alike, until they meet Him. The Salaf emphasized steadfastness through changing circumstances rather than a brief display of courage.

**“persevere”** is musābarah, to remain firm in the face of others’ endurance and not let those opposing the truth outlast the believers. The command concerns a community’s sustained commitment, not a license for private aggression. Believers should not abandon justice or break a treaty in the name of perseverance.

**“stand on guard”** translates murābaṭah, a word associated with being stationed and ready. In its historical setting it includes guarding vulnerable frontiers against attack. It also carries a devotional meaning in reports: maintaining the connection between prayers, including waiting for the next congregational salah after completing one.

These meanings do not authorize individuals to create armed groups or take the law into their own hands. The military sense belongs to a lawful community under legitimate authority, with protection of people and justice as its purpose. The devotional sense is open to all: maintain worship, wait for the next prayer, and stay prepared to obey Allah.

**“and be mindful of Allah”** means practise taqwa in every circumstance. Taqwa is the foundation of patience, perseverance, and vigilance. Without it, a person may appear steadfast while acting from pride, revenge, or desire for worldly fame. Mindfulness directs effort toward what Allah approves.

**“so you may be successful”** means attaining safety and lasting reward. Success includes deliverance from Allah’s punishment and entry into Paradise. It is not limited to victory in a particular worldly struggle.

### The four commands together

The verse does not list four unrelated qualities. Patience steadies the individual. Perseverance keeps the community from being worn down by opposition. Murābaṭah protects the community and sustains readiness in worship. Taqwa ensures that all three remain within Allah’s guidance.

The order also moves from the inner self toward collective responsibility. A person first holds themselves to obedience. They then endure with the community. They remain ready and attentive, whether through worship or a lawful defensive responsibility. Finally, they keep Allah in view as the standard for all conduct.

### Three forms of patience

Patience in obedience means continuing to do what Allah has commanded. A believer may find prayer difficult because of work, illness, or fatigue, but they seek a way to fulfil it according to their ability. The same applies to duties of honesty, charity, family care, and justice.

Patience against sin means stopping oneself from what is prohibited. A person may be tempted to take wealth unjustly, answer an insult with cruelty, or gain status by deception. Taqwa helps them restrain the impulse and choose what is lawful.

Patience in hardship means bearing pain without surrendering to despair. It may involve seeking medical care, asking others for help, protecting one’s family, and praying for relief. Patient endurance is not passive neglect of the means Allah has provided.

### Perseverance with others

Musābarah asks the believers to remain firm when opposition is persistent. It does not mean that anger or force is always the answer. The surrounding verses have praised patience, truthful conduct, and care for justice. Endurance may mean continuing to worship, teach, serve, or defend the vulnerable despite pressure.

The community’s unity is important. When believers support one another in good, they are less likely to be isolated by hardship. They can share knowledge, resources, and protection. Unity must be founded on truth and taqwa, not on tribal pride or uncritical loyalty.

### What murābaṭah means

Reports give murābaṭah more than one application. One is guarding a frontier and maintaining readiness to protect a community. Another is keeping a spiritual link by performing prayers and waiting for the next one. These meanings share attention, persistence, and readiness.

A hadith from Abu Hurayrah describes actions that erase sins and raise a person’s rank: performing wudu thoroughly despite difficult conditions, taking many steps to the mosque, and waiting for one prayer after another. The Prophet ﷺ calls this murābaṭah. The report is transmitted by Muslim and An-Nasa’i. It shows that vigilance in worship can be a form of steadfastness.

Other reports describe the reward of guarding a frontier. Sahl bin Saʿd as-Saʿidi reports that a day of murābaṭah in Allah’s cause is better than the world and what it contains. Al-Bukhari records this hadith. Salman al-Farisi reports that guarding for a day and night is better than a month of fasting and night prayer, and that the guard’s good deeds continue after death. These narrations praise a lawful duty of protection, not unauthorized violence. The Qur’an also calls for preparedness and trained horses as a deterrent (8:60), which does not authorize individuals to launch attacks.

Fadalah bin ʿUbayd reports that a person who dies while guarding in Allah’s cause continues to receive reward until the Resurrection. Ahmad records the report, and Abu Dawud and At-Tirmidhi transmit a version graded hasan sahih. Ibn Hibban also records it. A report from Ibn Abbas says that two eyes will not be touched by the Fire: an eye that wept from fear of Allah and an eye that kept watch in Allah’s cause. These narrations associate physical vigilance with spiritual accountability and the Day of Qiyamah.

Salman al-Farisi reports that a day and night of guarding is better than a month of fasting and night prayer, and that a person who dies while on duty continues to receive provision and is protected from the trial of the grave. The report is not a reason to underestimate the value of fasting or salah. It highlights the service of protecting others while accepting a demanding responsibility.

Another report from Sahl bin Saʿd as-Saʿidi says a day of guarding in Allah’s cause is better than the whole world and what it contains. These reports refer to a lawful frontier duty, with sincere intention and the protection of the Muslim community as its purpose. They should not be used to encourage a person to seek violence or enter a conflict without legitimate authority.

The reward described in these narrations depends on sincere intention and a legitimate duty. Some explanations compare the ongoing reward of frontier service with sadaqah, or charity, whose benefit continues. That comparison highlights how protecting a community can enable many people to worship and serve. It is not a reward for seeking conflict, harming civilians, or acting outside lawful authority. The point is the value of safeguarding the ummah and remaining steadfast in obedience to the sacred law.

### Prayer as a form of vigilance

The devotional meaning of murābaṭah is accessible to believers who are not stationed at a frontier. A person who attends congregational prayer, completes one salah, and waits for the next remains attentive to Allah. Wudu performed carefully, even in difficult conditions, and regular steps to the mosque are acts of worship.

A hadith from Abu Hurayrah, also spelled Abu Hurairah, says that careful ablution in uncomfortable conditions, many steps to the mosque, and waiting for the next salah erase sins and raise rank. Abu Salman ibn ʿAbdur-Rahman narrates a similar report. The Prophet ﷺ repeats that this is murābaṭah. Muslim and An-Nasa’i transmit the report. It shows that readiness can take a quiet devotional form, not only a military one.

A report from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb mentions a particularly great reward for guarding during Ramadan, though the narrator expressed uncertainty about one of the numerical details. The larger point is the virtue of sincere service and alertness. A believer should not depend on an uncertain number to fulfil a duty or make claims about guaranteed reward.

Waiting for prayer does not require neglecting family, work, or health. It means that worship remains important in the person’s schedule and that they prepare to fulfil it. The believer’s vigilance is not measured only by dramatic actions. It can be expressed through regular, quiet commitments.

### Reports about sacrifice and worship

Abdullah bin al-Mubarak sent a poem to al-Fudayl ibn ʿIyad contrasting the devotional work of people near the Two Holy Mosques with the hardship of those guarding a frontier. Muhammad bin Ibrahim bin Abi Sakinah carried the poem to al-Fudayl. Al-Fudayl wept after reading it and acknowledged the sincerity of the counsel. Al-Hafiz Ibn Asakir preserves the account in a biographical report about Ibn al-Mubarak.

The poem contrasts prayer and fasting with the physical danger of frontier service, describing dusty feet and the risk of battle. It uses sharp language to make its point, but it should not be read as a judgment that worshippers near the Sacred Mosques are insincere. The poem is rhetorical. It highlights a form of service that can be unseen, while the verse itself values all sincere obedience guided by taqwa. The account also recalls Al-Fudayl’s connection to the two holy cities and his response when the letter reached him.

A report relayed by Abu ʿAbdur-Rahman describes a person asking the Prophet ﷺ for an act equal to the reward of the mujahidin. The Prophet asked whether he could pray continuously and fast without breaking the fast. When the man said he could not, the Prophet explained that he could not reach the same rank by imitation. The comparison is not a command to exhaust oneself. It shows that each form of worship has its own demands, and people should not claim an equal reward without undertaking the relevant service.

Ibn Jarir records a report of Abu ʿUbaydah writing to Umar ibn al-Khattab about the Romans gathering their forces. Umar replied with reassurance that hardship would be followed by ease and reminded him of Allah’s promise. This account links patience to communal responsibility. The believer faces a real challenge while relying on Allah and using sound means.

Some spiritual teachers describe patience in different registers: patience of the self in obedience, of the heart under trial, and of the spirit in longing for Allah. In the language of the Tariqah, this inward patience accompanies remembrance and devotion. The Prophet’s Sunnah joins remembrance to service and discipline. Such reflections are metaphors for inward devotion. They do not replace the practical commands to fulfil duties, avoid sin, care for others, and remain vigilant.

### Taqwa and success

The final command gathers the preceding ones. Taqwa is not an extra quality added after patience and vigilance. It is what makes them sincere and lawful. A person may persist in a cause for the wrong reason, or remain on guard out of hostility. Taqwa keeps the intention and conduct directed toward Allah.

A report from Zayd bin Aslam describes Abu ʿUbaydah writing to Umar bin al-Khattab about Roman forces gathering. Umar replied that Allah would turn hardship into ease and reminded him that one hardship would not overcome two forms of ease. The account illustrates trust during a real danger, not a promise that preparation is unnecessary.

Muhammad bin Kaʿb al-Qurazi explains taqwa here as guarding what is between a person and Allah, so that they may succeed when they meet Him. The Prophet ﷺ told Muʿadh to be mindful of Allah wherever he was, follow a bad deed with a good one, and treat people well. This guidance places patience and guarding within everyday conduct as well as public duty.

The promise of success includes the present life, where patience can preserve a community and protect people, and the Hereafter, where Allah rewards the faithful. It does not guarantee that believers will never suffer defeat or loss. The chapter has already described severe tests. Success is measured by faithfulness to Allah and the final outcome He grants.

Some spiritual writers describe the soul’s patience through the story of Joseph and Zulaykha, using separation as a metaphor for longing for Allah. This is a figurative reflection, not an additional legal command. The plain guidance remains to persist in worship, avoid sin, endure hardship, and maintain taqwa.

### In short

- Sabr means patience in obedience, restraint from sin, and endurance through hardship.
- Musābarah and murābaṭah call for perseverance and vigilance, within lawful community responsibility.
- Taqwa guides every effort, and lasting success is with Allah.
