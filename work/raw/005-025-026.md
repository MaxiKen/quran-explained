<<<VERSE 5:25>>>
Musa (Moses) turns to Allah after his people's repeated refusal: "My Lord! I have no control over anyone except myself and my brother." He cannot compel their hearts to accept the command they have rejected. His prayer asks Allah to distinguish him and Harun (Aaron) from the people whose choice has made the intended advance impossible.

The words express both the limit of his authority over others and his own continued commitment. He does not join their refusal or abandon the command as unworthy. He places the separation between obedience and rebellion before the Lord who can judge it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Moses pleaded, “My Lord!"** introduces his appeal to the One who sent him. The account describes him as angry and deeply hurt by the refusal. He brings that pain to Allah instead of surrendering his own faithfulness.

**"I have no control over anyone except myself and my brother."** He can answer for his own obedience and is certain of his brother's support. He cannot make the rest of the people carry out what they have refused. This is not a denial that he conveyed the command clearly, but recognition of their refusal to act upon it.

**"So set us apart from the rebellious people.”"** asks for a distinction between the obedient and those who contravene Allah's command. Ibn 'Abbas explains ifruq as judging between the two sides, and Ad-Dahhak gives the same sense. Another explanation takes it as separation. The prayer asks that each receive the treatment appropriate to its conduct.

### Why is their refusal especially painful?

Musa had faced great hardship to bring them out of ignorance and subjection. They had seen Pharaoh's magicians accept the truth after the contest at his court. They had witnessed the proud ruler and his forces overcome, his wealth left behind and his power destroyed through drowning, while they themselves crossed safely.

The account describes Allah giving them the country and wealth over which Pharaoh had boasted, without a battle for those possessions. Their deliverance was therefore not merely something they had heard about from distant ancestors. They had seen it.

Against that background, their refusal to enter under a promise of victory was a bitter rejection of both divine favour and prophetic labour (5:21-22). Musa's steadfastness appears in the restraint of his appeal. He continues seeking Allah's judgment rather than allowing their ingratitude to overturn his own purpose.

### Why mention only Harun when two chiefs had also remained faithful?

Yusha' ibn Nun and Kalib ibn Yu'qina had supported the command and counselled their people. Their absence from this prayer is not explained as proof that their earlier counsel was false.

The explanation instead points to the certainty Musa had concerning his brother, who was himself a prophet under divine protection. The two chiefs, though righteous, did not have that same protected status. After repeated betrayal, Musa named the obedience about which he was certain: his own and Harun's.

His words therefore describe the limit of what he could reliably undertake at that moment. They do not require erasing the virtue already attributed to the two men.

### What judgment is he asking for?

Ibn 'Abbas explains the prayer as asking that the people's deserved consequence be assigned to them, and that Musa and his brother receive what was appropriate for their own conduct. They should not be treated as participants in a refusal they had opposed.

The next verse gives Allah's response through a period of exclusion and wandering. The prayer itself stops at entrusting the division to Him. Musa does not invent a punishment or claim independent power to impose whatever he pleases.

### In short

- Musa acknowledges that he cannot force a rebellious people into obedience.
- He remains certain of his own commitment and that of his prophet brother Harun.
- The prayer asks Allah to judge or separate the obedient from those who have refused His command.
- Pain caused by betrayal becomes an appeal to Allah, not abandonment of Musa's own responsibility.

<<<VERSE 5:26>>>
Allah answers Musa's (Moses') prayer with a consequence matching the refusal: "Then this land is forbidden to them for forty years, during which they will wander through the land." The people who had insisted on staying where they were would not reach the destination offered to them. Their confinement would occur in open country rather than behind a prison wall.

Musa is then told not to grieve over the rebellious people. Their punishment is not an injustice done to people who kept the covenant. It is the result of their own refusal, though the compassionate prophet would still feel pain at their hardship.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah replied, “Then this land is forbidden to them for forty years"** excludes the Children of Israel from entering the Holy Land during the stated period. The explanation understands this not merely as an instruction they might choose to ignore, but as an enforced inability to reach it. They could not obtain the destination while persisting in the refusal that had brought the sentence.

**"during which they will wander through the land."** They move without finding the way out, bewildered in the wilderness called At-Tih. Their attempts to go forward to Baytul-Maqdis or return to Egypt bring them back to where they began. Movement alone does not free them from Allah's judgment.

**"So do not grieve for the rebellious people.”"** comforts Musa. Prophets are naturally distressed by the suffering of those they guide. He is told not to be overcome by sorrow over a sentence their own disobedience has deserved.

### How can open land become a place of confinement?

The account emphasises that Allah does not need guards, iron gates or chains. The earth, atmosphere and every direction belong to His creation and obey His command. A people can therefore be unable to escape even where no visible barrier surrounds them.

Reports describe them travelling through the day only to find themselves at the starting place by evening. Others describe the same result after earnest travel through the night. Their freedom to walk did not amount to freedom to reach the goal they had rejected.

The reported dimensions vary. Muqatil gives thirty farsakhs in length and nine in width. With a farsakh taken as three miles, that account gives ninety by twenty-seven miles. Another report gives thirty by eighteen miles. A gloss describes a space of seven parasangs, while another attributes roughly nine parasangs, described there as thirty miles, to Ibn 'Abbas.

These measurements are the differing accounts supplied, not a single surveyed map. The population is also reported as about six hundred thousand. The central point is the same in each version: a very large people remained confined in a relatively limited open area for forty years.

### Why does the punishment fit the refusal?

They had told Musa that they would sit where they were instead of joining the commanded struggle. The explanation sees their detention in that very region as a consequence proportionate to those words. They were denied the entry whose conditions they had refused.

This did not annul Allah's promise concerning the land. It meant that the particular generation rejecting the opportunity would not receive it as though it had obeyed. The later fulfilment came through the people who remained and the generation raised during the interval.

The historical reports differ over how completely the older generation passed away. One account says those aged twenty or above died during the forty years. A report from Ibn 'Abbas recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim mentions those above forty. Other summaries say the disobedient generation disappeared, while some allow survivors. These differences should not be hidden behind a single unsupported age threshold.

One account dates the forty years from 1440 to 1400 before the common era. Those are the dates of that narration's presentation, not an independently established chronology here. The verse itself supplies the duration.

### Were there still blessings in the wilderness?

Yes. Punishment did not mean the absence of all mercy. Musa and Harun remained with the people, along with the faithful Yusha' ibn Nun and Kalib ibn Yu'qina. The wilderness was a place of hardship for the rebels but also a setting of divine gifts through those righteous people.

Clouds shaded them from the sun and followed their movement. A stone provided water when Musa struck it with his staff, with twelve streams for their needs. Manna and quails supplied food. Another account describes a pillar or tower of light that enabled activity during the dark hours.

The account also places the revelation of the Torah, the establishment of its law and the erection of the Tabernacle of the Covenant within this period. Detention did not stop the work of guidance.

These gifts are explained through the barakah, blessing, associated with the prophets' presence. A further possibility is that the people repented after witnessing the consequences of their conduct, and that some of the gifts came in response to that repentance. The possibility is offered as such, not asserted as a fully narrated event.

### What happened to Musa and Harun?

Both died during the wandering according to the reports supplied. One account places Harun's death first, followed by Musa's after either six months or a year. Their presence in the same place as the punished people did not make their own standing identical to theirs. The account describes their circumstances as mercy for them, though chastisement for the others.

A hadith relates that, as death approached, Musa asked Allah to bring him within a stone's throw of the Holy Land. His request was granted. He did not personally lead the later conquest described in these accounts.

Yusha', also called Yuwsha' son of Nun, Joshua, was then appointed as a prophet to guide the Children of Israel. One gloss places his becoming a prophet sometime after his fortieth year. When the period of wandering ended, he led those remaining toward the land.

### How did the later conquest fulfil the promise?

The people advanced under Yusha' and laid siege to Jerusalem. The account describes victory on a Friday afternoon. As sunset approached, Yusha' feared the beginning of the Sabbath before the task was completed. He addressed the sun as something under command, just as he himself was under command, and asked Allah to hold its setting.

Allah delayed the sunset until the conquest was achieved. One account gives an hour. Ahmad bin Hanbal records in his Musnad a hadith stating that the sun was not held back for any human being except Joshua on his march toward the Holy House. The event is thus narrated as Allah's act in answer to the prophet, not as independent control of the sun by a creature.

The wealth found after entry is described as extraordinary. But the people were not entitled to keep the spoils in that dispensation. They gathered them for a fire to consume, and the fire would not take them. Yusha' concluded that something had been withheld.

He called the twelve tribal leaders to pledge. One man's hand remained attached to his, identifying the theft. The man brought out a golden cow's head whose eyes were precious stones and whose teeth were pearls. Once it was returned to the gathered spoils, the fire consumed them. Ibn Abi Hatim records this account from Ibn 'Abbas, and the discussion notes supporting evidence in the Sahih.

The account also recalls the instruction to enter the gate bowing and saying hittah, asking that sins be removed. Some instead entered dragging themselves along and substituted words about a seed in a hair. That later act of disobedience is mentioned in connection with the fuller account in Surah Al-Baqarah. Victory did not remove the obligation to obey the instructions attached to it.

### What changed in the generation raised in hardship?

The parents had feared that an invasion would bring defeat and leave their children as plunder. The explanation notices that those children, after growing up, became the generation that entered and conquered. Conditions feared as a threat to their future became a means of developing strength.

Desert life demanded effort, realism, simplicity and courage. The account contrasts this with the settled habits of the earlier community in Egypt. It treats difficult circumstances as capable of bringing out capacities that prolonged ease and dependence had left unused.

The point is not that suffering is pleasant or that every hardship automatically reforms everyone. It is the explanation of this generational change in the narrated event. A community weakened by its accustomed life can be confronted with circumstances through which a different kind of character is formed.

### How does the story challenge claims of automatic favour?

The people had seen Pharaoh and his soldiers overwhelmed, yet refused an enemy described as possessing less than a tenth of Egypt's former strength. Musa, a messenger to whom Allah spoke, was among them and had conveyed a promise of victory. Their refusal therefore exposed the distance between receiving a blessing and faithfully responding to it.

The account uses this history to challenge the claim that being Allah's children and beloved ones guarantees privilege. Disobedience has consequences even for a community with a great religious inheritance. The warning concerns that breach, while the prophets, faithful men and later generation remain distinguished from those responsible for it.

Musa's consolation completes the judgment. His compassion is recognised, but he is not asked to treat the sentence as wrong. Allah's promise was ultimately fulfilled, and the people's refusal was neither ignored nor made stronger than that promise.

### In short

- Refusal to enter brought forty years of wandering and exclusion, without the need for visible prison walls.
- Mercy continued through the prophets' presence, provision and guidance even within the period of punishment.
- The reports differ over some measurements, ages and chronology. Their shared account places the later entry under Joshua after Musa and Harun had died.
- The later conquest fulfilled Allah's promise, while the stories of the spoils and the gate show that victory did not cancel obedience.
- Religious inheritance is not immunity from consequences. The final consolation asks Musa to trust Allah's just judgment despite his sorrow.
