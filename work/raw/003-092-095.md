<<<VERSE 3:92>>>
“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish. And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah” (3:92). The verse connects complete goodness with giving sincerely from what one values, not merely parting with unwanted things. Allah knows what is given and the intention behind it.

### Phrase by phrase

**“You will never achieve righteousness”** means that a person does not reach the fullest rank of al-Birr, goodness and righteousness, merely by making a claim. The word birr can describe the broad practice of good deeds and the faithful fulfillment of duties owed to Allah and other people. It is also used for the people of goodness, al-abrar. The Qur’an describes the righteous as enjoying bliss (83:22), and contrasts goodness with sin (82:14). The verse highlights a central sign of birr: being willing to give for Allah’s sake what one genuinely loves.

**“until you donate some of what you cherish”** does not require a person to give away everything or to neglect dependents. The phrase “some of what” indicates giving a portion. The point is to choose something valuable to oneself rather than routinely giving only what is damaged, useless, or unwanted. Allah also tells believers not to select poor-quality things for charity when they would not accept them for themselves (2:267). A person may give obligatory zakah or voluntary nafl charity, but the verse teaches that giving from what one loves is a mark of excellence in either case.

The Companions responded to this teaching with concrete acts. Abū Ṭalḥah, one of the wealthy Anṣār in al-Madīnah, most loved his garden of Bayruḥāʾ, which had a well whose water the Prophet ﷺ sometimes drank. When this verse was revealed, Abū Ṭalḥah offered the garden as charity, hoping for reward from Allah. The Prophet ﷺ praised it as profitable wealth and advised him to give it to relatives. Abū Ṭalḥah then distributed it among his family and cousins. This shows that supporting relatives can be charity, not only giving to strangers.

A report from Anas describes this gift. Ibn Jarīr also transmits accounts of the Companions’ response. ʿUmar asked what to do with his share of land at Khaybar, which he valued greatly, and the Prophet ﷺ directed him to retain the property and give its yield in Allah’s cause. The land itself could remain productive while its benefit was devoted to charity. Other reports recount Zayd ibn Ḥārithah giving a horse he loved, and people freeing beloved servants for Allah’s sake. These examples do not establish one required form of giving. They show the sincerity of offering something precious in a way that benefits others.

The verse does not say that only expensive gifts count. A small gift can be sincere and beloved to the giver. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī explains that even a single date given sincerely for Allah can be a means of great goodness. What matters is not the market price but the giver’s intention and whether the gift is something they value. A person who has little wealth is not shut out of righteousness. Worship, remembrance of Allah, Qur’an recitation, and voluntary prayer are among the other ways by which a person may draw near to Him.

### What does the verse teach about charity?

The command encourages generosity without commanding people to impoverish themselves. A believer gives from good things and keeps the rights and needs of dependents in view. Giving cherished possessions is especially praiseworthy, but giving surplus goods can still benefit others and earn reward. The warning is against making a habit of offering only what one considers worthless.

Righteousness includes both obligatory giving and voluntary charity. Zakah fulfills a duty, while nafl giving expresses generosity beyond that duty. Neither should be turned into a performance for praise. A person may give a valuable gift publicly or quietly, but the deed’s worth depends on the sincerity with which it is offered. The Prophet ﷺ praised truthfulness as connected with birr, and the Qur’an describes feeding others despite one’s own love and need (76:8, 59:9). The model is care for others, not disposal of unwanted objects.

The final clause, **“whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah,”** reassures the giver and cautions against pretense. Allah knows whether the gift was cherished, whether it was given willingly, and whether the purpose was His pleasure or people’s approval. No sincere act is hidden from Him. As reports about charity and remembrance indicate, a person’s circumstances differ, but every act done sincerely is known and can be rewarded. This knowledge should encourage those who can give much and those whose means are limited.

The Qur’an uses birr in a broad sense. In al-Baqarah, it describes faith and acts of care for others together (2:177). It also calls the prophets Ibrāhīm and Yaḥyā dutiful toward their parents (19:14, 19:32), and calls the righteous al-abrar (76:5). These uses help explain why giving cherished wealth is not an isolated ritual. It is one sign of a life ordered around fulfilling the rights of Allah and creation.

Wakiʿ relates that ʿAmr bin Maymūn understood the promise of birr here as attaining Paradise. The verse thus points beyond a single donation toward the final reward of a righteous life. A person does not purchase Paradise with a gift. Rather, a sincere gift is evidence of faith and obedience, and Allah knows and rewards it. A report associated with Abū Bakr connects truthfulness with birr and Paradise, while warning against falsehood and sin. The two Ṣaḥīḥs preserve reports about Abū Ṭalḥah’s garden and ʿUmar’s land, illustrating that charity can be given as a productive endowment as well as a direct gift.

An inward reflection distinguishes between birr, the goodness a person hopes to attain, and al-bārr, Allah, the Origin of all goodness. The Arabic mimmā means “from what,” so the verse asks for a portion of what is loved. This reading says that a person seeking goodness gives a portion of worldly attachment, while the one seeking nearness to Allah struggles to release the heart’s attachment altogether. This is not a demand to neglect lawful responsibilities. It is a way of examining whether possessions are being treated as more beloved than obedience to Allah.

The example of Bayruḥāʾ also clarifies the social shape of charity. The garden was a valued asset near the Prophet’s mosque in al-Madīnah, and its well was known for its water. Abū Ṭalḥah did not insist that the gift be distributed in only one way. He asked the Prophet ﷺ where it would be most useful, then accepted his advice to give it to relatives. ʿUmar’s Khaybar property likewise remained intact while its produce was dedicated to Allah’s cause. These choices show that the object may be preserved when its benefit can be continually shared. Charity is not defined by destroying wealth but by directing its value toward good.

The verse also protects people from measuring righteousness by the size of another person’s gift. A wealthy person may share land or a garden. A person with little may offer a small amount, service, remembrance, recitation, or prayer. Allah knows the means available to each person and the intention accompanying each act. The Qur’an praises those who feed others despite their own need (76:8) and those who give preference to others even when they themselves are in need (59:9). Such generosity is not a performance of wealth. It is a response of the heart.

### In short

- Birr is shown by giving sincerely from what one values.
- The verse calls for a portion of cherished wealth, not the careless abandonment of all one owns.
- Charity may support relatives, and even a small gift can be precious when given sincerely.
- Allah knows both the gift and the intention behind it.

<<<VERSE 3:93>>>
“All food was lawful for the children of Israel, except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.”” (3:93). The verse answers a dispute about what was lawful before the Torah and whether Abraham followed the restrictions later claimed by some of the Children of Israel.

### Phrase by phrase

**“All food was lawful for the children of Israel”** refers to the food that had been permitted before the Torah was revealed, apart from what Israel himself had made unlawful. Israel is Jacob, the prophet and ancestor of the Children of Israel. The verse does not say that every food remained lawful under every later revealed law. It locates the restriction in the period before the Torah and identifies a personal act by Jacob as its beginning.

A report from Ibn ʿAbbās explains that Jacob suffered from sciatica and vowed that, if Allah cured him, he would give up the food he loved most. After he recovered, he abstained from camel meat. Other explanations mention camel meat and milk. The point is that this began as Jacob’s vow, not as a prohibition imposed on Abraham. His descendants later treated the restriction as a religious rule. The story therefore answers the claim that camel products had always been unlawful to the prophets.

The verse’s reference to the Torah also serves the argument about changing laws. Some rulings differ across prophetic communities by Allah’s command. Earlier law permitted certain practices that a later revelation prohibited. Commentators give examples of changes in family and dietary rules, including rules associated with Adam, Noah, Abraham, and Jacob. These are presented to show that a change in a subsidiary ruling does not make a later revelation false. The essential call to worship Allah alone remains, even when particular legal rulings differ.

Reports describe a discussion in which the Jews challenged the Prophet ﷺ over his adherence to Abraham’s religion while he ate camel meat and drank camel milk. They claimed these things had always been forbidden. The answer was that they had been lawful for Abraham and that Jacob’s own vow was a later, personal reason for abstaining. A report about the questioning of the Prophet ﷺ also records the question about what Israel had forbidden himself. Al-Ḥākim and al-Tirmidhī are named in connection with reports about Jacob’s vow.

Another report recorded by Aḥmad describes a group of Jews asking the Prophet ﷺ several questions, including what Israel had forbidden himself, the signs of prophethood, and the Prophet’s angelic wali, the one who brought revelation. He answered that Jibrīl brought revelation by Allah’s command. The group said they would oppose him because of Jibrīl, and the Qur’an answers their hostility elsewhere (2:97). This episode places the question about Jacob’s food among a broader challenge to the Prophet’s message. It does not change the point of the present verse, which is to test their claim against the Torah.

The surrounding argument also concerns the authority of revealed law. The term naskh means that Allah may replace a ruling from an earlier shariah with a later one. This is not a contradiction in Allah’s knowledge. A rule can be suitable for one community and later be superseded by another command. Discussions of Abraham, Musa (Moses), Isa (Jesus), Nuh (Noah), Adam, and Yaqub (Jacob) illustrate that subsidiary rulings were not identical across all prophetic communities. The central call to worship Allah alone was shared. Hence Allah instructs the Prophet to ask for the Torah as evidence rather than accept a claim that every later restriction applied from the beginning.

A related Qur’anic passage asks why a person would prohibit what Allah has made lawful (66:1). Jacob’s personal vow and a divine prohibition should not be confused. His commitment to abstain after recovery was his own vow. The verse explains how the restriction began among his descendants, while the Torah later set out rulings for the Children of Israel. The distinction helps prevent people from turning a personal practice into a claim about Allah’s original law.

### Why are they told to bring the Torah?

**“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Bring the Torah and read it’”** is a direct challenge to substantiate the claim from the scripture they themselves recognize. If the Torah truly said that these foods had been forbidden to Abraham and all earlier prophets, they could produce and read the passage. The demand is not for an unsupported assertion but for evidence from the text being invoked.

**“if your claims are true”** exposes the difference between inherited claims and what the scripture actually says. The challenge calls for honest examination rather than selective appeal to tradition. It also shows that the dispute is not settled by simply declaring a food forbidden. A religious ruling must be attributed truthfully to Allah.

### In short

- Israel means Jacob, whose personal vow began the restriction described here.
- The verse challenges the claim that camel meat and milk were always forbidden to Abraham and the prophets before him.
- It calls the disputants to verify their claim by reading the Torah.

<<<VERSE 3:94>>>
“Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah, they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers” (3:94). After presenting the evidence about what Israel had made unlawful and challenging the claimants to bring the Torah, the verse warns against continuing to attribute false rulings to Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**“whoever still fabricates lies about Allah”** refers to anyone who knowingly invents a religious claim after evidence has clarified the matter. In this passage, the immediate issue is falsely saying that Allah prohibited foods for Abraham and the earlier prophets when the restriction began with Jacob’s own vow. The warning is not limited to a particular community. Anyone who knowingly misrepresents Allah’s law is included.

**“they will be the true wrongdoers”** means they have put falsehood in the place of truth and wronged themselves by turning away from clear evidence. The word rendered wrongdoers also conveys crossing a proper limit. A claim does not become true through repetition or by being attached to an honored scripture. The invitation to bring and read the Torah makes the evidence testable.

The sequence matters. The preceding verse describes a specific historical question, while this verse states a broader principle: after proof has been made clear, inventing a claim against Allah is serious wrongdoing. Sincere uncertainty can be answered by learning. Deliberate distortion after clarification is different, because it knowingly obscures what the evidence shows.

The larger passage gives examples of questions that the Jews raised about revealed truth. One report recorded by Aḥmad describes questions about Jacob, the Torah, and the Prophet’s angelic wali. The Prophet ﷺ identified Jibrīl as the angel who brought revelation, and the Qur’an addresses hostility toward Jibrīl elsewhere (2:97). That report is not a license to accuse every questioner of bad faith. It shows why the verse distinguishes a sincere request for evidence from knowingly persisting in a false claim.

The same distinction applies to rulings across prophetic communities. The Children of Israel received laws through Musa (Moses), and later prophets such as Isa (Jesus) called them back to Allah. Earlier laws associated with Adam, Nuh (Noah), Ibrahim (Abraham), and Yaqub (Jacob) included rulings that were not all identical. The principle of naskh, or the replacement of a ruling by a later revealed command, does not permit anyone to invent a rule. The Torah, Qur’an, and other revealed guidance are not interchangeable with people’s unsupported assertions. Hence Allah’s challenge to read the Torah is relevant to this warning.

The Qur’an also affirms that al-Birr includes faith and acts of kindness (2:177), including feeding people from what one loves (76:8). These examples show that religious truth is not an excuse to distort Allah’s commands. A person may not turn a personal vow into a universal prohibition, nor claim that a changed ruling makes Allah’s revelation false. A related warning asks why someone would forbid what Allah made lawful (66:1). Therefore Allah condemns the deliberate fabrication described here, not honest inquiry or a person who corrects an earlier mistake when the evidence becomes clear.

### In short

- A religious prohibition must not be falsely attributed to Allah.
- The challenge to produce the Torah makes the claim open to verification.
- Deliberate invention after the evidence is clear is wrongdoing.

<<<VERSE 3:95>>>
“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah has declared the truth. So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”” (3:95). The Prophet ﷺ is told to affirm Allah’s truth and call people back to the faith of Abraham: sincere devotion to the one God, without associating partners with Him.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Allah has declared the truth”** affirms that what Allah has revealed about Abraham, Jacob, and the lawful and unlawful is true. It also answers the dispute by directing people to Allah’s revelation rather than to claims that cannot be established from scripture. The command to say this is a clear public declaration, not a concession to the opposing claim.

**“So follow the Way of Abraham”** calls people to Abraham’s millah, his way of faith and worship. Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian in the later communal sense. He submitted to Allah and worshipped Him alone. The Qur’an elsewhere instructs the Prophet ﷺ to follow Abraham’s upright religion (6:161, 16:123). This does not mean that every later legal detail must be identical. The prophets share the foundation of submission to Allah, while some subsidiary rulings can differ between revealed laws.

**“the upright—who was not a polytheist”** explains the defining quality of Abraham’s path. He turned away from idolatry and directed his worship wholly to Allah. His uprightness was not merely a label or ancestry. It was sincere surrender, obedience, and freedom from associating partners with God. The verse rejects attempts to claim Abraham while opposing the pure monotheism that defined his way.

An inward reflection on this verse describes Abraham’s faith as wholehearted surrender to Allah, without holding back one’s devotion. It warns that treating anything besides Allah as an independent source of ultimate power can become a form of association. The practical call remains clear: affirm what Allah has revealed, worship Him alone, and follow the prophets in sincere submission.

The Jews’ challenge about food belongs to a wider dispute over whether Allah could give a later community a different ruling. In the same period, some objected to the direction of prayer changing from al-Bayt al-Maqdis in Jerusalem to the Kaʿbah. Abraham’s way is not tied to one location or a later communal identity. It is the sacred law of worshipping Allah alone, wherever He commands His servants to pray. That is the path of submission shared by the prophets. Differences in subsidiary law do not erase the shared foundation of faith.

Reports about the Prophet’s meeting with Jewish questioners include questions about the ruh, the Prophet’s sleep, and the wali among the angels who brought revelation. The Prophet ﷺ named Jibrīl. Aḥmad records a version of that discussion, and the Qur’an answers opposition to Jibrīl (2:97). These details reinforce that the message comes from Allah, not from an invented human preference. The same revealed guidance calls people to al-Birr and to care for others (2:177, 76:8), while rejecting shirk, the association of partners with Allah. Abraham’s path brings belief, worship, and upright action together.

The command to follow Abraham is therefore not a call to copy one isolated custom, such as a food restriction or a former direction of prayer. It is a call to follow his devotion to Allah and to accept the guidance He sends through His messengers. Musa, Isa, Nuh, Adam, and Yaqub each belong to the history of revelation, though their communities did not receive identical subsidiary laws. The Qur’an can affirm the Torah’s place in that history while also establishing guidance for the Muslim community. What remains constant is submission to Allah and the rejection of idolatry.

### In short

- Allah’s account of Abraham and the law is true.
- Abraham’s way is submission to Allah, not attachment to a later communal label.
- His uprightness means worshipping Allah alone and rejecting idolatry.
