Al-A'raf is the seventh surah of the Qur'an. It has two hundred and six verses, it was revealed in Makkah, and it opens with the letters "Alif-Lãl-Mĩm-Ṣãd". Its name means "The Heights". The surah takes up two large subjects, the return to the Hereafter and the prophets who were sent, and it carries them in turn. First comes the reckoning and the arrogance that provokes it, with the story of Adam and Eve at the heart of that part. Then comes the long roll of messengers and the nations that answered them or refused them. At the end the surah turns back to the Book itself and to the Prophet ﷺ who was charged with carrying it.

There is a second way to describe what the surah is doing, and it is worth holding beside the first. The Book that comes down at its head is, in its essence, a book of guidance. But a book of guidance is worth little to anyone who has not kept his conscience alive. For everyone else it amounts to nothing more than a warning of the grievous fate they are moving towards because of their arrogance. The preacher of truth is troubled by what he sees. What is paramount in his eyes, the absolute truth, is rejected by the majority of the people as a falsehood, and treated with unconcern, as if it had no substance, as if it were completely unreal. The surah is written for that situation. It shows what the truth is, what the messengers carried, what happened to the nations that refused them, and where the account of the nations finally comes to rest.

### What its name means

- **Al-A'raf**, "The Heights". The surah takes the name up in its middle, in verses 46 to 49, where the Heights are described as a barrier between Paradise and the Fire, and the people who are stopped there are given a place of their own.
- The letters that open it, "Alif-Lãl-Mĩm-Ṣãd", are the same kind of opening found at the head of a number of other surahs. Their meaning is not settled, and the surah does not explain it.

### The two subjects of the surah

Looking across the whole of it, most of what the surah deals with falls under one of two headings. The first is ma'ad, the return to the Hereafter, and it runs from the opening of the surah up to about the end of the sixth of the surah's sections. The second is risala, prophethood, and from about the eighth section to the twenty-first the surah gives a detailed account of past prophets, of what happened to their communities, of the reward and the punishment they met, and of the punishment that overtook those who refused them.

The first stretch of the surah is therefore about the return itself and about the reckoning that goes with it. It begins with the Book and what it is for, tells people to follow what has been sent down to them and to take no protectors besides their Lord, and warns that many a community before them was destroyed while its people slept or were at their midday rest. It asks what answer the messengers and the peoples they were sent to will give on the Day of Resurrection, then sets out the weighing of deeds on that day and the pride of Iblis that put human beings under the power of the devil. The account of Adam and Eve and the garden closes that first movement, with the clothing Allah gave them and the promise of pardon.

### What the surah covers

The middle of the surah carries on in shorter movements. It shows how the righteous answer a messenger and how the arrogant refuse one, and it insists that the people who follow a bad leader share in the reckoning of that leader. It describes the reward of the believers and the punishment of the disbelievers, and it pictures the promise of Paradise and the threat of the Fire as two voices addressing the same people. Between them stands the Heights, and the disbelievers are shown begging the believers for a drop of water on the Day of Resurrection and being refused. The Book is then named for what it is, a source of knowledge, of guidance and of mercy, and the Creator is named with the beautiful names and with the command to call on Him. Two parables follow, the rain that brings the dead land back to life, and the field that yields or the field that does not.

Then the surah settles into the stories of the messengers, and this is the longest run in the book. Nuh (Noah) comes first, and his people call him a liar, accuse him of following only what he wants for himself, and are swept away by the flood. Hūd is sent to the people of 'Ad, who are destroyed by a wind. Ṣāliḥ (Salih) is sent to Thamud, who carve their houses out of the rock and are destroyed by an earthquake. Lūt (Lot) confronts a people whose wrongdoing is described plainly, and the angels come to him and to his family. Shu'aib is sent to Madyan with the demand that his people give the full measure and take what is due, and the leaders of Madyan threaten him, and the destruction that follows settles the matter. After these the surah steps back and names the pattern: nations are tested with ease and with hardship, and the end of one is the beginning of the next.

Mūsā (Moses) fills the next long stretch. His mission to Fir'awn, the contest with the magicians, the magicians' conversion and the threat that follows it, the plagues sent on Egypt, the golden calf, the appointment with Allah and the tablets, and the prayer of the one who was faithful all belong to this part. After him the final prophet is described and the message is declared for all of humankind. The surah then turns to the Israelites and their covenants, to the man who deviated from the straight path, to the statement that guidance comes from Allah alone, to the beautiful names and to the creation of human beings and the trap of ingratitude. It ends with the true guardian, the return of the soul to its Maker, the Prophet ﷺ and his noble character, the whisperings of the devil, and the last instruction to remember Allah and to be humble before Him.

### How it connects to the surah before it

Al-An'am, the surah that comes before this one, ends on the question of the earth and of what is entrusted to the people placed on it. Al-A'raf opens at a different height. Its first theme is not the world and its stewardship but the Book and the purpose of the Book, and everything after that first theme is arranged around the two questions the surah names for itself: what the Hereafter is, and what a messenger is for.

### In short

- Al-A'raf is the seventh surah, two hundred and six verses, revealed in Makkah, and it opens with the letters "Alif-Lãl-Mĩm-Ṣãd".
- It has two subjects, the return to the Hereafter and the prophets who were sent, and it devotes its longest stretch to the messengers and the fate of their communities.
- The Book is guidance for those who keep their consciences alive and a warning of the grievous fate for everyone else.
- Its middle holds the Heights, where the people who are stopped between Paradise and the Fire are described, and the disbelievers' final begging of the believers.
- It closes on the Book, the Prophet ﷺ and the instruction to remember Allah with humility.
