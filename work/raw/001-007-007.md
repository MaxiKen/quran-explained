The last verse of the surah tells us which path the previous verse was asking for: "the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray". The request "Guide us along the Straight Path" could have been left general, but Allah defines the path twice over, once by the people who walk it and once by the people who have left it on either side. Allah's answer to this request has already been given in the hadith in which He divides the prayer with His servant: when the servant says "Guide us along the Straight Path, the Path of those You have blessed, not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray," Allah says, "This is for My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for."

The verse explains the straight path in a positive way and a negative way. In a positive way, it is the path of the guided, the sincere, those who obey Allah and His Messengers, keep His commands and stay away from what He has forbidden. In a negative way, it is not the path of those whose intentions are corrupt, who know the truth and turn away from it, and not the path of those who have lost true knowledge and wander in misguidance without finding the way. The believer who says the verse is asking to be kept from both of those roads: help us avoid the path of those who went astray, and the path of those who earned Your anger through their obstinacy. The surah ends here, and with it a picture is complete. The Opening of the Book is a miniature of Islamic belief, and the rest of the Qur'an is that picture enlarged.

### Phrase by phrase

**"the Path of those You have blessed"** stands in apposition to "the Straight Path" of the verse before: the straight path is this path. Who are those Allah has blessed? A verse of Sūrat an-Nisāʾ names them: "Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with those whom Allah has blessed, the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous. What excellent companions they are! Such is the bounty of Allah, and Allah suffices as Knower" (4:69-70). These are the four groups who have found favour with Allah, and the prophets are the greatest of them. The ṣiddīqīn, the truthful or the constantly true, are those who reach spiritual perfection and so hold the highest rank among a prophet's followers, the people ordinarily called the men of Allah or saints. The shuhadāʾ are the martyrs, those who give even their lives for their faith, or the witnesses to the truth, since the word allows both meanings. The ṣāliḥīn, the righteous, are those who follow the Sharīʿah completely, not only in what is obligatory but in what is commendable too, the people ordinarily called pious, virtuous or good. The blessing meant is the blessing of guidance. One reading takes those blessed as the followers of Mūsā (Moses) before Allah's blessings left them, in the days when He shaded them with a white cloud in the wilderness and sent down manna and quails for them, and another reading takes them as the prophets. In the inner reading the phrase has also been understood as "those You have blessed with submission and the Sunnah".

**"not those You are displeased with"** is ghayri l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim, those on whom anger has fallen. Grammatically the clause replaces the "those You have blessed" before it, and the subtle meaning of the substitution is that the guided are not these people. They are the ones who know Allah's commands quite well and deliberately go against them, out of perversity or in the service of their desires, the people who fall short in obedience. This was the general condition of the Jews, the Israelites, who were ready to sell their religion for a trivial worldly gain, insulted their prophets and sometimes killed them. The verse speaks of the religion of those Allah was angry with, whom He abandoned and whose hearts He did not protect until they grew complacent.

**"or those who are astray"** is wa-lā ḍ-ḍāllīn, those who have lost the way. They are the people who, out of ignorance or thoughtlessness, go beyond the limits Allah set and indulge in excess and exaggeration in religion. This has generally been the error of the Christians, who went too far in their reverence for a prophet and made him into a god, and who strayed from Islam in doing so. They lost their way and were not guided because they were careless in seeking the truth and in letting it guide them. The repeated "not" shows that both roads are roads of misguidance.

### The road between two ditches

The straight path, then, is the road with no twists and turns, the particular way of faith that avoids the two extremes of excess and deficiency. Whoever walks it neither exceeds the limits nor falls short of them, in belief and in practice alike. On one side is the rebellion of the Jews, who would not listen to Allah's prophets and went on to kill them. On the other is the excessive zeal of the Christians, who deified a prophet. So the essential meaning of the verse is that in asking for the straight path we are not asking for the path of people who are slaves to their desires, perverse in thought and action and remiss in their religious duties, nor for the path of people who are ignorant, heedless or misled and go to excess in religion, but for a path between the two that leans neither towards excess nor towards deficiency, as free of the promptings of desire as it is of doubts, confusions and false beliefs.

The path of the believers is to know the truth and act on it. The Jews gave up acting on the religion, and the Christians lost the true knowledge of it. That is why anger came down on the Jews, while "astray" fits the Christians better. Someone who knows the truth and refuses to act on it deserves anger in a way that the ignorant do not, and the Christians wanted the true knowledge but could not find it, because they did not look for it in its proper sources. Both communities in fact deserve both descriptions, but anger is the attribute more particular to the Jews, as in "those whom Allah cursed and was angry with" (5:60), and straying is the one more particular to the Christians, as in "who went astray before and led many astray and strayed from the right way" (5:77). The prayer is a warning to the believer to beware of both roads.

ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim heard this from the Prophet ﷺ himself, in a hadith recorded by Aḥmad and by at-Tirmidhī, who graded it ḥasan gharīb. The Prophet's ﷺ horsemen had captured ʿAdī's paternal aunt with other people, and when they were lined up before him she said, "Messenger of Allah, my provider is far away, my children have stopped coming, and I am an old woman with no one to serve me. Be gracious to me, and may Allah be gracious to you." He asked who her provider was, and when she said ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim he said, "The one who ran away from Allah and His Messenger?" and then set her free. A man beside him, who ʿAdī thought was ʿAlī, told her to ask for a mount, and the Prophet ﷺ ordered that she be given one. She came to ʿAdī afterwards and said, "He has done for me what your father, generous as he was, would never have done. So-and-so went to him and he was gracious to him, and so-and-so went to him and he was gracious to him." So ʿAdī went to the Prophet ﷺ, found women and children crowding around him so closely that he knew this was no king like Kisrā or Caesar, and heard him say, "ʿAdī, what makes you run away? Is it that 'there is no god but Allah' should not be said? Is there any god but Allah? Or that 'Allah is the Greatest' should not be said? Is there anything greater than Allah?" ʿAdī accepted Islam, and he saw the Prophet's ﷺ face light up with joy as he said, "Those who earned the anger are the Jews, and those who went astray are the Christians."

Even before Islam the two communities knew what rested on them. When Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl went with some friends to Syria in search of the true religion, the Jews told him, "You will not become a Jew without carrying a share of the anger of Allah that we have earned," and he answered, "It is Allah's anger I am running from." The Christians told him, "If you join us you will carry a share of Allah's displeasure," and he said, "I cannot bear that." So he kept to his natural faith, avoided idols and the practices of the polytheists, and became neither a Jew nor a Christian. His companions became Christians, finding that purer than Judaism. Waraqah ibn Nawfal was one of them, until Allah guided him through His Prophet ﷺ when he was sent, and Waraqah believed in the revelation that came to him.

### Why the path is defined by people and not by a book

A question opens the door to a deeper understanding of the verse. To define the straight path, would it not have been shorter and clearer to call it "the path of the Prophet ﷺ" or "the path of the Qur'an"? The whole Qur'an is an explanation of the straight path and the Prophet's ﷺ teaching an elaboration of it. Instead, two verses of this short surah are spent defining the path positively and negatively, and the positive definition points to people: if you want the straight path, find those whom Allah has blessed and take their way. The Qur'an does not say "the path of the Qur'an", because a book by itself is not enough to form a human being. It does not say "the path of the Prophet", because the Prophet ﷺ was not going to remain in this world forever and no prophet was to come after him. So in listing those whose teaching and example lead to the straight path, it names, alongside the prophets, people who will be found living among us until the last day: the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous.

The Prophet ﷺ did the same. When he told his Companions that his ummah, like earlier communities, would split into seventy or seventy-two sects, of which only one would be on the right path, they asked which it would be, and his answer again pointed to people: "The one that follows my way and the way of my Companions." Books and oral traditions on their own cannot teach, train and discipline a person. For that one must sit with people who know and learn from them. The real teacher and trainer of a human being is another human being, and a book cannot take that place by itself. The Urdu poet and humorist Akbar put it in a line that amounts to saying that courses teach words, but men train men. It holds in every field of life: no one ever became a doctor, an engineer or even a cook or a tailor just by reading a book. In the same way, reading the Qur'an and the hadith on one's own cannot be enough for a person's moral and spiritual training. Such study has to be carried on under a specialist, a genuine scholar, before it can be useful. Many educated people today imagine that they can master the Qur'an and the hadith by reading a translation, or at best a commentary, and the mistake is self-evident. Had a book been enough for guidance, there would have been no need to send prophets. Allah sent His Book and sent His Prophet ﷺ with it as teacher and guide, and in defining the straight path He named the servants who enjoy His special favour. All of this says that in trying to understand the Book of Allah and act on it, one cannot rely only on one's own reading and judgment but must turn to someone who knows.

Two things, then, are needed for a person's success in body and in spirit: the Book of Allah, which holds guidance for every sphere of life, and the men of Allah, who make that guidance effective. The way to benefit from the men of Allah is to measure them by the well-known principles of the Book of Allah, and not to count as men of Allah those who do not conform to it. But once genuine men of Allah have been found, one should seek their guidance in understanding the Book and acting on it. The sectarian divisions on this point come from two opposite errors. Some chose to follow the Book of Allah alone, dismissed the men of Allah entirely and gave no weight to their teaching and explanations. Others made the men of Allah the sole measure of truth and grew indifferent to the Book of Allah. Both roads lead to fatal error.

Knowing the straight path and following it is the real knowledge and the real achievement of this mortal life. A mistake in identifying it has taken whole peoples and nations to ruin, and even among non-Muslims there are those who say they are seeking God and take enormous pains over it. That is why the Qur'an defines the path so explicitly, by inclusion and by exclusion. Asking for the straight path is the essence of the surah.

### Islam and the Sunnah together

The inner reading of "those You have blessed" as "those You have blessed with submission and the Sunnah" ties the two together because, until they are joined, the servant has no straightness in his religion. It is related that ash-Shāfiʿī said, "I saw the Real in a dream. He said to me, 'Ask Me for a favour, son of Idrīs.' I said, 'Let me die in submission (islām).' He said, 'Say: and in the Sunnah. Ask Me for both.'" Islām does not exist without the Sunnah, and wherever the Sunnah is, there the true religion is. Hence the Prophet ﷺ, al-Muṣṭafā, said, "There are no words without deeds, no words and deeds without intention, and no words, deeds and intention without hitting the mark in the Sunnah." Islām has been likened to a spring of water: trees cannot do without a spring, and islām cannot do without the Sunnah. Every breast adorned with the glory of islām becomes a place where the light of the Sunnah shines on it, which is why Allah says, "Is the one whose breast Allah has opened to Islam, so that he walks in a light from his Lord" (39:22), and that light, it has been said, is the light of the Sunnah. A report describes the resurrection, when the people of the seven heavens and the seven earths are gathered, everyone's feet stuck fast in his own deeds, head down, helpless, confused, stumbling and getting up again, thirsty and naked. Suddenly a fragrant, perfumed figure comes out from the hiding places of the Unseen, and the scent reaches the people of felicity, who grow fragrant themselves and rejoice, and ask, "Lord God, what fragrance and comfort is this, what beauty and perfection?" The answer comes: "This is the face of the beauty of Our Messenger's Sunnah. Whoever followed the Sunnah in the house of the decree, I give him leave to set the foot of safety in the pavilion of his glory, and whoever was a stranger to the Sunnah there, I send down to the Fire, for today too he is a stranger and rejected." So become a follower of the Sunnah and hold to the religion in order to live, for everything but the religion is death, and all but the Sunnah is grief.

### The grace is Allah's, the deviation is theirs

The wording of the verse carries a lesson about where things come from. When Allah mentions His favour, He names Himself as the giver: "those You have blessed". When He mentions anger, He leaves the agent unnamed, "those You are displeased with", literally "those on whom anger is", although it is He who sent the anger down on them, just as He says elsewhere, "Have you not seen those who take as allies a people with whom Allah is angry" (58:14). And He attributes their straying to the strayers themselves, although they strayed by His just decree, for "whomever Allah guides is rightly guided, and whomever He leaves astray, you will find no protecting friend to lead him" (18:17), and "whomever Allah leaves astray, there is no guide for him, and He leaves them wandering blindly in their rebellion" (7:186), no walī, no guiding friend, being able to help them. These verses and many others show that Allah alone guides and leads astray, against the claim of the Qadariyyah sect that the servants create their own destiny by their own choice. They rely on ambiguous verses and avoid the clear ones that contradict their wishes, which is the way of people who follow their desires and their waywardness. An authentic hadith says, "When you see those who follow the unclear parts of the Qur'an, they are the ones Allah has named, so beware of them," referring to the verse "As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they follow the unclear part of it, seeking discord (fitnah) and seeking its hidden meanings" (3:7). No innovator in the religion has ever been able to produce sound evidence from the Qur'an for his innovation. The Qur'an came to separate truth from falsehood and guidance from error, and there is no discrepancy or contradiction in it, because it is a revelation from the Most Wise, the Praiseworthy.

### Saying āmīn

When the recitation of the surah ends it is recommended to say āmīn, which means "O Allah, answer our prayer." At-Tirmidhī took it to mean "do not disappoint our hope", while the majority of scholars said it means "answer us". It has also been explained as "so let it be" and as "our Lord, do with us as we have asked You." Wāʾil ibn Ḥujr said, "I heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ recite 'not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray' and then say 'āmīn', drawing out his voice." Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd and at-Tirmidhī recorded it, Abū Dāwūd's wording adds "raising his voice with it", and at-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan and noted that the same is related from ʿAlī and Ibn Masʿūd. Abū Hurayrah related that whenever the Prophet ﷺ recited "not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray" he said āmīn loudly enough for the first row behind him to hear, and Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah add that the mosque would then shake with the people's āmīn. Ad-Dāraquṭnī recorded it and called it ḥasan. Bilāl once said, "Messenger of Allah, do not finish your āmīn before I can join you," as Abū Dāwūd recorded. Al-Ḥasan and Jaʿfar aṣ-Ṣādiq are reported to have stressed the m in the word.

Saying āmīn is recommended for anyone reciting the surah outside the prayer and strongly recommended for the one praying, whether alone or behind an imam. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a hadith in both Ṣaḥīḥs, "When the imam says āmīn, say āmīn, for whoever's āmīn coincides with that of the angels has his past sins forgiven," and in Muslim's wording, "When one of you says āmīn in the prayer and the angels in heaven say āmīn, and the one coincides with the other, his past sins are forgiven." The coincidence has been understood as saying it at the same moment as the angels, and also as saying it with the same sincerity. Abū Mūsā related, in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, that the Prophet ﷺ said, "When the imam says 'wa-lā ḍ-ḍāllīn', say āmīn, and Allah will answer you." The word marks the believers out. ʿĀʾishah related that when the Jews were mentioned to him the Prophet ﷺ said, "They do not envy us anything as much as they envy us Friday, to which we were guided and from which they went astray, the qiblah, to which we were guided and from which they went astray, and our saying āmīn behind the imam." Aḥmad recorded it in his Musnad, and Ibn Mājah's wording is, "The Jews have never envied you anything as much as the greeting of peace and the saying of āmīn."

### The whole surah in seven verses

With this verse the surah is complete, and it can be read as one movement. Its seven verses contain the praise and thanks of Allah and His glorification by His most beautiful names and highest attributes. They mention the Hereafter, which is the Day of Resurrection. They direct His servants to ask of Him, to call on Him and to declare that all power and strength come from Him. They call to sincerity in worshipping Allah alone, to singling Him out as the only God, and to believing in His perfection and that He has no need of any partner, rival or equal. They direct the believers to ask Him for guidance to the straight path, which is the true religion, to keep them on it in this life, and to bring them across the actual Ṣirāṭ, the bridge over Hell that everyone must cross on the Day of Judgment, to the gardens of delight in the company of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous. The surah encourages good deeds, so that one is gathered with the doers of good on that Day, and it warns against the roads of misguidance, so that one is not gathered with the sinners, those who earned anger and those who went astray.

Put another way, the surah opens with praise of Allah, then comes man's affirmation that he worships Allah alone and turns to Him alone in need, which is the oath of allegiance he offers his Lord and Master, and finally a prayer that covers every human need and goal. Several further lessons arise from it.

**How to ask Allah for anything.** Through its very structure the surah teaches how to pray. First discharge the duty of praising Allah. Then pledge complete allegiance, acknowledging that no one but Allah deserves adoration and worship and that no one but Allah has real power to help in distress or need. Then ask for what you want. There is every hope that a prayer made this way will be granted, as al-Jaṣṣāṣ notes in Aḥkām al-Qurʾān. The surah also suggests asking for something so comprehensive that it contains every human goal in essence, which is what the request for the straight path is: whoever follows the straight path in all that concerns this world and the next, his material life and his spiritual life, need not fear stumbling or being hurt.

**Praising Allah is a demand of human nature.** We praise someone either for a quality in him or for a favour from him, and the opening verse names neither, which implies that Allah's blessings are beyond counting: "If you try to count the blessings of Allah you will never number them" (14:34, and again 16:18). Leave everything else aside and consider only your own being. A human being is a world in miniature, holding in small everything the great world holds: his body answers to the earth, the hair on it to vegetation, his bones to the hills, the veins flowing with blood to the springs underground. He is made of spirit and body, the spirit by far the more valuable and the body its servant, and in the lesser part alone there are thousands of wonders. There are said to be more than three hundred joints, each made so strong that through sixty or seventy years of constant movement it needs no repair: "It is We who created them and We who made their joints strong" (76:28). Take the eye: a lifetime would not exhaust the divine wisdom in it. Take a single glance and count the blessings it needs. Inside, the eye needs physical energy, which comes from food, air and water. Outside, it needs the light of the sun, which depends on a thousand other things. All the forces of the universe combine so that the eye can see once, and then reckon how many times it sees in a day, a year, a lifetime, and that the ears, the tongue, the hands and the feet each set the forces of the whole universe in motion in the same way. Blessings of this kind are available equally to every living person, king or beggar. Indeed all the greatest blessings, air, water, light, the sun, the moon and the stars, everything in the heavens and the earth and between them, are the common property of every living creature. Then there are the special blessings that divine wisdom distributes unequally, wealth, honour, health, peace, knowledge and the rest. The general blessings are far more important and more essential to life than the special ones, yet man in his naivety takes them for granted and never realises what gifts they are. Human nature itself therefore requires that, in acknowledgment of the countless blessings descending on him every moment, man should praise his Benefactor and go on praising Him, as far as he is able, and to mark this basic need the Qur'an makes al-ḥamd the first word of its first surah. Praising Allah accordingly ranks very high among acts of worship. The Prophet ﷺ said that when a servant receives a blessing from Allah and says "Praise belongs to Allah", it is as though he has given something better than what he received, a hadith al-Qurṭubī cites from Ibn Mājah on the authority of Anas. Another hadith says that if a person received every blessing in the world and said "al-ḥamdu lillāh", the saying would outweigh them all, on which al-Qurṭubī remarks, citing scholars, that the very ability to say the phrase is itself a blessing of Allah. Another authentic hadith says that it fills half the scale of good deeds in the Balance. As for what praising Allah actually involves, Shaqīq ibn Ibrāhīm explained that on receiving a gift from Allah one should first recognise the Benefactor, then be content with what He has given, and then never disobey Him as long as one has strength in the body, which is itself His gift.

**Praise belongs to Him alone.** The lām of lillāh, "for Allah", marks exclusive possession: not only is it man's duty to praise Allah, but all praise really belongs to Him and no one else in the universe deserves it. At the same time, as a further kindness and to teach man how to behave with his fellows, Allah commanded him to thank those through whom His gifts reach him, for one who sees no need to thank his human benefactor will not thank Allah either. A created being may not praise himself: "Do not claim purity for yourselves. He knows best who is God-fearing" (53:32), which means a man can be praised only if he fears Allah, and only Allah knows how much taqwā any man has. Allah praises Himself because man cannot praise His glory and greatness as they deserve. The Prophet ﷺ himself exclaimed, "I cannot praise You as You should be praised," and so Allah taught man the manner of praising Him.

**Rabb is Allah's alone.** The word Rabb applies to one who not only owns a thing but is fully able to nurture it and responsible for doing so, and no one can be that for the whole universe but Allah. Used absolutely, the word is His alone, and it is not permissible to address or describe anyone else as Rabb. A hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim expressly forbids a slave or servant to call his master rabb. In a relative sense, in relation to a particular thing, the word may be used of a man, as in rabb ad-dār, the master of the house, as al-Qurṭubī notes.

**Worship and help are His alone.** Ibn ʿAbbās explained "You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help" to mean that one worships Allah alone and no one else and turns for help to Allah alone and no one else, as Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Ibn Jarīr record. Great scholars and saints of the Salaf, the earliest centuries, said that al-Fātiḥah is the secret, the gist, of the whole Qur'an and that this verse is the secret of the surah, because its first sentence declares one free of shirk and its second declares one free of trust in one's own power and will, which leads naturally to placing oneself in Allah's hands in everything. "Worship Him and put your trust in Him" (11:123), "Say: He is the Most Compassionate, we believe in Him and in Him we put our trust" (67:29), "Lord of the east and the west, there is no god but He, so take Him as guardian" (73:9): all of these say that a true Muslim, in whatever he undertakes, relies neither on his own faculties nor on a fellow creature but entrusts himself wholly to Allah, who alone is all-powerful and alone the absolute helper.

Two points of doctrine follow. First, worshipping anyone but Allah is absolutely forbidden, and associating anyone with Him in worship is a deadly and unforgivable sin. Since worship is the utmost humility and willing self-abasement out of the deepest love and reverence, behaving so towards any created being is shirk. It follows that shirk is not only ascribing divine power to figures of stone or metal as idolaters do. To obey, love or revere someone to the degree reserved for Allah is also open association, ash-shirk al-jalī. Describing the shirk of the Jews and Christians, the Qur'an says, "They have taken their rabbis and monks as lords besides Allah" (9:31). ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, who had been a Christian, asked the Prophet ﷺ about this verse: why blame the Christians for taking their scholars as lords when they never worshipped them? The Prophet ﷺ asked whether it was not true that their scholars had declared forbidden many things Allah had permitted and permitted things He had forbidden, and that the Christians obeyed them in both. ʿAdī agreed, and the Prophet ﷺ said that this was exactly how they worshipped them. Allah alone has the right to decide what is lawful and what is forbidden, and whoever, knowing the divine rulings on ḥalāl and ḥarām, goes against them in the belief that someone other than Allah can also demand obedience in these matters is in effect worshipping that person and committing shirk. This verse in no way applies to ordinary Muslims who, not being qualified to understand the Qur'an and the Sunnah on their own or to derive rulings from them, depend on an imam, a mujtahid or a scholar and follow his instruction. They are acting on the Qur'an and the Sunnah, which say, "Ask the people of knowledge if you do not know" (16:43). Votive offerings to anyone but Allah are also shirk, and so is praying to anyone else in need or distress, since a hadith says that supplication is itself worship. So too is adopting the practices generally recognised as signs or symbols of association. When ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim came to the Prophet ﷺ on accepting Islam with a cross hanging round his neck, he was told to remove that idol, although by then the cross did not mean to him what it means to Christians: even the outward symbol was to be put away. Rukūʿ or sajdah, bowing or prostrating, to anyone but Allah, and going round a person or thing in the manner of the ṭawāf of the Kaʿbah, are among these symbols, and avoiding all of them is part of the pledge made in "You alone we worship".

Second, help is to be sought from Allah alone, and this needs explaining. There is a kind of help that everyone seeks from others and must seek, given the physical order of the universe: the tailor, the tinker, the carpenter and the blacksmith each serve others and everyone needs their help, and no religion forbids this or could, since it is part of the web of physical means Allah has provided. Among non-physical means it is likewise permissible to ask a prophet or a saint to pray to Allah on one's behalf, or to mention a prophet or saint as a means, a wasīlah, when praying directly to Allah, to draw His mercy on oneself. Explicit hadiths and the indications of the Qur'an justify this, and it would be wrong to call it forbidden or to count it as shirk. When, then, does asking someone other than Allah for help become shirk? In two cases. The first is asking an angel, a prophet, a saint or any creature for help in the belief that he is all-powerful like Allah, a heresy so plain that even idolaters generally recognise it as such, for even they do not regard their gods as all-powerful like Allah. The second is the idolaters' own position: admitting that Allah alone is all-powerful but believing that He has delegated part of His power to an angel, a prophet, a saint or a lesser god, who exercises full and independent authority in that area and may be asked for help within it. This is the asking the Qur'an forbids and warns against in "You alone we ask for help".

The confusion has a simple source. Allah appoints many angels to carry out many tasks even in the physical order, He brings about through prophets things beyond human power, called miracles, and through saints wonders called karāmāt. A careless observer takes the appearance for the reality and concludes that angels, prophets and saints could not have worked such things unless Allah had given them the power and authority to do so, and from this illusion grows the belief that prophets and saints hold, each in his own degree, an absolute power. They do not. Miracles and wonders are Allah's direct acts, shown through prophets and saints so that people may recognise their spiritual station, and the prophets and saints themselves have no power to produce them. Many verses say so. "When you threw, it was not you who threw, but Allah threw" (8:17) speaks of the Prophet's ﷺ miracle of throwing a handful of pebbles at an enemy army, which Allah made strike the eyes of the whole army, and attributes the throwing not to him but to Allah. When the people of Nūḥ (Noah) demanded that he bring Allah's punishment down on them to prove himself, he said, "Allah will bring it on you if He wills" (11:33), admitting that he could not do it as a miracle of his own. A group of prophets told their peoples, "We cannot bring you proof except by Allah's leave" (14:11), another admission that no miracle was in their power, since all power is in Allah's hand. A prophet or saint cannot show whatever miracle he likes whenever he likes. The disbelievers demanded specific miracles from the Prophet ﷺ and from earlier prophets, and Allah showed only the ones He chose. An ordinary example makes it clear. The lamp in your room gives you light and the fan gives you air, but neither has any power of its own to do so. They need the current from the power station, without which they do nothing, so that giving light and air is really the work of the current, not of the lamp and the fan. In the same way saints, prophets and angels depend on Allah in all they do. It is His power and will that make things happen, though it shows through prophets and saints as the current shows through lamps and fans. The example also shows that, although they have no power of their own, their presence is not irrelevant: you cannot have light and air in the room without a lamp and a fan, and you cannot have miracles and wonders without a prophet or a saint. There is one difference. With the wiring intact you still cannot have light without a lamp, whereas Allah can show miracles without the medium of prophets and saints if He wills. But His usual way is not to, for otherwise miracles would not serve their purpose. So one must hold firmly that everything that happens is brought about by Allah's power and will, and one must also recognise the need for prophets and saints and admit their importance. Without that, one will neither obey the divine commands properly nor win Allah's pleasure, like the man who, ignorant of the worth of lamps and fans, disregards them and goes without light and air. These problems perplex many minds, and the answer is simple. Taking prophets and saints as a wasīlah for drawing Allah's mercy on oneself is neither absolutely permitted nor absolutely forbidden. There is a condition. If one does it believing a prophet or saint to be all-powerful, it is shirk and forbidden. If one regards him simply as a means, it is permitted. People tend to one of two extremes here, outright rejection of wasīlah or exaggerated veneration, and the truth lies between them.

**Success here and hereafter.** Of all prayers, the one the Qur'an recommends to every person, in every situation and for everything he does, is this prayer for the straight path. Success in the Hereafter depends on the straight path that leads to Paradise, and on reflection success in every worldly matter also depends on keeping to a straight path, that is, on using the means and methods that habitually lead to one's goal, and failure is always the result of straying from it. Since the straight path is needed in this world and the next, this is the prayer that should be constantly on a Muslim's lips and in his heart, never as empty words but with a sincere intention and with the meaning fully present in the mind.

### The Companions of the Cave, and a prayer

In the inner reading, the path of those Allah has blessed has been called the road and the journey of the Companions of the Cave. The believers are saying: "Lord, complete our road for us without us, as You were bountiful to the Companions of the Cave and laid Your caress on them, seated them on the cushion of intimacy and Yourself undertook to draw them on, saying, in effect, go into this cave and sleep well, for We have counted your sleep as the worship of the people of the world. Lord, give us a share of that blessing and that caress. As Your bounty finished their work without them, let Your bounty finish ours without us. Whatever we do only loses us something, and whatever You do is the foundation of honour in both worlds." One of the masters of the Path said, "O Allah, we cannot finish this work without You, and we would not dare to finish it apart from You. Whenever we imagine we have arrived, we fall back into the bewilderment of our reckoning." He went on to ask where he would find again the day when Allah was his and he himself was not, saying that until that day came again he would live in fire and smoke, that to find it again in either world would be gain enough, and that if he found Allah's being for himself he would be content with his own nonbeing. And of the verse's last words: Lord, do not make us among those You have turned over to themselves, who have been wounded by the sword of separation and fastened with the nail of rejection. What load can a broken rope pull? And what good is the effort of an unworthy servant who lives estranged, who has fallen off the road today and takes the crooked road for the straight one?

The reciters of old closed the surah with words that fit it: Allah knows best what is right, to Him is the return and the final resort, Allah is sufficient for us and an excellent Guardian, and there is no power and no strength except with Allah, the High, the Tremendous. May Allah bless our master Muhammad ﷺ, his family and his Companions, and grant them everlasting peace.

### In short

- The straight path is defined by its travellers, the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, and by the two roads beside it: those under anger, who knew the truth and refused it, and those astray, who lost it through ignorance and excess. The Prophet ﷺ named the Jews and the Christians as the examples of each.
- It is the road between deficiency and excess, and because a book alone cannot train a human being, Allah pointed to people rather than to a book. The Book of Allah and the men of Allah are both needed, the men measured by the Book.
- Islam and the Sunnah go together. There is no straightness in religion until the two are joined.
- Grace is attributed to Allah and anger and straying are left to those who earned them, though all guidance and misguidance are from Him.
- Say āmīn when the surah ends. Whoever's āmīn coincides with the angels' is forgiven, and it is one of the things the Jews most envy the Muslims.
- The surah as a whole teaches how to pray, praise first and pledge second and request third, and its request for the straight path is the one prayer that covers success in this world and the next.
