<<<VERSE 3:154>>>
> “Then after distress, He sent down serenity in the form of drowsiness overcoming some of you, while others were disturbed by evil thoughts about Allah—the thoughts of ˹pre-Islamic˺ ignorance. They ask, “Do we have a say in the matter?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “All matters are destined by Allah.” They conceal in their hearts what they do not reveal to you. They say ˹to themselves˺, “If we had any say in the matter, none of us would have come to die here.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Even if you were to remain in your homes, those among you who were destined to be killed would have met the same fate.” Through this, Allah tests what is within you and purifies what is in your hearts. And Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart” (3:154)

After the shock of Uhud, Allah sent calm to some believers, while others remained anxious and questioned the wisdom of going out to battle. The verse contrasts trust with suspicion, affirms that life and death are under Allah’s decree, and explains that the event tested and purified the community.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Then after distress, He sent down serenity”** describes Allah’s care for the believers after the fighting and retreat. The relief was not merely an explanation of events. It affected them inwardly and helped them regain composure when they were exhausted and afraid.

**“in the form of drowsiness overcoming some of you”** refers to a sleepiness that came over a group of believers while they were still in a vulnerable situation. Abu Talhah reports that he was among those overtaken by sleep at Uhud. His sword fell from his hand more than once as he nodded off, and he picked it up again. Anas transmits his account. Al-Bukhari, At-Tirmidhi, and Al-Hakim record reports about this moment, with At-Tirmidhi describing one report as hasan sahih.

The drowsiness was a sign of reassurance, not carelessness. The companions had just experienced battle, fear, and confusion. A similar sleep is mentioned in connection with Badr in Surah al-Anfal, when Allah sent slumber as security (8:11). The verse also recalls that Allah’s help can calm the body as well as strengthen the heart.

**“while others were disturbed by evil thoughts about Allah”** describes another group whose fear did not settle. They worried about themselves and suspected that Allah would not help His Messenger or the believers. The Qur’an calls this **“the thoughts of pre-Islamic ignorance”** because it reflects an outlook without trust in Allah’s wisdom or promise.

A similar false suspicion appears in a different crisis, when some people thought wrongly about the Messenger and the believers (48:12). The verse does not say that every anxious thought is disbelief. It criticizes the settled accusation that Allah’s plan was unjust or that His support had failed.

**“They ask, ‘Do we have a say in the matter?’”** expresses the question of those who believed they should have controlled whether the community went out to Uhud. They had opposed the decision to meet the Quraysh outside Madinah and later claimed that if they had been in charge, the deaths would not have happened.

**“Say, ‘All matters are destined by Allah’”** means the final outcome is in Allah’s control. This does not erase human choice or responsibility. The believers had real choices: to obey the Prophet’s order, remain at their post, or leave it. Allah’s decree encompasses the outcome, while people remain accountable for their actions.

**“They conceal in their hearts what they do not reveal to you”** means their public words did not disclose the full extent of their suspicion. They feared that their private thought would be exposed or rejected. Allah knows what is concealed in every heart, including confidence, fear, and hypocrisy.

**“If we had any say in the matter, none of us would have come to die here”** is their claim that staying in Madinah would have prevented the deaths. The response does not mean that planning and safety are useless. It means no one can escape an appointed death simply by imagining a different human decision. The believer takes precautions without claiming control over what Allah has decreed.

**“Even if you were to remain in your homes, those among you who were destined to be killed would have met the same fate”** answers the boast of perfect foresight. The verse teaches that an appointed death will occur, but it does not tell people to expose themselves to danger. Muslims are still required to protect life, plan wisely, and obey sound guidance.

**“Through this, Allah tests what is within you and purifies what is in your hearts”** means the ordeal made inner qualities evident. The believers were tested in trust and obedience. The event also brought hidden doubts into the open so that they could be confronted. The test was not because Allah needed new knowledge.

**“And Allah knows best what is hidden in the heart”** closes with His complete knowledge. People may conceal fear or suspicion from one another, but nothing is concealed from Allah. He knows the believers’ sincere repentance and the doubts of those who sought only their own safety.

### Two responses to the same crisis

The believers who slept felt safe enough to rest, even with weapons in hand. The anxious group remained preoccupied with its own survival and interpreted the setback as proof that the mission had failed. The verse shows that the same external event can reveal very different inner responses.

A person should not imitate the false certainty of those who claim that avoiding every risk would have prevented all loss. Nor should they use divine decree to excuse a careless decision. The lesson holds both truths together: Allah controls the final outcome, and people answer for the choices they make.

The phrase “all matters are destined by Allah” therefore comforts the bereaved without abolishing responsibility. The companions could mourn, learn from the strategic error, and continue their duties. The destiny of life and death was not theirs to command, but obedience and careful planning remained within their responsibility.

Ibn Ishaq transmits a report through Abdullah ibn az-Zubayr from Az-Zubayr about hearing Muʿattib ibn Qushayr say that if the companions had controlled the affair, none would have died at Uhud. Ibn Abi Hatim also preserves this report. It illustrates the private suspicion described in the verse, and why the claim that one could have avoided every death was rebuked. Allah alone knows the full meaning of events, including the unseen state of each person’s ruh, or spirit.

Zajjaj offers a related explanation of the next verse: an earlier sin may give Satan an opening to encourage a later misstep. The spiritual meanings, al-maʿani, of the passage therefore include both accountability and hope. The believers’ jihad at Uhud was not meaningless, and their moment of fear did not make them beyond forgiveness. A related report about Hatib ibn Abi Bilta'ah concerns his letter to Quraysh in Makkah and the Prophet’s judgment of his intention. It prepares the reader to distinguish a sincere believer’s lapse from settled hypocrisy.

### In short

- Allah sent calm to some believers after Uhud, while others remained anxious and suspicious.
- Allah’s decree governs life and death, but people remain responsible for their choices.
- The trial brought hidden intentions to light, and Allah knows every heart.

<<<VERSE 3:155>>>
> “Indeed, those ˹believers˺ who fled on the day when the two armies met were made to slip by Satan because of their misdeeds. But Allah has pardoned them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing” (3:155)

The verse acknowledges that some believers fled during the battle of Uhud and says Satan made them slip because of earlier wrongdoing. It also states plainly that Allah pardoned them. The verse corrects the mistake without declaring that the companions had left Islam.

### Phrase by phrase

**“those believers who fled on the day when the two armies met”** refers to companions who retreated when the Muslim lines broke and the rumor spread that the Prophet ﷺ had been killed. The word “believers” matters. Their flight was a serious lapse under pressure, but the verse does not describe them as apostates or hypocrites.

The event followed the archers’ departure from their assigned position. Some companions left the hill after seeing the Quraysh retreat and went to collect spoils. When the cavalry attacked the exposed rear, the Muslim force became confused. Some fled toward Madinah or up Mount Uhud, while others stayed and defended the Prophet. The verse concerns those who turned away in the moment of battle. It does not erase the courage of those who remained.

**“were made to slip by Satan”** means Satan exploited their fear and prior mistakes, encouraging them to retreat and lose focus. Satan’s influence did not remove their responsibility. The verse says it happened **“because of their misdeeds”**: earlier faults had opened a way for further error. The Salaf taught that a good deed often leads to another good deed, while sin can make a later sin easier. This is a warning to address wrongdoing early and ask Allah for protection.

The verse does not say that Satan controls people or that every fear is a sin. A human being may feel frightened without choosing to abandon a duty. The companions’ actions occurred in the shock of a real battle, and the Qur’an’s direct response is both correction and pardon.

**“But Allah has pardoned them”** is explicit. Allah forgave the companions who fled. This pardon protects them from being treated as permanent traitors or unbelievers. It also teaches believers not to mock someone for an error Allah has already forgiven.

A report says Abdullah ibn Umar heard someone criticize Uthman al-Ghani and other companions for fleeing at Uhud. Ibn Umar replied that no one may criticize what Allah has expressly forgiven. Al-Bukhari records the statement. Uthman’s later answer to Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf also recalled that he had not fled at Uhud, had stayed behind at Badr to care for Ruqayyah, the Prophet’s daughter, and had not abandoned the Sunnah of Umar. He had received a share of the Badr spoils because the Prophet granted it to him. The exchange distinguishes a momentary error from a person’s whole record of service.

The verse is also read alongside the account of Hatib ibn Abi Bilta'ah, who sent information to Quraysh in Makkah before the Muslim entry. Umar asked permission to punish him, but the Prophet ﷺ explained that Hatib was a veteran of Badr and had acted out of a personal fear, not hypocrisy. The Prophet forgave him. Like the companions at Uhud, Hatib’s mistake was not concealed, but his sincere faith and prior service were taken into account.

**“Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing”** means Allah pardons sins and does not hasten to punish His servants. Forbearance gives people room to repent and correct themselves. It does not make disobedience harmless. The companions were admonished, then forgiven, and expected to learn from the event.

### Respecting the companions without denying their mistakes

The verse establishes a balanced approach. Companions were human and could make mistakes. The Qur’an itself names the error at Uhud. Yet Allah’s pardon means later Muslims should not use that lapse to abuse them or erase their service. Sunni tradition, including scholars such as Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn Humam, emphasizes this principle of honoring the companions and avoiding derogatory speech about them. Respect does not require pretending that no error occurred.

The term Ahl al-Sunnah wa al-Jamaʿah refers to those who adhere to the Prophet’s Sunnah and the community of his companions. In this understanding, it is wajib to speak of the companions with fairness and avoid mockery. The Qur’anic declaration of forgiveness is stronger than later accusations based on unreliable or selective reports. When historical accounts differ, they should be assessed carefully rather than used to fuel hostility.

The account of Hatib is another reminder that the Prophet distinguished between hypocrisy and a sincere person’s lapse. Umar’s anger was understandable, but the Prophet knew the circumstances and judged with knowledge. The community should be cautious about claiming to know another person’s inner state. Allah knows the heart, and He is the Most Forbearing.

Imam Ahmad records a report in which Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf answers al-Walid ibn Uqbah’s accusation that Uthman ibn Affan had fled at Uhud. Uthman points out that Allah had already forgiven the companions who fled, and explains that his absence from Badr was because he was caring for Ruqayyah, the Prophet’s daughter. The Prophet had granted him a share of the spoils. This report warns against using an old, forgiven mistake as a weapon in later disputes.

Zajjaj’s explanation of Satan’s role is that a sin may open the way to another, just as a good deed can lead to further good. This is a warning for all believers, not a claim that the companions were uniquely corrupt. The Ahl al-Sunnah wa al-Jamaʿah tradition therefore joins honest acknowledgement of the Uhud error with respect for the companions. Some creed manuals, including al-ʿAqidatul-Nasafiyah and Sharh al-Musamarah, express the duty to speak of them fairly. Ibn Taymiyyah likewise discusses avoiding mockery and relying on Allah’s pardon. Such respect is wajib, obligatory, and does not require denying the Qur’an’s correction.

The report about Hatib also involved a message sent to Quraysh in Makkah before the Muslim entry. It reminds the reader that the Prophet judged the person’s intention and history, not simply the outward appearance of the act. His decision to forgive Hatib, a veteran of Badr, shows that a grave mistake may be followed by sincere repentance and pardon. Al-Bukhari preserves the account.

### In short

- Some believers fled at Uhud after Satan exploited their fear and prior mistakes.
- Allah explicitly pardoned them, while still correcting their conduct.
- The verse calls for respect and fairness toward the companions, without denying their human errors.
