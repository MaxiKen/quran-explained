<<<VERSE 5:103>>>
Four pre-Islamic customs are named and denied: "Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels." Allah did not institute any of them. The verse then identifies what really happened, which is that lies were invented against Allah, and that most of those who practised them had no understanding.

The four terms are explained differently in the reports, and the differences are worth keeping, because each describes a distinct custom involving a distinct animal and condition. One account writes the four names as Bahīra, Sā’iba, Wasīla and Hām.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah"** denies the first practice. Al-Bukhari records Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib, also spelled al-Musayyab and al-Musaiyyib, describing the bahirah as a she camel whose milk was reserved for the idols and which no one was permitted to milk. Muslim and An-Nasa'i record the same report.

Ibn 'Abbas, in the report given by 'Ali bin Abi Talhah, describes it as a she camel that has delivered five times. They then examined the fifth delivery. If it was male they slaughtered it and gave it to the men alone, not to the women. If it was female they cut off its ears and announced that it was a bahirah which nobody might milk. As-Suddi and others give something similar.

Another account says that when a she camel delivered a male at its fifth birth, that male was sacrificed and both men and women ate from it, but if it delivered a female they split its ear. Her milk and benefits then belonged to the men alone, and when she died both men and women shared her meat.

A further explanation says the bahirah was the animal whose ears were cut, whose wool, hair and milk a man would not allow his wife, daughters or household to use, but which they shared if it died. Another describes it as a she camel whose ear is cut after it has given birth a certain number of times.

**"sâ'ibah"** is the second. Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib describes it as a she camel turned loose to pasture freely for the idols, on which nothing was allowed to be carried.

Mujahid applies the term to sheep and gives a meaning like that of the bahirah, describing a ewe that delivers six females and then a male, a female or two males, after which they slaughter the newborn and feed its meat to the men and not the women.

Muhammad bin Ishaq describes the sa'ibah as a she camel that delivers ten females with no male between them. It was then set free, and nobody might ride it, shear it or milk it, except a guest.

Abu Rawq gives a different origin. When a man went out on some business and succeeded in what he intended, he designated a sa'ibah from his property, a she camel or another animal, and set it free for the idols in gratitude. Whatever it later gave birth to was set free for the idols as well.

As-Suddi says the same in broader terms. When a man's affair succeeded, or he was cured of an illness, or his wealth increased, he set part of his wealth free for the idols, and whoever tried to take any of that sa'ibah property was punished in this world.

Another account describes people handing over whatever cattle they chose to the custodians of the idols, who offered it to passing travellers, to the men and not the women. The animals offered were male and never female, and when they died both men and women shared the meat. A further gloss describes it as an animal released in the name of the idols, like a bull let loose in some rural areas today.

**"waṣîlah"** is the third. Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib describes it as a she camel set free for the idols because she bore a female at her first delivery and another female at the second. They freed such a camel when she produced two females with no male between them.

Through Az-Zuhri, Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib explains that they called her a wasilah because she had connected two females with no male between, and that they cut off her ears and let her roam to pasture for their idols. A similar explanation is reported from Malik bin Anas.

Ibn 'Abbas applies the term to sheep. He describes a ewe that gives birth seven times. If the seventh delivery was a stillborn male or female, the men ate from it and not the women. If she bore a female, or a female and a male, they set them free, saying of the male that his sister had connected him to being forbidden to them. Ibn Abi Hatim records the statement.

Muhammad bin Ishaq describes the wasilah sheep as a ewe bearing ten females in five deliveries, two females at each. She was called wasilah and set free. Whatever she bore afterward, male or female, was given to the men and not the women, but if it was stillborn, men and women shared it.

Another account describes checking the seventh birth. A male was sacrificed and its meat shared by men and women. A female could not be used by women until she died, after which both shared her meat. Twins, male and female, were spared for the men alone until death, and then both shared the meat. A further gloss describes a sheep that had borne six offspring and, at the seventh delivery, had its ears and horns cut with the announcement that it had connected the deliveries, after which it was not slaughtered, struck or barred from any watering place.

**"and ḥâm camels."** is the fourth. Al-'Awfi reports Ibn 'Abbas saying that when a man's camel had performed ten copulations they called it a ham and set it free. Abu Rawq and Qatadah report something similar.

In another report Ibn 'Abbas describes the ham as a male camel whose offspring had themselves produced offspring. They would announce that this camel had protected its back, so nothing might be carried on it, its wool might not be cut, and it might not be prevented from grazing where it liked or drinking from any pool, even a pool that did not belong to its owner.

Ibn Wahb reports hearing Malik say that the ham is a male camel assigned a set number of copulations, and that when it had completed them, peacock feathers were placed on it and it was set free. Other opinions are also mentioned, including a description of a stud male that mates and produces a given number of offspring, and an account of an animal left entirely alone, not ridden or used for carrying, not barred from any water or pasture or from any she camel that came its way, whose meat was shared by men and women when it grew old or died.

The word used for the fourth is also given in the form of one who is protected, and the explanations differ because these were customs rather than fixed legal terms. It is possible that each word was applied to a different situation.

### Who began these practices?

Abu Hurayrah reports the Messenger ﷺ saying that he saw 'Amr bin 'Amir al-Khuza'i dragging his intestines behind him in the Fire, and that he was the first to begin the practice of the sa'ibah. Ahmad records 'Abdullah bin Mas'ud reporting the same, naming Abu Khuza'ah, 'Amr bin 'Amir, as the first to begin the sa'ibah and the worship of idols.

The 'Amr named is the son of Luhay bin Qam'ah, one of the chiefs of Khuza'ah, the tribe that looked after the House of Allah between Jurhum and the Prophet's ﷺ own tribe of Quraysh. He was the first to alter the religion of Ibrahim, al-Khalil, in Makkah, and to bring idol worship into the Hijaz, the western region of Arabia.

He called simple people to worship idols and offer sacrifice to them, and he began these ignorant rituals concerning animals along with other practices of the period of ignorance. One gloss identifies those who invented lies against Allah as 'Amr ibn Luhayy and his followers. Allah speaks in Al-An'am of people assigning to Allah a share of the tillage and cattle He created (3:136).

### What was wrong with these customs?

Beyond the shirk involved, they challenged a right Allah had given. He had made the meat, milk and use of these animals halal, and they imposed restrictions of their own on what was halal and what was haram, as though the office of making divine law belonged to them.

The bitter irony is that they treated these polytheistic customs as the means of gaining Allah's pleasure and nearness. The answer given is that Allah never appointed them. Their elders invented lies against Him, and the ignorant masses accepted them.

Allah did not legislate these invented rituals and does not regard them as acts of obedience. The idolaters turned them into rituals and acts of worship by which to draw near to Allah, and they did not and will not help anyone draw near Him. Such innovations harm only those who adopt them.

Most of them are described as having no understanding, which one gloss extends to all of them, meaning that they had no sense of Allah's command, of what He made lawful and of what He made unlawful. Another says they did not grasp that this was a lie, since they were merely following the example of their forefathers. Another describes them as unable to tell truth from falsehood, or the lawful from the unlawful.

### What does the two-fold warning amount to?

The passage gives a paired warning. It is a crime to bring hardship into divine injunctions by asking pointless questions, which the following verses address. It is a far more serious crime to declare things lawful or unlawful on the basis of personal opinion and desire, without the will and command of the Lawgiver.

A brief gloss describes the four as categories of domestic animals which the pre-Islamic Arabs dedicated to their deities. The verse denies that Allah ordained any of them.

### What did the Prophet ﷺ say to a man practising it?

Ibn Abi Hatim records it through Abu Ishaq As-Subay'i, who gives it from Al-Ahwas al-Jushami on the authority of his father Malik bin Nadlah, and Malik said that he came to the Prophet ﷺ wearing old clothes. Asked whether he had property, he said yes, and named camels, sheep, horses and slaves.

He was told that if Allah gives a person wealth, its effect should be visible on him. The Prophet ﷺ then asked whether his camels delivered calves with entire ears, and he answered that they did, asking whether camels ever give birth to anything but whole calves.

He was then asked whether he took a knife and cut off the ears of some of them, calling one a bahirah, and tore the ears of others, declaring them sacred. He said that he did. He was told not to do that, since all the wealth Allah has given a person is permitted to him, and the verse was recited.

The explanations of the four terms appear as an addition in that narration. In another version, from Abu Ishaq through Abu Al-Ahwas, 'Awf bin Malik gives the explanations in his own words rather than as part of the hadith, and this is described as the sounder form. Ahmad records the hadith on a chain running up from Malik bin Nadlah through his son Abu Al-Ahwas 'Awf bin Malik, then Abu Az-Za'ra' 'Amr bin 'Amr, and so to Sufyan bin 'Uyaynah, and that narration does not contain the added explanations.

### In short

- Four customs are denied, and the reports explain each with differing details that are preserved rather than merged.
- The bahirah, sa'ibah, wasilah and ham were animals dedicated to idols by condition of births, milk, ears or copulations.
- 'Amr ibn Luhayy is named as the first to begin the sa'ibah and idol worship, altering the religion of Ibrahim in Makkah.
- The wrong was twofold, shirk and the usurping of Allah's right to declare things lawful and unlawful.
- The Prophet ﷺ told a man practising it that all the wealth Allah gives is permitted to him.

<<<VERSE 5:104>>>
The reply of those addressed is quoted: “What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us.” The verse answers with a question rather than an insult, asking whether that would still be enough if their forefathers knew nothing and had no guidance.

The form of the answer is itself instructive. It states a principle about following anyone, and it does so without provoking the hearer.

### Phrase by phrase

**"When it is said to them, “Come to Allah’s revelations and to the Messenger,”"** describes being summoned to the religion, law and commands of Allah and to keeping away from what He forbade. One gloss sets the scene with the Prophet ﷺ addressing the idolaters of Makkah, calling them to treat as lawful what Allah clarified in the Qur'an and what the Messenger ﷺ showed them to be lawful. Another describes it as a call to the Qur'an and to the path of the Messenger ﷺ so that they may learn what is lawful and unlawful.

**"they reply, “What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us.”"** gives their only answer. The practices and ways they found their fathers and forefathers following are, they say, sufficient for them in religion and law. One gloss renders their words as claiming that the prohibition they inherited is enough for them.

**"˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance"** supplies the answer's weakness. Their fathers did not understand or recognise the truth and did not find its way. One gloss specifies ignorance of Allah's oneness and religion, and absence of guidance to the practice of any prophet.

The interrogative is meant as a disavowal. Another reading puts it as a plain question: were their fathers not wholly ignorant of the religion and unguided to the practice of the Prophet ﷺ, and how then can their example be followed?

The conclusion drawn is sharp. Only someone more ignorant and more astray than those forefathers would follow them.

### What lay behind this answer?

Following forefathers was one of many customs of the period of ignorance that involved people in every kind of evil and deprived them of every kind of virtue. As reported in Tafsir al-Durr al-Manthur from Ibn Abi Hatim, a fortunate person who listened to the truth and embraced Islam was shamed on the grounds that he had shown his forefathers to be worthless, since he chose another way at the cost of theirs.

That compounded error is what the verse answers. It is described as the master argument of Satan, which brought millions into error although they had at least average sense, knowledge and skill.

The Qur'an's one sentence gives a correct principle for following any person, group or party. It is a principle that gives eyes to the blind and the real shape of things to the ignorant and the inattentive.

### When does following make sense?

It makes sense for those who do not know to follow those who do, for the uninitiated to follow the experienced and for the ignorant to follow the learned. What makes no sense is to step aside from the measures of knowledge, reason and direction and settle on inherited practice as the thing to be preferred, without knowing where that leader stands or where he will lead, while feeling obliged to trail along behind him.

Some people take a crowd as the measure of right and wrong and move wherever they see many people going. That also makes no sense. The greater number of people anywhere is never made up of the wisest, the cleverest or the best in conduct. Following a crowd cannot be the test that separates truth from falsehood or good from bad.

### What are the two criteria of leadership?

The sentence gives two clear criteria for choosing a leader: knowledge and guidance. Knowledge means knowing the desired destination and the methods of reaching it. Guidance means actually travelling toward the chosen objective. Together they mean constant effort resting on sound knowledge.

Before choosing a leader a person must therefore establish whether he is fully acquainted with the objectives and methods to be followed. He must then see whether that person is himself travelling the same path in the same direction. He must also determine whether his conduct matches his knowledge.

A leader must be tried against reliable knowledge and consistent behaviour. None of the other options, whether ancestry and lineage, being followed by masses, wealth in money or property, or power and authority, deserves to be treated as a criterion of leadership in the real sense.

### Why is following an undeserving leader so dangerous?

The correct attitude is that a person first determines the aim of his life and the direction of his journey. He then looks for someone who knows that direction and is himself travelling along it. Following such a person can take him all the way to his destination.

This is the reality underlying the following of the mujtahid imams. They know the religion and they practise it, so those who do not know can, by following them, attain the main purpose of religion, which is obedience to Allah and His Messenger ﷺ and adherence to their commands.

As for someone already astray, who does not know the destination or who knowingly travels in the opposite direction, following him is a waste of effort and deeds in the sight of every wise person. It amounts to writing out one's own sentence of ruin.

It is a pity that in the present age of so-called knowledge and enlightenment even educated and far-sighted people overlook the fact that the greatest cause of upheaval in the world is blind adherence to leaders who are incompetent, undeserving and false.

### How does the verse criticise without insulting?

The Qur'an points out the error of people who follow ancestral custom blindly, and at the same time shows an effective method for identifying another person's error without hurting or provoking him.

The remark in the text is not frontal. Nowhere does it state that those forefathers lacked knowledge or had gone astray. It uses a subtle interrogative style, asking whether following one's forefathers could be reasonable in a case where they had neither knowledge nor guidance.

That method leaves the hearer to draw the conclusion himself. It is the same method the verse uses, and it is why the answer is a question.

### What happens to elders over time?

The earlier leaders of a community acquire a holy status after a certain passage of time. Many wrong practices then take place in the name of those long dead people. If they had established a custom of venerating sheep and camels, their successors would continue it without giving it a thought.

Misguided behaviour resting on a tradition of holiness takes root so firmly and deeply that it is very hard to dissuade people from it. Rising above such psychological complications is possible only when a person acquires a firm belief, in the real sense, that he must finally appear before Allah.

Whoever holds that belief accepts today what he will be compelled to accept after death. Acceptance at that later time will be of no use to anyone.

### In short

- The call is to revelation and to the Messenger ﷺ, and the reply is that inherited practice is enough.
- The verse answers with a question about knowledge and guidance rather than with a direct insult.
- Following makes sense only when the followed person knows the way and is travelling it.
- Ancestry, crowds, wealth and power are not criteria of leadership, and following an undeserving leader is self-destruction.
- Elders acquire holiness with time, and only firm belief in appearing before Allah breaks that hold.

<<<VERSE 5:105>>>
The believers are addressed about the limits of their responsibility: "You are accountable only for yourselves. It will not harm you if someone chooses to deviate—as long as you are ˹rightly˺ guided." The condition matters as much as the promise, and the verse ends with the return to Allah.

The apparent sense has been misunderstood, and the reports that correct that misunderstanding belong to the explanation of this verse.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! You are accountable only for yourselves."** is a command to take care of themselves, to preserve their own souls and to do what serves their best interest. Allah directs His believing servants to set themselves right and to perform as much righteousness as they can.

**"It will not harm you if someone chooses to deviate—as long as you are ˹rightly˺ guided."** promises that whoever reforms himself will not be harmed by the wickedness of the wicked, whether they are his relatives or not. One gloss describes the error of one who errs as unable to injure a person who is rightly guided to faith and who further shows the erring person his error. Another restricts it to the misguided among the People of the Scripture.

**"To Allah you will all return, and He will inform you of what you used to do"** closes the verse. The return is after death, and Allah will tell people what they used to do and say, of good and evil, and will requite them for it.

### What was the occasion?

One account says the verse was revealed about the idolaters of Makkah, at the time the Prophet ﷺ took the capitation tax from the People of the Book and afterwards would not take it from them. That story is described as already mentioned in Al-Baqarah.

### Why did Abu Bakr correct the way people read it?

Ahmad records Qays saying that Abu Bakr As-Siddiq stood, thanked and praised Allah, and then told the people that they recite this verse and explain it in the wrong way.

He said that he had heard the Messenger ﷺ say that when people witness evil and do not change it, Allah is about to send a punishment that will encompass them. Qays also heard Abu Bakr tell the people to beware of lying, because lying contradicts faith.

Sensing the doubt produced by a surface reading, Abu Bakr said in a khutbah that people recite this verse and use it out of context, as though commanding what is fair were not required. He insisted that he had himself heard the Prophet ﷺ say that whoever watches a sin being done and makes no effort, so far as he is able, to prevent it is likely to be seized by divine punishment along with the actual sinners.

At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah carry that narration. In Abu Dawud the words are that those who see an oppressor oppressing and do not stop him, to the best of their ability, will find that Allah seizes everyone in punishment.

### How do the scholars read the condition?

When this verse was revealed some people had doubts, and the Prophet ﷺ clarified that it did not contradict the injunctions about commanding what is fair. If they abandoned that mission they would be answerable for it.

Sa'id ibn Jubayr is reported in al-Bahr al-Muhit as saying that a person should keep doing what his religion obliges, which includes jihad and commanding what is fair, and that if, after doing all this, those who remain astray can bring no loss on him, the verse applies. Reflection on the words if you are rightly guided makes the point clearer, since the converse shows that whoever has abandoned the duty of commanding what is fair is plainly not rightly guided.

Being on the right path therefore includes encouraging good and forbidding evil. Only then does the deviation of others cease to harm a person.

A hadith of Abu Tha'laba al-Khushani, reported by al-Hakim and others, gives the same balance. He asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about this verse and was told to enjoin decency on one another and forbid indecency to one another, and that if a person then sees stinginess obeyed, whims followed, this world preferred and every clever man proud of his own opinion, he is still responsible for looking after his own soul.

### What practical advice is reported from Ibn 'Umar?

Al-Durr al-Manthur carries an account about 'Abdullah ibn 'Umar. In his presence someone described a grave quarrel between certain named people who were branding one another as polytheists.

Ibn 'Umar asked whether they thought he would tell them to go and fight those people. He said they should not. They should go and speak to them gently. If they listened, that was good. If not, they should stop worrying about them and busy themselves with their own souls. He then recited this verse as confirmation of his answer.

The advice distinguishes the duty of speaking from the duty of fighting, and it locates the point at which a person's responsibility is discharged.

### What do the terms commanding good and forbidding evil mean?

The word for what is fair comes from knowing, that is, understanding and recognising something after deliberation. Its antonym comes from not knowing and not recognising. The two are treated as opposites.

The Qur'an describes people who recognise Allah's blessings in the manifestations of His perfect power and then deny them as though they did not know them (16:83). Lexically, then, the fair is what is well recognised and its opposite is what is unrecognised.

Keeping that congruity in view, Raghib al-Isfahan gives their meanings in the terminology of the Shariah in his lexicon of the Holy Qur'an's vocabulary. Commanding the fair is known in that terminology as Al-Amr bil-Ma'ruf. The fair is what is known to be good in the light of reason and of revelation. Its opposite is what is unfamiliar and unrecognised, that is, what is taken to be bad. Commanding the fair therefore means bidding toward what is right, and forbidding its opposite means restraining from what is wrong.

### Why are these words used instead of obedience and disobedience?

The choice may indicate that in deep matters, and in problems requiring ijtihad because of brevity or ambiguity left in the Qur'an and Sunnah, two opinions can exist. That is the basis on which the sayings of Muslim jurists differ, and such matters are excluded from this scope.

The brilliance of the ijtihad of the great mujtahid imams is an accepted fact among the scholars and jurists of the Muslim ummah. Where two views differ on a religious problem, neither can be considered censurable in the Shariah. Both sides fall within what is fair.

In such problems a person who considers one opinion weightier has no right to censure the other in the way one censures sin. That is why, despite many differences in ijtihad and opposing opinions, it is nowhere reported that the Companions and their successors called one another sinners. They had debates, dialogues and polemics. They presented their views, explained the grounds of their preference and did not hesitate to question what others said, but nobody treated another as a sinner merely for a difference of opinion.

This refers to the views of competent mujtahids, equipped with deep knowledge of the Qur'an and Sunnah, who state a sincere view after doing their utmost to reach the truth. An incompetent person lacking the standard required for ijtihad, who declares a view out of whim and conjecture, cannot claim that his view rests on ijtihad and cannot claim immunity from being restrained.

Briefly, where an ijtihadi difference exists, every knowledgeable person with the requisite subject knowledge may take the side that seems weightier to him. He has no right to censure what another has done by treating it as evil and sinful. Writings that spread mutual hatred and hostility over ijtihadi questions therefore do not fall within commanding the fair or forbidding its opposite. Opening a front of war on the basis of such religious issues can arise only from unawareness or ignorance.

### What comfort does the verse give reformers?

Muslims who sacrificed greatly in their concern for reforming people are comforted here. They did their best to spread the call of truth and discharged the duty of conveying what was good for people.

If others then chose to hold on to their error, that was not for them to worry about, since the straying of others brings no loss on them. The comfort is real, but it is given to those who have already done their part.

### What does the inward reading say?

Beyond the plain explanation, the tongue of allusion addresses those who taste what the people of desire describe. The faithful are told to beware and to subdue their own soul before it subdues them, and to busy it with obedience before it busies them with disobedience.

Abu 'Uthman was asked about this verse and answered that a person's own soul is against him. If he occupies himself with making its corruption wholesome and concealing its shameful parts, he will be too busy to gaze at the creatures or occupy himself with them.

Husayn Mansur al-Hallaj advised his disciple that your own soul is against you, and that a soul left without work will find work for you. Muhammad ibn 'Ali said that your own soul is against you, and if you spare the creatures its evil you will have discharged most of what it is owed.

The soul is described as forever settled in this world and quick toward disobedience. It reckons disobedience a trifle and drags its feet over obedience. It is vain and acts for the sight of other people, and inside it sit associationism, show and duplicity. A saying describes it as two-faced in every condition, false in most of them and an associator in a few.

Abu Yazīd Basṭāmī is quoted saying that if the Lord told him in that world to make a wish, he would ask permission to enter the Fire to punish this soul, because in this world it had always made him writhe and suffer.

Muhammad al-Mustafa ﷺ said that your worst enemy is your soul between your two sides. The reason given is that when you come to terms with an enemy you become safe from his evil, but when you come to terms with your own soul you perish.

The contrast is drawn sharply. If you treat someone well he will thank you at the resurrection, and if you treat him badly he will complain. The soul behaves in the opposite way. Treat it well in this house and it becomes your antagonist in that house. Treat it badly here and it will thank you there.

### In short

- A person is accountable for himself, and the deviation of others does not harm whoever is rightly guided.
- The rightly guided are those who have discharged the duty of commanding the fair and forbidding its opposite, as Abu Bakr's correction makes plain.
- Ibn 'Umar's advice shows the duty as gentle speech rather than fighting, and the hadith of Abu Tha'laba locates the point where self-reform remains the responsibility.
- Differences requiring ijtihad are not censurable, and both sides fall within what is fair, provided the views come from competent mujtahids.
- The inward reading turns the verse on the soul, which must be subdued and kept busy with obedience before it does the reverse.
