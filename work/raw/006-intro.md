Al-Anʿām means "The Cattle". It is a long Makkan surah, 165 verses, and it spends the whole of that length on one subject: the oneness of Allah, argued out at length against people who readily agreed that Allah created the heavens and the earth and then shared their worship with something that cannot create anything. The app's translation gives its first words: "All praise is for Allah Who created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light." From that opening the surah moves through creation, through the signs the Meccans demanded and refused, through the fate of earlier nations, through the story of Ibrāhīm (Abraham) and his argument with his people, through the law about what may be eaten, and on to the Day when everyone will learn whose outcome is the abode. Abū Isḥāq al-Asfarāʾī said that this surah contains all the principles of the oneness of Allah and all its details as well.

### When and how it was revealed

ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās held that the surah came down in Makkah, and Aṭ-Ṭabarānī recorded his fuller statement: all of it was sent down in Makkah at night, accompanied by seventy thousand angels whose voices rose in glorifying Allah. The same report about the seventy thousand angels reached As-Suddi from Murrah, from ʿAbdullāh. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās also described this as one of the surah's distinctions: all of it, except for some verses, came down in a single descent in Makkah, with the company of seventy thousand angels following it and reciting Allah's name. Mujāhid, al-Kalbī, Qatādah and other early authorities said very much the same. Al-Awfiyy, ʿIkrimah and ʿAṭāʾ likewise transmit from Ibn ʿAbbās the ruling that the surah is Makkan.

### Why praise comes first

The surah opens with Allah praising and glorifying Himself, for making the heavens and the earth a dwelling place for His servants, and for making darkness and light so that night and day serve them. Saying it first is itself a lesson. It teaches the servants how to praise, as though He had told them: say, all praise belongs to Allah.

The opening is not there because praise is needed. Whether anyone praises Him or not, Allah is, in His own perfection, the Praiseworthy One by Himself. What the sentence does is straighten the reader's perspective, and it does so by naming a proof in the same breath. All praise belongs to Allah, and the very next words point to what He has made. A Being who holds power and wisdom of that size is plainly the One who deserves praise, and the worship that follows from praise belongs to Him alone.

### What the wording of verse 1 is doing

Some of the surah's argument sits in the grammar of its first line.

Darkness is put in the plural, while light is put in the singular. One reason given is that light is the more honored of the two. A second is more pointed: light stands for the correct way and the straight path, and that is one thing, while the layers of darkness stand for the paths of error, and those run into the thousands. The Qur'an elsewhere sets a singular and a plural together in the same way, as in "To the right and to the lefts" (16:48). Near the end of this surah the same thought appears as a command: "And verily, this is my straight path, so follow it, and follow no (other) ways, for they will separate you away from His way" (6:153).

The heavens are named in the plural and the earth in the single, although another verse states that the earth is seven, like the heavens (65:12). Māzhari suggested that the wording marks the difference the seven heavens have from one another in form and in attributes, while the seven earths resemble each other, so they are counted as one.

The verbs differ as well. The heavens and the earth are said to be created, while darkness and light are said to be made. The hint is that darkness and light are not independent things standing on their own the way the heavens and the earth do. They are accidents and attributes, which need something else to be there. And darkness is mentioned before light, since darkness is the base state of this world, while light belongs to particular causes. When those causes are present there is light, and when they are absent there is darkness.

### The beliefs the opening came to end

Having the oneness of Allah and its plain proof stated in one line is also a warning. It is aimed at people who do not believe in it simply, and at those who say they believe it and have lost its reality.

The first group named are the Magians, who hold two creators, Yazdan and Ahriman, one the maker of good and the other the maker of evil, and identify the two with light and darkness. Against them the verse says that Allah created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light. Both belong to Him, and neither is a rival.

Others are named for the same reason. The polytheists of India take gods beyond counting as partners in Allah's divinity. The Arya Samajists accept oneness, yet treat spirit and matter as things with no beginning, standing outside Allah's power and creation, which leaves the reality of oneness behind. The Christians profess one God and then take ʿĪsā (Jesus) and his blessed mother into divinity with Him, and prop the whole up with the Unity in Trinity, an idea that has to be accepted without being reasoned. The idolaters of Arabia went further than all of them in handing out godhood. In their judgment any rock on a hill was good enough to be the object of worship for the whole of mankind. The human being, whom Allah made the noblest of His creation and for whom the universe was made to work, sank to lying prostrate before sun, moon, stars, fire, water, trees, stones and crawling insects, expecting them to provide for him and untie his problems. Once the verse has said that Allah is the Creator of the heavens and the earth and the Maker of darkness and light, every one of those positions collapses. If He is the sole Creator and Maker of everything, giving Him partners is not a difference of opinion. It makes no sense.

### The argument the surah keeps making

The surah's way of proving oneness is to look at how the world is run and to ask what it takes. The system governing heaven and earth is coordinated and single, and the task is enormous. The universe is unimaginably great in its vastness, in its wisdom and in the fact that it means something. The earth turns in space around the bright sphere of the sun with flawless regulation, and light and darkness, day and night, come into being because it does. None of that is within human comprehension.

Two conclusions follow. A being who organises a universe to perfection has no gap in his work that a partner would have to fill. And a Lord whose whole creation runs as one system is one Lord. The order itself is the proof. The greatness involved also rules out the other half of the idea: Allah does not need an assistant, either in creating or in managing what He has created.

### What the surah covers

The opening three verses state the claim, and verses 4 to 11 show the Meccans treating Allah's signs with indifference, rejecting the truth once it arrived, and mocking it, with the ruin of earlier and stronger nations held up as the answer to the question of what that ends in. Verses 12 to 21 establish what Allah owns, what He has forbidden, who His witness is, and what happens to people who invent lies about Him. Verses 22 to 32 drag the partners into the open on the Day of Judgement, describe the spiritual blindness of those who turn away, and measure this life against the next.

Verses 33 to 39 console the Prophet ﷺ against rejection, set the limit on what anyone can do for a person who has decided not to listen, and point to the animals and birds as communities like theirs. Verses 40 to 55 trace the pattern of nations taken by degrees while their own devices answer for them, and then turn to the believers whom the Meccan leaders wanted pushed out of the Prophet's ﷺ company, with the ranks each of them holds. Verses 56 to 62 close the door on the demand to be given the unseen, set the Prophet's ﷺ ignorance of it against Allah's complete knowledge, and use sleep and death as signs of His power over souls. Verses 63 to 73 answer the question of who rescues people when the distress of sea and land arrives, warn against sitting with those who mock revelation, and contrast the dead who cannot hear with the blind who cannot be led out.

Verses 74 to 90 give Ibrāhīm's argument with his father and his people, his reading of the stars, the moon and the sun, his fearlessness, the rank Allah gave him, and the line of prophets he leads, ending with the guidance of all of them as one guidance. Verses 91 to 103 refute the claim that Allah never sent a human being as a messenger and the attribution of sons to Him, and set the Qur'an up as a blessed confirmation that leaves people without an excuse. Verses 104 to 113 deal with clear insights refused, with the ban on insulting what others worship, with the demand that angels or the whole of the unseen be produced, and with the way devils among humans and jinn decorate doubt for one another.

Verses 114 to 127 state that Allah's words are perfect in truthfulness and justice, warn against the majority who mislead away from His path, and lay out the rule for what may be eaten, with the command not to follow steps of satan and the reminder that Allah knows what people conceal and reveal. Verses 128 to 141 gather jinn and humankind to account, give everyone ranks by their deeds, and then expose the dishonest arithmetic by which the pagans set aside portions of their crops and cattle for Allah and for their idols, and killed their children out of fear of poverty. Verses 142 to 150 sort out the eight head of livestock, name the only things actually forbidden, list what was additionally closed to the Jews, and answer the argument that Allah could have destroyed the idolaters had He willed to.

Verses 151 to 158 gather the ten commands Allah gave, hand the reader back to the Torah as a completed guidance, present this Book as blessed, and warn what it is like when the signs of your Lord arrive and belief avails a person nothing. Verses 159 to 165 end the surah by walking away from people who split their religion into sects, promising the good a tenfold reward and the bad its like, and settling the question of who gets what: prayer, sacrifice, living and dying are for Allah, Lord of the worlds, with no partner, and each person carries their own burden, made a steward on earth and tested in what has been given.

### In short

- Al-Anʿām is a Makkan surah of 165 verses, reported to have come down at night in a single descent, accompanied by seventy thousand angels glorifying Allah.
- Its subject is the oneness of Allah, and Abū Isḥāq al-Asfarāʾī said it carries both its principles and its details.
- It opens with praise, and the creation of the heavens, the earth, the darkness and the light is offered as the proof that praise belongs to Allah alone.
- Its plural "darknesses" against its single "light" marks one straight path against many paths of error, and its choice of "made" for darkness and light marks them as attributes rather than independent powers.
- The surah argues from the ordered running of the universe, and it aims at every position that hands a share of divinity to something created, including the worship of stones and insects.
