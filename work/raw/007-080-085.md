The fourth story opens, and it opens on a deed that had never been seen in the world. "And ˹remember˺ when Lot scolded ˹the men of˺ his people, ˹saying,˺ “Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before"". The verse puts the question to the people, and the question is the beginning of the story.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And remember when Lot scolded the men of his people"** names the messenger and the people. His call was the same call to the oneness of Allah that every messenger brought. Lut was the son of Haran, who was the son of Azar, and his uncle was Ibrahim, peace be upon them both. The original homeland of the two of them was known as Babel, near Basrah in western Iraq. The worship of idols was common there, and even the family of Ibrahim was caught up in it.

In the whole family of Ibrahim, only two entered the faith at first, his wife Sarah and his nephew Lut, as the Book says of him, that he believed in him (29:26). It was with those two that Ibrahim left his homeland behind and moved to the land of Sham. After reaching the river Jordan he settled in the land of Canaan near Bayt al-Maqdis, in obedience to a divine command. In his own country his people had opposed him, and the opposition ended in the well known fire of Nimrud, and his father threatened to drive him out of the house.

Then Allah made Lut a prophet as well, and sent him to Sadum, near Bayt al-Maqdis, for the guidance of the people there. The area took in five chief cities, called Sadum, Amurah, Admah, Sububim and Bali or Sawghar, and the Book refers to their centre by several names. Those details are given in the reports of the place, among them Al-Bahr Al-Muhit. Sadum was counted the capital and the centre of the group, and it was there that Lut stayed. The land was fertile and green, with all kinds of grain and fruit growing in it.

**"Do you commit a shameful deed"** names the offence. The people of that district did things that no one among the children of Adam had ever done, and that had not even crossed anyone's mind. What they did was to go after males rather than females, and the practice was not known in the world before these people invented it. Amr bin Dinar said of the words that no male had ever lain with a male before the people of Lut.

The word used here is the word for an indecency, and it is given in the verse with the definite article. Where the Book speaks of adultery it uses the word for a shameful act in the indefinite form, as in the verse that calls it a shameful act (17:32), and here the word is made definite. The hint carried by that is that the unnatural practice of this community was, as it were, the collection of all indecencies together, and a far graver crime than adultery itself.

**"that no man has ever done before"** is the ground on which the offence is condemned here. The manner of a people is usually to fall into a sin because of the conditions they live in, or because they are blindly following their ancestors, and while that is no legal excuse in the sacred law, a person caught in it can in ordinary practice be allowed some measure of excuse. But when it is a sin that nobody has ever committed before, and one that carries no compulsion of its own, it becomes a curse of the highest order. The second ground is that such an act becomes a channel for making others accursed with the one who did it. A person who invents an evil act or an evil custom carries the sin and the punishment of his own deed, and along with him are affected all those who sink into the same sin through his example, down to the last day, and their curse and their punishment sit on the shoulders of the one who started it. That is what Abd al-Malik, the Umayyad ruler, gave voice to when he said that if this affair of the people of Lut had not been mentioned in the Book, he would never have suspected that a human being could do such a thing.

An ordinary look at the matter gives the same verdict. In the habit of a human being, as the Book puts it, when he finds freedom from need he begins to go past the limits (96:6-7). On these people too the doors of blessing had been opened. Driven on by that pattern of behaviour, drenched in wealth and possessions, they went to the farthest ends of luxury and desire. What they lost first was the sense of honour, of dignity and of modesty, which is among the most essential things a human being has, and with it went the very ability to tell the good from the bad. In the end they got themselves caught up in acts of unnatural indecency, which are abominable apart from being forbidden and sinful, and they stir hatred and distaste in the heart of anyone whose taste is sound and decent, to the point that even animals keep away from such things.

### The three kinds of indecency, in one reading

One strand of teaching reads the question of the verse against the state of the person who hears it. Each person's indecency fits the days and the states he is in. Look at where a man stands on the road he is travelling, and his indecency will take its measure from his own mortal nature at that point. When it comes to the creatures of this world, there are three groups and no more. They are the common people, then the elect, and then the elect of the elect.

For the common people, the indecency is the one the sacred law explains, and its penalty is the whip or the stoning. For the elect, the indecency is to look at the pleasures and the appetites of this world with the eye in the head, to see their enjoyment and their adornment, and to let them into oneself, even where they are permitted and far from anything doubtful. In the case of such people, the harm that comes from the permitted delights of this world is greater than the harm that comes from what is forbidden in the case of ordinary people. The penalty for that indecency, in the words of the master of the sacred law, is the command to lower the eyes and hold back the hands.

For the elect of the elect, the indecency is that they should look at anything other than the Real with the awareness of the heart, when the declaration has come from the Real, "Say, God, then leave them" (6:91). The sense of that is that the servant should not look at himself, but see everything as the act of his Lord, and that he should not put his Lord under an obligation by his own acts, but look at the success that is given to him. He should flee from his own marks and see only the love that comes to him. What has someone seized by that love to do with anything else? The heart is to be kept turned towards Him and the rest let go. The tumult of the world is all this talk of you and me, and if a person gives up the me, the whole world becomes his garden. And the word from God is that it is a wonder how anyone who has faith in Him can depend on anything other than Him, since if such people were to look at the subtleties of His kindness, they would not worship anything but Him.

### In short

- Lut was the nephew of Ibrahim and believed in him, and he left his homeland with the two who remained faithful to the call.
- He was sent as a prophet to Sadum and the cities around it, near Bayt al-Maqdis, a fertile district of five towns.
- The offence of his people was one that had never been committed before them by any creature, and it was the first appearance of that practice in the world.
- The verse carries the word for the shameful act in its definite form, which suggests that the offence gathers into itself every kind of indecency.
- The verse condemns it on two grounds, that nobody had ever done it before and that such an inventor of evil carries the sin of all who follow him.
- Their wealth and comfort had carried them past the limits, until they lost the sense of shame and the ability to tell good from bad.

### Phrase by phrase

**"You lust after men instead of women"** states the deed without dressing it up. What is meant is that they left the women whom Allah had created for them and went to men instead. This behaviour is evil and ignorant, since they had put things in a place that was never meant for them.

What the line hints at is that Allah has appointed marriage with women as the lawful way for the natural satisfaction of human desire. To walk around that way and choose an unnatural one is plain ugliness in the human self and a proof of a dirty mind.

**"You are certainly transgressors"** is the verdict. In one reading the word for wantonness is tied to the practice of idolatry, meaning that they crossed from the lawful into what is forbidden. The real disease of the people was that they went beyond the limits Allah had set in everything, and in their case the limit they jumped over was the very boundary of humanity. The same thing happened with desire, since they went past the bounds appointed by Allah and reached for a taste that runs against nature.

### What the community's scholars drew from it

The Companions, those who followed them, and the leading jurists have declared this crime to be far graver than other shameful acts. Imam Abu Hanifah said that the punishment given to the one who commits it should match the punishment that came upon the people of Lut by the command of Allah, when rocks rained down from the sky and the floor of the earth was turned over. His view was that such a person should be pushed down from a high mountain, and rocks thrown down on him from above.

In a narration of Ibn Abbas that Ahmad, Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah all carry, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said of the people who commit this act that both the doer of the deed and the one it is done to are to be killed. The Book lays the same weight on the matter when it speaks of the act among the sins of a later people.

- Marriage with women is the lawful way appointed for natural desire, and taking another way is a defect of the human self.
- The verdict is that they had gone past the limits, since they crossed from what is lawful into what is unlawful.
- The jurists have counted this crime graver than other shameful acts, and the Prophet ﷺ ordered that both parties to the act be put to death.
- The punishment the lawgiver prescribed for it matches the way the punishment came on the people of Lut themselves.

- One report says she never left, and another says she turned back to watch and was caught by the punishment.
- Her case shows that the justice of God is even-handed, since ties of family do not shield anyone, while righteous deeds can raise anyone.

<<<VERSE 7:85>>>
The fifth story opens. "And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib." He brings them the same call as the messengers before him. "He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him." Then the proof is named. "A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord." And with it come the commands about dealings between people. "So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order." And the close of it. "This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers". The story is the last of the series in this surah, and it joins the call of the prophets to a matter of plain dealing between people.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib"** names the messenger and the people. Shu'ayb, as Muhammad bin Ishaq said, is among the progeny of Madyan the son of Ibrahim, and he is also related to Lut. The descendants of Madyan the son of Ibrahim came to be known by the name Madyan as well, and the place where they lived took the same name, so that Madyan is the name of a people and of a city at once. The city still stands near Ma'an, on the way to the Hijaz from Sham. When the Book tells the story of Musa elsewhere and says that he arrived at the water of Madyan (28:23), it is speaking of that same place.

Shu'ayb was known for eloquent speech, and because of it he was called the orator of the prophets.

The people he was sent to are given in the Book both as the people of Madyan and as the people of Al-Aykah, and the word for Al-Aykah means a forest. Some of the authorities hold that these were two separate peoples living in separate settlements, and that Shu'ayb went to one of the two first. Once that people had been destroyed, he went to the other. The punishment that came on the two is described in different words in the Book. The punishment of the people of Madyan is given in some places as the sound and in others as the earthquake, while the punishment of the people of Al-Aykah is given as the shade of a cloud. In that form of it, intense heat struck the people of Al-Aykah first, and it almost roasted them in their own district. Then a dense cloud came over the forest nearby and cast its shade across the whole of it. Drawn by the shade and the cool breeze, all the people of the district came together under that cloud, and there, walking on their own two feet, they arrived at the very spot where they were to be destroyed. Once they were all gathered, fire rained from the cloud and the earthquake shook the earth, and that killed them all.

Others hold that the people of Madyan and the people of Al-Aykah were one and the same people, and that the three kinds of punishment named in the Book converged on them, the fire from the clouds first, then the piercing sound with it, and then the earthquake. That is the view preferred in the reports that carry the story.

Whichever it is, what matters is the message of truth that Shu'ayb brought them, and that is what the verses give. Before turning to it, there is a principle to be seen. The essence of the religion, which is the combined call of all the prophets, is the fulfilling of rights, and rights are of two kinds. There are rights owed directly to Allah, on whose fulfilment or neglect no visible human gain or loss depends, and prayer and fasting are of this kind. There are also the rights of the servants of Allah, which is what human rights mean in ordinary speech, and they have to do with other people. This community was heedless of both kinds, and was working against both.

By refusing belief in Allah, and in the messengers He sent, they were taking away what was owed to Him, and on top of that, by cutting down weights and measures, they were slicing away the rights of human beings. It went further than that with them, since such people would sit at the entrances of roads and streets and frighten everyone passing, rob them of what they carried, and warn them of worse to come if they believed in Shu'ayb. In that way they had made disorder the settled order of God's earth. It was to correct these things that Shu'ayb was sent to them.

**"O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him"** is the first of the three things he said to them. This is the call to the oneness of Allah that all the prophets have given down the ages, and it is the spirit of all true belief and all true action, and it is the road that the whole line of the messengers walked. These people were sold to the worship of what had been created, and were in consequence heedless of the sacred Being and His attributes, and neglectful of His rights. So this was the first message put to them.

**"A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord"** is the second. The clear proof means the miracles manifested at the hands of Shu'ayb, and the different forms those miracles took are given in Al-Bahr Al-Muhit. The sense is that Allah has set out the proof and the evidence for the truth of what he brought.

**"So give just measure and weight"** is the third, and it lays down a major rule of just conduct. The word kail covers measuring by volume, and the word mizan covers the weights, and the word for making short means to bring loss on someone by giving less than what is due. Rights of this kind, owed to other people, are what the scholars call the rights of the servants, the huquq al-Ibad, as against the rights owed to Allah. So the instruction is to give full measure and full weight, and to hold back from causing loss to people in what belongs to them.

The particular crime of giving short measure and short weight in buying and selling is prohibited in the first part of the verse, and then the prohibition is made general by what follows. What was being done to them in the markets was that merchants would avoid giving full weight and measure, and the Book gives the same warning elsewhere when it speaks of those who give less than is due in measure and weight (83:1), and asks its question about them before the Lord of all that exists (83:6). Those verses carry a stern warning and a sure promise.

**"do not defraud people of their property"** generalizes the command, and now it covers every kind of cutting back on what is due to others, whether the right in question concerns property or honour or anything else. In the same way that it is forbidden to give less than is due in weighing and measuring, it is forbidden to cut back on the rights of other people. Attacking the honour of a person, failing to give him the respect that his station calls for, falling short in the obedience owed to those who must be obeyed, and failing to show respect to those who must be respected, all of these fall under the crime that the people of Shu'ayb used to commit. In his famous khutbah at the farewell pilgrimage, the Prophet ﷺ declared that the honour of people deserves the same respect and defence as their blood, and that stands as a wider confirmation of the view.

These things are covered by the words for giving short measure wherever they occur in the Book. The leader of the community saw a man hurrying through his ruku and his sujud, the bowing and the prostration of the prayer, and said to him that he was guilty of giving short measure. What he meant was that the man had not given the prayer its due. So the shortcoming in an act of worship is described there with the same word that is used of a cheat in the market. That word, and the name given to those who deal in that way, is the one the Book uses when it speaks of the mutaffifin (83:1).

**"nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order"** carries the same command that appeared earlier in the surah, and the meaning is the same. The physical good order of conditions on the earth depends on using things properly, in the right measure and place, keeping to limits and holding to moderation, fairness and justice. The spiritual good order of it depends on the tie with Allah and on obedience to His commands. The opposite of that, which is disorder of both kinds, appears when these principles are set aside or abandoned. The people of Shu'ayb had done exactly that. They had let go of all of it, and the earth among them had become a hotbed of disorder both outward and inward. That is why they were told to hold back from the deeds that would carry corruption across the whole earth.

**"This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers"** closes the verse, and the words that come after it in verse 86 take up the same call from another side. The sense is that if they turn back from what they were doing, nothing but good waits for them, here and in the life to come. The call to the oneness of Allah, and the giving of full measure and weight, are better for them than the state they were in. As for the life to come, success in it plainly depends on obeying what Allah has commanded. As for this world, once people find that there is someone who is honest in his weights and his measures and careful about the rights of those who deal with him, his standing and his good name in the market become established, and his trade grows in volume and in trust.

### What the scholars drew from the market and the road

The words about giving full measure and weight and not making people short of their things were read as carrying a wider rule, and the instruction covers all kinds of reducing, slicing and short-changing of rights.

The command about the roads was explained in two ways. Some said the two statements carry the same sense, since these people used to stop and threaten those who came to Shu'ayb, and they were ordered not to do that. Others said the crimes were two separate ones. They sat on the thoroughfares, robbed and snatched what people had, and also prevented people from believing in Shu'ayb. On that reading the first statement describes the first crime and the second describes the other, and it is this view that Al-Bahr Al-Muhit and other works relied on.

Under the same command against robbing and snatching fall the checkpoints set up on the roads to collect taxes that are not permitted by the sacred law, and the words about blocking the road are understood by some as the prevention of people from the path of Allah. Al-Qurtubi said that the men who sit on the thoroughfares and make people pay taxes that the sacred law does not allow are criminals of the same kind as the people of Shu'ayb, and more oppressive and tyrannical than they were.

The moral state of the community is spelled out in the same passage. These people traced themselves to Ibrahim and professed his religion, and they were a trading community, so their wrongdoing came out in their dealings. They did not keep to honesty in weights, measures and transactions. Injustice in dealing with others goes against the balanced system that God has enforced, since the system of this world is made to work on the basis of perfect justice. Nothing in it takes more from others and gives less in return. Everything in it runs on principles of justice to the point of mathematical exactness, and when that is not the case, what has happened is a disturbance in the well-balanced world of God.

### In short

- Shu'ayb was sent to the people of Madyan, a people descended from Madyan the son of Ibrahim, and the town itself still stands on the route between Sham and the Hijaz.
- The people are also called the people of Al-Aykah, which means the forest, and the early authorities differ on whether these were one people or two.
- The punishments given for them in the Book are the fire from the cloud, the piercing sound and the earthquake, and on the view preferred in the reports all three came on one people.
- The essence of the religion is the fulfilment of rights, both the rights owed to Allah and the rights owed to other people, and this community was failing in both.
- The three things Shu'ayb said were the call to the oneness of Allah, the plain proof that had come to them, and the command to deal justly in measure and weight, and the whole of it was put as being for their own good.
- The command against making people short of their due covers property, honour, respect and every right, and short weight in the market and short measure in worship are named by the same word.
- The command against corruption repeats the one given earlier in the surah, and the road command covers both highway robbery and the checkpoints that collect taxes the sacred law does not permit. The report of the khutbah joins these verses to the honour owed to every person.
