The theme of righteousness begun in verse 177 continues. Six injunctions have appeared so far, on retaliation, bequest, fasting, iʿtikāf and abstention from the unlawful, and now come those of Ḥajj and jihād, prefaced by the clarification that lunar months and days govern Ḥajj and the other religious duties. "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, "They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage." Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors." It continues: "Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺. So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors, and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful". The verse also erases a custom of the Jāhiliyyah by which people in iḥrām, if they needed to go home, entered from the back, even through a hole bored in the wall, believing it forbidden to use the front door in iḥrām and even counting this as righteousness.

### Phrase by phrase

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon."**. Al-ʿAwfī relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that people asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about the crescents and this verse was revealed. Ibn ʿAbbās says that the Companions had a distinctive courtesy: out of great respect they asked their Prophet ﷺ very few questions, unlike the communities of earlier prophets who asked many and so failed in the etiquette due to a prophet. He counts fourteen questions from the Companions in the Qur'an. They include verse 186, "when My servants ask you", and this question. Six more occur in Sūrat al-Baqarah and six in other sūrahs. One account says the verse was revealed when Muʿādh ibn Jabal asked about the timing of the pilgrimage. The question concerned the new moon, which unlike the sun begins as a thin crescent, grows slowly to a full circle and then shrinks again. They may have asked about its cause or about the wisdom of its gradual waxing and waning. The answer states the wisdom and benefit. If that was what they asked, the answer matches the question. The alternative is a question about physical causes, regarded here as unlike the Companions' good manners. In that case, answering with benefits rather than physical reality carries a lesson. The reality of heavenly bodies is considered beyond human reach and unnecessary for practical purposes here or hereafter. On this reasoning, it is not worth asking about. What can usefully be asked and explained is the benefit that comes to people from the moon's waning and waxing, its setting and rising.

**"Say, "They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage.""**. The crescents mark appointed times, mawāqīt. They regulate transactions, contracts, debt repayments and women's waiting periods, ʿiddah. They also identify fasting and its end, completion of the zakāh year, blood-money deadlines and pilgrimage days. If the new moons always looked the same none of this could be known. ʿAbd al-Razzāq reports Ibn ʿUmar's account of the Messenger's ﷺ words. Allah made crescents signs of periods for people. Begin and end the fast upon sighting them. If the crescent is hidden, count thirty days. Al-Ḥākim records this in his Mustadrak with a ṣaḥīḥ chain, noting that al-Bukhārī and Muslim did not record it. The moon is an almanac of nature set in the sky so that people may plan their worldly affairs and their worship. The superstitious hold the days of the waxing moon auspicious and those of the waning moon inauspicious, but the phases are meant to serve only as measures of time.

**"Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors."**. Al-Bukhārī records from al-Barāʾ that in the Jāhiliyyah people entered their houses from the back after assuming iḥrām, and Allah revealed this. Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī also transmits al-Barāʾ's report. His wording describes the Anṣār returning from journeys and entering by the rear of their houses. Al-Ḥasan said that when people in the Jāhiliyyah set out on a journey and then decided not to travel, they would not enter by the door but climb over the back wall. One account names a group of Companions from Kinānah and Khuzāʿah who in iḥrām entered their houses from the back or from the roof as had been the pre-Islamic habit. They bored holes to enter and leave, ignoring the doors, and claimed it was piety. Since entering by the door is not forbidden there is nothing to abstain from, so this is neither obedience nor God-fearing.

**"Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺."**. Real righteousness lies in abstaining from what Allah has made unlawful and not contravening His commands, in iḥrām avoiding hunting and the like, and it belongs to whoever is mindful of Him outwardly and inwardly. Many people have come to regard mere outward observance as religiosity.

**"So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors"**. Enter by the doors you always used, even in iḥrām, for that is easier and less troublesome, and Allah does not burden you with difficulty.

**"and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful"**. Do what He commands and avoid what He forbids, fearing Him in the state of iḥrām, so that you prosper when you stand before Him tomorrow and He rewards you fully, are spared His anger and punishment, and attain what you hope for and escape what you fear. The centre of all these principles is that people keep fearing Allah so that they may hope for the best of both worlds.

### The lunar calendar

The verse says only that the moon helps count the months and days on which transactions and acts of worship such as Ḥajj depend. Sūrat Yūnus says the same, that Allah made the moon "a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺" (10:5). A verse of Sūrat Banī Isrāʾīl links the count to the sun as well: "We made the sign of the day ˹perfectly˺ bright, so that you may seek the bounty of your Lord and know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺" (17:12). Years and months can thus be counted by a solar calendar too, yet the Qur'an's words about the moon show that the lunar calendar is the fixed choice of the Sharīʿah, especially in acts of worship tied to a particular month and date. Ramaḍān, the Ḥajj and its days, Muḥarram and Laylat al-Qadr all depend on the sighting of the new moon, for this verse establishes that the lunar reckoning is the one trusted in Allah's sight, though months can be counted by the sun as well. The explanation connects the Sharīʿah's choice with a visible sign on the horizon. Scholars and the unlettered can see it, as can villagers, islanders and mountain-dwellers. In contrast, the solar calendar requires instruments and calculations. These cannot as easily become everyone's personal experience. In worship the lunar calendar is obligatory, and it is favoured in social and business dealings too, as the basis of Islamic worship and a symbol of Islamic identity. The solar calendar is not juristically prohibited, on condition that its use does not spread so far that people forget the lunar one, which would harm obligatory worship such as fasting and Ḥajj. A sample of this is visible today in offices and businesses, public and private, where the solar calendar is used so constantly that many people cannot even name all the Islamic months, a deplorable sign of a lack of will to uphold a matter of religious and national identity with self-respect. The recommendation is to use the solar calendar in office dealings involving non-Muslims. Other correspondence, private dealings and daily needs can follow the lunar calendar. This preserves identity and earns the reward of farḍ ʿalā al-kifāyah. That is an obligation whose performance by some suffices for the rest.

### Making the permissible a sin

The house-entry clause yields a legal principle. Nobody may independently make something necessary, wājib, or an act of worship when the Sharīʿah has not given it that status. Likewise, making a sin of what the law permits is itself sinful. That is exactly what those people did: entering by the door was permissible and they made it a sin, while breaking in through the back wall was not required and they made it necessary, and for this they were admonished. Innovations (bidʿah) in religion are impermissible for the same reason, because they make the unnecessary obligatory, as farḍ or wājib, or the permissible forbidden, and this verse makes clear that doing so is prohibited, giving a guiding rule for thousands of practices in vogue. The following verses carry the injunction on fighting the disbelievers. In Dhū al-Qaʿdah of the year 6 AH the Prophet ﷺ set out for Makkah to perform ʿumrah while the city was still controlled by the idolaters, who refused him and his Companions entry, and after much negotiation it was agreed that the Muslims would perform ʿumrah the next year, the pact known as the Peace of Ḥudaybiyyah. The expression "do not transgress" in those verses means, according to the majority, not to exceed the limit in fighting by killing women and children.

### The waxing and waning of the heart

In the devotional reading, the waxing and waning of the moon allude to the contraction and expansion of the recognisers and the awe and intimacy of the lovers. Contraction and expansion are for the elect what fear and hope are for the common people, and higher than both is awe and intimacy, which belongs to the elect of the elect. These correspond to the stations of the wrongdoers, the moderate and the foremost. The highest limit is the lovers' intimacy. In that state, a man may enter fire without awareness of it. Its heat leaves the repose of intimacy undisturbed. Abū Ḥafṣ al-Ḥaddād was a blacksmith who had lit a fierce fire and put iron in it, as smiths do. Someone passed by reciting a verse of the Qur'an. The shaykh rejoiced at the verse, intimacy overcame him, and he reached into the furnace and drew out the hot iron and held it until his apprentice cried out to ask how he could be holding it. He set it down and left his trade, saying that he had left it many times and come back, but this time the trade had left him.

### In short

- The Companions, who asked few questions, asked about the crescent moon, and were told its benefit rather than its cause: the phases fix the times of debts, waiting periods, fasting, zakāh and Ḥajj (Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn ʿUmar).
- The lunar calendar is the Sharīʿah's fixed choice for worship (cf. 10:5, 17:12), visible to everyone, and should not be forgotten through overuse of the solar one.
- Entering houses from the back in iḥrām, a Jāhilī custom thought pious (al-Barāʾ, al-Ḥasan), is no righteousness. Righteousness is taqwā.
- Treating the permissible as a sin or the unnecessary as obligatory is the root of bidʿah and is forbidden by this verse.
- Inwardly, the moon's phases mirror the heart's contraction and expansion, and the lovers' intimacy, as the story of Abū Ḥafṣ al-Ḥaddād shows.
