<<<VERSE 2:45>>>
After a string of commands to the Children of Israel, verse 45 tells them where the strength to obey will come from. "And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble", it says, and the next verse goes on to describe who those humble people are. On the surface the address is still to the Israelites who were being called to accept the Prophet ﷺ, but the instruction is worded for everyone who finds Allah's commands heavy, which is to say for all of us.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And seek help"** means ask for assistance in your affairs. Scholars read it in two complementary ways. Some take it as help in carrying out the duties Allah has prescribed and keeping away from what He has forbidden. Others widen it to help in sacred and worldly affairs alike, since patience and prayer bring a person close to Allah, and when a person is close to Allah, He helps him, protects him and lifts his difficulties from him. Allah commanded His servants to use these two means to win the good of this life and the next.

**"through patience"**, in Arabic ṣabr, is restraining the self in the face of what it dislikes. Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said the verse means: make use of patience and the obligatory prayer in your search for the Hereafter, and added that patience here is fasting. Similar reports come from Mujāhid. Al-Qurṭubī and other scholars noted that this is why Ramaḍān is called the month of patience, an expression found in the hadith literature. Another view is that patience here means holding back from evil, which explains why it is paired with the acts of worship and above all with prayer. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, in a report preserved by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, distinguished two kinds of patience: a good patience when calamity strikes, and a better one in staying away from what Allah has forbidden. Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is reported to have said much the same.

**"and prayer"**, aṣ-ṣalāt, is named separately to stress how much weight it carries. The prayer is among the best supports for holding firmly to Allah's commands. A hadith says that whenever something troubled the Prophet ﷺ he turned at once to prayer. The Qur'an itself says that genuine prayer should deter a person from indecency (al-faḥshāʾ) and wickedness, and that the remembrance of Allah is a greater deterrent still (29:45).

**"Indeed, it is a burden"** uses a pronoun, "it", that has been read in two ways. Mujāhid took it to refer to the prayer, and this was the preferred reading of Ibn Jarīr. It is also possible that it refers to the whole counsel just given, patience and prayer together. The Qur'an speaks in the same way elsewhere. When the people of knowledge rebuked those who envied Qārūn (Korah), they said that Allah's reward is better for those who believe and do good, and that none attain it except the steadfast (28:80). And when it commands that evil be answered with what is better, it adds that only the patient and the truly fortunate manage it (41:34-35). Whichever reading is taken, the point is that prayer is heavy, grievous and hard to keep up.

**"except for the humble"**, al-khāshiʿīn. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported Ibn ʿAbbās explaining them as those who believe in what Allah has sent down. Others describe them as people who are at peace in their obedience, or simply those who are humble before their Lord. For such people the burden lightens.

### Why were the Israelites told this?

One explanation is that the address is aimed at the Jews of Madīnah at the moment when greed and the love of leadership were standing between them and faith. They were therefore directed to forbearance, which was understood as fasting, and to prayer. The reasoning is neat: greed grows out of appetite, and fasting weakens appetite, while prayer breeds humility and kills pride. Seen in the light of the previous verses, the people who had found it so hard to give up their positions of honour were being handed the two tools that make such sacrifice possible. For the truly devout, worldly considerations of this kind are no hindrance at all, because they find in remembrance, spending and obedience the satisfaction that others seek in worldly gain.

### What is humbleness of heart?

The humility the verse praises is khushūʿ, and the Qur'an and hadith mean by it a settledness and lowliness of the heart that grows out of sensing Allah's greatness and one's own smallness beside it. Once a person has it, obedience and submission stop being a strain and become easy, even pleasant. It can also show on the outside: such a person carries himself with discipline and courtesy, is modest, and looks as if his heart has been broken, in the sense that vanity and self-admiration have gone out of him. The order matters, though. A person who does not carry real humility and fear of Allah inside has no khushūʿ, however modest his manner and however lowered his gaze. It is not even proper to put on the signs of it on purpose. When ʿUmar saw a young man sitting with his head hanging down, he told him to lift his head, because humbleness of heart lives in the heart. Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī said it is not a matter of rough clothing, coarse food and a bowed head. Rather, it is treating the high and the low alike wherever truth is at stake, and keeping the heart free for Allah and for the duties He has laid down. Ḥasan al-Baṣrī described ʿUmar as a man who spoke loudly enough to be heard, walked quickly and struck hard when he struck, yet no one doubted that his heart was truly humble. Al-Qurṭubī summed it up: deliberately wearing the look of a humble man is a form of self-deception and a trick of Satan, and is blameworthy, but if the signs appear in someone without his knowing it, he is excused.

A related word, khuḍūʿ, often appears alongside khushūʿ and the two are almost synonyms. The difference lies in the roots. Khushūʿ properly refers to the lowering of the voice and the glance when this comes from genuine modesty and fear, as in the verse describing the Day when all voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate (20:108). Khuḍūʿ refers to the bodily posture of humility, as in the verse about necks bent in submission before a compelling sign (26:4).

### Is prayer valid without it?

Scholars had to decide what place khushūʿ holds in the law of prayer. The Qur'an and the Sunnah press its importance again and again. Allah commands prayer "for My remembrance" (20:14), and since heedlessness is the opposite of remembrance, a person who drifts into forgetfulness of Allah during prayer is not fulfilling the duty of remembering Him. Another verse warns, "do not be one of the heedless" (7:205). The Prophet ﷺ said that prayer is nothing but lowliness and humility, and another hadith says that a man whose prayer does not hold him back from indecency and evil only moves further from Allah. A heedless prayer plainly does not restrain anyone from wrongdoing, so such a person moves further away.

Drawing on these texts in his Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm ad-Dīn, al-Ghazālī argued that khushūʿ must be a necessary condition of prayer, on which its acceptance depends. He cited the Companion Muʿādh ibn Jabal and jurists of the rank of Sufyān ath-Thawrī and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī as holding that prayer without khushūʿ is not valid.

The four Imāms of jurisprudence and the great majority of jurists took a different position. They regard khushūʿ as the very essence of prayer, yet they do not make it a condition for the whole of it. The one thing they require is that at the opening takbīr the worshipper should turn to Allah with his whole heart and form the intention (niyyah) of praying for His sake alone. If he fails to keep khushūʿ for the rest of the prayer, he earns no reward for that part, but from the standpoint of fiqh he is not counted as someone who abandoned prayer, and he is not liable to the punishment for giving up the prescribed prayers without excuse.

Al-Ghazālī himself explained why the two camps differ. The jurists do not deal with inner states of the heart (aḥwāl). Their task is to set out the outward rules of the Sharīʿah on the basis of what the limbs visibly do, and deciding who will be rewarded in the next world is not within the remit of fiqh. Since khushūʿ is an inner state, they did not make it a condition for the full duration of prayer but tied validity to its lowest degree, the turning of the heart to Allah at the start together with the intention to worship Him alone. A second explanation rests on a principle the Qur'an states clearly in other places: nothing is imposed on people beyond their capacity. Apart from a few gifted individuals, people simply cannot hold khushūʿ from the first takbīr to the final greeting, so the jurists, rather than bind people to something they cannot do, made it a condition only at the beginning.

Al-Ghazālī closed his discussion by saying that, for all the importance of khushūʿ, one may rely on Allah's boundless mercy and hope that the inattentive worshipper will not be classed with those who leave prayer altogether. After all, he tried to discharge the duty, turned away from everything else for at least a few moments, and had Allah alone in mind while forming his intention. A half-hearted prayer at least keeps a person's name off the roll of those who habitually disobey Allah and desert the prayer entirely. The matter therefore holds both fear and hope: fear of having earned punishment, and hope of eventual forgiveness. The right response is to fight one's own laziness and indifference as hard as one can, knowing that only Allah's mercy brings success in that fight.

### The homily on patience

The devotional reading of the verse hears in it a command to the Prophet ﷺ: tell your community to be patient in their affairs so that they may reach what they long for, since patience is the key to relief. Then comes a warning to anyone who would enter the path without that patience. Whoever lacks the patience of the Men should stay off their playing field. Whoever does not have the legs of men should not dress in men's clothes, and whoever lacks the wealth of having nothing should not boast of selflessness. The Prophet ﷺ himself, the paragon of the world, was not left an hour without sorrow once he stepped onto this field. If he sat cross-legged for a while, he was told to sit like a servant. If he put a ring on his finger, a verse of rebuke came down (23:115). If he set his foot boldly on the ground, the command came not to walk on the earth exultantly (17:37). When trial pressed on him from every side he sighed that no prophet had been tormented as he had been, and the answer from the Exalted Presence asked whether someone whose heart and spirit witness Him could complain about the weight of trial. All the venom of grief in the treasuries of the Unseen was poured into one cup and set in his hand, and a curtain was lifted from his innermost core so that he could drink it while gazing on Allah's beauty, in keeping with the words "be patient with your Lord's decree, for you are truly under Our Eyes" (52:48). The tongue of his state answered that poison poured by the Beloved's hand is sweet, that even if that hand were fire it would be a bed of roses, and that whatever comes from Him is sweet whether it heals or hurts.

On the closing phrase, the same reading says humbleness is a stipulation of the prayer and the mark of the servant's neediness. Those who are humble in prayer are praised by the Real and chosen from among His creatures, as in the opening of Sūrat al-Muʾminūn, where success is promised to the believers who humble themselves in their prayers (23:1-2). This humbleness, it adds, has both an outward side and an inward side.

### In short

- Patience (ṣabr) and prayer (aṣ-ṣalāt) are the two means Allah gives for carrying out His commands, and "patience" has been read as fasting, as holding back from sin, and as steadfastness under hardship.
- "It is a burden" refers to prayer, or to the whole counsel, and it is heavy for everyone except the humble, al-khāshiʿīn.
- Khushūʿ is a state of the heart, not a posture. ʿUmar, Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī all warned against faking its signs.
- Al-Ghazālī, with Muʿādh, ath-Thawrī and al-Baṣrī, held khushūʿ necessary for a valid prayer. The four Imāms and most jurists require it only at the opening takbīr, while agreeing that the heedless part of a prayer earns no reward.
- The right attitude to one's own distracted prayers is a mixture of fear and hope, together with hard effort against laziness.

<<<VERSE 2:46>>>
Verse 46 finishes the sentence begun in verse 45. Prayer is a burden for everyone except the humble, and the humble are "those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return". The verse therefore answers a practical question: what makes prayer light for some people and crushing for others? The answer is certainty about the meeting with Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**"those who are certain"** translates yaẓunnūna, a verb from ẓann, a word that in everyday Arabic usually means to think or suppose. Here it means certainty (yaqīn). Ibn Jarīr pointed out that the Arabs use ẓann for certainty as well as for doubt, and the Qur'an does the same elsewhere: when the wicked see the Fire and "realize" that they are bound to fall into it (18:53), the verb is the same, and there is no doubt left in that moment. Scholars agree that certainty is the plain and most obvious sense, and that what is intended is a settled reckoning (ḥusbān), not a hopeful guess.

**"that they will meet their Lord"** means they know they will be gathered and brought before their Lord on the Day of Resurrection. One reading takes the meeting literally, as seeing Him. A subtler observation is that the Arabic form mulāqū rabbihim fits past, present and future at once. For those who have recognised the truth of what the decrees of the unseen hold in store, the promised meeting is as good as already settled, and the unseen is present to them as if it were before their eyes.

**"and to Him they will return"** points to what follows death: the return to Allah in the Hereafter, where He rewards each person. Their affairs are entirely subject to His will, and He decides with justice whatever He decides. They are convinced that they will be held accountable for what they did.

### Why certainty makes the burden light

The link with the previous verse is simple. Because these people are sure that they will be returned to Allah and called to account, the acts of obedience come easily to them, and keeping away from what He forbade comes easily too. They do not pray as if paying a tax to an absent landlord. They pray as people who expect to stand before the One they are addressing. Take that certainty away and the same prayer becomes a weight, which is exactly the condition the previous verse described.

The devotional reading sharpens the contrast with a play on the word ẓann. There is a reckoning of certainty (ẓann al-yaqīn) and a reckoning of guesswork (ẓann at-takhmīn). Those who reckon with certainty taste the communion of lovers, and those who merely guess live in separation. The humble of verse 45 belong to the first group.

### The servant who did not expect the meeting

The opposite case is described in a hadith recorded in the Ṣaḥīḥ. On the Day of Resurrection Allah will say to one of His servants: did I not let you marry, honour you, make horses and camels serve you, and let you become a chief and a master among people? The man will say yes. Allah will ask whether he ever thought he would meet Him, and the man will say no. Then Allah will say: today I forget you, just as you forgot Me. This is the person who never "reckoned" on the meeting, and it is the condition the Qur'an describes in the verse about the hypocrites who neglected Allah, so that He neglected them (9:67), a subject scholars take up more fully in its own place. The verse before us describes the mirror image: people who think about the meeting so steadily that it governs how they live.

### Back to the Israelites

Read in its setting, the verse also explains why the people addressed in this passage found the call of the Prophet ﷺ so difficult. Those who have won religious standing through worldly position find it very hard to answer a call that costs them their position. The remedy offered to them was patience and prayer, and the engine that drives both is the certainty described here. A person who is certain of the return to Allah fears His punishment more than any worldly loss, and for him the "burden" of verse 45 turns into relief.

### In short

- The verb ẓann, which normally means "to suppose", here means certainty (yaqīn), as Ibn Jarīr showed from Arabic usage and from the Qur'an's own language.
- The humble are those who are sure of meeting their Lord at the Resurrection and of returning to Him for the reckoning.
- That certainty is what makes prayer and obedience light, while its absence makes them a burden.
- A hadith in the Ṣaḥīḥ describes the man who never expected the meeting and is told on that Day that Allah forgets him as he forgot Allah.
