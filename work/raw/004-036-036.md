<<<VERSE 4:36>>>
Since the start of this surah, human rights have been the main concern: orphans, women, inheritance. Now the surah turns to the rights of parents, relatives, neighbours and people in general: "Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him. And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, ˹needy˺ travellers, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession. Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful." These duties can be fully met only by someone whose bearing toward Allah, His Messenger and the Day of Reckoning is right and strong, and who keeps free of miserliness, arrogance and show, which block every good effort. That is why the verse begins with the oneness of Allah and ends with a warning against pride.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him"**. Allah orders that He be worshipped alone, with no partner, because He alone is the Creator and Sustainer, and He sends His favours and bounties on His creation in every situation. So He deserves to be singled out, with nothing and no one from among His creation joined to Him in worship. One short explanation says: acknowledge His divine oneness and set up no idol as a partner to Him. The Prophet ﷺ said to Muʿādh, "Do you know Allah's right over His servants?" Muʿādh answered, "Allah and His Messenger know best." He said, "That they worship Him and worship none with Him." Then he asked, "Do you know the servants' right over Allah if they do this?" and said, "That He does not punish them."

There are several wise reasons why the list of rights begins with this. One basic reason is the fear of Allah and a conscious concern to meet His rights. A person who neglects that cannot be expected to be careful about anyone else's rights. He will always find some excuse to keep his closest relatives and friends content, to keep his standing in society, or to escape the government's law. What truly forces a person to honour human rights, whether watched or unwatched, is the fear of Allah and the sense of answering to Him. This fear, this taqwā, comes only from tawḥīd, the firm belief in Allah's oneness, with nothing else allowed a share in it. So it was fitting to remind people of His oneness and of worshipping Him before describing the rights of relatives and others.

**"And be kind to parents"**. After the command to worship Him, parents come first. All blessings really come from Allah. But in terms of worldly means, the favours a person receives most often, next to Allah's, are those from his parents, who are the origin of his existence in the chain of causes. Allah made parents the reason servants came into being after they had not existed. In every stage from birth to youth, parents look after a person's being, upbringing and growth as best they can. So Allah joins the order to worship Him with the order to honour parents in many places. He says: "So be grateful to Me and your parents" (31:14). He says: "For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him. And honour your parents" (17:23). In the covenant with the Children of Israel He said: "Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy" (2:83). Short explanations ask for dutifulness, gentle manners, honour and respect.

The verse does not say to fulfil parents' rights or to serve them. The word is iḥsān, doing good, which is far wider. It includes spending on them as they need, serving them in person when needed, never speaking to them in a voice that is sharp or loud enough to be disrespectful, never saying what might break their hearts, and not treating their friends and well-wishers in a way that hurts them. Everything possible should be done to comfort and please them. Even if the parents themselves fell short of what they owed their children, the children should never take that as an excuse for treating them badly.

Muʿādh ibn Jabal said that of ten things the Prophet ﷺ told him to hold to, one was, "Do not join anything with Allah, even if you are killed or burned alive," and the second was, "Do not disobey your parents or hurt them, even if they tell you to leave your family and children." This is in the Musnad of Aḥmad. The Prophet's ﷺ sayings promise countless rewards for obedience and good treatment of parents. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record that he said that whoever wants his earnings and his life to grow through Allah's blessing should keep up the rights of his relatives. At-Tirmidhī has it that Allah's pleasure is in the father's pleasure and Allah's displeasure is in the father's displeasure. Al-Baihaqī gave, in Shuʿab al-Īmān, a saying that when a son who obeys his parents looks at them with love and respect, every glance earns him the reward of an accepted Ḥajj. Another narration from al-Baihaqī says that Allah forgives most sins, but a person who disobeys and hurts his parents will face troubles of every kind in this world, long before the reckoning in the Hereafter.

**"relatives"**. After parents, the verse stresses kindness to all relatives, men and women. A hadith says that charity given to a poor person is ṣadaqah, while charity given to a relative is both ṣadaqah and ṣilah, keeping the tie. Another verse covers the same ground, and the Prophet ﷺ often recited it at the end of his sermons. It is still recited in the Friday khuṭbah around the world: "Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, as well as courtesy to close relatives" (16:90). This includes helping one's relatives as much as one can, and staying in touch with them. Salmān ibn ʿĀmir reported that the Prophet ﷺ said that charity to the poor brings the reward of charity, but when it is given to a relative it brings twice the reward, one for the gift and one for keeping the tie of kinship. Aḥmad, an-Nasāʾī and at-Tirmidhī recorded it. The rights of parents come first and those of relatives in general come after. One short explanation says that Allah commands you to keep ties with your kin.

**"orphans, the poor"**. Orphans are mentioned because they have lost the people who would have spent on them. Allah commands kindness and compassion toward orphans, and, according to a short explanation, the protection of their wealth. Their rights were taken up earlier in the surah. Mentioning them again under the general heading of relatives hints that helping orphaned children and helpless people is as necessary as helping one's own relatives. The poor have many needs and cannot find what meets them. Allah orders that they be helped to get what they need in a sufficient way that ends their lack. A short explanation tells people to give alms to the needy. More on the destitute and the poor is found in Sūrat Barāʾah (9:60).

**"near and distant neighbours"**. The Arabic word is jār, neighbour, in two kinds, the near and the far. Ibn ʿAbbās said that the near neighbour is the one who is also a relative and the far neighbour is one who is not a relative. Qatādah, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, Zayd ibn Aslam, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, Maymūn ibn Mihrān, Mujāhid and ʿIkrimah said much the same. Most commentators say that the near neighbour lives next to your house and the far one at some distance. Others say that the near neighbour is a Muslim and the far one a non-Muslim. Mujāhid also said that "the neighbour who is a stranger" means the companion during travel. Another explanation counts everyone who works beside you in a job or trade as a distant neighbour.

The words of the Qur'an allow all these meanings. It is reasonable to have degrees among neighbours, based on kinship or on religion. But it is agreed that a neighbour, near or far, related or not, Muslim or not, has the basic right to be helped and cared for as far as one can. A person who has another right beside his right as a neighbour takes precedence. The Prophet ﷺ explained that some neighbours have one right, others two and others three. The neighbour with one right is a non-Muslim who is not a relative. The neighbour with two is a neighbour and a Muslim. The one with three is a neighbour, a Muslim and a relative. Another explanation gives the three rights of the neighbour who is a relative as kinship, Islam and neighbourhood, and the two rights of the other as Islam and neighbourhood.

**"close friends"**. This is "the companion at your side". The Arabic reads literally "the one by your side". It includes a travel companion who sits next to you on a train, plane, bus or in a car, and anyone who sits with you at a gathering. A colleague at work is also named in one explanation. Another says it means one's wife at home. In that case Allah commands kindness toward her. In a short explanation it is the friend you spend time with.

**"˹needy˺ travellers"**. This is the wayfarer, a person who comes to you, or becomes your guest, while you are travelling or he is. Since this stranger, Muslim or not, has no relative or friend around, the Qur'an takes his situation as a human being into account and makes his right an obligation. Treat him as well as you can. The wayfarer is also the person cut off on a journey, a traveller who has no means to complete it. Another explanation takes it as the duty to honour a guest. The right of a guest is to be honoured for three days. The honour shown after the third day counts as a charity from the host.

**"and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession"**. This is a command to be kind to them because they are weak and are held as captives by others. Their right to good treatment is equally binding. One must not fall short in giving them food and clothing, and they must not be given tasks beyond their strength. The same rules cover servants and employees, by analogy and on the strength of the Prophet's ﷺ sayings. They too have this right. There must be no stinginess or delay in paying their salary and giving them meals, and they must not be overloaded.

**"Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful"**. The proud person is arrogant and insolent, and he brags to others. He considers himself better than everyone else and thinks highly of himself, though he is nothing before Allah and is disliked by people. Mujāhid said that "proud" means arrogant, and "boastful" means one who boasts of what he has and does not thank Allah. This shows that such a person boasts to people about a blessing Allah gave him but is ungrateful to Allah for it. One explanation adds that he is proud in his walk and shows only wantonness and arrogance toward Allah's servants. Another says the conceited are those who boast before others of what they have been given.

This closing sentence ties together all that came before. Whoever falls short in the rights of the eight kinds of people named must be one of the arrogant and proud, those with an offensively inflated sense of superiority, who love to lord it over others. May Allah keep all Muslims safe from it. Abū Rajāʾ al-Harawī said that you find that the mean are also proud and boastful, and that those who are undutiful to their parents are arrogant and wretched too. He recited the words of the Qur'an about a mother: "and to be kind to my mother. He has not made me arrogant or defiant" (19:32). A man once asked the Prophet ﷺ, "O Messenger of Allah, advise me." He said, "Keep away from letting your garment hang below the ankles, because it comes from arrogance, and Allah does not like arrogance."

### The neighbour's rights in the hadiths

Aḥmad recorded from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

> "Jibrīl kept reminding me about the neighbour's right until I thought he would give him a share in the inheritance."

The two Ṣaḥīḥs recorded it. Aḥmad also recorded it from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr. Abū Dāwūd and at-Tirmidhī recorded it, and at-Tirmidhī called it ḥasan gharīb by this route. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ reported that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> "The best of friends to Allah are those who are best to their friends, and the best of neighbours to Allah are those who are best to their neighbours."

At-Tirmidhī said that it is ḥasan gharīb. It is also reported that the best person is the one who is best to his neighbours, and that it is not permitted for a neighbour to eat his fill while the neighbour next door goes hungry.

Aḥmad recorded that al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad said the Messenger of Allah ﷺ asked his Companions, "What do you say about adultery?" They said, "It is forbidden. Allah and His Messenger forbade it, so it is forbidden until the Day of Resurrection." He said:

> "For a man to commit adultery with the wife of his neighbour is worse than committing it with ten women."

Then he asked, "What do you say about theft?" They said, "It is forbidden, since Allah and His Messenger forbade it." He said:

> "For a man to steal from his neighbour is worse than stealing from ten houses."

Only Aḥmad recorded this. A similar hadith is in the two Ṣaḥīḥs. Ibn Masʿūd said, "I asked, 'O Messenger of Allah, what is the greatest sin?' He said, 'To set up a rival to Allah when He alone created you.' I said, 'Then what?' He said, 'To kill your child out of fear that he will share your food.' I said, 'Then what?' He said, 'To commit adultery with your neighbour's wife.'" Al-Bukhārī narrated that ʿĀʾishah asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, "I have two neighbours. To which should I send my gift?" He said, "To the one whose door is closer to you."

### The person next to you

The Sharīʿah makes the right of the companion at your side as binding as the rights of near and distant neighbours. This covers the person who sits next to you for a short time, in a meeting or on a journey. It includes everyone, Muslim or not, related or not, equally. The lowest degree of this kindness is that you cause no pain to anyone by word or deed. You say nothing that hurts his feelings, and you do nothing that embarrasses, annoys or troubles him, such as blowing cigarette smoke toward his face, spitting beside him, or sitting in a way that leaves him hardly any room.

If people followed this instruction, the usual disputes between passengers on trains, especially in countries with crowded transport, would end. Everyone would think that he needs just enough room for one person. That much is his right, and he has no right to more. The person beside him has as much right to sit on the train as he does.

### Treating servants and employees

In an authentic hadith the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, during the illness that led to his death, went on advising his community, "The prayer, the prayer, and those whom your right hands possess." He repeated it until his tongue went still. Aḥmad recorded that al-Miqdām ibn Maʿdīkarib said that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

> "What you feed yourself is charity for you. What you feed your children is charity for you. What you feed your wife is charity for you, and what you feed your servant is charity for you."

An-Nasāʾī recorded it, with an authentic chain. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr once asked his caretaker, "Have you given the slaves their food?" The man said no. He said, "Go and give it to them, for the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, 'It is enough sin for a person to withhold food from those he is responsible for.'" Muslim recorded it. Abū Hurayrah reported that the Prophet ﷺ said that a slave has the right to food and clothing and not to be given more work than he can bear, and Muslim recorded that too. He also said that when your servant brings your meal, and you do not seat him with you, you should at least give him a mouthful or two, or a meal or two, since he prepared it. Al-Bukhārī has this wording.

### Arrogance in the hadiths

Many hadiths give stern warnings against arrogance and the vainglory of the days of ignorance. Ibn Masʿūd said that the Prophet ﷺ said that a person with faith the weight of a mustard seed in his heart will not enter Hell, and a person with arrogance the weight of a mustard seed in his heart will not enter Paradise. This is in Mishkāt (page 433), from Muslim. In another hadith, which also defines arrogance, he said that whoever has in his heart arrogance the weight of a particle will not enter Paradise. Someone asked, "What about a man who likes his clothes and shoes to be good?" He said, "Allah is beautiful and loves beauty. Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people." This is also in Mishkāt (page 433), from Muslim.

### The inner reading: two kinds of tawḥīd

The verse opens with tawḥīd, and that shows its weight. Tawḥīd is where all knowledge begins and the secret behind every act of knowing Allah. It is the ground of the religion, the footing of a Muslim's life, and the line that divides Allah's enemies from His friends. An act of obedience without it carries no weight and ends in darkness and captivity. A fault that goes along with it ends in closeness and light. To say that Allah is one is one thing, and to be one for Him is another. Saying it belongs to the person who submits. Being it belongs to the one who truly knows Him.

The submitter's tawḥīd keeps the devil away, cleans off sin and opens the heart. It listens to advice, opens the door and bears fruit. The knower's tawḥīd goes further. It cuts the ties of attachment, rinses away creatures and brings realities to light. It wipes out the habits of human nature and burns the veil of mortality, so that intimacy can blow in like a breeze, the call from before time can arrive, and one friend can look at the Friend.

The submitter's tawḥīd means testifying to Allah's one Essence, His pure attributes, and His names and marks, none of which has a beginning. He is the only God, the maker of heaven and earth, and nothing in the universe is as faithful as He is. He is Lord above everything in rank and beyond everything in essence and attributes, the greatest Lord from first to last. What reason calls impossible is not beyond His power. His power needs no tools, and His self-sufficiency undergoes no change. He is safe from vanishing in His kingdom and exalted in essence and attributes. Everything created shows some mark of lack or fault, but the Eternal Maker is free of all lack, fault and defect. He neither eats nor sleeps, and no new thing or changing state takes place in Him. His attributes are not new, and He does not accept alteration. He is before "when", acts before there is action, creates before there is creation and is able before anything is made. In essence, attributes and perfection, He has always been exactly as He is now.

Shaykh al-Islām Anṣārī summed up the tawḥīd of the Muslims in three phrases: affirm the attributes without going too far, deny any likeness without stripping them of meaning, and go on by the plain sense without mixing. To affirm means to accept and submit to everything Allah said about Himself and everything Muṣṭafā, the Prophet ﷺ, said plainly about Him. A person keeps to the outward sense, makes no comparison, does not twist the wording, and does not roam around it in imagination, because Allah can be known but not imagined. He does not ask how it can be so, and he seeks neither strained reasoning nor interpretation. He does not shy away from saying it or hearing it. He knows that, in truth, all that creatures can know of Allah's attributes is the bare name. Perceiving it means accepting it. What is required is submission, and what explains it is remembrance.

So know Allah's essence by Allah's measure, and not as creatures grasp it by reason. Know His attributes as they befit Him, and not as creatures think about them. Know His power on His own scale, without the tricks of created beings. He is one Being, outside imagination and beyond any description. He does what He wants, not out of need, for He has none, but by will, pure knowledge, wisdom that came first and power that cuts through. His word is true, His promise is right and His Messenger is trustworthy. His speech is in the earth as a reality, joined to Him always. His proof stands by it, His decree cannot be turned back, and His commands and prohibitions are firm. "The creation and the command belong to Him ˹alone˺. Blessed is Allah—Lord of all worlds" (7:54). This is tawḥīd as it was handed down and knowledge as it was reported. By it people reach Paradise, escape Hell and stay clear of the Real's anger. Its opposite is the great shirk, and whoever is held back from this handed-down tawḥīd stays in it, far from Allah's forgiveness.

The other tawḥīd belongs to the knowers and is the ornament of the truly sincere. It cannot be spoken of by tongue or heart, since it is not made of water and clay. What can a person who voices it say with his tongue? His condition is itself his tongue, and any attempt to put it in words is a slander against it. This tawḥīd does not come from creation. It is a mark from the Real, a raising of the heart from the dead and a plundering of the spirit. No one has ever declared the unity of the One, since everyone who declares it denies it. The tawḥīd of those who describe it is a debt the One has cancelled. His tawḥīd is His own declaration of unity, and whoever describes it goes astray in the description.

The elder of the Ṭarīqah prayed, "O Allah, the knower knows You by Your light and cannot express the brilliance of finding. The one who voices tawḥīd knows You by the light of nearness and burns in the fire of love without turning from joy." He went on, "O Lord, he seeks You in the very act of finding You, since he is drowned in bewilderment, and he cannot tell seeking from finding." Pity the one who knows Him through His works and the one who looks for Him through proofs. From works one can ask only what they have room for, and from proofs only what fits them. The truth of tawḥīd cannot fit on the tongue of reports. It is not the tawḥīd reached by deduction and effort, or proved by evidence and works, or won by any means. It turns up in the middle of heedlessness, comes without being asked and keeps the servant busy with itself. Lit by contemplation of the Near and the sight of togetherness, the servant gains from a love with no beginning and loses both worlds. The saying goes, "If seeing You costs the spirit, I will pay the spirit's loss with my spirit."

The elder also said, "O Allah, the mark of this work took the world from me and hid me even from the mark." He said, "Seeing You has left me without a spirit. Love for You took away profit, and I lost both worlds." Then he asked, "O Allah, do You know what has made me glad? I did not come to You by myself." The passage ends there.

### In short

- Allah's oneness comes first, because only the fear of Him makes a person honour the rights of others, watched or not.
- Parents come next, with iḥsān in all it includes, then relatives, orphans, the poor, near and far neighbours, the person beside you, the wayfarer, and servants and employees. A neighbour has the basic right to help even if non-Muslim or unrelated, and a person with more than one tie has more than one right.
- The Prophet ﷺ said that Jibrīl pressed the neighbour's right so hard that he thought it might become a share of inheritance. He forbade withholding food from a servant and warned that the arrogant will not enter Paradise.
- Allah does not like the proud and boastful, and arrogance is the root of falling short in these duties.
- Inwardly, tawḥīd has two levels, the tawḥīd of those who submit, which is to affirm Allah's attributes without likeness, and the tawḥīd of the knowers, which words cannot carry.
