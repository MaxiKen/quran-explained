After the rulings of fasting and jihād, Allah explains the rites, and the eleventh injunction in the series on righteousness that began in verse 177 concerns the Ḥajj. "Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺, then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford. And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination." The verse continues: "But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment ˹requiring shaving˺, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering. In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford." And it ends: "Whoever cannot afford that ˹offering˺, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning ˹home˺—completing ten. These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House. And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment". Because the Ḥajj is tied to Makkah al-Mukarramah and the Kaʿbah, some related questions were covered under the subject of the qiblah in verses 125 to 128 of this sūrah. The saʿy between Ṣafā and Marwah was taken up in verse 158 as a corollary. Now verses 196 to 203, eight consecutive verses, present the injunctions and questions of Ḥajj and ʿumrah.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah."**. By the strong consensus of the Ummah the Ḥajj is a cardinal pillar of Islam, emphasised in many verses and numerous authentic hadiths. According to the majority it became obligatory in the third year of the Hijrah, the year of Uḥud, through the verse of Sūrat Āl ʿImrān which makes the pilgrimage to the House a right of Allah upon people, stating its conditions and sternly warning those who neglect it despite ability. The present verse was revealed, by agreement, about the event of Ḥudaybiyyah. Therefore, its purpose is not to declare the obligation, already stated, but to convey special rulings governing Ḥajj and ʿumrah. To complete them means to finish their rites once begun, which is why the verse goes on to speak of being prevented, and the scholars agree that beginning Ḥajj or ʿumrah makes finishing them binding, just as voluntary prayers and fasts must be completed once started. Sūrat Āl ʿImrān mentions only Ḥajj. This verse mentions ʿumrah without saying whether it is obligatory in itself. Therefore, the question is not settled here. Jābir asked the Prophet ﷺ whether ʿumrah was wājib and he replied that it was not, but that performing it was better and meritorious, a report of al-Tirmidhī, who grades it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ, Aḥmad and al-Bayhaqī, and on this basis Abū Ḥanīfah, together with Mālik and others hold ʿumrah to be a sunnah rather than wājib. Makḥūl said that completing means beginning them from the mīqāt, the stations the Prophet ﷺ appointed for assuming iḥrām. ʿAbd al-Razzāq relates from al-Zuhrī that ʿUmar explained it as performing each separately, with ʿumrah outside the months of Ḥajj. This is because Allah says the Ḥajj is in the well-known months. Al-Suddī took it as maintaining their performance. Ibn ʿAbbās said that Ḥajj is ʿArafāt and ʿumrah is ṭawāf. Al-Aʿmash relates from Ibrāhīm that ʿAlqamah cited Ibn Masʿūd's reading "complete the Ḥajj and ʿumrah to the House", so that one does not go beyond the House during the ʿumrah, and Ibrāhīm added that Saʿīd ibn Jubayr attributed the same to Ibn ʿAbbās, while Sufyān and al-Thawrī relate the reading "perform the Ḥajj and ʿumrah to the House" from Ibrāhīm and ʿAlqamah. "For Allah" means with sincerity, so that He accepts them, carrying the Ḥajj to full completion and the ʿumrah to the Sacred House. Ḥajj takes place once a year in Dhū al-Ḥijjah, while ʿumrah may be performed at any time. Any worship done purely for Allah is valuable in His sight.

**"But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺, then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford."**. These words came down in the sixth year of the Hijrah, the year of Ḥudaybiyyah, when the polytheists of Quraysh stopped the Messenger of Allah ﷺ from reaching the House and Sūrat al-Fatḥ was revealed. The Muslims were allowed to slaughter the sacrificial animals they had with them, seventy camels, and to shave their heads and leave iḥrām. When the Prophet ﷺ ordered them to shave and leave iḥrām they hesitated, expecting the order to be abrogated, until they saw him go out having shaved his head, and then followed him. Some only shortened their hair, which is why he prayed for Allah's mercy on those who shaved, and only at the third asking added those who shortened. Every seven men shared one camel. They were fourteen hundred, camped at Ḥudaybiyyah outside the Sacred Area, though it is also said they were within its boundary. Allah knows best. The ransom for release from iḥrām is thus the sacrifice of whatever animal is easy to obtain, a goat or sheep, a cow or a camel. Imam Mālik reports that ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib used to say the offering includes a sheep. Ibn ʿAbbās said that the hady comprises eight kinds of animal, camels, cows, goats and sheep, and ʿAbd al-Razzāq relates from him that it means as much as one can afford, al-ʿAwfī relating from him camels for one who can, otherwise cows or sheep, while Hishām ibn ʿUrwah relates from his father that it depends on the price. The proof that a sheep suffices when one is prevented is that Allah required whatever hady is available. The hady is any kind of cattle, which is the view of Ibn ʿAbbās, the Prophet's ﷺ cousin and the scholar of tafsīr, and the Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from ʿĀʾishah that the Prophet ﷺ once offered sheep as hady. Being prevented (ḥaṣr) is not confined to enemy action. Imam Aḥmad records from al-Ḥajjāj ibn ʿAmr al-Anṣārī that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that whoever suffers a broken bone or lameness has left iḥrām and must perform Ḥajj again. The account also states that when he mentioned this to Ibn ʿAbbās and Abū Hurayrah they confirmed it. The Four Collections report it, the versions of Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah adding illness. Ibn Abī Ḥātim relates that Ibn Masʿūd, together with Ibn al-Zubayr, ʿAlqamah, together with Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib and ʿUrwah ibn al-Zubayr, as well as Mujāhid, together with al-Nakhaʿī, together with ʿAṭāʾ and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, held that prevention covers an enemy, illness or a fracture, and al-Thawrī said it covers everything that harms a person. Abū Ḥanīfah and other jurists likewise hold that one unable to complete the rites through sickness may use this concession, since "if prevented" is general. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from ʿĀʾishah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ visited Ḍubāʿah bint al-Zubayr ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, who said she wished to perform Ḥajj but was ill, and told her to perform it and stipulate that her place of release would be wherever Allah halted her. Muslim records the like from Ibn ʿAbbās. Therefore, making such a condition is permitted. The Prophet's ﷺ own practice shows that although one may leave iḥrām after the sacrifice in such an emergency, it remains obligatory to perform the Ḥajj or ʿumrah afresh as qaḍāʾ when circumstances allow, which is why he and his Companions performed the ʿumrah the following year in place of the one missed at Ḥudaybiyyah.

**"And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination."**. This clause continues "Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage" and does not depend on the clause about being prevented, as Ibn Jarīr mistakenly held. In the year of Ḥudaybiyyah the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions shaved and sacrificed outside the Ḥaram, but in normal conditions, when the House can be reached safely, no one may shave until the hady reaches its place of sacrifice, after which the rites of Ḥajj, ʿumrah or both are concluded. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record that Ḥafṣah asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ why the people had left their iḥrām for ʿumrah while he had not. He replied that he had matted his hair and garlanded his hady and would not leave iḥrām until he had sacrificed. Since release from iḥrām is lawful only after shaving or close-trimming the hair, the verse makes shaving the signal of release and so proves that shaving or trimming in iḥrām is forbidden. "Reaching its destination" means, according to Abū Ḥanīfah, that the animal is taken into the sacred precincts and slaughtered there, personally or through someone authorised to act on one's behalf. Al-Shāfiʿī likewise places the sacrifice within the enclosure, made with the intention of release and divided among the needy, after which the head is shaved. Another view is that one prevented from entering the Sanctuary sacrifices wherever he has been stopped, but if he can enter he does so on the Day of Sacrifice, the tenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, or at least within the days of tashrīq, the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth.

**"But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment ˹requiring shaving˺, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering."**. If one is forced by sickness or by irritating lice to shave the head or any part of the body, one may do so to the extent of the need but must redeem it by fasting, charity (ṣadaqah) or sacrifice. Al-Bukhārī records from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Aṣbahānī that ʿAbdullāh ibn Maʿqil sat with Kaʿb ibn ʿUjrah in the mosque of Kūfah and asked him about the fidyah of fasting. Kaʿb said the verse was revealed about his own case in particular but applied to everyone in general: he was carried to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ with lice falling thickly on his face. The Prophet ﷺ said he had not imagined his suffering had reached such a point, asked whether he could afford a sheep, and when he said no, told him to fast three days or feed six poor people half a ṣāʿ each and shave his head. So a general ruling was derived from a particular case. Imam Aḥmad records Kaʿb saying that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ passed him while he was lighting a fire under a pot with lice falling from his head or eyelids, asked whether they troubled him, and told him to shave and then fast three days, feed six poor people or sacrifice an animal, the narrator Ayyūb noting that he did not know which option was mentioned first. The Qur'an lists the options from the easiest to the hardest, fasting, feeding, sacrifice, while the Prophet ﷺ advised Kaʿb beginning with the most rewarding, a sheep, then feeding six, then three fasts, and each text suits its place. The sacrifice must be offered within the Ḥaram, where the feeding is also directed to the poor of the Sanctuary. By contrast, fasting and charity have no fixed place. The Qur'an does not state the number of fasts or the amount of charity, but the hadith of Kaʿb fixes three days or half a ṣāʿ of wheat to each of six needy persons, about 1.632 kilograms or its value, also reckoned as three cubic measures of the staple food of the town. The word "or" shows free choice. The case of one who shaves without excuse is included all the more. Redemption is likewise due from one who, with or without excuse, enjoys such things as scent, clothing or oils in iḥrām even without shaving. One reading adds that a sick person unable to remain where he is may return home before his offering reaches its place.

**"In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford."**. When you are secure from the enemy and recovered from sickness, and able to complete the rites, whoever joins ʿumrah to Ḥajj must offer whatever animal is available, the least being a sheep or a seventh share of a camel or cow. One reading takes "in safety" as the command to make up the following year what Allah made obligatory, whether Ḥajj or ʿumrah. Before Islam the Arabs would not combine Ḥajj and ʿumrah in the months of Ḥajj, from the start of Shawwāl, thinking it a sin. This verse corrects their misconception. Combining them takes two forms. In the first, one enters iḥrām for both from the mīqāt and remains in it until the last day of Ḥajj, which the hadith calls qirān. In the second, one enters iḥrām for ʿumrah alone, performs its rites in Makkah, leaves iḥrām, and then on the eighth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, when setting out for Minā, enters iḥrām for Ḥajj from within the Ḥaram, which is tamattuʿ in the technical sense. Literally tamattuʿ means enjoying the benefit of combining the two. Therefore, the Qur'anic words cover both methods, and the authentic hadiths show both, some narrators saying the Prophet ﷺ performed tamattuʿ and others qirān, with no difference between them concerning the hady. Between the ʿumrah and the Ḥajj the pilgrim enjoys what is forbidden in iḥrām, scent and clothing, until he enters iḥrām for Ḥajj in its months. The offering is then sacrificed after entering iḥrām, ideally on the Day of Sacrifice. Those who combine the two must show gratitude for being allowed two acts of worship in one journey. The sacrifice is that expression. A cow is permitted as well, for al-Awzāʿī reports from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ slaughtered cows on behalf of his wives when they performed tamattuʿ, as Abū Bakr ibn Marduwyah records. This proves that tamattuʿ is legislated. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record that ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn said they performed tamattuʿ in the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime, that the Qur'an was revealed about it, that nothing came down forbidding it and he did not forbid it until he died. The account also states that a certain man then said what he wished by his own opinion, al-Bukhārī noting that he meant ʿUmar. It is authentically reported that ʿUmar discouraged tamattuʿ, saying that if they followed Allah's Book they should complete the pilgrimage, yet he never called it unlawful. He only wished people to visit the House more often, making Ḥajj in its months and ʿumrah through the rest of the year, as he himself explained.

**"Whoever cannot afford that ˹offering˺, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning ˹home˺—completing ten."**. Whoever finds no animal, because none is available or he lacks the means, fasts three days in the Ḥajj season, in a state of iḥrām, completing them by the ninth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah. Al-ʿAwfī relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that the three days are fasted before the day of ʿArafah. If ʿArafah is the third day the fast is complete, with seven more on returning home. Abū Isḥāq relates from Wabarah from Ibn ʿUmar that one fasts the day before Tarwiyah, the day of Tarwiyah, which is the eighth, and the day of ʿArafah, the ninth, and Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad relates the same from his father from ʿAlī. For this reason one should enter iḥrām before the seventh of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, and ideally before the sixth, since fasting on ʿArafah is discouraged. Whoever has not fasted the three days, or some of them, before the Day of ʿĪd may fast them in the days of tashrīq. ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿUmar said, as al-Bukhārī reports, that fasting the days of tashrīq was allowed only for those who had no hady. ʿAlī said that whoever did not fast the three days during Ḥajj should fast them in tashrīq, a view shared by ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr al-Laythī, together with ʿIkrimah, together with al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and ʿUrwah ibn al-Zubayr on the general wording. Muslim's report from Qutaybah al-Hudhalī that the days of tashrīq are days of eating, drinking and remembering Allah is general, while the report of ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿUmar is specific. In the view of the Companions and Abū Ḥanīfah, one who fails to fast the three days during Ḥajj must offer a sacrifice instead, arranged in the Ḥaram through someone he knows as soon as possible, as al-Jaṣṣāṣ notes. As for the seven, there are two opinions: when one returns to the camping grounds, or when one reaches home. ʿAbd al-Razzāq relates from Sālim that Ibn ʿUmar explained it as going back to one's family. The same is reported from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, together with Abū al-ʿĀliyah and Mujāhid, from ʿAṭāʾ, together with ʿIkrimah and al-Ḥasan, and from Qatādah, together with al-Zuhrī and al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, though the seven may also be completed in Makkah or on the way home at a time of one's choosing. Al-Bukhārī records from Sālim ibn ʿAbdullāh that Ibn ʿUmar described the Farewell Ḥajj: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ performed tamattuʿ, joining ʿumrah to Ḥajj, drove his hady from Dhū al-Ḥulayfah, began with iḥrām for ʿumrah and then for Ḥajj. The people did the same, some bringing animals and some not. On reaching Makkah he told whoever had brought a hady not to leave iḥrām until completing the Ḥajj. Whoever had not to perform ṭawāf of the Kaʿbah and between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah, shave or shorten the hair, leave iḥrām, and later assume iḥrām for Ḥajj and offer a sacrifice, fasting three days during Ḥajj and seven on returning home if he could not afford one, as the Two Ṣaḥīḥs record. The words "completing ten" emphasise the ruling in the Arabic manner of saying "I saw with my eyes, heard with my ears and wrote with my hand", as in "winged birds soaring in the sky" (6:38), "nor could you write at all" (29:48) and "thirty nights then added another ten—completing his Lord's term of forty nights" (7:142). It is also said that the phrase stresses that ten days, no fewer, must be fasted.

**"These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House."**. This concerns the residents of the Ḥaram area, who do not perform tamattuʿ. ʿAbd al-Razzāq relates from Ṭāwūs that tamattuʿ is for those whose families do not live in the Ḥaram and not for the people of Makkah. The account also states that Ibn ʿAbbās said the same. Residents have no need of it. This is because they are constantly there and can perform ʿumrah and ṭawāf freely at any other time of year. The prohibition on combining Ḥajj and ʿumrah in the Ḥajj months is thus restricted to those living within the limits of the mīqāt, who can easily return for ʿumrah after the season, while for those coming from beyond the mīqāt it is permitted. This is because a separate journey for ʿumrah from such distances is not easy. The mīqāt are the stations appointed by Allah on every route to Makkah, at which everyone proceeding for Ḥajj or ʿumrah must assume iḥrām. Passing them without iḥrām is a sin. "Those who do not live near the Sacred House" means those whose families live outside these stations.

**"And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment"**. Fear Allah in what He commands and forbids, following His sacred law and respecting His limits. Taqwā, a Qur'anic term with no English equivalent, denotes fearing and abstaining from anything against the rules Allah has set. The warning is that His punishment is severe for whoever knowingly acts against His injunctions. Pilgrims today are widely negligent of this: many make no effort to learn the rules of Ḥajj and ʿumrah. Many who know them do not follow them strictly, while bad guides or careless companions make them miss even obligatory acts, let alone the sunnah and etiquette of the pilgrimage. May Allah grant us all the ability to correct what we do. Pilgrims carry provisions for the road, but the best provision is to live in fear of Allah, for taqwā is not the observance of outward forms but a state of heightened consciousness in which the mind is filled with Allah's greatness and beauty. A believer does not live for his desires but for the goal of abstaining from everything Allah has forbidden and from misdeeds and quarrels with his fellows, and the Ḥajj serves this moral training.

### The House and its guests

In the devotional reading, Wahb ibn Munabbih relates that Allah revealed to Ādam: I am the Lord of the world and its folk, the Creator of all, the King who does what He desires. I am the Lord of Bakkah. Those who dwell there are My neighbours. Those who visit it are My delegates and guests under My protection. I will people this spot with the folk of heaven and earth, who will come in droves from every direction, their hair dishevelled and their faces dusty from the hardship of the road, crying "Allah is greater" and "Here I am", the earth coloured with the blood of sacrifice. Whoever visits this House in purity is My guest and one of those near to Me. It befits My majesty to honour him and bring him to forgiveness through the gift of mercy. Among your children is a prophet named Ibrāhīm, My bosom friend, by whose hand I will establish it and whom I will command to build it, making its eminence appear, bringing its watering place to light, marking its sanctuary and teaching him how to worship Me there. After him the world's folk will keep it inhabited with reverence in their hearts. Next comes Muḥammad the Arab, Seal of the Prophets and lamp of heaven and earth, whose birthplace and origin it will be, the place where revelation falls on him and the home of his honour, its watering place, deputyship and rule placed in his hand. After that, love for it will be set by Me in the hearts of the faithful in every corner of the earth. They will come bareheaded and barefoot, their goods set aside and their lives in their hands, to circle the House and ask My forgiveness. If anyone asks what I will do with them, answer that My knowledge is with them, their souls find Me and their hearts have My presence, the cure of their pain, hidden from their eyes but face to face with their spirits.

The ḥajj of the common people is one thing and the ḥajj of the elect another. The first sets out for the street of the Friend and the second for the face of the Friend. One goes to the Friend's house, the other to the Friend. The common people go with their souls and see doors and walls, while the elect go with their spirits and find conversation and vision. Whoever goes with the soul finds suffering and carries burdens to circle the Kaʿbah, and whoever goes with the spirit finds rest and ease, and the Kaʿbah itself circles his house. Hence the story of Ibrāhīm Khawāṣṣ, who in his deprivation once found himself wandering bewildered in Byzantium, as the Men of the Path fall anywhere. The news there was that the king's daughter had gone mad and her father had bound her as madmen are bound, the physicians helpless, while she sometimes breathed a cold sigh and rained hot tears, now weeping, now laughing. He went to the king's door and said he had come to cure the sick girl. The king asked if he was a physician. He said he had a Lord who was a physician. The king pointed to the battlements, where severed heads were set. Said that this was the recompense of anyone who failed to cure her. Ibrāhīm said he had nothing to fear, for how can a lover fear his own destruction? The king sent him to his daughter's room. Before he had set foot inside he heard the words "Tell the believing men to lower their gaze" (24:30). He stood still, bewildered by her state, and heard another call: son of Khawāṣṣ, this drink only deepens thirst. This food only increases consternation! From behind the curtain he asked her what this state and this ecstasy were. She told him that she had been sitting in joy among her slave girls and friends when suddenly a pain fell into her heart and a sorrow reached her spirit, so that she was annihilated from herself and enraptured. Before she returned to her room the pain had taken hold and the work was complete. When she came to some ease she found herself in chains, approved His judgment and was content with His decree, knowing that He wants no evil for His friends. Ibrāhīm proposed that they contrive a way for her to reach the Abode of Islam and cultivate the faith there. This is because it would be a shame to leave one so dear in the Abode of Unbelief. She answered that there was no manliness, son of Khawāṣṣ, in tending the faith where faith already reigned. The true man, she said, embraces Islam in the land of unbelief and nurtures it there in spirit and heart.

### In short

- Once begun, Ḥajj and ʿumrah must be completed. Ḥajj is a pillar made obligatory in 3 AH, while ʿumrah is a sunnah according to Abū Ḥanīfah and Mālik (the hadith of Jābir).
- Revealed at Ḥudaybiyyah, the verse allows one prevented by an enemy, and by extension illness or injury (al-Ḥajjāj ibn ʿAmr, together with al-Thawrī, Abū Ḥanīfah), to sacrifice whatever animal he can, even a sheep, and leave iḥrām, making up the rite later. A condition may be stipulated at iḥrām (Ḍubāʿah).
- No shaving before the offering reaches its place. Therefore, shaving in iḥrām is forbidden, and one forced to shave by illness or lice redeems it by three fasts, feeding six poor people half a ṣāʿ each, or a sheep (Kaʿb ibn ʿUjrah).
- Tamattuʿ and qirān, combining ʿumrah with Ḥajj in the Ḥajj months, are lawful for those from beyond the mīqāt, not for Makkans, and require a sacrifice or ten fasts, three in Ḥajj before ʿArafah or in tashrīq and seven after returning (the Farewell Ḥajj of Ibn ʿUmar, ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn). ʿUmar discouraged tamattuʿ without forbidding it.
- Taqwā is the pilgrim's best provision. Inwardly the pilgrims are Allah's guests, the common people reaching His house and the elect His face (Wahb ibn Munabbih, together with Ibrāhīm Khawāṣṣ).
