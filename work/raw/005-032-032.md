<<<VERSE 5:32>>>
The killing of Adam's son leads into a solemn statement about the sanctity of every life: "and whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity." The opposite comparison is equally severe. Whoever unjustly kills one person attacks the principle by which every person is protected.

The verse states what Allah ordained for the Children of Israel, then rebukes those who continued transgressing after receiving clear guidance. Knowledge of a sacred law is not enough when conduct contradicts it. Respect for life must govern what people permit themselves to do.

### Phrase by phrase

**"That is why We ordained for the Children of Israel"** connects the ordinance with Qabil's wrongful killing of Habil, Cain and Abel. Allah legislated and made the command known to the Children of Israel. A brief explanation places it in the Torah.

**"that whoever takes a life"** concerns the killing of a human being without a valid justification. The wording gives the life of one person immense weight. An innocent person cannot be treated as expendable merely because the number killed is small.

**"unless as a punishment for murder or mischief in the land"** distinguishes wrongful killing from the legal exceptions named. The explanations include retribution for murder and punishment for grave corruption, such as armed attacks, waylaying, treason or waging war. Some classical glosses also name unbelief, idolatry or fornication within their discussion of corruption. These are explanations within a legal framework, not permission for an individual to declare another person guilty and attack them.

**"it will be as if they killed all of humanity"** expresses the violation of life's sanctity. The protection that applies to this person is not a different principle from the one protecting everyone else. Treating one protected life as lawful to destroy breaks that common protection.

**"and whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity."** includes refraining from killing someone whose life Allah has made sacred and recognising that prohibition. It also includes protecting a person from oppression. The lives of others are safe from a person who honours the principle rather than making arbitrary exceptions for personal desire.

**"˹Although˺ Our messengers already came to them with clear proofs"** recalls Al-Bayyinat, evident signs and proofs. The messengers brought commands, prohibitions and miracles. The people were not left without knowledge of what Allah required.

**"many of them still transgressed afterwards through the land"** condemns exceeding the limits despite that instruction. The explanations mention killing, disbelief, associating partners with Allah and opposition to the messengers. The verse says many, not every member of the community.

### How can one killing be compared with killing everyone?

Ibn 'Abbas explains the comparison through violating or protecting the sanctity of a soul. The issue is not that one body numerically becomes all bodies. It is that the killer disregards a protection shared by every innocent life.

In a report through 'Ali bin Abi Talhah, Ibn 'Abbas explains saving life as not killing a person whose blood Allah has forbidden. Mujahid similarly says that the lives of people are safe from someone who refrains from killing them. Sa'id bin Jubayr frames the same principle through the blood of a Muslim: regarding one such life as forbidden or permitted reflects the attitude taken toward protected life generally.

Another report from Mujahid explains the comparison through the gravity of punishment. Intentional murder of a believing soul brings the warning of the Fire, Allah's anger, curse and tremendous punishment, described as the same threatened consequence as if all had been killed. A brief gloss likewise connects wrongful premeditated killing with Hell and sparing a life with Paradise.

These explanations emphasise sanctity and accountability rather than turning the verse into a calculation that makes further crimes unimportant. The first killing is already a grave violation. No numerical argument can make it a small matter.

### How did 'Uthman apply the warning during the siege?

Abu Hurayrah relates entering upon 'Uthman while he was besieged and offering to fight in his defence. 'Uthman asked whether he wished to kill all the people, including him. When Abu Hurayrah said no, 'Uthman invoked the principle that killing one person was as killing all.

He then allowed Abu Hurayrah to leave, praying that he would receive reward and remain free of the burden. Abu Hurayrah withdrew without fighting. The report is transmitted through Abu Salih by Al-A'mash and others.

The incident shows how seriously 'Uthman treated the danger of bloodshed around him. It is an account of his response in that siege, not a complete ruling governing every case of lawful defence.

### Why does a single attack threaten the wider society?

Respect for life is built through a long history of people recognising one another's protection. Violence breaks more than the immediate victim's security. It gives others reason to fear that the same principle will no longer protect them either.

The explanation extends this social concern to attacks on honour and property as well as life. One such incident should not be dismissed as irrelevant to everyone else. It indicates a breach in the safety on which all depend.

Those who establish violence as a practice do grave harm to the community. Once the habit of mutual respect has been broken, restoring it can take a long time. Protecting one threatened person therefore upholds something whose benefit reaches beyond that person alone.

### What contradiction is exposed in the people's conduct?

The account recalls Jewish groups in Al-Madinah, including Banu Qurayzah, An-Nadir and Qaynuqa', joining their respective allies among Aws and Khazraj in the wars of the period of ignorance. Afterward they would ransom captives and pay compensation for those killed.

Their concern for ransom did not excuse their earlier participation in killing and expulsion. Surah Al-Baqarah rebukes accepting one part of the scripture while acting against another: the same covenant that concerned captives had forbidden shedding one another's blood and driving people from their homes (2:84-85).

This is the force of the final reproach here. Clear signs and inherited knowledge should have restrained transgression. Religious concern displayed after the damage cannot make deliberate disregard of the earlier prohibition into obedience.

### In short

- Unjustly killing one person violates the shared sanctity on which every protected life depends.
- Saving life includes refusing forbidden bloodshed and protecting people from oppression.
- The named legal exceptions are not a licence for private accusation or violence.
- A single attack harms the wider community's security, while protecting one person upholds a principle that benefits everyone.
- Clear guidance increases responsibility. Selective observance cannot excuse violating the very covenant one claims to honour.
