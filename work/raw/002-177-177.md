<<<VERSE 2:177>>>
With this verse Sūrat al-Baqarah reaches its halfway mark and changes direction. Until now the message was addressed mostly to those who denied the Qur'an: its truth was established, those who accepted and rejected it were described, Allah's Oneness and the prophethood were proved, His favours on the descendants of Ibrāhīm were recounted up to the words "when Abraham was tested by his Lord" (2:124). Then the issue of the qiblah ran on until it was settled by the identification of Ṣafā and Marwah as a sign of Allah (verse 158), followed by the refutation of shirk in its roots and branches. The second half of the sūrah is primarily education of the Muslims in the principles and details of their religion. It opens with the comprehensive concept of birr, righteousness. "Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets;" then "who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives;" then "who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle." The verse ends: "It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of Allah˺". This verse contains many great wisdoms, encompassing rulings and correct beliefs. What follows to the end of the sūrah is an elaboration of it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west."**. Allah first commanded the believers to face Bayt al-Maqdis and then the Kaʿbah, and some Muslims, as well as some People of the Book, found the change difficult. This verse clarifies the wisdom behind the command: the objective is obedience to Allah, adherence to His commands, facing wherever He commands and implementing whatever He legislates. That is birr, taqwā and complete faith. Facing east or west is not in itself righteousness or obedience unless Allah has legislated it, just as He says of the sacrificial animals: "Neither their meat nor blood reaches Allah. Rather, it is your piety that reaches Him" (22:37). Abū al-ʿĀliyah said that the Jews faced west in prayer and the Christians east. Allah answered the claims both made. Al-Ḥasan and al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas said the same. The debate over the qiblah is thus closed in a unique way: faith cannot be reduced to turning west or east, for these are mere directions and cannot be made the whole object of faith to the exclusion of the other injunctions of the Sharīʿah. The address may be to Jews, Christians and Muslims together: real righteousness and reward lie in obedience to Allah, and whatever direction He appoints becomes correct and meritorious by that fact alone. While the command was to face Bayt al-Maqdis, obeying it earned reward. Now that the command is to face the House of Allah in Makkah, obeying this earns reward. One reading takes "righteousness" here to mean faith, and another to mean "all of righteousness". Al-birr is also read al-barr, the dutiful person.

**"Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets;"**. Al-Thawrī recited this phrase and said that what follows it are the types of birr. He spoke truly, for whoever acquires the qualities named embraces all of Islam and every kind of righteousness. Believing in Allah is believing that He alone deserves worship and trusting Him in the full sense. Believing in the Last Day is believing in resurrection after death and giving the Hereafter priority over this world. The angels are the emissaries between Allah and His messengers, running the world at His command. The Books are the revealed Scriptures given to the prophets, finalised by the most honourable of them, the Qur'an, which supersedes and abrogates all earlier Books, contains every type of righteousness and the way to happiness in both worlds, and testifies to all the prophets from the first to the last, Muḥammad ﷺ. Believing in the prophets is accepting these mortals as Allah's chosen messengers, all of them without distinction. Faith in these matters must so pervade a believer that it issues in the deeds that follow.

**"who give charity out of their cherished wealth"**. They give money away while desiring it and loving it, and often while having little of it. The Ṣaḥīḥayn record that Abū Hurayrah related the Prophet's ﷺ saying that the best charity is what you give while healthy and thrifty, hoping to become rich and fearing poverty. Allah praises those who "give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive" (76:8), saying "We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you" (76:9), and says: "You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish" (3:92). The Anṣār who "give ˹the emigrants˺ preference over themselves even though they may be in need" (59:9) represent a still higher rank. This is because they give away what they themselves need. By contrast, those described here give what they covet. The phrase "out of love for it" (ʿalā ḥubbihi) has three possible meanings. The pronoun may refer to Allah, in which case spending must not be driven by material motives or display but by love for Allah, with the perfect sincerity (ikhlāṣ) His majesty requires. It may refer to the wealth, in which case only the part of one's possessions that one loves earns merit. Giving away cast-offs in the name of charity is no charity, though giving them to someone who can use them is better than throwing them away. Or it may refer to the act of giving, meaning that the heart should be fully content with what is spent, rather than the hand giving while the heart aches. Al-Jaṣṣāṣ suggested that all three meanings may be included.

**"to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives;"**. Relatives have the first right to one's charity, as the hadith says: ṣadaqah to the poor is charity, but ṣadaqah to relatives is both charity and the nurturing of kinship. This is because they are the most deserving of your kindness. Orphans are children who have lost their fathers while still young and weak, before the age of work, with no one to look after them. The miskīn (plural al-masākīn) is one who lacks sufficient food, clothing or shelter and should be given enough to meet his needs. The Ṣaḥīḥayn record from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said the miskīn is not the one who goes around and is satisfied with a date or two or a bite or two, but the one who does not have enough and whom people do not notice so as to give him charity. He is the one who shies away from asking. The wayfarer is the traveller who runs out of money and is given what will take him home. Whoever sets out on a permissible journey is given what he needs for the journey and the return. Guests are included: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported Ibn ʿAbbās as saying that the son of the road is the guest hosted by Muslims, and Mujāhid, together with Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, together with Abū Jaʿfar al-Bāqir, together with al-Ḥasan, together with Qatādah, together with aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, together with al-Zuhrī, together with al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said similarly. Those who ask are those who beg and are given from zakāh and general charity. Freeing captives means slaves who seek to buy their freedom by contract but cannot find the money, as well as captives, and one reading adds those who fight in Allah's way. The first five recipients are introduced in one way and this last head in another, with the preposition "in", to show that the money does not pass into the slave's hands as his own to spend at will, but is spent on buying slaves from their masters and setting them free.

**"who establish prayer, pay alms-tax"**. Those who pray on time and give the prayer its due of bowing, prostration, attention and humility, perfecting the five daily prayers. The zakāh is the obligatory charity due on wealth, as Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān stated. The term also covers what was given as charity before zakāh was made obligatory. Two forms of spending other than zakāh have been mentioned before zakāh itself, perhaps because of the common negligence of those rights on the assumption that paying zakāh is enough. Financial obligations do not end with zakāh, as al-Jaṣṣāṣ and al-Qurṭubī note. Spending on relatives who have a valid excuse for not earning is necessary. If a needy person is dying of poverty after you have paid your zakāh, it becomes obligatory to save his life on the spot. Building mosques and schools for religious education also belongs to these obligations. The difference is that zakāh has its own detailed law that must be followed in all conditions. By contrast, these other duties depend on need: where there is need, spending is obligatory. Where there is none, it is not.

**"and keep the pledges they make;"**. They fulfil their covenants with their Lord and with other people, as in "those who honour Allah's covenant, never breaking the pledge" (13:20). The opposite is hypocrisy. This is because a hadith says the signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks his promise. When he is entrusted he betrays the trust, and in another version, when he vows he breaks his vow and when he disputes he is foul. Here the style changes from verbs to nouns, which signifies a continuing habit of keeping one's word, since an occasional fulfilment, which even a disbeliever or sinner manages now and then, does not qualify a person for this list. Keeping promises is chosen to represent all dealings with people because abiding by one's undertakings is the essence of every transaction, buying and selling, leasing, renting and partnership. The true believer treats a contract with people as a covenant with Allah.

**"and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle."**. Patient in extreme poverty, fear and hardship, in illness, pain and hunger, and on the battlefield when facing the enemy without fleeing, as Ibn Masʿūd and Ibn ʿAbbās explained, along with Abū al-ʿĀliyah, together with Murrah al-Hamdānī and Mujāhid, and likewise Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, together with al-Ḥasan and Qatādah, with the same view from al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, together with al-Suddī, together with Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, together with Abū Mālik, together with aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and others. Of all the inner qualities only ṣabr is named, because patience means controlling the self and guarding it from evil. That is the essence of every inner deed, the means by which high morals are gained and low ones removed. The word for "the patient" is in the accusative of praise (naṣb ʿalā al-madḥ) rather than following the pattern of the preceding phrase, which means that among the righteous the patient deserve special praise, since patience gives special power for righteous deeds, and the suffering that accompanies these situations makes it all the more important.

**"It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of Allah˺"**. Whoever acquires these qualities is truthful in his faith and in his claim to righteousness, having realised faith in the heart, on the tongue and in deed. They are the muttaqūn, the God-fearing. This is because they avoid what is forbidden and perform what is commanded, keeping their pledges and refraining from breaking them.

### The chapters of righteousness

Birr is an umbrella word in Arabic for what is good in the absolute sense, combining every act of righteousness and obedience, inward and outward. Its essence is obedience. The verse stresses belief, charity, keeping promises and patience, which together contain the principles behind all the injunctions of the Qur'an: the articles of faith, the deeds that follow from them, and morals. These three departments are all housed in the verse. The articles of faith come first, under belief in Allah and the rest. Then comes acting on them, in worship, up to the payment of zakāh, and in dealings with people, under keeping pledges, concluding with morals under patience. The final statement is that those who follow all of this are true Muslims and the ones who may be called God-fearing. From here to the end of the sūrah the details of this comprehensive attribute unfold as needed: retaliation, bequests, fasting, jihād, Hajj, spending, menstruation, the oath of abstention, divorce, marriage, the waiting period (ʿiddah) and dower (mahr), with jihād and spending in Allah's way stressed again, some aspects of trade and witnessing, and a finale of good tidings and the promise of mercy and forgiveness.

A religious act is in essence aimed at discovering Allah as the light of the heavens and the earth, who is closer to a person than his jugular vein. What brings a person close to Him is not mere adherence to outward forms but actions done in total sincerity for His sake alone, until He becomes part and parcel of one's being and one's attachment to Him is so strong that one does not waver from the straight path even in the most trying circumstances. His pleasure is earned by true faithfulness, not by turning the face in one direction or another. Whoever has these qualities earns Allah's blessing in this world and the next.

### The wage-earner and the recogniser

In the devotional reading, the outward meaning of the verse is what the Sharīʿah stipulates. Its inner meaning concerns the Ḥaqīqah, which is to the Sharīʿah as the spirit is to the body, for what is a body without a spirit? The Sharīʿah is the house of the servitors, where all the people gather and maintain it through worship and service. The Ḥaqīqah is the house of the sanctuary, where the recognisers gather and keep it flourishing with veneration and contemplation. The distance between the two is the distance from familiarity to love: familiarity is the attribute of wage-earners, love the attribute of recognisers. The wage-earner brings all the kinds of piety named in the verse and then worries that if the wind blows against them or anything is taken away he will lose his wage. The recogniser performs all of it exactly as stipulated and then fears that if any of it remains to his account good fortune will be withheld from him. Every discourse that keeps you from the road, be it unbelief or faith, and any picture that holds you back from the Friend, be it ugly or beautiful, is an obstacle. The wage-earner says, my prayer, my fasting, my almsgiving, my patience in trials, my loyalty to covenants. In abasement, the recogniser asks how he could deserve the cloak of loyalty to You, or to speak of You with a spirit's scent on my lips? The Pīr of the Path said: how should I have known that the wage-earner's share is everlasting paradise, while the recogniser hopes for one glance? The wage-earner wishes for houris and palaces. The recogniser is drowned in light in the sea of face-to-face vision. When Abū ʿAlī Rūdbārī lay dying he told his sister Fāṭimah that the doors of heaven had opened, the Gardens had been adorned and the maidservants were calling from the battlements that all of it had been built for him. The tongue of his state answered: O God, how could I be happy with paradise and houris? Grant me a single breath, and I shall make a paradise from it. Your rightful due is that I look on no one with the eye of love until I see You.

### In short

- The verse closes the qiblah dispute by relocating righteousness from directions to obedience: whatever Allah commands is the merit, not east or west (22:37, Abū al-ʿĀliyah).
- It is a summary of the whole religion, as al-Thawrī observed: belief in Allah, the Last Day, angels, Books and prophets, then charity from cherished wealth (3:92, 76:8-9, 59:9) to relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers, beggars and for freeing slaves, then prayer and zakāh, keeping covenants (13:20), and patience in poverty, illness and battle.
- Financial duty does not end with zakāh (al-Jaṣṣāṣ, al-Qurṭubī), keeping one's word is the essence of all dealings. Patience is the essence of all inner virtue and receives special praise.
- Those who combine these are the truthful and the God-fearing, and the rest of the sūrah elaborates the chapters of this birr.
- Inwardly, the wage-earner serves for paradise, while the recogniser, like Abū ʿAlī Rūdbārī, wants nothing but the Friend.
