Surah Al-Ma'idah, "The Table Spread", is a Madinan surah of one hundred and twenty verses. It was revealed late, after the Prophet ﷺ had settled in Madinah and the community had grown into a state with treaties, boundaries and a public life. Several reports place it among the very last surahs to come down: At-Tirmidhi recorded that 'Abdullah bin 'Amr said the last surahs revealed were Surat Al-Ma'idah and Surat Al-Fath, and At-Tirmidhi graded that report Hasan, Gharib. Al-Hakim collected a similar narration in his Mustadrak and called it Sahih by the criteria of the two Shaykhs. Ibn 'Abbas is reported to have said that the last surah revealed was "When there comes the help of Allah and the Conquest" [110:1]. Al-Hakim also narrated from Jubayr bin Nufayr that he performed Hajj and visited 'A'ishah, who asked him whether he read Al-Ma'idah; when he said yes, she said it was the last surah to be revealed and that whatever permissible matters are found in it should be treated as permissible and whatever impermissible matters are found in it should be treated as impermissible. Imam Ahmad recorded a version in which Jubayr added that he asked 'A'ishah about the conduct of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and she answered, "The Qur'an." An-Nasa'i also recorded this. The practical lesson drawn from this is that the surah is a closing word on law and obligation: a believer's life is a regulated life, and even where he is free to act, he accepts the supremacy and Lordship of Allah and makes himself subject to regulation.

The name Al-Ma'idah comes from the table spread with food that the disciples of 'Isa (Jesus) asked for, an event the surah itself narrates. The surah's central concern is covenant and obligation: what Allah has permitted, what He has forbidden, and the duty of those who claim faith to honour their treaties, their rituals and their trusts. It opens with a command to believers to fulfil their obligations, and it closes with the scene of the Day of Resurrection when 'Isa (Jesus) is asked about what his people did. Between these two points it lays down food laws, rules of Hajj and Ihram, the sanctity of the Sacred Month and the symbols of Allah, the penalties for hunting in Ihram, the obligation of justice even toward enemies, the prohibition of helping one another in sin, the laws of wudu and tayammum, the ruling on wine and gambling, the command to obey Allah and His Messenger ﷺ, the duties of witnesses, the stories of the two sons of Adam, of Musa (Moses) and the Children of Israel, of the Sabbath breakers, of the table that was sent down, and of the disciples. It is a surah of law and of history, and it keeps returning to one theme: a covenant taken and then either kept or broken.

### When it was revealed

The material places the surah in Madinah, after the Hijrah and after the community had become responsible for a public order. Its laws presuppose a people who travel for Hajj, who hunt, who trade, who marry, who judge disputes and who live alongside Jews and Christians. The opening verses speak of Ihram, of the Sacred House, of garlands on sacrificial animals and of hunting, all of which belong to a community already performing Hajj. The report of 'A'ishah, that it was the last surah revealed, gives the surah the character of a final review of obligations: whatever permissible matters are found in it are to be treated as permissible and whatever impermissible matters are found in it are to be treated as impermissible.

### What the surah covers

The first passage commands believers to fulfil their obligations, declares lawful all beasts of cattle except what is announced, forbids hunting while in Ihram, and forbids violating the sanctity of the symbols of Allah, the Sacred Month, the sacrificial animal, the garlands and the people coming to the Sacred House. It then commands justice even toward those who once stopped the believers from the Sacred Mosque, and it commands helping one another in Al-Birr and At-Taqwa while forbidding help in sin and transgression. The next passage explains the lawful and unlawful beasts in detail, the ruling on a fetus found in a slaughtered animal, and the exception for one forced by necessity without wilful disobedience. The surah then turns to wudu, tayammum, and the prohibition of wine and gambling, to the command to obey Allah and His Messenger ﷺ, and to the duty of witnesses to be just. The story of the two sons of Adam is told as a warning against envy and murder. The story of Musa (Moses) and the Children of Israel follows, with the breaking of the Sabbath, the sending down of the table, and the request of the disciples. The surah addresses the People of the Book, distinguishes those who believe from those who turn away, and returns again and again to the theme of covenant: the covenant of the Children of Israel, the covenant of the Christians, and the covenant of the believers. It ends with the scene of the Day of Resurrection and the question put to 'Isa (Jesus) about what his people did.

### Its virtues

The surah carries a special weight in the reports. At-Tirmidhi recorded the statement of 'Abdullah bin 'Amr about it being among the last revealed, graded Hasan, Gharib. Al-Hakim collected a similar narration and called it Sahih by the criteria of the two Shaykhs. 'A'ishah's instruction to Jubayr bin Nufayr, to treat its permissible matters as permissible and its impermissible matters as impermissible because it was the last surah revealed, is the most direct statement of its authority in matters of law. Imam Ahmad recorded that 'Abdur-Rahman bin Mahdi related that Mu'awiyah bin Salih added the statement that when Jubayr asked 'A'ishah about the conduct of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ she answered, "The Qur'an." An-Nasa'i also recorded it. The surah is also connected to the practice of Hajj: the Prophet ﷺ, when he intended to perform Hajj, spent the night at Dhul-Hulayfah, which is also called Wadi Al-'Aqiq, and in the morning made rounds with his wives, performed Ghusl, applied perfume, prayed two Rak'ahs, then garlanded the Hady and announced aloud his intention to perform Hajj and 'Umrah. The Prophet's Hady at that time consisted of plenty of camels, more than sixty, among the best and healthiest animals.

### How it connects to the previous surah

Surah An-Nisa' before it deals with family law, inheritance, orphans, women, and the People of the Book, and it too returns to the theme of covenant and to the question of obedience. Al-Ma'idah takes up the same themes at a higher level of public obligation: where An-Nisa' speaks of the household and the clan, Al-Ma'idah speaks of the community as a whole, of treaties between peoples, of the symbols of religion, of Hajj and of justice in the public square. The closing verses of An-Nisa' speak of the Book and of those who are given a share of it, and Al-Ma'idah opens by demanding that the believers fulfil their obligations, as if to say that the law given in the previous surah is now to be carried out in full.

### In short

- Al-Ma'idah is a Madinan surah of one hundred and twenty verses, revealed late and treated in the reports as one of the last surahs to come down.
- Its subject is covenant and obligation: food laws, Hajj and Ihram, wudu and tayammum, wine and gambling, justice, and the stories of the prophets and the People of the Book.
- It commands believers to fulfil their obligations, to help one another in Al-Birr and At-Taqwa, and not to help one another in sin and transgression.
- It carries a special authority in matters of law, expressed in 'A'ishah's instruction to treat its permissible and impermissible matters as final.
- It follows Surah An-Nisa' and raises the same themes of covenant and obedience to the level of the whole community.
