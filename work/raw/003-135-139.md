<<<VERSE 3:135>>>
> “˹They are˺ those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness and do not knowingly persist in sin—and who forgives sins except Allah” (3:135)

The verse explains that the mindful are not people who never make mistakes. When they sin, they remember Allah, seek forgiveness, and do not knowingly continue in wrongdoing. Repentance restores their relationship with Allah and calls them to repair harm where another person’s rights are involved.

### Phrase by phrase

**“upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves”** includes a range of sins, whether a person has committed a grave offense or fallen short in a lesser matter. Wrongdoing harms the soul, but it may also harm other people through betrayal, unjust speech, or taking what belongs to them. The verse does not minimize sin. It describes the way a believer responds after recognizing it.

Reports about the occasion of revelation differ. One account, related through Ibn Abbas, describes a date seller who made an improper advance toward a woman and then regretted it. Another report concerns a man from the Helpers who was entrusted with the family of his Thaqifi brother during an expedition and violated that trust. These reports are not a license to excuse misconduct or shift blame to the person harmed. Their shared lesson is that the wrongdoer must acknowledge the breach, feel remorse, and seek Allah’s forgiveness. The principle applies beyond any one story.

**“remember Allah”** means that the person becomes conscious of Allah’s command and the consequences of the act. Remembrance interrupts the excuses that allow a wrong to continue. The repentant person does not wait until the sin feels convenient to stop. The Qur’an also calls people to remember Allah and seek pardon, and says that Allah accepts repentance from His servants (9:104).

**“and seek forgiveness”** means asking Allah sincerely to cover the sin and pardon it. Repentance includes regret, stopping the wrongdoing, and resolving not to return to it. If the wrong involved another person’s property, reputation, or safety, the person should also restore what was taken or seek to make amends. Asking Allah for pardon does not erase another person’s rights by itself.

**“and do not knowingly persist in sin”** means they do not deliberately remain settled in a wrong while knowing it is wrong. It does not mean that a repentant person will never stumble again. If someone sincerely repents and later falls, they must repent again rather than conclude that the door has closed. The verse condemns knowingly persisting, not the struggle of a person who keeps returning to Allah.

**“and who forgives sins except Allah”** affirms that ultimate forgiveness belongs to Allah alone. A person may ask another person to pardon a personal wrong, but no human being can erase sin before Allah or guarantee someone’s salvation. The Qur’an says that a person who does evil and then asks Allah’s forgiveness will find Him forgiving and merciful (4:110).

### A return, not an excuse

The verse follows the qualities of generosity, anger-control, and pardon described in 3:134. It turns from the rights people have toward one another to the believer’s duty to Allah. Both dimensions matter. One cannot use a private prayer for forgiveness as a reason to continue harming people, nor should a person believe that a past sin makes repentance useless.

The repentant person is honest about what happened. They do not call wrongdoing a virtue or blame the victim. They ask Allah for help, stop the act, and take reasonable steps to repair what can be repaired. If the harm cannot be undone, they still owe sincere remorse and a change in conduct. This is part of ihsan, acting with awareness that Allah sees what is hidden and what is public.

### Reports about repentance

Abdur-Razzaq transmits a report from Anas bin Malik about the revelation of this verse. Another report, attributed to Ibn Abbas through al-Kalbi, describes a man from the Helpers who violated the trust of his Thaqifi brother. In that account, the man went away in remorse and sought a way to repent. The report says that Gabriel brought news of acceptance. These accounts are reminders of divine mercy, not a reason to excuse the breach of trust or to make a public spectacle of someone’s private sin.

Ibn Umar is also associated in the reports with the teaching that restraining anger for Allah’s sake is a virtuous act. Ibn Marduwyah and Ibn Jarir transmit narrations about controlling anger and seeking Allah’s pleasure. Ubayd bin Umayr explained that whoever truly repents will find Allah accepting repentance. These reports connect the passage’s teaching on pardon and good character with its instruction to seek forgiveness after personal wrongdoing. As the later verse 138 says, the Qur’an is a guide and reminder for the muttaqin.

### In short

- Believers may sin, but they remember Allah and seek forgiveness.
- Repentance means regret, stopping the wrong, and resolving not to persist.
- When another person has been harmed, their rights must also be addressed.

<<<VERSE 3:136>>>
> “Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺” (3:136)

This verse promises forgiveness and Paradise to those who repent and do good. Their mistakes do not define them if they return sincerely to Allah and change their conduct.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord”** means Allah covers their sins and accepts their repentance. The word “Lord” emphasizes care and generosity: Allah nurtures His servants and opens the way back to Him. The believer does not rely on the act of repentance as a payment that forces a reward. They hope in Allah’s mercy while taking responsibility for their choices.

The verse completes the description in 3:135. Those who remember Allah after wrongdoing are not left only with a warning. The Qur’an offers hope for the person who regrets, stops, and seeks pardon. Allah’s forgiveness is not a reason to plan a sin in advance. It is a reason not to despair after a sincere return.

**“and Gardens under which rivers flow”** describes Paradise as a place of lasting abundance and comfort. The Qur’an elsewhere describes the couches of Paradise as lined with brocade (55:54) and urges people to race toward forgiveness and a Garden (57:21). These descriptions give believers hope, while reminding them that the unseen reward exceeds what worldly language can fully convey.

**“staying there forever”** means the reward is lasting, not a temporary pleasure followed by loss. The believer’s hope is not merely to escape punishment but to be welcomed into Allah’s mercy and remain in the Garden.

**“How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺”** praises those who do good deeds. Their actions include repentance, charity, obedience, and the good character described just before this passage. Righteous work matters, but the believer does not boast as though deeds alone purchased Paradise. The Prophet ﷺ taught that no one enters Paradise solely by their deeds, even the Messenger, unless Allah covers them with mercy. This teaching is preserved in the Two Sahihs.

A report from Abu Hurayrah describes Al-Firdaws as the highest part of Paradise and encourages believers to ask Allah for it. The promise of the Garden should therefore inspire both aspiration and humility: work sincerely, ask for Allah’s acceptance, and hope for more than one deserves by their own calculation.

### The lasting reward and the path to it

The next verse describes the muttaqin as people who spend, control their anger, and pardon others (3:134). These qualities are part of the righteous work mentioned here. The Qur’an also promises a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth (55:54, 57:21), and calls believers to hasten toward forgiveness (3:133). Surah al-Hadid highlights this same hope. The reward is not limited to one community or one class of people, but belongs to those who turn to Allah and live by faith.

A narration from Ibn Umar about controlling anger is transmitted by Ibn Marduwyah, Ibn Jarir, and Ibn Majah. It links restraint in this life to reward in the Hereafter. Ubayd bin Umayr’s explanation of repentance also belongs beside this promise: a sinner who returns to Allah is not excluded from divine mercy. Abdur-Razzaq relates a report from Anas bin Malik about turning to Allah after wrongdoing. The reports encourage hope without suggesting that anyone can sin deliberately and presume forgiveness.

A devotional reflection recounts an address to Moses about remembering Allah and the remembrance Allah grants to those who remember Him. Its lesson is that repentance is not merely a formula spoken once. It renews the person’s awareness of Allah and commitment to do better. The believer seeks Al-Firdaws and other blessings, while remembering that the gift is from Allah.

### In short

- Allah forgives those who repent sincerely.
- Paradise is a lasting reward for those who work righteousness.
- Deeds are important, but entry into Paradise is by Allah’s mercy.

<<<VERSE 3:137>>>
> “Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers” (3:137)

The verse reminds believers that earlier communities also faced changing fortunes. It invites them to learn from history and consider what happened to those who rejected their messengers and persisted in wrongdoing.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Similar situations came to pass before you”** means that hardship, conflict, and reversals are not unique to the believers at Uhud. Earlier communities also met trials, and those who followed their prophets experienced opposition. The verse places the current crisis in a longer pattern rather than treating one painful day as the whole story.

The Muslims had been tested at Uhud after their earlier victory at Badr. At Uhud, mistakes and disobedience brought loss and grief, but these did not prove that faith was false or that Allah had abandoned the believers. The surrounding verses continue by explaining that days of victory and defeat alternate, and that believers are tested (3:140-142). The lesson is not to celebrate every disaster as deserved. It is to reflect honestly on what happened and learn from it.

**“so travel throughout the land”** invites people to examine the traces left by earlier societies. Travel can turn history into reflection: ruins, abandoned homes, and vanished power remind the visitor that worldly strength is temporary. The Qur’an elsewhere asks whether people expect to enter Paradise without facing trials like those before them (2:214), and asks whether those who claim faith will be left untested (29:1). These reminders help believers avoid despair when hardship comes.

**“and see the fate of the deniers”** points to the end of those who rejected truth and acted unjustly. The verse calls for learning, not for a believer to take revenge upon people they label as deniers. Allah judges individuals with complete knowledge. The historical warning is that arrogance and oppression cannot secure lasting success.

The Qur’an’s call to remember Allah connects this verse to the earlier command to give in charity (2:274) and to the promise of forgiveness and Paradise (55:54, 57:21). Surah al-Hadid likewise urges believers to race toward forgiveness. Righteous conduct includes both turning back from sin and continuing to benefit other people.

### In short

- Earlier believers also faced trials and reversals.
- History invites reflection on the rise and fall of unjust communities.
- A setback at Uhud should lead to learning, not despair or revenge.

<<<VERSE 3:138>>>
> “This is an insight to humanity—a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing” (3:138)

The verse describes the Qur’an as a clear message for humanity, a source of guidance, and a reminder that benefits those who are mindful of Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**“This is an insight to humanity”** means the Qur’an explains realities people need to understand: the pattern of faith and denial, the consequences of injustice, and the lessons of hardship. It addresses all people, not a single tribe or generation. Its accounts invite reflection rather than empty curiosity.

**“a guide”** means the Qur’an directs people toward belief, worship, justice, and right conduct. It offers principles for navigating the path of guidance in both private choices and communal life. The verses about Uhud help believers understand that a painful reversal does not erase the message or remove their responsibilities.

**“and a lesson to the God-fearing”** means those who have taqwa are especially ready to benefit from the reminder. They listen, examine their own conduct, and change course when they recognize a mistake. This does not mean the Qur’an is only for them. Its message is for all humanity, but a receptive heart is more likely to act on it.

The passage follows the account of earlier nations and prepares the believers for the explanation of the days of victory and loss (3:140-142). In this way, the Qur’an offers knowledge of the past as guidance for the present. The mindful do not read it merely to criticize others. They allow its warning and encouragement to correct their own path.

### In short

- The Qur’an offers insight and guidance to all people.
- The mindful benefit by listening and changing their conduct.
- The lesson of history helps believers face hardship with understanding.

<<<VERSE 3:139>>>
> “Do not falter or grieve, for you will have the upper hand, if you are ˹true˺ believers” (3:139)

The verse consoles believers after the losses at Uhud. It tells them not to let grief turn into helplessness, while making their eventual strength conditional on remaining true to faith.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Do not falter”** means do not lose heart or abandon the responsibilities that remain. The Muslims had suffered wounds and the loss of companions at Uhud. The archers’ departure from their assigned position had contributed to the reversal, and a rumor that the Prophet ﷺ had been killed deepened the shock. The verse does not tell them to pretend the mistakes or losses did not happen. It calls them to recover and continue with discipline.

**“or grieve”** means do not let sorrow over what has passed make you unable to act in the future. Grief for the dead and wounded is human. The instruction is not to suppress love or deny pain. It is to avoid becoming so overwhelmed that the community cannot learn, rebuild, and serve Allah.

Uhud had cost the Muslims about seventy companions, and the Prophet ﷺ himself was injured. Some of the believers were devastated by what they had witnessed. Ibn Abbas is cited in reports about the companions’ attempt to protect their position when the Quraysh cavalry approached. The Prophet ﷺ also prayed for vulnerable believers, including al-Walid ibn al-Walid, in another supplication after attacks on Muslims. Yet the opposing force eventually withdrew rather than destroying the Muslim community. The earlier victory at Badr had not meant that every later battle would be easy. In the same way, the setback at Uhud did not mean that the believers had no future.

**“for you will have the upper hand”** promises eventual strength to the believers, not immunity from every hardship or immediate success in every conflict. In the long term, the message would prevail if the believers remained faithful. This does not license arrogance toward opponents. The verse grounds hope in faith and obedience, not in a claim that one’s side is automatically right in every dispute.

**“if you are ˹true˺ believers”** makes the promise conditional. Faith includes patience, taqwa, listening to the Messenger, and learning from errors. The next verses explain that days of success and setback alternate, and that believers are tested through hardship (3:140-142). Earlier communities also endured trials before receiving relief (2:214, 29:1). The test reveals whether faith is a declaration only or a commitment that survives difficulty.

### Recovering after a setback

The lesson is not to dwell endlessly on a past mistake. The believers had to examine what went wrong at Uhud, follow the Prophet’s leadership, and prepare for what came next. Regret is useful when it leads to correction. It becomes harmful when it turns into despair or excuses inaction.

The Prophet ﷺ taught believers not to wish for an encounter with the enemy, but to ask Allah for well-being. If they were forced to meet the enemy, they were to remain patient. The Two Sahihs transmit this hadith. It discourages seeking violence and places courage within patience and reliance on Allah. The saying about Paradise being under the shade of swords refers to steadfastness in a battle that has come upon the believers, not a call to seek battle.

The word “opening” in this context is not a promise of effortless victory. It is the opening that comes when a shaken community regains faith, learns from its mistakes, and takes the means available to it. The Muslims were to continue their jihad on the path of obedience to Allah, with sabr and trust, rather than surrender to grief.

### In short

- Do not let grief over Uhud become despair or inaction.
- The promise of strength is tied to true faith, patience, and obedience.
- Believers should seek well-being, and remain patient if conflict cannot be avoided.
