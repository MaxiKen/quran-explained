<<<VERSE 2:278>>>
The prohibition reaches claims still awaiting payment: "O believers! Fear Allah, and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺ believers." A transaction's having begun earlier does not justify collecting its prohibited increase after the command. Faith is tested in surrendering a claim that can seem valuable to its holder.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! Fear Allah,"** Taqwa means guarding conduct through obedience and remembering His watchfulness. The command prepares the heart before announcing a rule that may be difficult to accept. A financial claim must be judged before Allah, not only by its owner's preference.

**"and give up outstanding interest"** Leave the unpaid riba still held against other people. The permission concerning past receipts does not extend to collecting an addition that remains unpaid. Principal and interest must be separated.

**"if you are ˹true˺ believers"** Iman, faith, requires accepting His permitted trade and prohibited riba. The opening appeal to fear and closing appeal to belief surround the practical rule. The account treats them as preparation for obedience, not merely ornament around a legal formula.

### What dispute prompted the instruction?

Zayd bin Aslam, As-Suddi, Ibn Jurayj and Muqatil bin Hayyan connect it with Bani 'Amr bin 'Umayr of Thaqif and Bani Al-Mughirah of Bani Makhzum. Outstanding riba transactions remained from the age of ignorance.

Thaqif claimed payment, while Bani Al-Mughirah refused to pay riba after accepting Islam. The matter was referred to 'Attab bin Usayd, the Prophet's deputy in Makkah. He wrote for guidance, and the Prophet ﷺ sent the revealed command back to him.

They then declared repentance, abandoned their unpaid riba and recognised that they could not face war with Allah and His Messenger. The instruction was therefore implemented in a concrete dispute, not left as a general sentiment.

A fuller account identifies the claimants as sons of 'Amr ibn 'Umayr ibn 'Awf al-Thaqafi: Rabi'ah, 'Abd Yalayl, Hubayb and Mas'ud. The brothers' demands were subject to the same rule as everyone else's.

### How do the accounts differ in detail?

Ibn 'Abbas's report through al-Kalbi begins with a reservation: this was what they had heard, and Allah knew best. It says Banu'l-Mughirah complained that others' interest had been cancelled while they were still expected to pay. The claimants relied on their treaty as requiring payment to them.

Another discussion places the dispute after the conquest and describes mutual peace arrangements. It names Mu'adh as governor in one report and 'Attab ibn 'Asid in another. The account elsewhere uses 'Attab bin Usayd. These reported alternatives are retained without inventing a single resolution of the names.

The underlying point is that accepting the relevant Islamic law through a treaty did not preserve a special right to riba. The cancellation governed both Muslim claimants and others bound by that agreement.

### What other reported occasion is given?

'Ata and 'Ikrimah connect the verse with Al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib and 'Uthman ibn 'Affan lending dates. When repayment fell due, the owner said paying everything would leave too little for himself and his dependants.

He proposed paying half then and increasing the interest on the rest. They agreed, and later asked for the agreed payment. The Prophet ﷺ heard and warned them against proceeding. That report concerns an added charge for postponement, while the tribal account concerns disputed outstanding claims.

Both expose the same problem: need or a previous arrangement does not authorise collecting the forbidden addition. The verse demands abandoning it once the instruction is received.

### Why begin cancellation with the Prophet's uncle?

In the Farewell Hajj address, the Prophet ﷺ declared the riba of ignorance abandoned and began with the large claims of Al-'Abbas. The announcement allowed principal but no increase.

That example removed the appearance that the law served a private financial interest. Muslim claims against others were cancelled too. The purpose was reform for humanity, not a rule that transferred advantage to the messenger's household or community.

The detailed Farewell report and the rule protecting principal belong to the following verse. Here its relevance is the relinquishment of an outstanding claim even where the claimant has a close connection with the Prophet ﷺ.

### What choice does the inward reading describe?

The contrast is between maw'ud, what the Lord promises, and maqsud, what the lower self seeks. Contentment with the divine promise is better than attaching the heart to an acquisition desired by the self.

The self's demand can deceive, while the Real's promise is secure. Letting go of riba becomes an instance of that wider discipline: the heart trusts Allah's return rather than treating its immediate desire as indispensable.

### In short

- Outstanding interest must be abandoned, not collected because an agreement began earlier.
- The reported disputes show the prohibition applied to concrete financial claims.
- Cancellation did not exempt the Prophet's family or favour Muslim creditors.
- Faith and taqwa prepare the heart to obey even when relinquishing a claim is difficult.
- The inward lesson prefers Allah's promise to the lower self's demands.

<<<VERSE 2:279>>>
The warning is uncompromising, but repentance remains possible: "If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger!" Those who abandon riba may recover principal without oppressing the debtor or having their own right erased. The rule opposes injustice on both sides.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If you do not,"** If the outstanding riba is not abandoned, the person is knowingly refusing the command. The warning follows a clear opportunity to stop.

**"then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger!"** Ibn 'Abbas explains this as certainty of war. He also describes riba-consumers being told at resurrection to take up arms. Another gloss joins punishment in the Hereafter with coercive enforcement by the messenger in this life.

**"But if you repent,"** Stop and resolve not to return. The condition is more than a verbal claim while continuing the same conduct. The discussion distinguishes practical sin from denying the prohibition and confronting lawful authority.

**"you may retain your principal"** The original amount lent remains due in the ordinary repentant case. 'Amr bin Al-Ahwas reports the Prophet's Farewell Hajj announcement cancelling every pre-Islamic riba claim and beginning with Al-'Abbas bin 'Abd al-Muttalib. Ibn Abi Hatim records that account.

**"neither inflicting nor suffering harm"** Taking more than principal wrongs the debtor. Returning less wrongs the creditor. Another explanation extends the words generally to not harming others and not being harmed through the debt.

### Why is this warning so severe?

The discussion describes the declaration of war as an exceptional warning, surpassed only by the warnings concerning kufr, disbelief. Riba is not a harmless private preference where another party has agreed under pressure.

The inward reading says wilful persistence carries no standing or dignity before Allah. Miqdar and akhtar, measure and honoured standing, are denied to that defiance. An impressive account or worldly position does not neutralise it.

Ibn 'Abbas also gives a historical enforcement ruling: the Muslim ruler requires a persistent offender to repent and, if refusal continues, applies the severe penalty described. The passage specifies execution in that ruling. It is an account of public authority's enforcement, not permission for individuals to attack a creditor.

### What distinctions are made about withholding principal?

One legal view asks why getting back the principal is linked to repentance. If refusal arises from declaring riba halal, lawful, it treats that denial as apostasy. It gives two rules for the property. What was earned while Muslim passes to Muslim heirs. What was earned after apostasy goes to the Baytul-Mal, the state's treasury.

Another case concerns a person who knows riba is haram but rebels against the Islamic government. The same account says the state takes the property and holds it in trust until repentance. These special cases concern the person's status and defiance of public authority. They give no general right to steal from a sinful lender.

The broader justice explanation stresses that wrongdoing in one field does not erase rights in other fields. The offender's prohibited interest is withheld, but capital is returned. The two points are distinct. The usual debt rule protects principal. The further legal cases concern apostasy or armed rebellion, judged by public authority.

### What does repentance restore?

It removes the prohibited demand and preserves the rightful amount. The debtor is not required to pay a penalty disguised as interest, and the lender is not required to surrender principal merely because interest had once been stipulated.

The subsequent instruction adds mercy where actual repayment is impossible. It does not contradict the right described here, but governs its exercise during hardship.

### In short

- Refusal to abandon riba receives a severe warning of war with Allah and His Messenger.
- Repentance leaves the original principal due without the forbidden increase.
- Justice protects the debtor and the creditor's rightful claim together.
- The additional enforcement rulings concern historical public authority, not private violence.

<<<VERSE 2:280>>>
The protection of principal does not justify tormenting an insolvent debtor: "If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease." Allah requires respite during genuine hardship and encourages forgiving the debt as charity. The creditor's opportunity is to show mercy, not to turn helplessness into another charge.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt,"** The person lacks means rather than merely refusing an affordable payment. The explanation connects the case with Banu Makhzum, whose creditors had now abandoned riba and asked for principal.

**"postpone it until a time of ease."** Wait until repayment becomes possible. The readings maysara and maysura both concern ease. Unlike the pre-Islamic choice between immediate payment and added interest, the delay is not sold to the distressed person.

**"And if you waive it as an act of charity,"** Forgiving all, or in another explanation part, of the debt becomes sadaqah. The charitable act is the released claim itself. The creditor need not first extract the money and then hand it back to make the relief real.

**"it will be better for you, if only you knew"** Its benefit before Allah exceeds the apparent loss. Respite is required in the hardship described, while remission is offered as a greater good. The instruction asks the creditor to recognise a return beyond immediate collection.

### What reported dispute is connected with respite?

Al-Kalbi says Banu 'Amr ibn 'Umayr told Banu'l-Mughirah to return the principal and they would forgo its interest. The debtors asked for time until harvest because circumstances were difficult.

The report then describes refusal and revelation of this instruction. Its final attribution of the refusal names Banu'l-Mughirah, although the preceding dialogue makes them the requesting debtors. That wording is a textual difficulty, not a basis for inventing a new sequence. The essential connection is the request for respite in hardship.

### How can cancellation be better than recovery?

One answer concerns the enduring blessings of Paradise in place of a temporary gain. Another concerns barakah in what remains. A small amount may meet many needs without a numerical increase.

The reflection contrasts that with money whose intended benefit never arrives or which must be spent on distress, treatment and consultation. It gives health and effective ordinary treatment as examples of blessing. It does not make illness proof of wrongdoing or claim all poor people are always healthy.

The general social effect is also beneficial. Compassion, sympathy and goodwill grow when weakness is not punished. Justice protects principal, while mercy governs its exercise and may go further by relinquishing it.

### What reward is given for postponement?

Ahmad records through Sulayman bin Buraydah that his father heard the Prophet ﷺ promise charity equal to the debt for each day of time granted. He also heard a promise of twice that amount and asked about the difference.

The explanation distinguishes the periods. Before the repayment term ends, the daily reward is the equivalent amount. Once it ends and further time is granted because of hardship, the daily reward is doubled.

The account values waiting as an act rather than inactivity. Keeping the distressed person free from harassment is a continuing form of good even where no money is newly transferred.

### Why did Abu Qatadah forgive a debt?

Ahmad records that Abu Qatadah sought a man owing him money who repeatedly hid. A boy revealed that the man was inside eating, and Abu Qatadah called him out. Asked why he hid, the debtor said he had no money and faced hardship.

Abu Qatadah asked him to confirm it by Allah, then wept. He recalled the Prophet ﷺ saying that someone granting respite or forgiving debt would receive shade of Allah's Throne at resurrection. Muslim also records the hadith.

The episode joins verification with compassion. The creditor did not simply dismiss the claim as an excuse, and the confirmation moved him towards mercy instead of another demand.

Al-Tabarani's Mu'jim also contains the encouragement for someone desiring divine shade to give lenience, deferment or forgiveness. A similar saying is recorded by Muslim. Another saying encourages respite for the person seeking an answered prayer or removal of misfortune, without supplying an additional collector here.

### What happened to the lenient merchant?

Abu Ya'la Al-Mawsili records from Hudhayfah a saying of the Prophet ﷺ about a servant summoned for account. He first says he has no deed equal even to an atom to offer, repeating his answer. He then remembers his dealings as a wealthy merchant.

He gave easy terms to those who could pay and time to those in difficulty. Allah declared Himself more worthy of lenience and admitted him to Paradise. Al-Bukhari, Muslim and Ibn Majah also record the account from Hudhayfah. Muslim gives a related wording from 'Uqbah bin 'Amir and Abu Mas'ud Al-Badri.

The story does not present a licence to neglect every other duty. It reveals the value of mercy that its doer had barely counted among good works. Lenience in business can be a decisive act of obedience.

### What follows when insolvency is proved before a judge?

The legal and inward discussion says continued detention is not permissible once the detained person's insolvency is established. Proof of that condition is retained in connection with the adversary's claim, but the debtor receives respite.

It asks what profit or commercial transformation can be expected from a person without goods or capital. Jurists say a healthy person should seek work, while bodily weakness limits what can be required. Someone bankrupt in strength as well as money must rely on the provision appointed for debtors.

The reflection connects this with Allah's knowledge of human weakness, difficulty and sincere need. His mercy is not detached from those realities. The legal demand should likewise distinguish unwillingness from incapacity.

### In short

- Genuine inability to repay requires postponement without charging for the delay.
- Waiving the debt is a greater charity, with benefit beyond the amount surrendered.
- Prophetic reports promise continuing reward and divine shade for lenience.
- Proven insolvency limits detention and demands attention to actual capacity.
- Mercy in financial dealings nurtures both personal reward and communal goodwill.

<<<VERSE 2:281>>>
The financial commands end by returning every transaction to its final account: "Be mindful of the Day when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah." Claims, repayments and gifts all belong to a life that ends. On that day neither apparent success nor an incomplete worldly record will conceal what a soul has earned.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Be mindful of the Day"** Taqwa directs present conduct towards the reality of judgment. The warning closes the riba instruction with accountability rather than leaving it as an external regulation alone.

**"when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah,"** The event is resurrection. The readings turja'una and tarji'una express being returned and returning. Neither leaves anyone outside the encounter.

**"then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done,"** Believer and disbeliever receive the recompense of good and evil. The wealth of this life will vanish, but responsibility for its use will not.

**"and none will be wronged"** No good deed is diminished and no extra sin added. The account is complete and just. The command to avoid injustice in debt therefore ends in assurance of Allah's perfect justice.

### Is this described as the last revelation?

An-Nasa'i records from Ibn 'Abbas that this was the final verse revealed. Ad-Dahhak and Al-'Awfi also transmit that account from him.

One discussion says the Prophet ﷺ died thirty-one days later. Other reports give only nine days. The alternatives are preserved rather than forcing one chronology. Their common effect is to place the return to Allah at the close of the revealed instruction.

### Why is law alone not enough?

The social reflection says reform requires people willing to uphold a system. Even a secular order depends on a degree of public character and acceptance. An Islamic order likewise cannot be fully produced by imposing regulations on people without taqwa.

The Qur'an therefore addresses faith, fear of Allah and concern for the Hereafter alongside legal commands. It works upon the individual who must choose to obey when an opportunity for evasion appears.

In this explanation, personal reform is the direct aim and social reform its collective manifestation. A community changes as its members develop the required spirit. Law has a place, but cannot substitute for that inward willingness.

### Why discuss other riba passages here?

The extended review brings the instruction into a wider Qur'anic perspective. It counts seven relevant verses in Al-Baqarah, one in Al 'Imran and two in al-Nisa. A verse in al-Rum has differing interpretations. Its totals reflect whether that last verse is included, rather than announcing an additional undisclosed ruling.

The review's purpose is to clarify whether the prohibition depends upon an extreme rate or compounding, and whether every use of the word riba concerns the same practice. Those questions affect how the financial commands are received.

### Does multiplied interest alone fall under the prohibition?

The discussion cites verse 130 of Al 'Imran, warning against riba multiplied many times. Mujahid's account describes the familiar pattern: when a loan fell due and could not be paid, an extension added another charge, followed by further increases at subsequent deadlines.

The phrase condemns that prevailing method. It is not a condition making a smaller increase lawful. Other passages prohibit riba without that qualification.

An analogy is the warning against taking a paltry price for divine signs. Even a whole worldly kingdom would be paltry compared with revelation. The wording does not mean a sufficiently large price would make the exchange permissible.

The review also notes how apparently simple interest can become repeated increase when receipts are added to the creditor's funds and lent again. Reinvestment can multiply the gain even without calling the initial arrangement compound interest. Its conclusion is that neither simple nor compound interest escapes the prohibition described.

### What is recalled concerning earlier law?

Verses 160 and 161 of al-Nisa are discussed as describing people who obstructed Allah's way, took riba despite its prohibition and consumed others' property falsely. The consequence included good things previously permitted becoming forbidden and a painful punishment for disbelievers among them.

The explanation connects the prohibition with the law of Musa (Moses). It describes transgression by the people in the passage, not an inherent quality of all their descendants. The point is that riba was not a newly incomprehensible practice first named for this community.

These are the verse numbers and chapter names supplied by the review. No additional corrected references are imported from memory.

### What are the two readings of the verse in al-Rum?

The discussion cites (30:39). One reading concerns interest that appears to increase property but does not increase it with Allah. It contrasts that with zakah and sadaqah, whose apparent reduction produces multiplied good.

The bodily analogy compares swelling with unhealthy increase and therapeutic cleansing or bloodletting with an apparent reduction that can benefit health. The contrast concerns useful results, not approving growth or condemning reduction merely by quantity.

Another reading takes riba there as a gift offered to obtain a larger gift in return. The discussion gives gifts at marriage ceremonies as an example. Such giving aims at personal advantage rather than Allah's pleasure and therefore receives no reward as expenditure in His cause.

It connects that meaning with (74:6), an instruction to the Prophet ﷺ not to give a favour for an increased return. The verse then concerns self-interested gifts rather than directly legislating loan interest.

### Why is the gift interpretation preferred in that discussion?

Al-Rum is Makki, connected with revelation in Makkah, while the specific prohibition of riba came in Madinah. Not every verse in a Makki chapter must have that origin, but the classification supplies a strong presumption unless evidence shows otherwise.

The preceding subject also concerns giving relatives, poor people and travellers their due while seeking Allah's pleasure. The next distinction naturally clarifies that expecting a bigger personal return is not the same purpose.

The discussion therefore considers the gift reading weightier here. It does not use that interpretation to cancel the explicit prohibition elsewhere. The added nuance concerns this particular verse's meaning, not a permission for loan interest.

The review of those passages consequently leaves the central conclusion unchanged: riba is haram, unlawful, whether doubled, repeatedly multiplied or initially simple. The declaration of war against defiance remains part of that conclusion.

### What inward return occurs before death?

The allusive reading distinguishes the future return of bodily selves from the heart's return in every breath. The latter is pictured as payment made now rather than a promise deferred to resurrection.

Immediate attentiveness to Allah's claim is described as more fitting for the intimate servant than waiting until the final reckoning to recognise it. It contrasts the ordinary call to fear a day with the direct call in (2:41) to fear Allah Himself.

This does not deny the future return. It draws a present discipline from it: remember the Lord now, fulfil duties now and do not postpone the heart's obedience until death makes new action impossible.

### In short

- Every soul returns for a complete account without injustice.
- Reports identify this as the final revelation while differing over the days until the Prophet's death.
- Faith and taqwa make financial law part of personal obedience rather than mere external compliance.
- The wider review rejects limiting the prohibition to extreme or compound interest.
- Present inward return prepares the servant for the unavoidable final return.
