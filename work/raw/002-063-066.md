<<<VERSE 2:63>>>
Verse 63 recalls the solemn moment at which the Israelites bound themselves to the Torah, and the extraordinary scene that accompanied it. "And remember when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you", Allah says, and then gives the terms: hold firmly to that "which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful". The verse that follows tells how quickly the pledge was broken.

### Phrase by phrase

**"when We took a covenant from you"**. Allah reminds the Children of Israel of the pledges, covenants and promises He took from them: to believe in Him alone without partner, to follow His Messengers, and to act according to what is in the Torah. In the words of one commentary, He took their consent. The covenant concerned the teachings they had received from God, the Ten Commandments and the Book that contained them.

**"and raised the mountain above you"**. The story behind this clause is told in the reports. Having received the Torah, Mūsā (Moses) came back from Mount Ṭūr (Sinai) and recited it to the Israelites. Its injunctions were strict, but their conduct and cast of mind called for strict discipline. They first said they would not obey until Allah Himself told them that this was His Book, and the seventy men whose story was told under verse 55 went to the Mount and heard Allah attest it. On their return they testified that the Torah was Allah's Book, but added something of their own: they told the people that Allah had permitted them to act on its injunctions only as far as they were able, and had promised to forgive whatever they could not manage. A people already inclined to rebellion, faced with stern commands, now had a fresh excuse for negligence, and they flatly refused to obey, insisting that such demanding rules were beyond their endurance. In answer to this insolence, Allah commanded the angels to lift Mount Ṭūr and hold it in the air over their heads, as a threat that it would fall and crush them if they did not keep their covenant. Left with no choice, the Israelites submitted. The parallel verse describes the mountain hanging over them "as if it were a cloud", while they were sure it would fall on them (7:171). In the hadith about the trials, Ibn ʿAbbās said that their refusal to obey was what made Allah lift the mountain over their heads, so that they would listen. One commentary puts it that the mountain was uprooted from the earth and set above them when they refused to accept the Torah, and another that it was raised to inspire awe and to warn them against breaking the contract. A Jewish tradition preserved in the Talmud tells the same story, of God holding a mountain upside down over them and telling them to accept the Torah's teachings or be destroyed on the spot.

The mountain is aṭ-Ṭūr, the same mountain named in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, according to ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrimah, ʿAṭāʾ, Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās and others, and this is the more obvious view. Another report from Ibn ʿAbbās explains that ṭūr is the name for a mountain on which vegetation grows, and that a bare mountain is not called ṭūr.

**"Hold firmly to that which We have given you"** means the Torah, as al-Ḥasan said. Mujāhid read it as a command to adhere to it strictly. Other glosses bring out the force of the Arabic: take it forcefully, that is, seriously and with effort, comply with it in earnestness and diligence, and do not treat it casually or lazily.

**"and observe its teachings"**, literally "remember what is in it". Abū al-ʿĀliyah and ar-Rabīʿ said this means read the Torah and put it into practice. One commentary spells out what is to be remembered: its promises of reward and its threats of punishment, and what it declares lawful and unlawful. Another adds: reflect on it.

**"so perhaps you will become mindful"**. The aim of the whole command is taqwā. Holding to the Book and acting on it is how a person wards off Allah's wrath and punishment, obeys Him, and guards himself against the Fire and against acts of disobedience, taking care about what he earns with his Lord.

### Was this compulsion in religion?

A doubt naturally arises. The Qur'an says elsewhere that no one is to be forced into religion, yet here a mountain is held over people's heads. The answer is that no one was being forced to change his religion. The Israelites had already accepted Mūsā as Allah's prophet and had willingly covenanted with Allah that they would act on His Book if one were given to them. They now stood as rebels against a law they themselves acknowledged to be divine, and they were being threatened with punishment for persisting in rebellion. Even a secular state treats rebels quite differently from foreigners or enemies, leaving a rebel only two choices, to submit or to forfeit his life. This is the same reason why, in the Islamic Sharīʿah, it is the apostate (murtadd) who is liable to capital punishment and not the outright disbeliever. The Israelites were threatened as offenders against a law they recognised but refused to obey.

### Every believer's covenant

What happened to Mūsā's people happens, in its own way, to everyone who embraces true faith. To believe is to contract with God to live and to die according to His will, and the pledge is a weighty one: on one side a tiny, helpless speck in God's world, on the other the God whose might upholds the universe. Whoever keeps his word is granted God's eternal blessings. Whoever turns away from his commitment stands in grave danger of being cast into the Fire, never to come out. Everyone who binds himself by the contract of faith should therefore live in fear of breaking his religious vows and bringing catastrophe on his own head.

### Sight without insight

The devotional reading widens the covenant to all responsible beings. Some responded willingly, because God made Himself known to them and they declared His oneness. Others responded unwillingly, because He veiled them and they did not wish to recognise Him. No proof could be more powerful than seeing with one's own eyes the mountain, Ṭūr, lifted up overhead, but the Israelites lacked the light of inner sight (baṣīrah), and so the witnessing of physical sight (baṣar) did them no good.

### In short

- The covenant was the Israelites' pledge to believe in Allah alone, follow His Messengers and live by the Torah.
- When they refused the Torah's demands, Mount Ṭūr was lifted over their heads until they submitted, as 7:171 also records.
- "Hold firmly" means take the Book seriously and act on it, remembering its commands, prohibitions, promises and threats, so as to attain taqwā.
- This was not compulsion in religion but the treatment of rebels who had already accepted the law, which is why the Sharīʿah punishes the apostate and not the mere disbeliever.
- Faith is itself a covenant, and seeing miracles is useless without inner sight.

<<<VERSE 2:64>>>
Verse 64 records the sequel to the covenant: "Yet you turned away afterwards". It then reminds the Israelites why they still exist to be addressed: had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy, "you would have certainly been of the losers".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Yet you turned away afterwards"** means that after giving so firm a pledge, under a mountain, you still deviated and broke it. You turned your back on the covenant and rebelled. The devotional reading stresses the "afterwards": they went back to disobedience after witnessing the signs with their own eyes.

**"Had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy upon you"**. The breach of the covenant was so grave that one might have expected utter destruction to come down on them as punishment. Allah in His mercy spared their physical existence, though they will answer for their treason in the next world. Scholars explain the grace and mercy in several complementary ways: Allah forgave them, He went on sending them prophets and messengers, He deferred His punishment, He bore with them, and He accepted their repentance when they asked forgiveness. Scholars distinguish two kinds of divine mercy. The general mercy extends to believers and disbelievers alike and shows itself in worldly well-being and prosperity. The special mercy belongs to believers only and will show itself above all in the next world, in salvation and nearness to Allah.

**"you would have certainly been of the losers"** means losers in this life and in the Hereafter because of breaking the covenant, among those duped and left exposed to Allah's punishment. Had it not been for His decree of respite and His forbearance through acts of grace, says the devotional reading, He would have punished them without delay and brought a great calamity down on them, and their deeds would have been a total loss.

### Who is being addressed

The last phrase of the verse appears to be addressed to the Jews who were the Prophet's ﷺ contemporaries. Faith in him is itself part of the covenant, so these Jews too are included among those guilty of breaking it. Allah asks them to recognise that it is His mercy alone that has kept Him from sending down on them, despite their treason, the kind of catastrophic punishment in this world that used to fall on earlier unbelievers and traitors. A number of authentic hadiths state that it is through the barakah of the Prophet ﷺ that such sweeping punishments no longer descend on any people, and for that reason some commentators identify the grace and mercy of this verse with the sending of Muḥammad ﷺ as Prophet and Messenger. One early commentary reads the verse exactly so: His grace in deferring your punishment, and His mercy in sending Muḥammad ﷺ to you.

To drive the point home, the next verse turns to another group of earlier transgressors and the dreadful punishment that overtook them all at once.

### In short

- After the pledge under the mountain the Israelites still broke their covenant.
- They were spared the destruction they deserved by Allah's grace and mercy: His forgiveness, His patience, His continued sending of prophets, and, on one reading, His sending of Muḥammad ﷺ, through whose barakah sweeping punishments ceased.
- Allah's general mercy reaches everyone in this world. His special mercy, in the next, is for believers alone.
- Without that mercy they would have been losers in both worlds.

<<<VERSE 2:65>>>
Verse 65 turns from the broken covenant to one group of covenant-breakers whose punishment the Jews of Madīnah knew well. "You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath", it says, and then records the sentence passed on them: "Be disgraced apes!" The story is told at length in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, and here it is used as a warning.

### Phrase by phrase

**"You are already aware"**. The Arabic opens with a particle of oath: you certainly know, and you have heard, what happened to the people among you who transgressed. The story was part of their own history, told of their own ancestors. Allah is saying: remember, O Jews, that Allah sent His torment on the town that disobeyed Him and broke its pledge and covenant to keep the Sabbath holy.

**"those of you who broke the Sabbath"**. The episode belongs to the time of Dāwūd (David). Allah had appointed Saturday, the Sabt, as the sacred day of the Israelites, set apart for prayer and worship, and fishing was forbidden on it. The town in question lay by the sea, and one commentary identifies it as Aylah (Eilat). Its people were very fond of fish, and the test was sharpened by the fact that on the Sabbath the fish came in abundance, plainly visible, while on other days they stayed away (7:163). The people devised ways round the prohibition. Before the Sabbath they set out nets and ropes and dug pools, so that when the fish came in on Saturday they were trapped, and after the Sabbath ended at nightfall they went and gathered them. Another description has them fastening a cord to a fish's tail on Saturday, tying the far end to something on the shore and leaving the fish in the water, then hauling it out to eat on Sunday. Al-Qurṭubī says that at first they invented all kinds of clever pretexts of this sort for catching fish on Saturday, and gradually began doing it openly. The town divided over the matter. The transgressors stood on one side, and on the other stood a number of scholars and pious men who tried to dissuade them. When their warnings were ignored, they broke off from the sinners altogether and moved to a separate quarter of the town.

**"We said to them, 'Be disgraced apes!'"**. One day the pious party noticed that no sound was coming from the other quarter. Curious, they went to look and found that all the transgressors had been turned into apes. Allah changed them from human beings into monkeys, the animal whose form is closest to the human, and the punishment matched the crime: their deceitful devices looked lawful on the surface while being wicked underneath, just as an ape resembles a man without being one. Al-ʿAwfī reported Ibn ʿAbbās saying that their bodies were changed into those of apes and swine, the young becoming apes and the old swine, and Qatādah said the same of the two groups. Shaybān an-Naḥwī reported Qatādah describing them as howling apes with tails, who had been men and women. The apes could recognise their relatives and friends, and would come up to them weeping in remorse, looking for sympathy and help. After three days they all died. One commentary glosses the sentence as "become humiliated and base apes", and another notes that they were rejected and cast out because of their trickery.

### Are today's apes their descendants?

No. Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded Ibn ʿAbbās saying that those who violated the Sabbath were turned into apes and then perished, leaving no offspring. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported Ibn ʿAbbās saying that Allah turned them into apes because of their sins, that they lived only three days, since no transformed person ever lives longer than that, and that they neither ate, drank nor bred. Allah changes the shape of whomever He wills, doing what He wills with whom He wills, but the apes, the swine and the rest of creation were created in the six days that He mentions in His Book. The rule, then, is that people whom Allah transforms into beasts as a punishment cease to breed and leave no progeny. A hadith recorded by Imām Muslim from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd settles the point: some people asked the Prophet ﷺ whether apes and swine were descended from the transformed Jews, and he reminded them that apes and swine existed in the world before that, and said that when Allah sends this kind of punishment on a people, their line ends with it and there is no further breeding.

### Was the change only moral?

Some modern Muslims have tried to explain the metamorphosis away, suggesting that these Jews did not physically become beasts but only acquired the evil characteristics of apes and swine. Scholars reject this. It denies an explicit statement of the Qur'an, which no Muslim can do and remain a Muslim, and in denying the possibility of physical change it sets limits to Allah's power. Even as exegesis it is flimsy, because these people had been showing beastly traits, a swine's greed and an ape's cunning, before the punishment ever fell. If nothing more than that was meant, where was the change that the Qur'an presents as a punishment for the offenders and a warning to everyone else? The same writers, it is observed, readily accept Darwin's speculation that apes evolved into man, for which no verifiable data has been gathered, while looking askance at the Qur'an's statement that men were changed into apes, though the one change is rationally and logically as possible as the other.

### The metamorphosis of hearts

Alongside the bodily event, the devotional reading points to an inner one: the metamorphosis (maskh) of this community took place in their hearts. When the Israelites abandoned God's command and disdained the laws laid on them, the punishments came quickly, in their disgrace, in their transformation, and in the other things the Qur'anic text includes. For violating the covenant and rejecting the limit God had set, the community was punished with the transformation of their hearts and the alteration of their states, as Allah says elsewhere that He turns away the hearts and eyes of those who refused to believe at first (6:110). A punishment that falls on the heart is far worse than one that falls on the body. On this theme a poet is quoted: you who ask me how I have fared since he left, I suffered what tormented me while it made him glad. I strutted about in our days of togetherness, feeling safe from the tricks of time, but his turning away from me went on until nothing of what I had known remained.

A modern commentator draws the moral plainly. The Israelites were commanded to keep the Sabbath holy and refrain from worldly pursuits on it. They violated it, working on the Sabbath as on any other day, and then went further and dressed up what they were doing as conformity to God's will. That audacity brought God's displeasure, and they became apes. Whoever turns against the law of God puts himself on a level with animals, who follow no code of ethics, and those who play games with divine law should fear losing their human dignity and being brought down to the level of beasts.

### In short

- In the days of Dāwūd the people of a seaside town evaded the Sabbath ban on fishing by trapping fish on Saturday and collecting them afterwards, while their pious neighbours warned them in vain (7:163).
- Allah turned the transgressors into apes, the young into apes and the old into swine according to Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah, and they died within three days without offspring.
- A hadith in Muslim confirms that today's apes and swine are not their descendants.
- The transformation was physical, and reading it as merely moral contradicts the Qur'an, though scholars also speak of an inner transformation of hearts that is worse than any change of body.

<<<VERSE 2:66>>>
Verse 66 states the purpose of the punishment just described. "So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing", it says. A punishment that is remembered keeps on working long after the people who suffered it are gone.

### Phrase by phrase

**"So We made their fate an example"**. The Arabic word is nakāl, an exemplary punishment designed to deter others from doing what these people did. Allah made the people of this town, who violated the Sabbath, an example through the manner in which He punished them, just as He says of Pharaoh that He overtook him and made him an example in this life and the next (79:25). One commentary adds that the punishment was also a chastisement for the sins they had committed before.

**"to present and future generations"**, literally "to what was before it and what was after it". Ibn ʿAbbās explained: We made this town an example to the towns around it by the way We punished its people, which matches the verse in which Allah says He destroyed the societies around the Makkans after varying the signs for them so that they might return (46:27). Others take the phrase of time rather than place: an example to the people of that age and to those who came after them, so that they would not do the same and earn the same fate. Allah, whose oneness they had pledged to uphold, made them a warning to their contemporaries and a reminder to later generations by preserving their story.

**"and a lesson to the God-fearing"**. The word is mawʿiẓah, an admonition and reminder. The torment that overtook this town came from indulging in what Allah had forbidden and from deceit, so those who have taqwā, al-muttaqīn, should take care to keep clear of such conduct lest what befell that town befall them. The God-fearing are singled out because they, unlike others, are the ones who actually benefit from the lesson, being mindful of the consequences of their actions in the life to come and of Allah's repayment of those who transgress His limits. One early commentary identifies the God-fearing here with Muḥammad ﷺ and his Companions. Scholars also note that those who saw the event, or heard about it, fell into two groups. For the disobedient it was a deterrent and a warning that moved them to repent, and for the obedient it was a lesson and a reminder to stay firm in obedience. It serves both purposes still.

The Prophet ﷺ drew the practical lesson himself. Imām Abū ʿAbdullāh ibn Baṭṭah recorded from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: do not do what the Jews did, making lawful what Allah has forbidden by means of the flimsiest tricks. This hadith has a good (jayyid) chain.

### Tricks and legal devices

The Jews who were turned into apes had used certain ruses to justify their sin in their own eyes, and this raises a delicate question of fiqh. Some modern writers have accused the master jurists of Islam of inventing elaborate stratagems (ḥiyal, plural of ḥīlah) to help the rich and powerful get round the Sharīʿah. The charge is a petty slander, but it has had some success with the uninformed because it plays on the Arabic word ḥīlah. A word's dictionary meaning is one thing and its technical meaning within a science is another. Literally, ḥīlah does mean a trick or stratagem. As a technical term of fiqh it means a device for giving legitimacy to an action in a situation of need, by making the modifications to it that the Sharīʿah requires. It is not a way of bypassing the Sharīʿah but a way of helping people conform to it in circumstances where necessity or human weakness would otherwise push them to break it.

This verse helps to make the distinction clear. The transgression of the Jews was not an open and explicit breach of the divine law. It was the use of tricks that necessarily amounted to negating the command, such as the cord tied to the fish on Saturday and pulled in on Sunday. A ruse of that kind not only cancels an injunction of the Sharīʿah but mocks it, and that is why those who practised such tricks were judged disobedient and rebellious and were punished so terribly. It does not follow that the ḥiyal of the jurists are impermissible. Those devices serve the opposite purpose. Not only are they an integral part of fiqh, some were suggested by the Prophet ﷺ himself. Exchanging one measure of good dates for two measures of poor dates is, under the Sharīʿah, a form of usury. The Prophet ﷺ indicated a device for avoiding the breach when such a transaction is needed: rather than barter one commodity for another, use money as the medium, first selling the two measures of poor dates for cash and then buying one measure of good dates with the cash. The intention here is to conform to the Sharīʿah, not to evade it, and no negation of it is intended or takes place. The same principle governs all the devices the jurists have proposed to keep people from doing what the Sharīʿah prohibits, and to compare them with the tricks by which the Jews negated and mocked the law is a travesty of the truth.

### A lesson in loss

The devotional reading sees the people of this verse as a picture of all who are tried by abandonment and branded with disappointment. Their condition became a lesson, and the mere sight of what became of them is enough to make one choke on grief. Whoever falls into misery after enjoying status becomes a target of scorn for every ignoble person. This is what remains for those brought low after the wrath of kings and the cold shoulder of chieftains, as the poet lamented: the youths surrounded me and ganged up on me, and from behind they set the dogs on me.

### In short

- The Sabbath-breakers were made a nakāl, an exemplary punishment, for the towns around them and for all later generations, as Pharaoh was (79:25, 46:27).
- The lesson is addressed to the God-fearing because they are the ones who take it to heart, deterring the sinner and steadying the obedient.
- The Prophet ﷺ warned against imitating the Jews by making forbidden things lawful through flimsy tricks.
- The legal devices (ḥiyal) of the jurists are the opposite of such tricks: they help people obey the Sharīʿah in hard cases, as in the Prophet's ﷺ own advice on exchanging dates through a cash sale.
