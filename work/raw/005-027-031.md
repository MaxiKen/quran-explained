<<<VERSE 5:27>>>
The story begins with two offerings and two different responses to Allah's judgment: "Abel’s offering was accepted while Cain’s was not." The rejected brother turns his disappointment into a threat against the accepted one. His brother answers by identifying the real issue: acceptance depends on devotion to Allah, not on removing someone else's blessing.

The passage follows the account of people who refused a commanded struggle. It now condemns the opposite error, unjust violence. Neither shrinking from a rightful obligation nor attacking an innocent person is faithfulness. The two stories distinguish obedience from both cowardice and aggression.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Relate to them in truth ˹O Prophet˺ the story of Adam’s two sons"** commands Muhammad ﷺ to convey the account faithfully. The immediate warning is directed toward envious wrongdoers among the People of the Book, while its instruction reaches the wider community. The majority identification is Adam's own sons, Habil, Abel, and Qabil, Cain, rather than two later descendants called sons of Adam in a general sense.

**"how each offered a sacrifice"** describes an offering made to seek nearness to Allah. Qurban means something through which nearness is sought. In religious usage it can mean a sacrificial offering. Here Habil offers from his flock and Qabil from his produce.

**"Abel’s offering was accepted while Cain’s was not."** distinguishes the outcome according to their inward condition. The accounts describe a heavenly fire consuming Habil's offering while leaving Qabil's untouched. The acceptance of one does not itself wrong the other.

**"So Cain threatened, “I will kill you!”"** shows envy becoming an intention to destroy. Instead of asking how to correct his own worship, he treats the accepted brother as the cause of his disgrace.

**"His brother replied, “Allah only accepts ˹the offering˺ of the sincerely devout"** points to taqwa, being mindful of Allah, obeying His commands and avoiding what He forbids. Ikhlas, sincerity, requires truthful speech, action and a heart directed toward Him. Habil does not answer anger with another threat. He states the principle through which his brother could seek reform.

### What occasion led to the offerings?

The reported background describes Adam and Hawwa', Eve, having twins, a boy and a girl, with each pregnancy. At the beginning of human life there were no unrelated families from which spouses could be found. A special ruling permitted marriage between a boy and a girl born in different pregnancies, while forbidding marriage between the twins of the same pregnancy.

Qabil wanted to marry his own twin sister, described as more beautiful than the sister born with Habil. Adam refused the demand under the ruling then given. To settle the dispute, he directed them to offer sacrifices, with acceptance identifying the one entitled to the marriage. His confidence was that Allah would confirm the rightful outcome, not that an offering could change a forbidden marriage into a permitted one.

Habil, who kept sheep and goats, presented a fine lamb. Qabil, a farmer, brought grain or green crops. Fire consumed the former offering and left the latter. The account from Ibn 'Abbas recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim is described as having a chain better than good. The special marriage arrangement belongs to the initial circumstances described and is not a permission concerning later sibling marriage.

A further gloss says Qabil concealed his envy until Adam left on pilgrimage, then confronted his brother. The Qur'anic passage itself centres the offering, threat and answer rather than narrating every surrounding detail.

### Why insist that the story be told in truth?

Truthful narration must not be altered through invention, misleading omissions or changes that distort its meaning. A small adjustment in wording can give a very different impression of an event. Religious history is therefore not material for decorative storytelling unconcerned with accuracy.

The same emphasis appears in the true account concerning 'Isa (Jesus) in Al 'Imran (3:62), the narrative of the youths in Al-Kahf (18:13), and the statement concerning 'Isa son of Maryam in Surah Maryam (19:34). Al-Haqq means the truth, and joining that word to these narratives underscores their purpose and reliability.

The Prophet ﷺ had not received human instruction in these ancient events. His truthful account is presented as evidence that he receives revelation. At the same time, the Qur'an's purpose is not to supply an uninterrupted chronicle. It selects the part of a history that conveys guidance, moral warning and the basis of religious duties.

### What is the cure for the envy shown here?

Habil directs Qabil away from attacking another person's favour and toward examining himself. The rejected offering calls for repentance and corrected conduct. Destroying the accepted brother would not make the rejected offering acceptable.

Whatever blessing a person receives comes from Allah. Envy that seeks to take it away becomes resistance to His giving. The person may be allowed to act within this world's test, but that temporary ability is not divine approval of the act.

The lesson concerns the envy displayed here, not a rule that every worldly deprivation proves a person's wickedness. Qabil's fault has been identified in the story: he is warned about sincerity and devotion, yet chooses hostility instead of reform.

### Why did the righteous fear for the acceptance of their deeds?

'Amir ibn 'Abdullah wept near death despite a life of worship because he did not know whether his deeds had been accepted. The verse's condition remained before him. Long activity in worship was not a reason to become careless about the heart with which it was offered.

'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud said certainty that one deed had been accepted would be worth more to him than a world turned to gold. Abu Ad-Darda', in a report recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim, valued the acceptance of a single salah, prayer, above all worldly possessions.

'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz counselled someone by letter to hold fast to taqwa, connecting acceptance, mercy and reward with it. He observed that many urge it upon others while few practise it. Al-Murtada similarly taught that a deed accepted through taqwa cannot be dismissed as insignificant, however small it seems.

These examples turn the verse toward self-examination. The proper response is not to count one's deeds proudly or resent another's acceptance, but to seek sincerity in what one offers.

### In short

- The true account warns against envy, injustice and the attempt to destroy another person's blessing.
- The two offerings are distinguished by sincerity and taqwa, not merely by their outward presentation.
- Habil answers the threat with a principle calling his brother to reform himself.
- Truthful historical narration requires care, while the Qur'an selects events for guidance rather than entertainment.
- Even a small accepted deed is precious. Concern for acceptance should produce humility, not rivalry.

<<<VERSE 5:28>>>
Habil, Abel, refuses to turn his brother's threat into a mutual contest of murder: "If you raise your hand to kill me, I will not raise mine to kill you". His reason is fear of Allah, not lack of strength. He will not escape another person's wrongdoing by taking the same wrongdoing upon himself.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If you raise your hand to kill me"** acknowledges the threat directly. Habil knows that Qabil, Cain, intends to kill him without right. His reply does not pretend the danger is imaginary.

**"I will not raise mine to kill you"** rejects answering the threatened evil with the same evil. He chooses patience rather than becoming a willing participant in wrongful bloodshed. One gloss explicitly supplies the qualification of killing wrongfully in both halves of the statement.

**"because I fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds"** gives the controlling reason. Allah is Lord over every created being, and His judgment matters more to Habil than prevailing over his brother. Fear restrains the act even where physical strength might make it possible.

### Was Habil simply unable to defend himself?

'Abdullah bin 'Amr says that Habil was the stronger brother. What held back his hand was fear of Allah. The account therefore presents moral restraint, not mere helplessness disguised as virtue.

Habil's accepted offering had already shown his devotion. The threat now tests whether that devotion governs his response under danger. He refuses to become like the person threatening him and chooses endurance rather than murderous rivalry.

### Why does intention matter in mutual violence?

A hadith in the Two Sahihs says that when two Muslims confront one another with swords, both the killer and the one killed face the Fire. Asked why the victim shared the warning, the Prophet ﷺ explained that he too had intended to kill his opponent.

The warning concerns two willing participants intent on one another's destruction. It does not erase the distinction between an innocent victim and an aggressor. Habil preserves that distinction by refusing the murderous intention his brother embraces.

One explanation draws a strong practical lesson for conflict among Muslims: refusing retaliatory killing can stop the sequence of attack and counterattack through which a whole society is drawn into bloodshed. It applies Habil's example even to the threat of assassination. Another gloss focuses specifically on refusing wrongful killing. These emphases should be kept visible rather than expanded into a complete ruling on every circumstance of self-defence.

### How was this example recalled during civil turmoil?

Ahmad records Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas speaking at the beginning of the calamity surrounding 'Uthman. He testified that the Prophet ﷺ had warned of fitnah, a time of trial and civil disorder. In it, remaining seated was better than standing, standing better than walking, and walking better than rushing into the conflict.

When asked about a person entering one's home and reaching out to kill, the Prophet ﷺ directed the questioner toward the example of the righteous son of Adam. The report teaches withdrawal from the murderous turmoil being described rather than eagerness to become one of its actors.

At-Tirmidhi records the report and calls it hasan, good. He also notes related reports from Abu Hurayrah, Khabbab bin Al-Aratt, Abu Bakr, Ibn Mas'ud, Abu Waqid and Abu Musa. Their mention locates the warning within transmitted counsel concerning fitnah.

### In short

- Habil rejects wrongful killing because he fears Allah, not because he lacks strength.
- The intention to kill matters: two willing murderers do not become morally different merely because only one succeeds.
- His example is invoked in counsel against joining civil bloodshed and escalating revenge.
- The explanations supplied concern wrongful violence and fitnah, not a complete treatment of every self-defence question.

<<<VERSE 5:29>>>
Habil, Abel, tells his brother where the responsibility will lie if the threat is carried out: "I want to let you bear your sin against me along with your other sins". He will not join in the wrongful intention. Qabil, Cain, must answer for the crime he chooses and the sins already upon him.

### Phrase by phrase

**"I want to let you bear your sin against me along with your other sins"** is principally explained as the sin of murdering Habil added to Qabil's earlier wrongdoing. Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, Ad-Dahhak, As-Suddi and Qatadah give that sense, recorded by Ibn Jarir. The expression does not require treating the innocent brother as having committed the murder.

**"then you will be one of those destined to the Fire."** warns of the consequence of unjust killing before the deed is carried out. The warning concerns accountability before Allah, not simply an earthly struggle between two brothers.

**"And that is the reward of the wrongdoers.”"** identifies the Fire as the recompense of those who transgress. One explanation reads this closing statement as Allah's declaration confirming the warning. The term wrongdoers describes people who cross His limits.

### What choice is Habil making?

He does not want to take on the crime himself by killing his brother. His words express the decision to remain free of the wrongful bloodshed Qabil is planning. If Qabil persists, the responsibility for that act will belong to him.

The principal explanation therefore connects the sin against Habil with his being killed, alongside the murderer's existing sins. Another brief gloss joins the language of the sin against the victim and the guilt of shedding his blood. These forms all centre the responsibility created by the aggressor's own act.

A further explanation develops the contrast between mutual violence and patience. Where both intend destruction, both incur guilt. Where one remains patient and prayerful, the aggressor carries the burden while the victim avoids the retaliatory sin he might otherwise have committed. That is an additional moral explanation of the contrast, not a reason to replace the principal gloss with an unrestricted transfer of all personal sins.

### Why speak about the Hereafter at this moment?

The full reward of deeds belongs to the next life, even though acceptance may sometimes be shown here, as it was through the offerings. Qabil's envy has fixed his attention on an immediate humiliation. Habil redirects him to the much greater consequence of acting upon it.

The accepted brother is described in the broader account as receiving forgiveness and Paradise, while the murderer loses in both lives. Outward success in an attack is therefore not the measure of who has truly gained. The warning still addresses Qabil before the murder, giving him reason to abandon it.

### In short

- The main explanation is that Qabil would carry the sin of murdering his brother in addition to his previous sins.
- Habil refuses to acquire the same guilt through wrongful retaliation.
- The warning places the threatened act before Allah's judgment and the reality of the Fire.
- A victim's patience and an aggressor's outward success must not be confused with moral defeat and victory.

<<<VERSE 5:30>>>
The warning does not stop Qabil, Cain, from persuading himself to commit the crime: "Yet Cain convinced himself to kill his brother". His soul makes the wrong seem acceptable, and he kills Habil, Abel. What he imagines as a solution to his grievance makes him a loser in this life and the next.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Yet Cain convinced himself to kill his brother"** describes an inward process of permission and persuasion. His evil-prompting self makes the act appear manageable or justified, despite the counsel he has heard. The murder is not presented as a conclusion forced upon him by his brother's conduct.

**"so he killed him"** marks the move from intention to action. Envy has passed through excuses into irreversible harm. The warning about Allah's judgment was heard before the deed, but Qabil chose not to submit to it.

**"becoming a loser"** concerns both worldly and final loss. Killing does not obtain divine acceptance or remove the guilt of the rejected response. It brings punishment and establishes an evil example whose effects extend beyond this one victim.

### What happened to his conscience?

Habil's words caused hesitation because Qabil could recognise that killing his brother had no just basis. Envy nevertheless remained. He supplied excuses until the resistance within him was silenced and the decision seemed defensible to himself.

The moral explanation treats this questioning of conscience as part of the test Allah places before a person. Success lies in responding honestly to the warning, not in finding language that allows the intended evil to proceed. Rationalising a deed does not change its standing before Allah.

A blessing given to another person remains Allah's gift. Trying to erase it through violence is not a way of correcting an injustice done to oneself. The temporary freedom to act in this world must not be mistaken for approval of the act.

### What do the reports say about the murder?

The narrated details describe Qabil not knowing how to kill. One report, recorded by Ibn Jarir and Ibn Abi Hatim, says Shaytan demonstrated with an animal and stones after Qabil tried twisting his brother's neck. Qabil then imitated the act against Habil.

Another report, through 'Abdullah bin Wahb from 'Abdur-Rahman bin Zayd bin Aslam from his father, describes Shaytan directly instructing him to use a stone. Ibn Abi Hatim records it. These are reported details attached to the verse, not details stated in the verse itself or supplied with a new authenticity grade here.

That account continues with Shaytan telling Hawwa', Eve, what had happened. She asks what killing means and learns that Habil will no longer eat, drink or move. She weeps, and Adam repeatedly asks what is wrong. The report ends with a saying about the women inheriting weeping while Adam and his sons are apart from that practice. The statement is preserved as part of that narration, not turned into a general command about grief or an independently established description of all men and women.

### How can one murder create a continuing burden?

Ahmad records 'Abdullah bin Mas'ud reporting the Prophet ﷺ that every unjust killing leaves a share of responsibility upon Adam's first murderous son, because he was the first to establish the practice. The hadith is also recorded by the group of collectors mentioned in the account except Abu Dawud.

Ibn Jarir records 'Abdullah bin 'Amr expressing the same consequence: Qabil carries a burden from the bloodshed that follows his precedent until the Resurrection. This does not reduce the responsibility of later murderers. Their imitation is still their own choice and their own sin.

A person's loss can therefore extend beyond what he immediately sees. The act that creates a pattern of evil carries consequences beyond its first occasion. Here the evil precedent is added to the murder of his own brother.

### In short

- Qabil's soul made wrongful killing seem acceptable after he had already heard clear warning.
- Silencing conscience with excuses is failure in the test, not a change in the moral character of the deed.
- The reported details of Shaytan's involvement remain distinguished from the verse's own concise account.
- The first murderer bears a continuing share from the precedent he established, without relieving later killers of their guilt.

<<<VERSE 5:31>>>
After the murder, Qabil, Cain, cannot even resolve what to do with his brother's body: "Then Allah sent a crow digging ˹a grave˺ in the ground ˹for a dead crow˺". He learns burial by watching a bird. His attempt to overpower another person ends in humiliation at his own ignorance and regret over what he has brought upon himself.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Then Allah sent a crow digging ˹a grave˺ in the ground ˹for a dead crow˺"** describes the lesson Allah placed before him. The bird scratches and turns the earth, using its beak and feet, and covers a dead bird with soil.

**"in order to show him how to bury the corpse of his brother."** The action demonstrates how to conceal and cover Habil's body. Some glosses emphasise the exposed condition of the corpse. Qabil had not known how to bury it, and the demonstration supplies that missing knowledge.

**"He cried, “Alas! Have I ˹even˺ failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?”"** expresses shame at falling below a bird in this practical task. The brother he chose to kill has become a responsibility he does not know how to discharge.

**"So he became regretful"** describes the sorrow that follows his loss. Al-Hasan Al-Basri explains it as Allah making him feel regret after the ruin he had earned. Other glosses identify the immediate object of the regret more narrowly, as his inability to cover the body or his having carried it around.

### What did he see the crow do?

As-Suddi reports from the Companions that Qabil first left the body on the ground without knowing how to bury it. Allah sent two crows, which fought until one killed the other. The surviving bird dug and covered the dead one, showing the action Qabil needed to perform.

'Ali bin Abi Talhah reports from Ibn 'Abbas a version in which a crow comes to another crow's body and covers it with soil. That version does not require the preceding fight. Both accounts explain the same teaching through the burial of a bird.

Another gloss says Habil was the first of Adam's children to die on earth and that Qabil carried him on his back before learning what to do. After observing the crow, he dug a hole and covered his brother. These details explain the practical helplessness behind his cry.

### Does regret here necessarily mean repentance from murder?

Not every distress over consequences is the same as repentance for the wrong itself. One explanation expressly says he regretted being unable to cover the body, not the act of killing. Another says his remorse concerned the burden of carrying it.

The broader explanation describes the sorrow following his loss without specifying that his murder was forgiven. The verse therefore should not be made to certify a completed repentance beyond what the supplied accounts say. It shows the miserable end to the act he had persuaded himself was worthwhile.

### Why is the crow's lesson humiliating?

Qabil had acted as though his desire entitled him to remove his brother. He then discovers that a bird knows something about dealing with death that he does not. Human ability becomes no safeguard against disgrace when emotion governs action without restraint.

The lesson also turns back toward the earlier stage of intention. Had he put away the evil wish before acting, he would not have faced the shame of covering a body. Burying an evil intention is not the same as merely hiding evidence after a crime. The body may be hidden from sight, but the deed remains known to Allah.

### What punishment is described beyond this immediate regret?

A prophetic hadith warns that transgression and cutting family ties are especially liable to punishment hastened in this world as well as what remains in the Hereafter. Qabil's act combined both: he unjustly attacked a person and destroyed his own brother.

A further report through Mujahid says Qabil's lower leg became stuck to his thigh and that he lay neglected until dying in distress. This is presented as a transmitted account of his worldly misery, without adding a grade not supplied with it.

The warning does not depend on reproducing that condition in every later offender. The narrative already shows that the act intended to satisfy envy produced helplessness, shame and loss. It gives reason to stop an evil design before it becomes a deed whose effects cannot be undone by concealment.

### In short

- Allah taught burial through a crow, exposing the murderer's helplessness after his act of aggression.
- The reports differ over the birds' preceding fight and what Qabil did with the body before burial.
- His regret is not automatically a declaration of accepted repentance. Some explanations connect it specifically with the difficulty of covering the corpse.
- Transgression and broken family bonds bring grave consequences. An evil intention should be rejected before it becomes lasting harm.
