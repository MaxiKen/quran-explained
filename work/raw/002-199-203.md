<<<VERSE 2:199>>>
The third verse on the rules of Ḥajj was revealed against a particular background. "Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. And seek Allah's forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful". Having commanded the remembrance at al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām after the return from ʿArafāt, Allah now insists that everyone, without exception, must first go to ʿArafāt and come back from there with the multitude.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims."**. The Quraysh, as custodians of the Kaʿbah, enjoyed a unique position of influence in Arabia. In the Jāhiliyyah, other pilgrims went to ʿArafāt. Quraysh and their religious allies, the Ḥums, instead remained at Muzdalifah. They argued that custodians of the Ḥaram should not leave its boundaries. Muzdalifah is inside those boundaries, whereas ʿArafāt is outside. They used this distinction to justify standing and departing from Muzdalifah. They claimed special standing as inhabitants of Allah's town and servants of His House. Al-Bukhārī records ʿĀʾishah's account of this distinction. The Ḥums stayed at Muzdalifah while the other Arabs stood at ʿArafāt. Islam brought the command for the Prophet ﷺ to stand with them at ʿArafāt and depart from there. She identifies that command as the meaning of this verse. Abū Bakr ibn Khaythamah relates a report through Sufyān ibn ʿUyaynah and ʿAmr ibn Dīnār. Muḥammad ibn Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim describes his father losing a camel on the day of ʿArafah. Searching for it, he saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ standing among the people there. He wondered why a man of the Ḥums was standing at that place. The hadith is in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, Muslim recording it through ʿAmr al-Nāqid from Ibn ʿUyaynah. Abū Sufyān explains aḥmas as someone particularly strict in religious observance. Quraysh were known as the Ḥums. Satan misled them with a whisper about their Sacred Precinct. Honouring another place, he suggested, would make others think less of their own. The truth was that they loved to display their pride and kept the common people at a distance. So Allah commanded them to go where everyone goes, into the plain of ʿArafāt, and return from there with everyone else. The word afīḍū, rendered here "go forth", literally means to flow or pour out, suggesting the mingling with the multitude that the Quraysh had shunned. That the place meant is ʿArafāt is the explanation of Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿAṭāʾ, Qatādah, al-Suddī and others, and Ibn Jarīr chose it and reported consensus (ijmāʿ) on it. One early report takes the words to mean returning from where the people of Yemen returned, and says the verse was revealed about the Ḥamsiyyūn who refused to press on from the Sacred Mosque to ʿArafāt. Al-Bukhārī also records from Ibn ʿAbbās a second application, that the going forth refers to proceeding from Muzdalifah to Minā to stone the pillars, and Allah knows best. The pilgrims are thus told to leave ʿArafāt as Ibrāhīm used to, without omitting the standing there as the people of ignorance did.

**"And seek Allah's forgiveness."**. Those who wanted a distinct status by staying at Muzdalifah were told that their act was a sin for which they must seek forgiveness, so that Allah might pardon them and grant His mercy. The pilgrim also asks forgiveness for any defects in his performance of the Ḥajj and for his sins generally, for Allah frequently orders His remembrance when acts of worship are finished. Muslim records that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ used to ask forgiveness three times after finishing the prayer, and the Two Ṣaḥīḥs record that he encouraged tasbīḥ, taḥmīd and takbīr, saying subḥān Allāh, al-ḥamdu lillāh and Allāhu akbar thirty-three times each after it. Ibn Marduwayh collects the hadith that al-Bukhārī records from Shaddād ibn Aws, in which the Prophet ﷺ taught the master supplication for forgiveness: the servant acknowledges Allah as his Lord beside whom there is no god, as his Creator whose servant he is, declares that he holds to His covenant and promise as far as he is able, seeks refuge with Him from the evil he has done, admits His favour and his own fault, and asks forgiveness since none forgives sins but He. Whoever says this at night and dies that night enters Paradise, and whoever says it by day and dies that day enters Paradise. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs also record from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr that Abū Bakr asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ to teach him a supplication for his prayer, and was told to say that he had wronged himself greatly, that none forgives sins but Allah, and to ask for a forgiveness from Allah's own presence and for mercy, since He is the Forgiving, the Merciful. There are many other hadiths on this subject.

**"Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"**. He forgives those who repent and is merciful to those who die in a state of repentance, merciful to the believers.

### Human equality in practice

Claiming special standing by keeping apart from others is arrogance. It should always be avoided, especially during Ḥajj. Iḥrām and the shared place and purpose teach human equality. Wealth, learning and social importance give no exemption from that lesson. A display of assumed distinction, and in iḥrām at that, makes the offence worse. The verse also teaches a social principle. People of higher standing should not isolate themselves from those below them. Eating, staying and travelling as one family builds brotherhood, concern and love. It removes barriers between rich and poor and between employers and employees. In his sermon at the Farewell Ḥajj the Prophet ﷺ declared for all time that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab and no white person to a black, and that superiority depends on taqwā and obedience. The devotional reading rejects distinction based on patched clothing, dress or another outward quality. The pilgrim should see himself simply among humanity. A thought of independent achievement or of events happening through him or for him calls for repentance. He should seek forgiveness and renew faith, because the reading identifies such self-regard as hidden shirk in the heart.

### In short

- The Quraysh and the Ḥums stood at Muzdalifah out of pride, inside the Ḥaram, while everyone else stood at ʿArafāt (ʿĀʾishah in al-Bukhārī, Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim's surprise at seeing the Prophet ﷺ at ʿArafah).
- All pilgrims must stand at ʿArafāt and flow back from there with the multitude (Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿAṭāʾ, Qatādah, al-Suddī, consensus per Ibn Jarīr), or, in a second reading, from Muzdalifah to Minā.
- Seeking forgiveness after worship is the Prophet's ﷺ practice: three times after prayer, the master supplication for forgiveness (Shaddād ibn Aws), Abū Bakr's prayer.
- The Ḥajj teaches human equality: no Arab over non-Arab, no white over black, only taqwā counts, and even the sense of having achieved something is a hidden shirk to be repented of.

<<<VERSE 2:200>>>
Verses 199 to 201 correct several customs of the Jāhiliyyah, and this verse deals with what the Arabs did once the rites were over. "When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately. There are some who say, "Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world," but they will have no share in the Hereafter".

### Phrase by phrase

**"When you have fulfilled your sacred rites,"**. When you have completed the devotions of your pilgrimage, having stoned Jamrat al-ʿAqabah, performed the ṭawāf and the sacrifice and settled at Minā, remember Allah. Allah commands that He be remembered after the rituals are performed. The devotional reading distinguishes the due, ḥaqq, of servanthood, ʿubūdiyyah, from the due of love. The rites fulfil servanthood through the self, nafs. Remembrance fulfils love through the heart, qalb, for Allah's sake. That undertaking continues through each moment and throughout life.

**"praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately."**. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr relates Ibn ʿAbbās's account of ancestral boasting during the Jāhiliyyah pilgrimage season. People recalled fathers who fed the poor, paid to settle disputes or supplied blood money, diyah. Recounting such deeds was their only dhikr. Allah revealed this verse to Muhammad ﷺ in response. Mujāhid said that in the season of pilgrimage the people of the Jāhiliyyah recalled their fathers, their exploits and their lineages and boasted of them to each other, and al-Ḥasan said that when the bedouins related anything they would swear by one's father that they had done this and that. After the rites at ʿArafāt and Muzdalifah, the ṭawāf and sacrifice, they settled at Minā. There they gathered for poetry and praise of ancestral achievements. These gatherings lacked remembrance of Allah. They wasted blessed days on matters unrelated to the purpose of pilgrimage. So they were told that as soon as they had completed the rites of iḥrām and come to stay at Minā they should devote their stay to the remembrance of Allah, in tahlīl and takbīr, and abandon the reminiscences and boastful claims about their forefathers. Better still was to remember Allah, not them, and with greater attachment and fervour. The "or" does not express doubt about which is greater but urges people to remember Him more than they ever remembered their fathers, as in "like a rock or even harder" (2:74), "feared those ˹hostile˺ people as Allah should be feared—or even more" (4:77), "at least one hundred thousand people" (37:147) and "two arms-lengths away or even less" (53:9). Grammatically, "more intensely" qualifies the remembrance that is the object of the command. One early explanation is that one should say "O Allah" as one used to say "father", or remember Allah for the blessings He showered on one as one used to recall the kindness of one's fathers. Another explanation develops the childhood analogy. A child remembers the father on whom everything depends. An adult's continuing dependence on Allah is greater still. Real honour therefore comes through dhikr of Allah rather than pride in ancestry. In the same vein the devotional reading offers a series of comparisons: just as others boast of their fathers, let your boasting and delight be in Us. If your fathers have a right over you for raising and educating you, Our right is more binding, since Our favours are more perfect. Your ancestors may have glorious deeds, but Our sublimity is beyond anything in the best of their states. If you never weary of remembering your father, remember Us continually without weariness, boredom or forgetfulness. If you are not pleased when someone speaks ill of your lineage, do not listen to those who err about Us, but defend Us. A father is remembered with respect and deference, so remember Us with awe, even as you also remember the kindness of nearness and the best of caring. He said "fathers" and not "mothers" because the father is remembered out of respect and the mother out of compassion, and Allah shows mercy and does not receive it. And "yet more intensely" because the Real (al-ḥaqq) is more deserving (aḥaqq): one may become estranged from a father, whereas no one who knows Allah can think of opposing what He has made obligatory in the smallest way.

### Wasting the days of Ḥajj

The Qur'an thus guides the Muslims to shun the customs of the age of ignorance, especially in the great days of Ḥajj, which are reserved for worship and dhikr, have merits of their own, and are a gift that may not come again if lost through carelessness. Ḥajj requires a demanding journey and absence from family and business. It costs both time and money. Even after such effort, unforeseen circumstances may prevent it. If Allah in His grace has removed every obstacle and allowed one to complete the obligation, the occasion calls for gratitude, which demands keeping busy in remembering Him and avoiding wasteful gatherings and conversations. Dhikr is all radiance and benefit for this world and the Hereafter, whereas the forefather tales of the Jāhiliyyah profited no one here or there. Muslims today hold no poetic recitals in praise of their family trees, but thousands from all over the world still spend these precious days in idle gatherings, entertainments, amusements, shopping and similar pursuits of personal satisfaction, and this verse is enough to warn them.

### The one-sided prayer

**"There are some who say, "Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world,""**. Allah encourages supplication following remembrance, which helps its acceptance. He also criticises requests concerned only with this life. Ignoring the Hereafter is an example others are warned not to imitate. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that some bedouins would come to the standing place and pray for a year of rain, fertility and good child-bearing, never mentioning anything of the Hereafter, and the verse was revealed about them. The old reports describe their requests as camels, cows, sheep, slaves and wealth. Some people of the Jāhiliyyah performed devotional acts yet prayed only for comfort, wealth and honour. Their requests showed no concern for the life to come. The verse corrects using the great occasion of Ḥajj for small present gains while forgetting the Hereafter. Such people are given their lot in this world. Their prayer notably omits the word "good". Material desire seeks constant satisfaction without considering what is truly beneficial. They do not ask whether the object is good or bad, properly or improperly obtained, or how others regard their conduct.

**"but they will have no share in the Hereafter"**. They have no portion in the Hereafter, no share of Paradise through their pilgrimage, because their conduct shows that they went through the obligation merely in a formal manner or to earn prestige in their society, and pleasing Allah and salvation in the Hereafter are objectives alien to them. The explanation identifies disbelievers whose belief and requests are confined to worldly life. They ask for health, wealth and children. Attachment to this life and neglect of the next leave them without the Hereafter's gifts prepared for Allah's faithful servants.

### In short

- After the rites, at Minā, the pilgrim must fill his time with remembrance of Allah, not the ancestral boasting of the Jāhiliyyah (Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan).
- "Or even more passionately" means more than you ever remembered your fathers, like the "or" of 2:74, 4:77, 37:147 and 53:9, and Allah's claim on remembrance far exceeds any father's.
- Wasting the days of Ḥajj on idle talk, amusement and shopping is the modern form of the same error.
- Those who pray at the standing place only for rain, herds and wealth, as some bedouins did, are given their lot here and have no share in the Hereafter.

<<<VERSE 2:201>>>
Against the one-sided prayer of the previous verse, Allah describes His virtuous and favoured servants, some of whom may be less prosperous in material terms. "Yet there are others who say, "Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire."".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Yet there are others who say, "Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world"**. The earlier statement depicts the manner of the idolaters and this one the way of the believers, and the contrast is meant to encourage people to ask for good in both abodes, for which a reward is promised. The word ḥasanah includes everything good, inward and outward. Worldly good includes health for oneself and family, and a lawful livelihood with increase and barakah, blessing. It includes fulfilled needs, beneficial knowledge, virtuous deeds and sound morals. Honour and prestige are mentioned alongside strong faith, straight guidance and complete sincerity in worship. Other descriptions list well-being, a spacious dwelling, a pleasing spouse, sufficient provision, a good profession, a comfortable mount and good repute, all of which are only part of the good sought in this life, and the early reports mention knowledge, worship, preservation from sins, martyrdom and booty. The devotional reading gathers worldly good into knowledge and worship, witnessing mysteries and successful service. It also includes purification through obedience, the Sunnah and community, firmly established faith and the sweetness of obeying Allah.

**"and the Hereafter,"**. The Hereafter's good includes Paradise and its innumerable blessings without end. It extends to Allah's pleasure and the privilege of seeing Him. Its best part is Paradise, which includes safety from the greatest terror at the place of gathering, a light reckoning and the other favours of that life. In the devotional reading, the beautiful thing of the next world is Paradise and the vision, seeing with the eyes, the realisation of union, deliverance from burning and separation, the encounter, and the "serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss" (56:89). This reading joins good here with obedient deeds, and good there with the pain of recognition. A long road separates the stage of deeds from that pain. Deeds win houris and palaces, while the one who carries this pain is drowned in light in the ocean of seeing face to face.

**"and protect us from the torment of the Fire.""**. Safety from the Fire includes being guided in this world to what leads to that good end, such as avoiding the prohibitions, sins of every kind and doubtful matters. The early reports gloss it as being spared the torment of the grave and the torment of Hell, and the request is that Allah not make one enter the Fire. The prayer is thus a compendium of all prayers, encompassing the whole range of man's worldly and other-worldly objectives: peace in this world, peace in the Hereafter and, at the end, safety from the punishment of the Fire. Al-Qāsim ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān said that whoever is given a grateful heart, a remembering tongue and a patient body has been given good in this life, good in the Hereafter and safety from the Fire. This is why the Sunnah encourages reciting this duʿāʾ. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Anas ibn Mālik that the Prophet ﷺ used to pray very frequently in these words, and it is masnūn to say it particularly during ṭawāf. Aḥmad records, and Muslim too, that Anas said the Messenger of Allah ﷺ visited a Muslim who had wasted away until he was as weak as a sick chick, and asked whether he had been praying for anything. The man said he used to ask Allah to give him in this life whatever punishment was stored up for him in the Hereafter. The Prophet ﷺ exclaimed that he could not bear that, and told him he should have asked for good in this world and good in the Hereafter and protection from the torment of the Fire. The man began reciting this prayer and was cured. There is a subtle point here: whoever wants this world alone is held back from the reward of the next, for Allah says that such a one "would have no share in the Hereafter" (2:102), and the Prophet ﷺ said that whoever loves this world harms his next world, and whoever loves his next world harms this world, so prefer what subsists to what perishes. But whoever wants both this world and the Hereafter is not held back from either, for Allah gives him what he wants. It is reported that Allah is ashamed that a servant should lift his hands to Him and be disappointed, and that He is ashamed to refuse a grey-haired person who observes propriety and holds to the Sunnah.

### Moderation in religious and worldly pursuits

Prayers, especially those offered on the pilgrimage, are the outward expression of an inward state. Prayer expresses what the heart values. Attachment to worldly riches and grandeur leaves little room for other requests. Seeking the next world instead places it at the centre of devotion. The believer's prayer is not a request for worldly riches, for material gain is only part of the test paper, and no one would ask to make his test harder. It is rather a request that Allah give him what is best for him in Allah's sight in this world and what is best in the Hereafter, and save him from eternal doom. The verse is a serious warning to Muslims who, even at the unique time of Ḥajj and in such sacred places, pray chiefly for material ends. Many rich people pray, or ask those they respect to pray, not for deliverance from the reckoning of the Hereafter but for increase in wealth and growth in business. Many who multiply litanies and voluntary acts of worship come to believe themselves very devout and special, while the same love of this world hides behind the facade. Others keep up relations with pious elders and revere departed saints mainly in the hope that their prayers and charms will keep worldly hardship away and bless their belongings. The whole affair rests with Allah, the All-Knowing and All-Aware. Everyone must examine deeds and intentions during Ḥajj or ziyārah. This includes litanies, voluntary prayer, supplication and ṣalāh. At the same time, the verse corrects those ignorant dervishes who think real worship lies in praying for the Hereafter alone and claim not to care about this world. The claim is false, for man depends on the fulfilment of his worldly needs not only to live but to perform his worship and obedience, without which the objectives of faith cannot be served. The blessed practice of the prophets was to pray for the good of both worlds, and whoever thinks that asking Allah for worldly needs is beneath piety is unaware of the station of prophethood and of man's role in this world. What is required is not to treat material needs as the purpose of life, but to show far more concern for the Hereafter alongside them and to pray for it. In the devotional reading there remains a further group, who have recognised the reality of approval, surrendered to Allah's decree and are content with His determination. They do not turn from praising Him to asking of Him, and seek neither this world nor the next, and of them the Lord of the Worlds says that whoever is kept from asking by remembering Him is given the most excellent of what He gives those who ask.

### In short

- The believers' prayer asks for ḥasanah in both worlds: health, lawful provision, knowledge, good character and sincerity here, and Paradise, Allah's pleasure and His vision there, plus safety from the Fire.
- It is a compendium of all supplication, the Prophet's ﷺ most frequent prayer (Anas, al-Bukhārī and Muslim), masnūn in ṭawāf, and he taught it to a man wasting away who had asked for his punishment in this life.
- Prayer reveals the heart: asking only for wealth, even behind a show of piety, betrays love of this world, while refusing to ask for worldly needs at all is false asceticism, for the prophets prayed for both.
- Whoever wants this world alone forfeits the next (2:102), whoever wants both is given both, and those content with Allah's decree receive better than what the askers receive.

<<<VERSE 2:202>>>
Allah promises a return to those who ask for good in both lives: "It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning." Their prayer belongs with their righteous conduct. Neither the good they sought nor the work they performed is lost from His knowledge.

### Phrase by phrase

**"It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done."** They receive a share from their pilgrimage, deeds and supplication. The explanation describes reward in both worlds and a great portion in Paradise. Allah praises the people whose request embraces this life and the next.

**"Surely Allah is swift in reckoning"** His knowledge and power do not depend on calculations or instruments. He can assess everyone's lifelong deeds and dispense their return without difficulty. The coming account is therefore not delayed by the size of creation.

### Does working for passage to pilgrimage prevent reward?

Al-Hakim records from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that a man asked Ibn 'Abbas about an agreement with his employers. Part of his wages was that they would take him to perform hajj. He wanted to know whether this was acceptable.

Ibn 'Abbas included him among those receiving a share for what they had earned. The circumstance of his passage did not exclude him from the promise. Al-Hakim calls the report authentic according to the criteria of the Two Shaykhs, while noting they did not record it.

### What meanings are given to swift reckoning?

One hadith describes the account of all creation taking half a worldly day. The statement reinforces the ease of the task for Allah, not dependence upon human machinery.

Other explanations understand the words through His swift protection or the severity of punishment for those displaying devotion to be admired. The familiar final reckoning occurs at judgment, while the elect are pictured as accounting for their conduct at every breath.

The distinct explanations all keep the person's action under divine attention. Good receives its return, and evil remains answerable.

### What further group is pictured inwardly?

The passage names those asking only for this world and those asking for both worlds. The devotional reading adds an unmentioned group: people content with Allah's decree who surrender every request.

It also connects the portion with what was earned. Good brings a good portion, while something else brings another outcome. Contentment in the further allusion does not erase the praise given to the prayer actually described.

### In short

- Those seeking good in both worlds receive their share from deeds and prayer.
- Ibn 'Abbas includes the worker whose wages provided passage to hajj.
- Allah needs no instruments to reckon with creation.
- The inward reading joins constant self-accounting with contentment in His decree.

<<<VERSE 2:203>>>
The last of the eight verses on the rules of Ḥajj asks the pilgrims to fill the final days with remembrance of Allah, so that the Ḥajj ends perfectly and the life after it becomes correct and fruitful. "And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺." It closes: "And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered".

### Phrase by phrase

**"And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days."** Ibn ʿAbbās identifies the appointed days with tashrīq: the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah. He distinguishes them from the "known days", the month's first ten.

ʿIkrimah explains remembrance here through takbīr after obligatory prayers. The jurists regard this takbīr as wājib, necessary. Other descriptions include tahlīl and tasbīḥ alongside takbīr. One account extends the period to five days: ʿArafah, the Day of Sacrifice and the three following days.

Imam Aḥmad records ʿUqbah ibn ʿAmr's report that these days are Islam's festival, for eating and drinking. Aḥmad also records Nubayshah al-Hudhalī's account, transmitted by Muslim too. It describes tashrīq as days for food, drink and remembrance of Allah.

Ibn Jarīr records a report from Abū Hurayrah about days of eating and dhikr. The Prophet ﷺ sent ʿAbdullāh ibn Ḥudhāfah to announce this at Minā. His proclamation also forbade fasting on those days.

Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim's hadith includes all tashrīq days within the days of sacrifice. The report of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Yaʿmar al-Diylī gives three days for Minā.

Miqsam conveys another explanation from Ibn ʿAbbās: four appointed days, beginning with the Day of Sacrifice. Ibn al-Zubayr, Ibn ʿUmar and Abū Mūsā also give this view. It comes from Mujāhid and ʿAṭāʾ, and from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and ʿIkrimah. Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī and Abū Mālik are named alongside al-Ḥasan and Yaḥyā ibn Abī Kathīr. Further holders include al-Zuhrī, al-Suddī, Qatādah and al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas. The view is also given by Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, by Mālik ibn Anas and ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, and by others. The following permission to hasten away in two days supports this reading. Those words concern the three days after sacrifice.

The verse directs dhikr at slaughter, after prayers and in supplication generally. It also includes takbīr with each day's pebbles at the Jamarāt. Abū Dāwūd and others record a hadith connecting these rites with remembrance. Ṭawāf, saʿy between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah, and casting pebbles were prescribed so that Allah would be remembered.

**"Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺,"**. Next comes a clarification of the length of the stay at Minā and the deadline for stoning the three pillars, an issue the people of the Jāhiliyyah debated. Some held it necessary to stay until the thirteenth and stone on that day, calling anyone who returned on the twelfth a sinner, while others held return on the twelfth necessary and staying through the thirteenth a sin. Both were corrected: whoever hurries away on the second of the days of tashrīq, after casting his stones, commits no sin, and whoever delays, staying for the third night and casting his stones, commits no sin either. The pilgrim has the choice, and sin is precluded in both cases, though it is better and preferable to stay through the thirteenth, which was the practice of the Prophet ﷺ. The jurists say that one who leaves Minā before sunset on the twelfth need not perform the stoning of the third day, but if the sun sets while he is still in Minā he may no longer leave until he has stoned for the third day, for which there is a special concession allowing it between the morning and noon. One early reading takes "no sin" on the one who delays to mean that he leaves with his sins forgiven, and another makes the hastening the right of one who avoids hunting until the third day.

**"so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺."**. Permission to leave on either day eases the pilgrimage for those fearing and obeying Allah. Such people truly deserve the Ḥajj. The Qur'an elsewhere limits acceptance to the God-fearing. Whoever was steeped in sins long before the Ḥajj, remained negligent during it and did not abstain from sin afterwards gains no benefit from it, although the formal obligation is validly discharged and he is cleared of the crime of not having performed it. The one who fears Allah during his pilgrimage and performs it as instructed is the true pilgrim.

**"And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered"**. This closing line is the essence of all the Ḥajj injunctions. The first procession was from ʿArafāt. The second disperses the gathered pilgrims towards their homes. Allah links these assemblies with the final gathering before Him: "to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered" (23:79). There, hidden and open deeds are accounted for and recompensed. During the special days, the pilgrim must guard against shortcomings in prescribed acts. Afterwards, fear of Allah should prevent pride in the achievement and a return to sin. When deeds are weighed, sins can consume the good deeds. A hadith says that one who returns from a completed Ḥajj is as cleansed of sin as on the day he was born, which is why pilgrims are told to maintain taqwā. Purification should be followed by careful avoidance of temptation. That safeguards good in both worlds. Returning to a life of sin forfeits the benefit of that earlier forgiveness. The scholars have said that one who returns from Ḥajj with his heart free of the love of this world and drawn to concern for the Hereafter has had his Ḥajj accepted, his sins forgiven and his prayers answered. Throughout the Ḥajj people pledge obedience to Allah before His House, and such pledges should not be thoughtlessly broken afterwards. A pious elder recalled temptation after returning from Ḥajj. A voice from the unseen twice asked whether he had not performed the pilgrimage. That reminder became a barrier between him and the sin. By contrast, a pious man from Turkey, a disciple of Mawlānā Jāmī, used to see a halo of light over his head, went on Ḥajj and found on his return that the state was gone. His master told him that before the Ḥajj he had the gift of humility and wept before Allah thinking himself a sinner, and after it he had grown proud and put on the airs of someone righteous, so that the very Ḥajj had become the cause of his pride and the loss of his radiance. The final emphasis on taqwā also guards against self-righteousness. Satan can introduce that thought after a great act of worship and spoil its deeds. Fear and obedience are therefore necessary before, during and especially after Ḥajj. Otherwise the worship risks being wasted. "You shall be gathered before Him" is the greatest lesson of the pilgrimage, given on the plain of ʿArafāt where millions gather every year, a gathering that symbolises the final assembly of all mankind on the Day of Judgment. Practical results come from actions, not words.

### The two sanctuaries

In the devotional reading, there are three sorts of remembrance: the remembrance of habit, which has no worth, for it is the heedlessness of the inmost core, the remembrance of calculation, which has no adornment, for it seeks a wage, and the remembrance of companionship. The fearful remember from dread of being cut off and cling to supplication, the hopeful from wishing to find what they seek and cling to praise, and the lovers from the tenderness of burning, having heard the call beforehand with the ear of love. There are two sanctuaries, the outward one surrounding the Kaʿbah and the inward one surrounding the heart of the believer. In the midst of the outward sanctuary stands the Kaʿbah, the qiblah of the faithful, and in the midst of the inward one a Kaʿbah that is the target of the All-Merciful's gaze, the goal of the pilgrims in the first case and the locus of lights in the second, "so they are enlightened by their Lord" (39:22). The outward sanctuary is kept free from the hands of evil-doers and unbelievers, the inward from seeing and thinking of others. An article found in the outward sanctuary is left where it lies until its owner appears, but what is found in the inward sanctuary cannot be pursued, for it is nothing but Allah's secret.

### In short

- The appointed days are the days of tashrīq, the eleventh to thirteenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah (Ibn ʿAbbās), days of eating, drinking and dhikr that may not be fasted (ʿUqbah ibn ʿAmr, Nubayshah al-Hudhalī, Abū Hurayrah), with takbīr after the prayers, at the slaughter and at the Jamarāt.
- Leaving Minā on the twelfth after stoning or staying for the thirteenth are both sinless, ending the Jāhilī quarrel, though staying is better, and one still in Minā at sunset on the twelfth must stone on the thirteenth.
- The concession is for the God-fearing, whose Ḥajj alone is truly accepted.
- Taqwā after the Ḥajj matters most, guarding the cleansed pilgrim against relapse and against pride in his achievement (the voice from the unseen, Jāmī's disciple), and the gathering at ʿArafāt foreshadows the gathering before Allah (23:79).
- Inwardly, remembrance may be of habit, of calculation or of companionship, and the believer's heart is a second sanctuary with its own Kaʿbah, the object of Allah's gaze (39:22).
