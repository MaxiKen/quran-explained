<<<VERSE 2:199>>>
The third verse on the rules of Ḥajj was revealed against a particular background. "Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. And seek Allah's forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful". Having commanded the remembrance at al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām after the return from ʿArafāt, Allah now insists that everyone, without exception, must first go to ʿArafāt and come back from there with the multitude.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims."**. The Quraysh, as custodians of the Kaʿbah, enjoyed a unique position of influence in Arabia. In the Jāhiliyyah, while everyone else went out to ʿArafāt, they and those who followed their religion, known as the Ḥums, would stop at Muzdalifah and stay there, saying that as caretakers of the Ḥaram it was not proper for them to leave its limits, and since Muzdalifah lies within the Ḥaram while ʿArafāt lies outside it they seized on this excuse, stood at Muzdalifah and returned from there, calling themselves the people of Allah's town and the servants of His House. Al-Bukhārī records from ʿĀʾishah that the Quraysh and their allies, who used to be called the Ḥums, would stay at Muzdalifah while the rest of the Arabs stood at ʿArafāt, and that when Islam came Allah commanded His Prophet ﷺ to stand at ʿArafāt and proceed from there, which is the meaning of this verse. Abū Bakr ibn Khaythamah relates through Sufyān ibn ʿUyaynah and ʿAmr ibn Dīnār, from Muḥammad ibn Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim, that his father lost a camel on the day of ʿArafah and went out looking for it, and saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ standing with the people at ʿArafah, and said to himself that this man was one of the Ḥums, so what was he doing there. The hadith is in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, Muslim recording it through ʿAmr al-Nāqid from Ibn ʿUyaynah. Abū Sufyān explained that an aḥmas is someone very strict in observing his religion, that the Quraysh were called the Ḥums, and that the devil led them astray by whispering that if they honoured any place other than their own Sacred Precinct, other people would belittle it. The truth was that they loved to display their pride and kept the common people at a distance. So Allah commanded them to go where everyone goes, into the plain of ʿArafāt, and return from there with everyone else. The word afīḍū, rendered here "go forth", literally means to flow or pour out, suggesting the mingling with the multitude that the Quraysh had shunned. That the place meant is ʿArafāt is the explanation of Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿAṭāʾ, Qatādah, al-Suddī and others, and Ibn Jarīr chose it and reported consensus (ijmāʿ) on it. One early report takes the words to mean returning from where the people of Yemen returned, and says the verse was revealed about the Ḥamsiyyūn who refused to press on from the Sacred Mosque to ʿArafāt. Al-Bukhārī also records from Ibn ʿAbbās a second application, that the going forth refers to proceeding from Muzdalifah to Minā to stone the pillars, and Allah knows best. The pilgrims are thus told to leave ʿArafāt as Ibrāhīm used to, without omitting the standing there as the people of ignorance did.

**"And seek Allah's forgiveness."**. Those who wanted a distinct status by staying at Muzdalifah were told that their act was a sin for which they must seek forgiveness, so that Allah might pardon them and grant His mercy. The pilgrim also asks forgiveness for any defects in his performance of the Ḥajj and for his sins generally, for Allah frequently orders His remembrance when acts of worship are finished. Muslim records that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ used to ask forgiveness three times after finishing the prayer, and the Two Ṣaḥīḥs record that he encouraged tasbīḥ, taḥmīd and takbīr, saying subḥān Allāh, al-ḥamdu lillāh and Allāhu akbar thirty-three times each after it. Ibn Marduwayh collects the hadith that al-Bukhārī records from Shaddād ibn Aws, in which the Prophet ﷺ taught the master supplication for forgiveness: the servant acknowledges Allah as his Lord beside whom there is no god, as his Creator whose servant he is, declares that he holds to His covenant and promise as far as he is able, seeks refuge with Him from the evil he has done, admits His favour and his own fault, and asks forgiveness since none forgives sins but He. Whoever says this at night and dies that night enters Paradise, and whoever says it by day and dies that day enters Paradise. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs also record from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr that Abū Bakr asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ to teach him a supplication for his prayer, and was told to say that he had wronged himself greatly, that none forgives sins but Allah, and to ask for a forgiveness from Allah's own presence and for mercy, since He is the Forgiving, the Merciful. There are many other hadiths on this subject.

**"Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"**. He forgives those who repent and is merciful to those who die in a state of repentance, merciful to the believers.

### Human equality in practice

Behaving as someone special and staying apart from others is an act of arrogance to be avoided always, and particularly in the days of Ḥajj, when the garment of iḥrām and the sameness of place and purpose teach that all human beings are equal, with no distinction of rich and poor, learned and ignorant, great and small. A display of assumed distinction, and in iḥrām at that, makes the offence worse. The verse teaches a principle of social living: people of higher status should not cut themselves off from those of lower status but should live like members of one large family in how they eat, stay and move, which creates brotherhood, concern and love and removes the walls between rich and poor, employer and employee. In his sermon at the Farewell Ḥajj the Prophet ﷺ declared for all time that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab and no white person to a black, and that superiority depends on taqwā and obedience. In the devotional reading, the pilgrim should not see himself as different from others because of patched clothing, a manner of dress or any outward quality, but regard himself as one among all humanity, and whenever the thought comes that he has done something, or that anything happens through him or for him, he should seek Allah's forgiveness and renew his faith, for such a thought is a hidden shirk infecting the heart.

### In short

- The Quraysh and the Ḥums stood at Muzdalifah out of pride, inside the Ḥaram, while everyone else stood at ʿArafāt (ʿĀʾishah in al-Bukhārī, Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim's surprise at seeing the Prophet ﷺ at ʿArafah).
- All pilgrims must stand at ʿArafāt and flow back from there with the multitude (Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿAṭāʾ, Qatādah, al-Suddī, consensus per Ibn Jarīr), or, in a second reading, from Muzdalifah to Minā.
- Seeking forgiveness after worship is the Prophet's ﷺ practice: three times after prayer, the master supplication for forgiveness (Shaddād ibn Aws), Abū Bakr's prayer.
- The Ḥajj teaches human equality: no Arab over non-Arab, no white over black, only taqwā counts, and even the sense of having achieved something is a hidden shirk to be repented of.

<<<VERSE 2:200>>>
Verses 199 to 201 correct several customs of the Jāhiliyyah, and this verse deals with what the Arabs did once the rites were over. "When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately. There are some who say, "Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world," but they will have no share in the Hereafter".

### Phrase by phrase

**"When you have fulfilled your sacred rites,"**. When you have completed the devotions of your pilgrimage, having stoned Jamrat al-ʿAqabah, performed the ṭawāf and the sacrifice and settled at Minā, remember Allah. Allah commands that He be remembered after the rituals are performed. In the devotional reading, the performance of the rites alludes to carrying out the rightful due (ḥaqq) of servanthood (ʿubūdiyyah), an undertaking through the self (nafs), while remembering Allah as one remembers one's fathers alludes to carrying out the due of love, an undertaking for His sake through the heart (qalb), continuously in the moment and throughout one's life.

**"praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately."**. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that in the Jāhiliyyah people would stand during the Ḥajj season and one would say that his father used to feed the poor, settle disputes with his money and pay the blood money (diyah), and so on, their only dhikr being to recount the deeds of their fathers, and so Allah revealed to Muhammad ﷺ this verse. Mujāhid said that in the season of pilgrimage the people of the Jāhiliyyah recalled their fathers, their exploits and their lineages and boasted of them to each other, and al-Ḥasan said that when the bedouins related anything they would swear by one's father that they had done this and that. Once they had finished the rites at ʿArafāt and Muzdalifah, performed ṭawāf and sacrifice and settled at Minā, they held gatherings to recite poetry and eulogise the achievements of their ancestors, gatherings empty of the remembrance of Allah, wasting blessed days on things of no consequence to what they had come for. So they were told that as soon as they had completed the rites of iḥrām and come to stay at Minā they should devote their stay to the remembrance of Allah, in tahlīl and takbīr, and abandon the reminiscences and boastful claims about their forefathers. Better still was to remember Allah, not them, and with greater attachment and fervour. The "or" does not express doubt about which is greater but urges people to remember Him more than they ever remembered their fathers, as in "like a rock or even harder" (2:74), "feared those ˹hostile˺ people as Allah should be feared—or even more" (4:77), "at least one hundred thousand people" (37:147) and "two arms-lengths away or even less" (53:9). Grammatically, "more intensely" qualifies the remembrance that is the object of the command. One early explanation is that one should say "O Allah" as one used to say "father", or remember Allah for the blessings He showered on one as one used to recall the kindness of one's fathers. Some have developed the analogy: one should remember Allah as one remembered one's father in childhood, when one depended on him for everything, and the grown, rational man is far more dependent on Allah at every moment than any child ever was on his father, and real honour comes through the dhikr of Allah, not through ancestral pride. In the same vein the devotional reading offers a series of comparisons: just as others boast of their fathers, let your boasting and delight be in Us. If your fathers have a right over you for raising and educating you, Our right is more binding, since Our favours are more perfect. Your ancestors may have glorious deeds, but Our sublimity is beyond anything in the best of their states. If you never weary of remembering your father, remember Us continually without weariness, boredom or forgetfulness. If you are not pleased when someone speaks ill of your lineage, do not listen to those who err about Us, but defend Us. A father is remembered with respect and deference, so remember Us with awe, even as you also remember the kindness of nearness and the best of caring. He said "fathers" and not "mothers" because the father is remembered out of respect and the mother out of compassion, and Allah shows mercy and does not receive it. And "yet more intensely" because the Real (al-ḥaqq) is more deserving (aḥaqq): one may become estranged from a father, whereas no one who knows Allah can think of opposing what He has made obligatory in the smallest way.

### Wasting the days of Ḥajj

The Qur'an thus guides the Muslims to shun the customs of the age of ignorance, especially in the great days of Ḥajj, which are reserved for worship and dhikr, have merits of their own, and are a gift that may not come again if lost through carelessness. The Ḥajj is reached only after a long and exacting journey, separation from family and business, and great expense of money and time, and unforeseen circumstances may frustrate it despite every effort. If Allah in His grace has removed every obstacle and allowed one to complete the obligation, the occasion calls for gratitude, which demands keeping busy in remembering Him and avoiding wasteful gatherings and conversations. Dhikr is all radiance and benefit for this world and the Hereafter, whereas the forefather tales of the Jāhiliyyah profited no one here or there. Muslims today hold no poetic recitals in praise of their family trees, but thousands from all over the world still spend these precious days in idle gatherings, entertainments, amusements, shopping and similar pursuits of personal satisfaction, and this verse is enough to warn them.

### The one-sided prayer

**"There are some who say, "Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world,""**. Allah encourages calling on Him in supplication after remembering Him, since the remembrance makes the supplication more likely to be accepted, and He criticises those who ask Him only about the affairs of this life while ignoring the Hereafter, a criticism meant to discourage others from imitating them. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that some bedouins would come to the standing place and pray for a year of rain, fertility and good child-bearing, never mentioning anything of the Hereafter, and the verse was revealed about them. The old reports describe their requests as camels, cows, sheep, slaves and wealth. Some of the people of the Jāhiliyyah, though otherwise engaged in acts of devotion, concentrated all their prayer on worldly needs such as comfort, wealth and honour, showing no concern for the life to come, and the verse corrects this approach by describing those who use the great occasion of Ḥajj to pray for the insignificant gains of the present life and forget the Hereafter. Such people are given their lot in this world. It is noteworthy that their prayer lacks the word "good": they do not even care to have what is really good in this world, but are so intoxicated by material things that nothing short of the constant fulfilment of their desires will satisfy them, and they do not stop to ask whether a thing is good or bad, rightly or wrongly procured, or what people think of them.

**"but they will have no share in the Hereafter"**. They have no portion in the Hereafter, no share of Paradise through their pilgrimage, because their conduct shows that they went through the obligation merely in a formal manner or to earn prestige in their society, and pleasing Allah and salvation in the Hereafter are objectives alien to them. These are disbelievers who believe only in this worldly life and ask Allah only for its blessings, health, wealth and children, and receive nothing of what Allah has prepared for His faithful servants in the Hereafter, because of their desire for this world and their neglect of the next.

### In short

- After the rites, at Minā, the pilgrim must fill his time with remembrance of Allah, not the ancestral boasting of the Jāhiliyyah (Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan).
- "Or even more passionately" means more than you ever remembered your fathers, like the "or" of 2:74, 4:77, 37:147 and 53:9, and Allah's claim on remembrance far exceeds any father's.
- Wasting the days of Ḥajj on idle talk, amusement and shopping is the modern form of the same error.
- Those who pray at the standing place only for rain, herds and wealth, as some bedouins did, are given their lot here and have no share in the Hereafter.

<<<VERSE 2:201>>>
Against the one-sided prayer of the previous verse, Allah describes His virtuous and favoured servants, some of whom may be less prosperous in material terms. "Yet there are others who say, "Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire."".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Yet there are others who say, "Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world"**. The earlier statement depicts the manner of the idolaters and this one the way of the believers, and the contrast is meant to encourage people to ask for good in both abodes, for which a reward is promised. The word ḥasanah includes everything good, inward and outward. The good of this world covers personal and family health, increase and blessing (barakah) in lawful livelihood, the fulfilment of needs, virtuous deeds, desirable morals, beneficial knowledge, honour and prestige, strength of faith, guidance to the straight path and perfect sincerity in worship. Other descriptions list well-being, a spacious dwelling, a pleasing spouse, sufficient provision, a good profession, a comfortable mount and good repute, all of which are only part of the good sought in this life, and the early reports mention knowledge, worship, preservation from sins, martyrdom and booty. In the devotional reading the beautiful thing of this world wanted by the faithful is knowledge and worship, witnessing the mysteries, success in service, self-purification through obedience, the Sunnah and the community, the firm fixity of faith and the sweetness of obedience.

**"and the Hereafter,"**. The good of the Hereafter covers everything from Paradise with its countless and endless blessings to the pleasure of Allah and the privilege of seeing Him. Its best part is Paradise, which includes safety from the greatest terror at the place of gathering, a light reckoning and the other favours of that life. In the devotional reading, the beautiful thing of the next world is Paradise and the vision, seeing with the eyes, the realisation of union, deliverance from burning and separation, the encounter, and the "serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss" (56:89). For this world's good there must be deeds with obedience, and for that world's there must be pain with recognition, and the road from deeds to pain is long. Deeds win houris and palaces, while the one who carries this pain is drowned in light in the ocean of seeing face to face.

**"and protect us from the torment of the Fire.""**. Safety from the Fire includes being guided in this world to what leads to that good end, such as avoiding the prohibitions, sins of every kind and doubtful matters. The early reports gloss it as being spared the torment of the grave and the torment of Hell, and the request is that Allah not make one enter the Fire. The prayer is thus a compendium of all prayers, encompassing the whole range of man's worldly and other-worldly objectives: peace in this world, peace in the Hereafter and, at the end, safety from the punishment of the Fire. Al-Qāsim ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān said that whoever is given a grateful heart, a remembering tongue and a patient body has been given good in this life, good in the Hereafter and safety from the Fire. This is why the Sunnah encourages reciting this duʿāʾ. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Anas ibn Mālik that the Prophet ﷺ used to pray very frequently in these words, and it is masnūn to say it particularly during ṭawāf. Aḥmad records, and Muslim too, that Anas said the Messenger of Allah ﷺ visited a Muslim who had wasted away until he was as weak as a sick chick, and asked whether he had been praying for anything. The man said he used to ask Allah to give him in this life whatever punishment was stored up for him in the Hereafter. The Prophet ﷺ exclaimed that he could not bear that, and told him he should have asked for good in this world and good in the Hereafter and protection from the torment of the Fire. The man began reciting this prayer and was cured. There is a subtle point here: whoever wants this world alone is held back from the reward of the next, for Allah says that such a one "would have no share in the Hereafter" (2:102), and the Prophet ﷺ said that whoever loves this world harms his next world, and whoever loves his next world harms this world, so prefer what subsists to what perishes. But whoever wants both this world and the Hereafter is not held back from either, for Allah gives him what he wants. It is reported that Allah is ashamed that a servant should lift his hands to Him and be disappointed, and that He is ashamed to refuse a grey-haired person who observes propriety and holds to the Sunnah.

### Moderation in religious and worldly pursuits

Prayers, especially those offered on the pilgrimage, are the outward expression of an inward state. Whatever one cherishes in the heart one expresses in prayer. One who has set his mind on worldly riches and grandeur will pray for little else, while one who seeks the next world makes it the central theme of his devotions. The believer's prayer is not a request for worldly riches, for material gain is only part of the test paper, and no one would ask to make his test harder. It is rather a request that Allah give him what is best for him in Allah's sight in this world and what is best in the Hereafter, and save him from eternal doom. The verse is a serious warning to Muslims who, even at the unique time of Ḥajj and in such sacred places, pray chiefly for material ends. Many rich people pray, or ask those they respect to pray, not for deliverance from the reckoning of the Hereafter but for increase in wealth and growth in business. Many who multiply litanies and voluntary acts of worship come to believe themselves very devout and special, while the same love of this world hides behind the facade. Others keep up relations with pious elders and revere departed saints mainly in the hope that their prayers and charms will keep worldly hardship away and bless their belongings. The whole affair rests with Allah, who is All-Knowing and All-Aware, and everyone must assess his deeds and especially the intention behind whatever he does during Ḥajj or ziyārah, including his litanies, voluntary prayers, supplications and ṣalāh. At the same time, the verse corrects those ignorant dervishes who think real worship lies in praying for the Hereafter alone and claim not to care about this world. The claim is false, for man depends on the fulfilment of his worldly needs not only to live but to perform his worship and obedience, without which the objectives of faith cannot be served. The blessed practice of the prophets was to pray for the good of both worlds, and whoever thinks that asking Allah for worldly needs is beneath piety is unaware of the station of prophethood and of man's role in this world. What is required is not to treat material needs as the purpose of life, but to show far more concern for the Hereafter alongside them and to pray for it. In the devotional reading there remains a further group, who have recognised the reality of approval, surrendered to Allah's decree and are content with His determination. They do not turn from praising Him to asking of Him, and seek neither this world nor the next, and of them the Lord of the Worlds says that whoever is kept from asking by remembering Him is given the most excellent of what He gives those who ask.

### In short

- The believers' prayer asks for ḥasanah in both worlds: health, lawful provision, knowledge, good character and sincerity here, and Paradise, Allah's pleasure and His vision there, plus safety from the Fire.
- It is a compendium of all supplication, the Prophet's ﷺ most frequent prayer (Anas, al-Bukhārī and Muslim), masnūn in ṭawāf, and he taught it to a man wasting away who had asked for his punishment in this life.
- Prayer reveals the heart: asking only for wealth, even behind a show of piety, betrays love of this world, while refusing to ask for worldly needs at all is false asceticism, for the prophets prayed for both.
- Whoever wants this world alone forfeits the next (2:102), whoever wants both is given both, and those content with Allah's decree receive better than what the askers receive.

<<<VERSE 2:202>>>
Allah now states the end of the second class, those who pray for the good of both worlds. "It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning".

### Phrase by phrase

**"It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done."**. They will be rewarded in both worlds for their correct and righteous conduct and for their prayers, receiving a great portion in Paradise out of what they earned through their pilgrimage, their deeds and their supplication. Allah thus praises those who ask for the affairs of both this life and the Hereafter. Al-Ḥākim records that Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said a man came to Ibn ʿAbbās and told him he had worked for some people, taking as part of his wages that they would take him with them to perform Ḥajj, and asked whether this was acceptable. Ibn ʿAbbās replied that he was among those of whom Allah said that they will have a share for what they have earned. Al-Ḥākim commented that the report is authentic according to the criteria of the Two Shaykhs though they did not record it. In the devotional reading, if what they earned was good their portion is good, and if it was something else their portion is something else. Two groups are mentioned, those who ask only for this world and those who ask for this world and the next, and a third, unmentioned, are those content with His decree, submitted to His command, who forgo every request.

**"Surely Allah is swift in reckoning"**. His all-pervading knowledge and perfect power suffice to assess the lifelong deeds of every person who ever breathed, with no need of the computing devices on which man depends, so the time will soon come when He calls man to account and dispenses reward and punishment on that basis. One hadith states that He will reckon with the whole of creation in half a day of this world. When He takes people to task His reckoning is swift, and some say the phrase means swift in His protection, or that His punishment is severe towards those who show off in their devotions. For ordinary people the reckoning falls on the Day of Judgment, and for the elect it occurs at every breath. The believers' share is thus the great reward for their good actions, and Allah rewards those who do good and takes those who do evil to task.

### In short

- Those who ask for good in both worlds receive their reward in both, from their pilgrimage, deeds and prayer, and even a man who worked his passage to Ḥajj is among them (Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥākim).
- Allah's reckoning is swift because His knowledge and power need no instruments, the whole creation being reckoned in half a day.
- The elect are reckoned at every breath, and a third group, content with His decree, asks for nothing at all.

<<<VERSE 2:203>>>
The last of the eight verses on the rules of Ḥajj asks the pilgrims to fill the final days with remembrance of Allah, so that the Ḥajj ends perfectly and the life after it becomes correct and fruitful. "And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺." It closes: "And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered".

### Phrase by phrase

**"And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days."**. Ibn ʿAbbās said that the appointed days are the days of tashrīq, the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, while the "known days" are the first ten of the month. ʿIkrimah said that the remembrance meant is the takbīr, Allāhu akbar, after the obligatory prayers in the days of tashrīq, and the jurists hold the takbīr in these days to be necessary (wājib). Others describe the dhikr as takbīr, tahlīl and tasbīḥ, and extend the days to five, the day of ʿArafah, the Day of Sacrifice and the three days after it. Imam Aḥmad records from ʿUqbah ibn ʿAmr that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that the day of ʿArafah, the Day of Sacrifice and the days of tashrīq are the festival of the people of Islam, days of eating and drinking, and from Nubayshah al-Hudhalī, in a hadith Muslim also records, that the days of tashrīq are days of eating, drinking and remembrance of Allah. Ibn Jarīr records from Abū Hurayrah that the Prophet ﷺ called them days of eating and remembering Allah, and that he sent ʿAbdullāh ibn Ḥudhāfah to Minā to proclaim that these days were not to be fasted, being days of eating, drinking and remembering Allah. The hadith of Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim adds that all the days of tashrīq are days of sacrifice, and that of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Yaʿmar al-Diylī that the days of Minā are three. Miqsam relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that the appointed days are four, the Day of Sacrifice and the three after it, which is also the view of Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn al-Zubayr and Abū Mūsā, of ʿAṭāʾ, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, of Abū Mālik, Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, Yaḥyā ibn Abī Kathīr and al-Ḥasan, of Qatādah, al-Suddī, al-Zuhrī and al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, and of aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, Mālik ibn Anas and others, and the apparent sense of the words that follow, about hastening in two days, supports it, since they point to the three days after the Day of Sacrifice. The verse directs remembrance when slaughtering the animals, after the prayers, in supplication generally, and in the takbīr said while throwing the pebbles at the Jamarāt each day of tashrīq. Abū Dāwūd and others record the hadith that ṭawāf around the House, saʿy between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah and the throwing of the pebbles were legislated so that Allah might be remembered.

**"Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺,"**. Next comes a clarification of the length of the stay at Minā and the deadline for stoning the three pillars, an issue the people of the Jāhiliyyah debated. Some held it necessary to stay until the thirteenth and stone on that day, calling anyone who returned on the twelfth a sinner, while others held return on the twelfth necessary and staying through the thirteenth a sin. Both were corrected: whoever hurries away on the second of the days of tashrīq, after casting his stones, commits no sin, and whoever delays, staying for the third night and casting his stones, commits no sin either. The pilgrim has the choice, and sin is precluded in both cases, though it is better and preferable to stay through the thirteenth, which was the practice of the Prophet ﷺ. The jurists say that one who leaves Minā before sunset on the twelfth need not perform the stoning of the third day, but if the sun sets while he is still in Minā he may no longer leave until he has stoned for the third day, for which there is a special concession allowing it between the morning and noon. One early reading takes "no sin" on the one who delays to mean that he leaves with his sins forgiven, and another makes the hastening the right of one who avoids hunting until the third day.

**"so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺."**. The freedom to return on the second or the third day is for the convenience of one who fears Allah and obeys His commands, for he is the one who truly deserves the Ḥajj, as the Qur'an says elsewhere that Allah accepts only from the God-fearing. Whoever was steeped in sins long before the Ḥajj, remained negligent during it and did not abstain from sin afterwards gains no benefit from it, although the formal obligation is validly discharged and he is cleared of the crime of not having performed it. The one who fears Allah during his pilgrimage and performs it as instructed is the true pilgrim.

**"And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered"**. This closing line is the essence of all the Ḥajj injunctions. Having mentioned the first procession from ʿArafāt and now the second, when the people who gathered for the rites and at the standing places disperse to their homes, Allah reminds them of the gathering before Him, as He says, "to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered" (23:79), where He will make them account for their open and hidden deeds and requite them. The pilgrim should fear Allah during the special days, guarding against any shortcoming in the prescribed acts, and fear Him after the Ḥajj, guarding against pride in his performance and abstaining from sins, for on the day deeds are weighed his sins will eat away his good deeds. A hadith says that one who returns from a completed Ḥajj is as cleansed of sin as on the day he was born, which is why pilgrims are told to maintain taqwā. Having been purified, one must take every precaution against what tempts to sin so as to earn the best of both worlds, for if the pilgrim goes back to a life of sin after such a cleansing, the forgiveness of his past sins will be of no use to him. The scholars have said that one who returns from Ḥajj with his heart free of the love of this world and drawn to concern for the Hereafter has had his Ḥajj accepted, his sins forgiven and his prayers answered. Throughout the Ḥajj people pledge obedience to Allah before His House, and such pledges should not be thoughtlessly broken afterwards. A pious elder related that after his return from Ḥajj a suggestion of sin crept into his heart, and he heard a voice from the unseen asking him twice whether he had not performed the Ḥajj, and that voice became a wall between him and the sin. By contrast, a pious man from Turkey, a disciple of Mawlānā Jāmī, used to see a halo of light over his head, went on Ḥajj and found on his return that the state was gone. His master told him that before the Ḥajj he had the gift of humility and wept before Allah thinking himself a sinner, and after it he had grown proud and put on the airs of someone righteous, so that the very Ḥajj had become the cause of his pride and the loss of his radiance. The emphasis on taqwā at the end of the Ḥajj injunctions holds a further secret: once a great act of worship is accomplished, Satan injects the thought of self-righteousness into the heart, which ruins all one's deeds, so the final word concerns fearing and obeying Allah not only before and during the Ḥajj but still more carefully after it, lest the whole worship go to waste. "You shall be gathered before Him" is the greatest lesson of the pilgrimage, given on the plain of ʿArafāt where millions gather every year, a gathering that symbolises the final assembly of all mankind on the Day of Judgment. Practical results come from actions, not words.

### The two sanctuaries

In the devotional reading, there are three sorts of remembrance: the remembrance of habit, which has no worth, for it is the heedlessness of the inmost core, the remembrance of calculation, which has no adornment, for it seeks a wage, and the remembrance of companionship. The fearful remember from dread of being cut off and cling to supplication, the hopeful from wishing to find what they seek and cling to praise, and the lovers from the tenderness of burning, having heard the call beforehand with the ear of love. There are two sanctuaries, the outward one surrounding the Kaʿbah and the inward one surrounding the heart of the believer. In the midst of the outward sanctuary stands the Kaʿbah, the qiblah of the faithful, and in the midst of the inward one a Kaʿbah that is the target of the All-Merciful's gaze, the goal of the pilgrims in the first case and the locus of lights in the second, "so they are enlightened by their Lord" (39:22). The outward sanctuary is kept free from the hands of evil-doers and unbelievers, the inward from seeing and thinking of others. An article found in the outward sanctuary is left where it lies until its owner appears, but what is found in the inward sanctuary cannot be pursued, for it is nothing but Allah's secret.

### In short

- The appointed days are the days of tashrīq, the eleventh to thirteenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah (Ibn ʿAbbās), days of eating, drinking and dhikr that may not be fasted (ʿUqbah ibn ʿAmr, Nubayshah al-Hudhalī, Abū Hurayrah), with takbīr after the prayers, at the slaughter and at the Jamarāt.
- Leaving Minā on the twelfth after stoning or staying for the thirteenth are both sinless, ending the Jāhilī quarrel, though staying is better, and one still in Minā at sunset on the twelfth must stone on the thirteenth.
- The concession is for the God-fearing, whose Ḥajj alone is truly accepted.
- Taqwā after the Ḥajj matters most, guarding the cleansed pilgrim against relapse and against pride in his achievement (the voice from the unseen, Jāmī's disciple), and the gathering at ʿArafāt foreshadows the gathering before Allah (23:79).
- Inwardly, remembrance may be of habit, of calculation or of companionship, and the believer's heart is a second sanctuary with its own Kaʿbah, the object of Allah's gaze (39:22).
