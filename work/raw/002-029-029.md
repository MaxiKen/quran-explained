Verse 28 pointed to the blessing that each person carries in his own being: he was nothing and Allah gave him life. This verse widens the view from the individual to the whole world he lives in. "He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has perfect knowledge of all things." Having shown the proof of His power in the creation of human beings themselves, Allah now points to a second proof that everyone can see: the creation of the earth and the heavens. The verse is at once a reminder of generosity, an account of how creation was ordered, and an argument that the One who built all this can certainly raise the dead.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you"** is a short phrase that gathers up every benefit a person draws from the earth and what it yields. The animals, the plants and everything else were made subservient to people as a blessing from Him. The countless rivers and trees from which people benefit and take pleasure are included, and so is the earth itself, created so that people may profit from it and learn lessons from it. The little word "for" (the Arabic lām in khalaqa lakum) expresses purpose: all of this was made for your sake. Elsewhere the same favour is stated even more widely: "He also subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, all by His grace. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect" (45:13).

**"Then He turned towards the heaven"** uses the Arabic verb istawā. Its first meaning is to stand upright or to climb, from which it comes to mean turning one's attention to something, and in a wider sense taking a straight and firm decision that nothing can hinder. Here it means that He turned to the creation of the heaven. The implication is that for the All-Knowing and All-Powerful there was no difficulty at all in creating the universe once He had decided to do so. The word "then" raises a question about the order of creation, and that question is taken up below.

**"forming it into seven heavens"** means that He made the heaven into seven heavens, one above the other, positioned vertically above the earth, just as the seven earths lie one below the other. A grammatical point lies behind the translation: the Arabic pronoun attached to the verb (fa-sawwāhunna) is plural, "them," although it refers back to "the heaven" in the singular, because the sentence already implies the heavens as a plurality. The same construction appears in "so He formed the heaven into seven heavens" (41:12). The number also settles a historical dispute. The ancient Greek astronomers, and some Muslim philosophers who followed them, used to speak of nine heavens. The verse shows that this was nothing more than a conjecture.

**"And He has perfect knowledge of all things"** closes the verse. His knowledge encompasses the whole of His creation, as He says in another place, "How could He not know His Own creation? For He alone is the Most Subtle, All-Aware" (67:14). It covers things in their totality and in their individual detail, including every detail of the creation of the heavens and the earth. The phrase also completes the argument begun in verse 28. Will you not think, then, that the One who had the power to create all of this at the start, which is a far greater thing than you are, can also bring you back after you die?

### Was the earth made before the sky?

The verse says that Allah created what is in the earth and "then" turned to the heaven. Taken at face value, this means that the earth was created first, and most scholars understand it that way. Mujāhid said that Allah created the earth before the heaven, and that when He created the earth, smoke burst out of it, which is why He says, "Then He turned towards the heaven when it was still like smoke, saying to it and to the earth, 'Submit, willingly or unwillingly.' They both responded, 'We submit willingly'" (41:11). The surah known as Ḥā Mīm as-Sajdah lays the whole sequence out: Allah created the earth in two Days, placed firm mountains on it, blessed it and measured out its means of sustenance in four Days in all, then turned to the heaven while it was smoke and formed it into seven heavens in two more Days, assigning to each its mandate, and adorned the lowest heaven with lamps for beauty and for protection (41:9-12). These verses indicate that creation began with the earth and that the heaven was afterwards made into seven heavens. This is the normal order of building, the lower storeys first and the upper ones after, as scholars of tafsīr have often observed.

There is, however, a verse that seems to say the opposite. In Sūrat an-Nāziʿāt Allah asks, "Which is harder to create: you or the sky?" and describes how He built it, raised it high, formed it flawlessly, darkened its night and brought out its daylight, and only then says, "As for the earth, He spread it out as well, bringing forth its water and pastures, and setting the mountains firmly upon it, all as a means of sustenance for you and your animals" (79:27-33). The word for "spread it out" is daḥā. Scholars resolve the apparent tension in two ways.

The first is to say that "then" in our verse relates to the order in which the information is told, not to the order in which the events happened. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported this explanation from Ibn ʿAbbās.

The second, and more widely held, is to distinguish between creating the earth and spreading it out. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī records that when Ibn ʿAbbās was asked about this matter, he answered that the earth was created before the heaven, and that it was spread out only after the heaven had been created. Many scholars of tafsīr, early and recent, have said the same. On this understanding, "He spread it out" (79:30-32) means that after the work of creating whatever would dwell on earth and in heaven was finished, the earth's treasures were brought up to its surface. When the earth was spread out in this way, water burst out onto its surface, plants of every type, colour, shape and kind grew, and the stars began their courses together with the planets that revolve with them. In short, although the earth already existed when the heavens came into being, its final shape was given to it afterwards. "He spread out the earth after this" (79:30) therefore does not mean the earth was created after the skies. And Allah knows best.

### Everything was created for you

Because the verse says that everything in the earth was created "for you," scholars have drawn out several consequences.

The first is that there is nothing in the universe from which a human being does not derive some benefit, directly or indirectly. Some things he uses physically, as food or medicine. Other things serve him without his being aware of it. Poisonous and dangerous things bring him some good as well, and things forbidden to him in one respect may prove quite beneficial in another. And almost everything can teach him a lesson or shed light on his path toward the Hereafter. The great Sufi Ibn ʿAṭāʾ remarked on this verse that Allah created the universe for you so that it should serve you while you serve Allah. A wise person therefore knows that what was created for him will certainly reach him, and does not, in worrying about it, forget the One for whom he himself was created. This is recorded in al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ.

The second concerns the law. Some scholars concluded from this verse that since everything in the world was created for human beings, the use of everything is in principle lawful (ḥalāl) and permitted (mubāḥ), except for what the Sharīʿah has forbidden. On this view a thing remains lawful to use so long as the Qur'an or the hadith does not prohibit it. Other scholars took the opposite position: the mere fact that a thing was created for human benefit does not make its use automatically lawful, so the use of everything is in principle unlawful unless an explicit text of the Qur'an or the hadith, or an argument based on them, establishes that it is permitted. A third group of reliable scholars declined to take sides. Abū Ḥayyān, in his commentary al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ, pointed out that this verse cannot support either view, because the lām in khalaqa lakum expresses causation, "created for your sake," and nothing about the lawfulness or unlawfulness of using particular things can be deduced from that. The rulings on what may and may not be used are given elsewhere in the Qur'an and the hadith, and it is those rulings that must be followed.

### Two notes carried over from verse 28

Two further points are usually discussed together with this verse because they complete the thought of the verse before it.

First, verse 28 shows that a person who does not openly deny Allah but refuses to accept the Prophet ﷺ as His Messenger and the Qur'an as His Book is still counted among those who do not believe in Allah.

Second, verse 28 mentions only one life after physical death, the life that begins on the Day of Resurrection, and says nothing about life in the grave, even though the Qur'an and the hadith speak explicitly of people being questioned in their graves about their faith and receiving some reward or punishment there. This life in the grave is an intermediate state (barzakh) between the life of this world and the life of the next. It is something like the life a person has in dreams. One might call it a supplement to this life and a prelude to the life to come. Since it is not a distinct life in its own right, it did not need to be mentioned separately.

### The chain of favours

Put together, verses 28 and 29 make a simple case. God created human beings from nothing, and that alone is reason enough for complete surrender to Him. He did not stop there but gave them an earth fitted perfectly to their needs, and set the heavens above them. And immediately after death each of them will stand before the Lord of the universe to be judged. A situation like this calls for a person to give himself wholly to God, remembering Him and obeying Him throughout his life and remaining His humble servant.

### A reading for the inner life

Heard as an address from Allah to His servant, the verse says something like this: I created everything in the kingdom of the earth for you and placed it at your disposal. My bestowal is not small, and My generosity toward those who burn for Me is not trivial. There will never be any going back on My kindness to you. Nor did I confine Myself to the kingdom of the earth. I raised the heavens too, so that you could gaze on them, so that your eyes could delight in them, and so that your daily provision could be stored in them. The servant entering the way of the divine covenant does not know the good news shared among the inhabitants of heaven and earth. Nor does he see their mutual congratulations. But I know, for I know everything and reach everyone.

There is a subtle point in the wording. Allah did not say, "Who created you for all that is in the earth." He said, "Who created for you all that is in the earth." In other words: all that fills the kingdom of earth and heaven was created for your sake, while you were created for My sake. To Mūsā (Moses) in particular He said, "And I have selected you for My service" (20:41), and to His creatures in general He said, "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me" (51:56). The one who understood the worth of this declaration best, and gave thanks for this blessing, was the Prophet ﷺ himself, al-Muṣṭafā, the Chosen One. On the night of nearness and generosity, when he was taken up to the heavens, all of creation and the kingdoms of both worlds were scattered at the feet of his truthfulness, and that paragon did not so much as glance at them from the corner of his eye. He said, in effect, "I was not created for this." As the Qur'an says of that night, "The Prophet's sight never wandered, nor did it overreach" (53:17). Abū Yazīd al-Basṭāmī, who walked the road of the Prophet's ﷺ Sunnah so beautifully and kept such fine courtesy before the Divine Presence, said that he kept crossing perils until he found the kingdoms laid before him, and the lesson of his words is the same: the kingdoms are for the servant, but the servant is for Allah.

### In short

- Everything on the earth was created for human benefit, and the heavens were then formed into seven, a second visible proof of Allah's power after the proof of our own creation in verse 28.
- "Then" has prompted discussion: Ibn ʿAbbās explained that the earth was created before the heaven but spread out and finished after it, which reconciles this verse with 79:27-33.
- The seven heavens rule out the nine of the Greek astronomers, and "He has perfect knowledge of all things" (compare 67:14) completes the argument for the Resurrection.
- Scholars differ on whether "for you" makes the use of things lawful by default, and Abū Ḥayyān held that the phrase decides neither view.
- Inwardly, the verse teaches that the world was made for you while you were made for Allah.
