The passage turns from wealth given in mercy to wealth taken through a prohibited increase: "Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan’s touch." Riba is not made lawful because it produces a profit. Allah distinguishes it from permitted trade, offers a way back to those who stop and warns those who persist after receiving the instruction.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan’s touch."** The standing is their rising from the graves. Ibn 'Abbas describes seizures and disordered movement, an explanation also transmitted from Qatadah, As-Suddi, 'Awf bin Malik, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Muqatil bin Hayyan and Sa'id bin Jubayr. Consumption includes taking and using riba, not only buying food with it.

**"That is because they say, “Trade is no different than interest.”"** They challenge the prohibition by pointing to profit in both activities. The comparison is deliberately reversed: rather than presenting riba as a form of lawful trade, they make riba their standard and compare trade with it. The explanation treats this as defiance of Allah's ruling, not merely an innocent request for clarification.

**"But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest."** Similarity in one feature does not make two transactions identical. Allah knows their full benefits and harms, including consequences individuals cannot encompass. Permitted trade benefits people, while riba oppresses and wrongfully consumes their wealth.

**"Whoever refrains—after having received warning from their Lord—may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah."** The explanation concerns gains received before the prohibition and a person who stops when the instruction reaches them. The past is pardoned in the outward ruling. The sincerity of repentance and the course of the person's remaining life belong to Allah's judgment.

**"As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire."** Returning after knowledge establishes responsibility. A past pardon is not permission to resume. The warning concerns practical disobedience and, in the discussion of permanent punishment, also the claim that the forbidden is lawful.

**"They will be there forever"** One explanation connects eternity with unbelief expressed through declaring riba lawful. Another explains the wording as a very long stay for those still believing in Allah's oneness, since only disbelievers remain eternally. A further gloss says they remain until Allah wills. These explanations preserve the severity while distinguishing a believing sinner from someone denying the ruling.

### Why is the punishment described through disordered rising?

The discussion sees correspondence between offence and punishment. A person intoxicated by greed can lose concern for the poor and shame before others. Calling riba the same as trade also presents a failure of judgment as reason. Their disturbed rising exposes that disorder rather than rewarding its appearance of calculation.

Another observation distinguishes the image from unconsciousness in which the sufferer feels nothing. The comparison need not mean complete insensibility. It can picture agitation and confusion while the punishment remains fully felt.

The explanation also accepts the possibility of demonic influence as one cause among different causes of fits or disturbance. Ibn Qaiyyim al-Jawziyyah is cited as saying physicians and philosophers recognised several causes, including this possibility. The claim is presented within that interpretation, not as a diagnosis of every earthly illness or permission to treat sufferers as culpable for riba.

Eating is a vivid term for possession that has been fully used and cannot simply be recovered. The same metaphor appears in other languages, including Persian and Urdu, and in English expressions about devouring property. Clothing, housing and furnishings purchased through riba are included in its application.

### What did the Prophet's dream portray?

Al-Bukhari records from Samurah bin Jundub the long dream account of the Prophet ﷺ. They came to a river in which a man swam. Samurah recalled it as red like blood, with a reservation concerning the remembered wording.

Another man stood at the bank beside a collection of stones. When the swimmer approached and opened his mouth, a stone was thrown into it. The explanation identified the swimmer as someone consuming riba.

The scene reinforces the warning through an image distinct from the disordered rising. The small uncertainty about the river's colour belongs to the narration and should not be turned into absolute precision. The identity given in its explanation is the relevant point.

### Why is Allah's distinction enough to reject the comparison?

The objection reasons from one shared aim: each transaction produces gain. The reply does not concede that gain exhausts their moral character. A thing may benefit one person while harming a community, or appear beneficial now while carrying greater loss later.

Allah's knowledge includes the smallest details and the widest consequences. An individual can recognise some advantage or disadvantage but cannot claim complete knowledge of every effect upon everyone. His permission and prohibition therefore disclose a distinction beyond the objector's narrow calculation.

The explanation praises His mercy as greater than a mother's care for her infant. The ruling is not arbitrary indifference to human needs. His knowledge of what helps and harms is part of the reason to trust it even where the servant does not yet understand every consequence.

Reasoned reflection is still undertaken in the extensive discussion that follows. The priority is that the servant does not suspend obedience until personally recreating the whole divine judgment. Reflection should clarify the ruling rather than make limited personal gain its final judge.

### What happened to earlier claims of interest?

The Prophet ﷺ publicly annulled the riba of the age of ignorance, beginning with the claims of his uncle Al-'Abbas. One account places this declaration at the conquest of Makkah. The extended historical discussion also describes it in the address of the last hajj.

The two placements are retained as the reports give them rather than combined into an invented single occasion. The common point is impartial enforcement: the Prophet's own family did not keep a privilege withheld from others.

Interest already consumed before prohibition was not ordered back in the account. Sa'id bin Jubayr and As-Suddi explain the past gains in that sense. Outstanding claims, however, were cancelled once the prohibition applied. Past receipt and future collection are therefore not the same legal question.

The historical explanation places the relevant revelation in the eighth year of hijrah. It says the Companions immediately abandoned the familiar practice, as they had abandoned intoxicants when prohibited. Muslims cancelled sums due to them, and disputes about payments owed to non-Muslims were referred through the governor of Makkah to the Prophet ﷺ.

The impartial cancellation prevented claiming that only non-Muslims suffered a loss under the new ruling. It was enforced as law, not left as an optional moral recommendation.

### What does leaving the case to Allah mean?

Outwardly, the person who stops under the instruction keeps the gains permitted by the past ruling. Other people are not authorised to declare an inward repentance false without knowledge. Allah knows whether the renunciation is heartfelt or merely a pretence.

Sincere repentance benefits the person before Him. A hypocritical performance does not. The wording therefore neither abandons the legal rule nor makes public appearance the whole measure of salvation.

One gloss also understands the person's future as left to Allah, who may protect them from relapse or leave them to their chosen failure. The proper response is dependence, vigilance and continued obedience rather than confidence that one declaration settles the rest of life.

### What did riba mean before the Qur'an?

Riba was a familiar Arabic term and transaction before the Prophet's mission. It was also known in the period of the Torah and the community of Musa (Moses), where the discussion says it had already been prohibited. The extended argument rejects treating the basic term as an unsolved mystery invented by later jurists.

Ibn Jarir reports from Mujahid that the practice consisted of a loan for a fixed term with a stipulated addition above principal. If it was not repaid on the date, more time was granted in exchange for a further increase. Qatadah and others give the same account.

Abu Hayyan al-Gharnati also describes the lender advancing money, taking a profit on it and increasing the charge when repayment was extended. Al-Razi mentions the monthly receipt of profit and a further increase upon delay. The basic exchange was money lent against an added payment required because of time.

Ibn al-'Arabi explains riba lexically as increase and, here, as an increase with no countervalue except the loan and its duration. Al-Jassas similarly describes a loan for a period on condition that more than principal be returned.

A hadith states that a loan drawing profit is riba. The discussion cites al-Jami al-Saghir, and al-Azizi calls it hasan, good. That attribution and grade are retained without adding a different assessment.

### Are interest and very high usury the same explanation?

One short gloss translates riba as usury and explains it as lending at unfairly high rates. The larger legal argument does not restrict the prohibition to an especially high rate. It identifies the stipulated increase on a loan, whether described as profit or interest, as the familiar riba prohibited by the Qur'an.

That difference in emphasis should remain visible. The short definition highlights oppressive rates, while the extended account insists that the prohibited loan increase is not defined away merely by reducing its percentage or changing its name.

The banking question discussed here consequently concerns interest on loans as such. It is not resolved by showing that the rate is lower than a notorious private moneylender's charge.

### What additional transactions were included?

The Prophet ﷺ brought certain barter transactions within the prohibition even though Arabs had not ordinarily called them riba. The discussion lists gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates and grapes. The list is preserved as supplied, without replacing an item from outside knowledge.

When one of the listed commodities is exchanged with the same kind, the explanation requires equality and immediate hand-to-hand exchange. An additional measure or a credit arrangement falls within the prohibited category described. The difficult juristic question concerns extension to other commodities and the criterion for that extension.

Muzabanah is described as exchanging fresh fruit still on the tree for already gathered or dried fruit through estimation. Muhaqalah concerns standing grain still in its ears exchanged for harvested, dried and cleaned grain. Wheat and chickpeas illustrate the latter.

The estimate may conceal an inequality. Quality and equivalent quantity become known securely only after the drying or preparation described. Their prohibition closes a route into riba rather than waiting for the hidden disparity to become certain.

Abu Dawud records from Jabir that the Prophet ﷺ warned those failing to abandon mukhabarah to expect notice of war from Allah and His Messenger. Al-Hakim records it and calls it Sahih according to Muslim's criteria, while saying Muslim did not record it.

Mukhabarah is explained in this account as cultivating land for a share of its produce. The explanation groups it with the prohibited arrangements just discussed. Its statement is retained as given rather than supplemented with alternative agricultural rulings absent from this job.

### What did 'Umar find difficult?

'Umar bin Al-Khattab wished the Prophet ﷺ had given fuller decisions concerning the grandfather's inheritance, kalalah and some forms of riba. Kalalah concerns someone leaving neither descendants nor ascendants as heirs. His wish identifies difficult cases, not doubt about every part of the prohibition.

Ahmad records through Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib that 'Umar said the riba verse was among the last revealed and the Prophet ﷺ died before its details were fully explained to them. He urged abandoning doubtful transactions as well as clear riba. Another discussion cites the statement with reference to Ibn Majah.

The extended argument locates his difficulty in the added barter cases: were the specified commodities exclusive, or did others share a legal cause requiring the same rule? Different jurists supplied different answers. Familiar interest-bearing loans did not present that uncertainty.

It therefore rejects the conclusion that 'Umar's words make the entire prohibition abstract and leave ordinary loan interest freely permissible. Ibn al-'Arabi argues that revelation addressed people in their own language concerning an increase they already recognised. The ambiguity of some extensions cannot erase the clarity of the primary practice.

### How do the jurists organise the two categories?

Al-Razi distinguishes riba in loans from riba in trade. Al-Jassas distinguishes the buying-and-selling category from the category without sale, identifying the latter with the time-based increase on loans known in the age of ignorance.

Ibn Rushd describes the prohibition of profit on loans as established through Qur'an, sunnah and the community's agreement. Al-Tahawi likewise says the Qur'anic riba plainly concerned loans, while the other category became known through prophetic statements. Differences arose in some of the latter's details.

Shah Waliullah distinguishes primary riba from transactions annexed to its prohibition. He explains Al-Bukhari's saying that riba is only in nasi'ah through the primary loan category, not as permission for every unequal barter prohibited elsewhere.

The discussion names primary riba as riba al-Qard, the riba of loans, or riba al-Qur'an. It says its unlawfulness was never disputed in the Muslim community. Juristic disagreements over the added category do not displace that agreement.

It supports the prohibition through seven Qur'anic verses, more than forty hadiths and consensus. Those counts describe the argument's evidentiary scope. They do not introduce verse numbers not supplied in this job.

### Why avoid doubtful means as well?

The shariah blocks means leading to what is unlawful. Conversely, something without which an obligation cannot be completed becomes required for that purpose. Both principles concern the practical path into an act, not merely its final name.

The Two Sahihs record from An-Nu'man bin Bashir that lawful and unlawful matters are clear, with doubtful matters between them. Avoiding the doubtful protects religion and honour. Indulging them resembles a shepherd grazing beside a restricted pasture and likely to enter it.

Al-Hasan bin 'Ali reports in the Sunan that the Prophet ﷺ advised leaving what causes doubt for what does not. The counsel supports caution where knowledge is insufficient. It does not claim all trading questions are doubtful or that ordinary lawful trade must cease.

Ibn Majah records from Abu Hurayrah a warning describing seventy kinds of riba, with the least likened to intercourse with one's mother. The shocking comparison conveys its seriousness. No grading beyond what the discussion provides is added.

### Can a formal contract make a forbidden act lawful?

Ahmad records from 'A'ishah that when the riba verses were revealed, the Prophet ﷺ recited them in the mosque and prohibited trade in alcohol. The discussion says the six collections record the report except At-Tirmidhi.

The Two Sahihs also record a condemnation of people who, after being forbidden animal fat, melted and sold it and consumed the price. Changing the form did not remove the underlying wrong. The account refers specifically to that evasion rather than supplying a judgment about unrelated people.

'Ali and Ibn Mas'ud report the curse upon the one consuming riba, the one paying it, the two witnesses and the scribe recording it. Witnesses and paperwork can make the agreement look legitimate to observers, but the ruling concerns its reality. Intention and substance cannot be repaired by a respectable-looking form.

That principle matters throughout the discussion of modern arrangements. An old wrongdoing does not become acceptable merely because institutions make it appear refined, orderly or beneficial.

### Must a prohibited practice have no benefits at all?

The economic reflection first rejects that test. Harmful creatures and poisons can have uses, and even theft, bribery and robbery give someone an advantage. A benefit somewhere does not settle the moral balance.

The comparison is with intoxicants and gambling, which have some benefit but greater harm. Riba may likewise bring an immediate gain to its recipient while imposing a heavier worldly and spiritual cost. A transitory personal advantage is not equal to lasting welfare.

A robber's gain is obvious, but the community loses security. No sound judgment would call the act beneficial simply because the robber prospers. In the same way, the reflection insists that riba be examined through its effects beyond the lender.

The religious argument also includes the Hereafter. A brief profit followed by an enduring loss cannot be judged solely through the brief profit. An economic calculation excluding that consequence is already narrower than the verse's judgment.

### Why can custom conceal harm?

The reflection compares habitual practice with an anaesthetic. Familiarity can make people insensitive to harms they would notice in an unfamiliar setting. Once a system becomes fashionable, its smallest apparent gains can overshadow its gravest effects.

It describes modern riba as an epidemic mistaken for a cure. A reformer may be dismissed because the practice seems indispensable. But a physician who calls disease health merely because treatment is difficult has failed the patient.

Prophets similarly call towards reform without waiting until everyone promises obedience. Were acceptance a condition for speaking truth, unbelief, kufr, and association of partners, shirk, would remain unchallenged. The preaching of Allah's oneness began despite widespread opposition.

The reflection therefore distinguishes popularity from rightness. This is its reason for continuing the warning even when banking and commerce appear built around interest. Practical difficulty requires effort, not a reversal of the ruling.

### Why is the prosperity of some countries not the whole answer?

The argument criticises pointing only to wealthy marketplaces in the United States or the European economic community as evidence of the system's universal benefit. It says the costs borne by less fortunate peoples must also be examined.

A stark analogy compares observing the prosperity of man-eaters with ignoring their victims. The comparison is rhetorical, aimed at judging a gain only where it is enjoyed. Its point is not an independently demonstrated description of every foreign market.

The reflection contends that some capitalists grow through resources drawn from a wider community, while poverty, hunger and crises fall upon others. Its criticism follows the distinction already made between individual gain and collective harm.

These are the economic claims advanced in the discussion, not a new statistical survey. Their moral use is to resist assuming that a prosperous beneficiary proves the justice of the whole arrangement.

### How does the banking critique describe concentration of wealth?

The account contrasts individual moneylending with joint-stock banks pooling many people's deposits. Supporters say this lets people lacking skill or sufficient capital receive a small interest return and enables larger businesses to obtain loans.

The critique answers that small deposit returns do not ordinarily maintain a household. Depositors still seek labour or employment, while their accumulated resources become available to borrowers with large assets and strong credit. The small saver does not receive comparable access.

Its example is a borrower owning one hundred thousand who operates a business of one million through nine hundred thousand borrowed. A one-percent business return represents ten percent of the borrower's own capital. Someone trading only with the same personal hundred thousand receives merely the one-percent return on that amount, before considering expenses.

The purpose is to show how access to pooled capital magnifies the large borrower's opportunity. It is an illustration of the mechanism described, not a complete balance sheet incorporating every financing cost.

Large buyers also obtain discounts on raw materials unavailable to small competitors. The account says dominant firms may even force a temporary market fall to drive an entrant out, accepting a loss they can survive but the entrant cannot. In that way business becomes concentrated rather than universally accessible.

### What harms are claimed for competition and prices?

The first claimed harm is exclusion from independent enterprise. People who supplied the pooled savings become dependent upon employment and whatever return the large holders choose to offer.

The second concerns control of commodity prices. Dominant holders can sell high or restrict sale to raise prices further. The wider community then pays through reduced purchasing power while the few increase their holdings.

The proposed contrast is a wider field of businesses operating from personal capital. More independent enterprises would support more workers and broaden participation in profit. Competition would give sellers a reason to reduce margins and improve availability.

The reflection does not supply a separate technical model proving every outcome. It identifies the feared concentration and presents dispersed enterprise as its desired corrective. The claim must remain attached to that argument rather than being silently converted into a universal empirical law.

### Why does the account emphasise losses as well as profits?

Another example describes a business of one hundred thousand supported by ten thousand personal capital and ninety thousand borrowed. When the business collapses, the account says the borrower's own contribution forms only a tenth of the lost resources, while the greater exposure belongs to pooled community funds.

It criticises a pattern in which profit is privately enjoyed but institutional losses return to the public. Writing off a loss at a bank does not make the missing resources disappear from society's burden.

At the same time, the failed borrower suffers personal ruin and an unpayable debt. The reflection contrasts that with losing only personal capital in an interest-free enterprise, where the person may become penniless without the same added borrowing burden. Those are two sides of the described risk, not mutually exclusive claims that the borrower never suffers.

### What example is given from Pakistan's cotton trade?

The account cites the cotton crisis of 1954. It says traders had invested mainly borrowed bank funds with comparatively little personal capital. Prices fell from 125 rupees to ten, leaving traders unable to cover bank margins.

The market was closed and government assistance sought. The government then bought stocks at ninety rupees instead of the depressed ten, absorbing losses counted in millions. The discussion asks whose money financed the rescue and answers that it belonged to the nation, including the poor.

This is the historical example supplied for muhaq, destructive loss. It is retained as the account gives it, without outside correction or a claim of independent verification. Its role is to illustrate the public bearing of a private venture's losses.

### How are insurance and the stock exchange criticised?

The discussion says losses arise through calamities such as a ship burning or sinking, and through prices falling below purchase costs. It views insurance and stock-market arrangements as institutions used to shift those exposures further towards pooled public resources.

Its criticism of insurance focuses on who receives the large payments and the use of communal premiums. It mentions fraudulent damage or theft claims for vehicles and contrasts substantial capitalist recoveries with a small number of ordinary accident beneficiaries.

Its criticism of the stock exchange describes speculative arrangements as spreading losses from price slumps across many people. Both criticisms concern the shifting of burdens in the economic picture already presented.

The passages contain strong rhetorical condemnation. They do not supply every legal distinction among insurance contracts or every form of share ownership. Such distinctions should not be invented here, nor should the criticism be represented as a new comprehensive financial ruling beyond what is stated.

### Why is high taxation said not to cure the problem?

The account recognises governments trying to reverse concentration through very high income-tax rates, sometimes approaching the whole income. It says businesses then resorted to fictional accounts and concealment of capital.

The result, in its argument, was continuing private concentration rather than recovery of the resources for public benefit. A poetic comparison pictures locking the door while overlooking an enemy already inside the house.

The diagnosis therefore remains interest-oriented concentration, not merely insufficient taxation. The reflection calls for changing the underlying arrangement rather than repeatedly treating the surface result. Its proposed principle is enterprise using one's own capital instead of private command over a nation's pooled capital through interest borrowing.

### Would ending interest simply freeze money in chests?

An objection asks what would happen to savings if banks did not offer interest. Even a small return appears better than money buried and useless to everyone.

The answer pairs prohibition of riba with zakah. Qualifying idle wealth remains subject to a recurring fortieth each year. Hoarding does not exempt it. This gives its owner a reason to place it in useful enterprise rather than watch the holdings diminish.

Proper payment simultaneously helps poor and needy people. The discussion therefore proposes a double effect: prompting investment and sharing resources. It does not present the prohibition as an isolated command without any other institution of economic life.

It expects wider enterprise when large and small owners alike seek productive uses for their own capital. Profit and work would be distributed among more participants, while the poor also receive the required zakah. That is the positive alternative advanced against the buried-chest objection.

### Is interest-free banking said to be impossible?

The discussion acknowledges that abruptly abandoning a widespread system is difficult. It rejects the conclusion that difficulty makes the prohibition false or commerce necessarily impossible.

It recalls (22:78), which denies a burden of hardship in religion. It argues that a way must be sought to preserve national and international commerce while avoiding riba. Patience, courage and steady reform are required, just as treatment of an epidemic can be difficult without being useless.

The proposed task is cooperation between experts in shariah and banking to reconstruct operating principles. The institution of a bank need not be identified with every interest-based rule currently governing it. Banking could remain and become more beneficial through a different design.

A note describes an earlier draft of interest-free banking prepared with scholars and considered practical by some experts. Attempts to implement it did not endure because of businessmen's apathy and absence of government sanction. The detailed operating rules are not supplied in this passage and are not invented here.

### How are emergency needs to be met?

The discussion distinguishes commercial borrowing from accidental necessities faced by poor people. For the latter it proposes properly functioning zakah and obligatory charities.

It criticises complete nonpayment, inaccurate calculation and merely handing money away without identifying entitled recipients. Proper payment requires finding deserving people and transferring ownership into their hands. Removing money from one's pocket is not the complete duty.

The argument claims that full and correct observance would eliminate Muslims' need for interest-bearing emergency loans. It then describes a just Islamic government collecting relevant zakah in a Baytul-Mal, a public treasury.

That treasury could meet needs, provide larger loans without interest and help unemployed people run shops or enter productive work. A remark from an unnamed European expert praises the potential of the zakah system. No name or further external authority is added to it.

The proposed remedy thus joins personal compliance, sound distribution and institutions. It does not rest on telling a distressed individual merely to endure while the community ignores its obligations.

### What moral harm is described?

Sacrifice and generosity allow someone to accept personal discomfort to ease another's distress. The reflection says habitual dependence on interest weakens that refinement. The lender begins to see need as an opportunity for extraction rather than mercy.

It also describes jealousy of others' independent rise, reluctance to share and increasing intoxication with wealth. Greed then narrows judgment until the person no longer attends to right, wrong or the final consequence.

These descriptions explain why the warning reaches character as well as accounting. Riba opposes the preceding pattern of charitable mercy not only in the direction of money but in the habits it can cultivate.

### Why distinguish committing a sin from declaring it lawful?

The discussion acknowledges that individuals can struggle to escape an entrenched system unless a government, nation or determined group undertakes sustained reform. That is a call for organised responsibility, not a justification of riba in principle.

At minimum, the person should recognise the ruling truthfully. Taking or paying interest is a sin in this account. Trying to establish it as halal, lawful, is a further and greater wrong against belief and knowledge.

Confessing the practical wrong does not itself close a business. It leaves the way open to repentance and the search for a lawful arrangement. Defending the wrong as right obstructs that return and turns one offence into two.

The warning even describes people observing tahajjud, pre-dawn voluntary salah, and dhikr, remembrance, yet entering a shop or factory without recognising interest and gambling as sin. Visible devotion does not make financial conduct exempt from obedience.

### What is the inward warning about self-authorised interpretations?

The allusive reading criticises ta'wil used to excuse turning from the command. Such licence produces neither secure independence now nor recovery later. Its paired expressions contrast loss in the immediate life, 'ajilihim, with no profit in the coming life, ajilihim.

Those responding to admonition restrain the bridle of desire instead of letting it run freely. They receive respite and an opportunity to change. Returning to the blameworthy state renews exposure to sudden uprooting and punishment.

The image returns the economic debate to the servant's own response. Institutions may need reform, but the heart must not use their complexity to silence a clear warning. Acknowledgment, restraint and a sincere return begin there.

### In short

- Allah distinguishes permitted trade from riba despite their shared appearance of gain.
- The primary loan increase is treated as clear, while some added barter applications require juristic interpretation.
- The warning reaches consumers, payers and helpers, and formal paperwork cannot legalise the substance.
- The extensive economic reflection criticises concentrated gain, transferred losses and the corruption of mercy by greed.
- Repentance, lawful reform and properly functioning zakah are proposed responses, not declaring the forbidden lawful.
