<<<VERSE 4:12>>>
The law of shares now widens from blood to marriage and then to the strangest corner of the system, the heir with no parent and no child: "You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother's side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺. ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing".

### Phrase by phrase

**"You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless"** gives the husband his share. Where the wife dies leaving no child, her husband takes half of her property, after her debts are paid and her will executed, and the remaining half passes to her other heirs, her parents or her brothers and sisters.

**"But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth"** lowers the husband's share when children exist. The child may be one or more, male or female, born of this husband or of a previous one. The rest, three-fourths, goes to the other heirs.

**"And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless"** gives the wife her share when the husband dies childless, and **"your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate"** lowers it when he leaves a child, whether from the present wife or from another. The rule reaches to the grandchildren as much as the children, even to great-grandchildren and further, on which there is consensus among the scholars. If the deceased had more than one wife, all alive at his death, the prescribed fourth or eighth is divided equally between them. Each woman does not receive a fourth or an eighth of her own. They share the single portion.

**"And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother's side˺"** opens the case of the kalālah, and **"they will each inherit one-sixth"** gives the single maternal sibling a sixth. **"But if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate"** caps the group: whether one brother with one sister, or two of each, or any number more, they share a third equally, and here the male does not receive twice the female.

**"after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺"** repeats the order of every estate and adds a guard: the will must be fair and free of every kind of harm, neither depriving rightful heirs of all or part of their shares, nor adding to the fixed portion Allah ordained for anyone. Whoever does that has disputed with Allah concerning His decision and His division.

**"˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing"** closes with the same authority as the verse before it. He apportioned these shares knowing every heir's true state, He knows who obeys and who contravenes, and though He may hold back the punishment in this world, the one who disobeys should not deceive himself that he escaped.

### The husband's share first

The verse names the husband's share before the wife's, and there is a likely reason in the world it entered. After the death of the wife, the husband becomes attached to some other family. If the wife dies in her parents' home, with her assets there, her people may be tempted to avoid giving the husband his due. Naming his right first shuts the door on that practice.

One ruling completes the picture of the marriage bond. Before any distribution of inheritance, it must be ascertained that the mahr (dower) of the wife has been paid. If the deceased did not pay it, the mahr counts as a debt and is settled first from the total property, like every other debt, and only then is the inheritance distributed. Having received her mahr, the woman still receives her fixed share as a competent inheritor. If the estate is worth no more than the dower, the entire property goes to her against the debt of the mahr, and no heir receives anything from it.

### What a kalālah is

The word was debated from the earliest day. Lexically it is a derivative of iklīl, the crown that surrounds the head, and the sense here is that the person's heirs come from other than the first degree of relative. Ash-Shaʿbī reported that when Abū Bakr aṣ-Ṣiddīq was asked the meaning of kalālah, he said:

> I will say my own opinion about it. If it is correct, the correctness is from Allah. If it is wrong, it is my error and the evil effort of Shayṭān, and Allah and His Messenger have nothing to do with it. Kalālah refers to the man who has neither descendants nor ascendants.

When ʿUmar became the khalīfah, he said: "I hesitate to contradict an opinion of Abū Bakr." Ibn Jarīr and others recorded it. And on his deathbed, among the last to see ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb was Ibn ʿAbbās, to whom he said: "What you said was the correct opinion." Asked what he had said, he explained: that kalālah is the person who has no child and no parent.

The same view is held by Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, by Zayd ibn Thābit and Ibn Masʿūd, by an-Nakhaʿī and ash-Shaʿbī, by Qatādah and Jābir ibn Zayd, by al-Ḥakam and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and by the people of al-Madīnah, Kūfah and Baṣrah, the Seven jurists of Madīnah, the Four Imams, and the majority of the scholars of the past and present, so that some scholars declared a consensus on it. Al-Qurṭubī gathers the definitions, and al-Ālūsī, in his Rūḥ al-Maʿānī, adds the root: kalālah is really a verbal noun from kalal, to become exhausted, denoting weakness. The name is applied to every relationship other than that of father and son, because that relationship is weak beside it. It came to be applied to the deceased who left no son or father to inherit, and to the inheritor who is neither the son nor the father of the deceased, with the word dhu, possessor, understood but unspoken: one having a weak relation. It even came to be applied to the property left by such a deceased.

### Brothers and sisters of the mother

The siblings in this verse are the akhyaḳī brothers and sisters, from the same mother and different fathers. The restriction is not spelled out in the verse, but consensus holds it. Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqās, among the salaf, read the verse as "a brother or sister from his mother", a rendition reported by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ālūsī and al-Jaṣṣāṣ. It did not come through tawātur, a mass transmission, but the agreement of the entire Muslim ummah makes it the reading to practice by. The clear proof is at the end of this surah, where the inheritance of the kalālah appears again. There a single sister takes half, a single brother takes the whole, two sisters take two-thirds, and several brothers and sisters divide with the male taking twice the female. That ruling concerns the full siblings and the siblings of the same father. Were they included in our verse as well, the two injunctions would contradict each other.

Maternal siblings stand apart in four ways. First, they inherit on account of their mother. Second, their males and females take exactly the same share. Third, they inherit only in the kalālah estate, for they have no share where the deceased leaves a father, grandfather, child or grandchild. Fourth, they never take more than a third, however many they are.

### Why the will is named before the debt

In all three places where this section mentions the will, the word comes before the debt, though the practice of the Prophet ﷺ, as the scholars of the early generations settled it, was that debt is settled before the will is executed, and there is consensus on this among the scholars. One of the leading figures of the first generation removed the misunderstanding plainly: you recite the verse as "settling the will they might have made, or debt", but in practice the Prophet ﷺ settled the debt before the will.

Why, then, does the wording put the will first? Because the beneficiary of a will deserves it without paying any price, often without even a kinship to the testator, and the inheritors, seeing property they hoped for going elsewhere, might ignore the will or delay it. Not every person incurs a debt, and a debt, once due, is claimed by creditors whom the inheritors cannot refuse. The will has no such claimant once the deceased is gone, and it was the deceased's earnest hope, a running charity on his behalf. The verse puts the will first to protect it.

### No will for an inheritor

Making a will in favour of an heir is not lawful. A will for a son, a daughter, a husband or a wife, or anyone else who will already receive a share, is not enforceable. They receive their fixed shares and nothing beyond. In his famous address during his last ḥajj, the Prophet ﷺ said: "Surely, Allah has given every person his or her right. So there is no will for an inheritor." It is recorded in the Mishkāt with reference to Abū Dāwūd. One exception is allowed: where the other inheritors permit, the will in favour of a particular inheritor may be executed, and the rest distributed by the sharīʿah, with the inheritor still taking his fixed share. The hadith narrations carry the exception "unless the inheritors wish", as given in al-Hidāyah.

### Without harm to anyone

The words "without harm" guard the whole order of debts and wills against misuse. There are many ways to wound heirs through them. A man may lie about being in debt so the amount goes to a friend. He may declare what he owns personally to be a trust held for someone else, to keep it out of the estate. He may will more than a third. He may lie that a loan he gave was repaid, so it never reaches the inheritors. He may, in his final illness, gift away more than a third of his property. Every one of these is causing harm, strictly forbidden and a major sin. Though the words appear only with the kalālah rules, the rule they lay down is general, and is read at the two earlier mentions of debt and will as well. Every person about to leave this world should stay far from all of it in the last moments of life.

### The inner reading

Inheritance and worthiness to receive it are established in two ways: by a cause or by a lineage. The cause is marriage, and marriage is the cause of love, as Allah says: "He placed between you love and mercy" (30:21). Lineage is assistance and strength, as the report says: a man is many through his brothers. When a relative by lineage or a near one by cause dies, the wound lands on the heart and the pain on the spirit, and the Lord of the Worlds places balm on the pain. After the suffering, He commands consolation through the wealth of the one who passed. As the pain came from his going, the balm comes from what he left. This is His custom with His friends: if He places suffering on them through the prescribed Law, He makes a treasure appear afterward with the attribute of relief. Shaykh al-Islām Anṣārī said:

> How should I have known that suffering is the mother of happiness, and that beneath one disappointment lie a thousand treasures? How should I have known that hope is the courier of union, and that beneath the cloud of munificence despair is impossible? How should I have known that the Lovingly Kind is so forbearing that His gentleness to the sinner is beyond reckoning? How should I have known how servant-caressing is the Possessor of Majesty, and how much joy His friends have in Him?

He added that once, at the hour of dawn, the image of that comfort of the spirit came to him and asked: "How are you, wounded one?" He answered: "In my passion for You, that is the first opening."

### In short

- A husband takes half of his wife's estate if she died childless, a fourth if she left a child. A wife takes a fourth of her husband's estate if he died childless, an eighth if he left a child. Co-wives share the single portion equally, and grandchildren count as children.
- An unpaid mahr is a debt, paid before any distribution, and the wife then takes her fixed share on top of it.
- The kalālah is the deceased who leaves neither parent nor child, as Abū Bakr aṣ-Ṣiddīq defined it and ʿUmar confirmed on his deathbed. In such an estate, maternal siblings take a sixth each, or share a third equally, male and female alike, never more.
- Debts are settled before wills in practice, though the wording names the will first to protect it, and no will may be made for an inheritor or stretched past a third.
- The words "without harm" forbid every trick that empties the estate before the heirs: false debts, invented trusts, exaggerated wills, disguised gifts in the final illness.
- Read inwardly, the verse is balm: where death wounds the heart, the inheritance the Law assigns is the relief placed beside the wound.

<<<VERSE 4:13>>>
The shares end and the warning begins: "These ˹entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph". It is the consistent style of the Qur'an: once the beliefs and injunctions are laid down, promises of reward and warnings of punishment follow, pressing the believers to obey.

### Phrase by phrase

**"These ˝entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah"** names the farāʾiḍ for what they are, His ḥudūd, His bounds: the commands and prohibitions by which His servants ʿibād show their devotion, and what He has allotted each heir according to the degree of relation and the degree of dependence. Bounds are not to be infringed. The root of servanthood, ʿubūdiyya, is preserving the ḥudūd and maintaining the promises, the ʿuhūd. Whoever keeps within His limit, nothing bad or harmful reaches him. The root of every affliction is going beyond the bounds.

**"Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger"** means obeying regarding the inheritance: adding nothing to the fixed shares by tricks and plots, subtracting nothing from them, giving each heir the appointed share as Allah commanded and decided.

**"will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow"** is the reward, read in one rendering as "He will admit him" and in another as "We will admit him". The rivers run beneath the habitations and trees, rivers of wine, water, honey and milk.

**"to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph"** is the dwelling that never ends and never breaks, the greatest salvation there is, which no other success comes near.

### Further limits the sunnah sets

Around the fixed shares stand further rules, settled in the sunnah and the books of fiqh, that guard who may inherit at all.

A Muslim does not inherit from a disbeliever, and a disbeliever does not inherit from a Muslim, whatever the lineage between them. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The Muslim does not inherit the kafir, nor does the kafir inherit the Muslim." This applies where the two religions are fixed from birth. A person who was Muslim and turned apostate is different: whatever he earned while Muslim goes to his Muslim inheritors, and whatever he earned after apostasy is deposited in the Bayt al-Māl, the public treasury. For a woman who apostatizes, all her property, from her days of Islam or of apostasy, goes to her Muslim inheritors. An apostate, man or woman, inherits from no one at all.

The killer does not inherit. Whoever kills a person from whose property he was entitled to a share is cut out of that inheritance, as the Prophet ﷺ said: "The killer shall not inherit." Some forms of accidental killing are excepted, the details of which stand in the books of fiqh.

The unborn child inherits. If a man leaves children and his wife is pregnant, the child in the womb is counted among the inheritors, and since its sex and number cannot be known, the distribution should be postponed until the birth. If it must be made at once, two situations are supposed, a boy or a girl, the inheritors are given the lesser portion of the two, and the remainder is held for the child.

The woman in her ʿiddah, the waiting period, may still inherit. If a man divorces his wife revocably and dies before the divorce takes effect and before her waiting period expires, she inherits, for the marriage was still in force. If he divorces her during the sickness that ends in his death, even irrevocably or with the three pronouncements, and dies before her waiting period expires, she still inherits, and the longer of the two waiting periods is applied for her: the three menstrual periods of divorce, or the four months and ten days of widowhood, whichever lasts longer, so that she receives the share as far as possible. But if he divorced her irrevocably or thrice before any death-illness, and he died a few days later while she was still waiting, she does not inherit, unless the divorce was revocable. And if the wife secures separation by khulʿ, at her own instance, during the sickness that kills him, she does not inherit, even if he dies within her waiting period.

### The residue and its people

Twelve heirs hold shares fixed by the sharīʿah, the aṣhāb al-furūḍ, the possessors of obligatory portions, as explained in the verses of this section. Where there is no such heir, or where property remains after they are paid, the residue goes to the ʿaṣaba, the agnatic residuaries. A person can inherit in both capacities at once. The children of the deceased and his father become ʿaṣaba, and so do the offspring of the father, the brother. An example shows the whole machine: Zayd dies leaving a wife, a daughter, a mother and an uncle. His property is divided into twenty-four shares. Twelve go to the daughter, her half. Three go to the wife, her eighth. Four go to the mother, her sixth. The residue of five goes to the uncle as the nearest male agnate. Where there are no ʿaṣaba at all, the residue returns to the fixed-share heirs in proportion to their shares, the principle of radd, the return. The husband and the wife are the exception: they never receive more than their specified shares.

### In short

- The fixed shares are Allah's bounds, and obeying them, exactly, without additions or subtractions, admits a person to Gardens beneath which rivers flow, forever.
- Bounds in inheritance include who may inherit: no cross-religion inheritance, no inheritance for the killer, full rights for the unborn child, and the protection of the divorced wife still within her waiting period.
- After the fixed-share heirs are paid, the residue goes to the agnatic residuaries, and where there are none, it returns proportionally to the fixed-share heirs, except the spouse.

<<<VERSE 4:14>>>
The counterpart of the promise: "But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and exceeds their limits will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever. And they will suffer a humiliating punishment". To change what Allah has ordained is to dispute His judgement, and that is the behaviour of people who do not accept what Allah has decided and divided. So He punishes them with humiliation in an eternal, painful torment.

### Phrase by phrase

**"But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger"** means disobeying regarding the division of estates, contravening the legal rulings through deviation and refusal.

**"and exceeds their limits"** is to step over the bounds He set for His servants so they would act within them, not against them.

**"will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever"** is the counterpart dwelling, read with the same two renderings as the promise of Paradise, "He will admit" and "We will admit", and the abiding has no end, as long as Allah wills.

**"And they will suffer a humiliating punishment"** is the sharpest edge of the sentence. There are two punishments, one immediate and one postponed, and ignominy joins them both. If created beings tried to humiliate the disobedient as their disobedience has already humiliated them, they could not manage it. The saying is put plainly: whoever spends the night committing a sin wakes with his degradation upon him, wa-ʿalayhi madhallatuhu, and whoever wakes and passes the day in pious acts has his dignity upon him, wa-ʿalayhi mahābatuhu. One reading of the closing words takes them as a shameful doom, another as a severe torment.

### The seventy-year warnings

Two hadiths fix the seriousness of a will. Imam Aḥmad recorded from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

> A man might perform the actions of righteous people for seventy years, but when it is time to compile his will, he commits injustice. So his final work will be his worst, and he thus enters the Fire. A man might perform the deeds of evil people for seventy years, yet he is fair in his will. So his final work will be his best, and he thus enters Paradise.

Abū Hurayrah then said: read, if you will, from "These are the limits set by Allah" to "a humiliating punishment". Abū Dāwūd, in the chapter of his Sunan on injustice in wills, recorded with Abū Hurayrah again the parallel report:

> A man or a woman might perform actions in obedience to Allah for sixty years. Yet, when they are near death, they leave an unfair will and thus acquire the Fire.

At-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah also recorded it, and at-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan gharīb. The math of the verses is terrible and simple: a lifetime can be spent in obedience and undone by the document signed at the end, or spent in sin and redeemed by one fair division.

### In short

- Transgressing the limits of inheritance, changing the shares, loading the will against the heirs, is disobedience to Allah and His Messenger with Hell as its stated end.
- The punishment is humiliating: sin degrades a person immediately, and the disgrace continues into the Hereafter.
- The last act weighs enormously. Seventy years of righteousness can be lost to an unjust will, and seventy years of evil redeemed by a fair one.
