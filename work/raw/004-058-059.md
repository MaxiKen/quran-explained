<<<VERSE 4:58>>>
A verse revealed inside the Kaʿbah, and large enough to hold a whole theory of the state: "Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people, judge with fairness. What a noble commandment from Allah to you! Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing". Two commands, one about what is held in trust, one about how disputes are decided, and both addressed to everyone who holds anything at all.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners"** commands that the trusts be rendered back to those to whom they are due. The addressee is everyone who holds anything in trust, the masses and those in authority alike. The Holy Qur'an deliberately uses the plural, amānāt, and the plural is a hint that a trust is not simply cash or kind left for safe-keeping. The command covers all things one is expected to look after, Allah's rights on His servants, such as praying, zakāh, fasting, penalties for sins and vows, and the rights of the servants on each other, including the cases that are never recorded or documented.

**"and when you judge between people, judge with fairness"** commands justice between people, addressed primarily to those in authority who decide cases, and secondarily to all appointed to arbitrate, since among the common people the practice is to appoint a neutral arbitrator.

**"What a noble commandment from Allah to you!"** praises the whole instruction, the returning of trusts, the judging with justice, and all His complete, perfect and great commandments and laws.

**"Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing"** means He hears the statements and knows the actions, even of the person with no ability to speak, no power or means to protest. That is why His principles are practice-worthy in every country, every age, always, while principles made by human minds stay within the parameters of their environment and must change when circumstances change.

### The key of the Kaʿbah

The verse was revealed in the background of the Kaʿbah's custodial service, an office of great honour even before Islam, with the areas of service distributed over different people. From the days of Jāhiliyyah, the providing of drinking water to pilgrims from the well of Zamzam during Ḥajj, the siqāyah, was entrusted to al-ʿAbbās, the revered uncle of the Prophet ﷺ, and similar services were in the charge of Abū Ṭālib, another uncle. The custodial duty of keeping the keys of the House and opening and closing it on fixed days belonged to ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah al-Ḥajabī.

Before the Hijrah, the Prophet ﷺ came with some Companions to enter the Kaʿbah, and ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah, then an unbeliever, stopped him rudely. The Prophet ﷺ showed great restraint, tolerated the harsh words, and said: "O ʿUthmān, a day will come when you would perhaps see this key in my hands, when I shall have the power and choice to give it to anyone I choose." ʿUthmān answered that then the Quraysh would be uprooted and disgraced, and the Prophet ﷺ said no, they would be established and very honourable. ʿUthmān's own account continues: the soul-searching convinced him it would happen, and he resolved to embrace Islam then and there, but his people vehemently opposed him, and he could not. At the conquest of Makkah the Prophet ﷺ called for the key, and ʿUthmān presented it. Some narrations say he had climbed over the Baytullāh with the key, and ʿAlī, in deference to the Prophet's order, took it forcibly from his hand.

So the Prophet ﷺ went in, offered his prayers, and coming out, returned the key, saying: "Here, take it. Now this key will always remain with your family right through the Last Day. Anyone who will take this key from you will be a tyrant." Al-Muṣṭafā ﷺ also instructed him to use whatever money or things came in return for this service in accordance with the rules set by the shariah of Islam. As ʿUthmān walked off delighted with the key, the Prophet ﷺ called him back: "Remember, ʿUthmān, did I not tell you something way back? Has it come to pass, or has it not?" And that was when ʿUthmān recited the Kalimah and entered Islam, as al-Mazharī relates from Ibn Saʿd. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb testified that the Prophet ﷺ came out of the Kaʿbah reciting this very verse, which he had never heard from him before. Ibn Jarīr recorded the report through Ibn Jurayj, that the verse was revealed concerning ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah, and it is popular as the reason for its revelation, though the application is general, which is why Ibn ʿAbbās and Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyyah said this verse is for the righteous and the wicked alike. At his death, ʿUthmān passed the key to his brother Shayba, and it remained in his descendants' keep.

The returning itself was a judgment, for al-ʿAbbās and ʿAlī had both requested the office of the key, since the water supply and custodial services were already in their charge. The Qur'an directed the Prophet ﷺ, in verse 58 itself, to bypass their request and return the key to none else but ʿUthmān, taking into consideration the nature of the trust involved. One reading of the judgment places it between ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah and al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAbd, of the house of al-Muttalib, giving the key back to ʿUthmān and the water to al-ʿAbbās. That the ruler's choice is bound by the command is the first lesson of the scene: the real command is from Allah, and all rulers of the mortal world are carriers of it.

### Trusts of every kind

Ḥasan al-Baṣrī narrated from Samurah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "Return the trust to those who entrusted you, and do not betray those who betrayed you." Imam Ahmad and the collectors of the Sunan recorded this hadith. Those who do not implement the command in this life will have it extracted from them on the Day of Resurrection, qiyāmah, for it is recorded in the Ṣaḥīḥ: "The rights will be rendered back to those to whom they are due, and even the sheep that does not have horns will take revenge from the horned sheep."

Anas said it must have been a rare sermon in which the Prophet ﷺ did not say: "One who does not fulfil trust obligations has no faith, ʾīmān, with him, and one who does not stand by his word of promise has no religion with him", reported by al-Baihaqī in the Shuʿab al-ʾĪmān. And in narrations from Abū Hurairah and Ibn ʿUmar in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, among the signs of hypocrisy stands: give him a trust, amānah, and he will commit a breach, khiyānah.

The key of the Kaʿbah was not a financial asset, and that is precisely the verse's hint. Offices and ranks of government, whatever they may be, are handed over as trusts from Allah, and their holders are trustees. This covers everyone who wields the powers of appointment and dismissal. It is not permissible to give any office or job to someone undeserving of it in practical expertise or intellectual capability. If the most perfect candidate cannot be found, preference goes to the one superior in capability and trustworthiness from among those available, the best of the lot. The Prophet's ﷺ warning is stark on the appointment of the undeserving. Whoever, charged with a responsibility relating to the general body of Muslims, gives an office away on friendship or connection alone, without weighing capability or merit, draws the curse of Allah on himself. No act of worship he was obligated with, fard, is accepted from him, nor any voluntary offering, nafl, all along until he enters the Fire. In some narrations, entrusting an office to one person while knowing another is more suited and deserving is itself a breach against Allah, His Messenger and all the Muslims. When the responsibility of affairs is placed under the charge of those unfit for it, the instruction recorded in the Kitāb al-ʿIlm of the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī says: wait for the Day of Doom, for that chaos has no solution. The corruption seen in many systems of governance, with offices distributed on connections, recommendations and bribes, is the sad consequence of ignoring this teaching.

The kinds multiply in the hadith. Meetings are a kind of trust, so that reporting what transpired without the participants' permission is not permissible. The consultant is a trustee, bound to give the counsel he judges most beneficial, and if he gives advice he knows is not in the seeker's best interest, he has breached the trust right there. A secret confided becomes an article of trust, and disclosing it without permission is a breach. A trust is not to be doled out to a beggar out of pity, or to a relative or friend to fulfil their rights, for what is held in trust goes to its rightful owner, properly and duly, and to no one else.

### Judge with fairness

Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb, Zayd ibn Aslam and Shahr ibn Ḥawshab said the judging command came down about those in authority, the judges between people. A hadith states: "Allah is with the judge as long as he does not commit injustice, for when he does, Allah will make him reliant on himself", and a saying goes that one day of justice equals forty years of worship. The just person is loved by Allah and closest to Him, while the unjust is cast far from His mercy and grace, and the first to go under the shade of Allah will be those who hasten to accept truth when it appears, who spend their wealth when asked, and who judge between people as fairly as they would have done for themselves.

The verse says "between people", not between Muslims or between believers. All human beings are equal in the sight of the decider: Muslims or non-Muslims, friends or enemies, same country or another, same colour and language or different. The judge is duty-bound to cast all these connections aside and let the decision follow truth, right and justice. And the one who lacks the ability and power to maintain equity should not become a judge at all, for judging with fairness is itself a trust too heavy for the weak and incapable. When Abū Dharr requested the governorship of some place, the Prophet ﷺ answered:

> O Abū Dharr, you are weak and this is an office of trust, which may, on the Day of Doom, become the cause of disgrace and remorse, except for one who has fulfilled all his trust obligations, fully and duly.

### The state the verse sketches

Four principles emerge from the verse's opening. First, by beginning with "Surely, Allah commands you", the real command and rule are shown to be from Allah, and the supreme authority, the ultimate sovereignty, belongs to none but Him. Second, the offices of government are not the rights of a country's residents to be distributed by the ratio of population, which error has forced fixed quotas into many constitutions, so that a person from another province cannot be appointed no matter how deserving, while the local man may be an evil-doer. Offices are trusts, given only to those capable and deserving of them, whatever their province, though preferring a local appointee is sound where ability and trustworthiness are first verified. Third, man's rule on this earth is legitimate only as a deputy or trusteeship, bound and guided in lawmaking by the principles laid down by Allah, the Absolute Sovereign, given through revelation. Fourth, it is the standing duty of those in authority to judge with equity, without discrimination of race, country of origin, colour, language, even religion and creed.

Trust comes before justice in the verse's order, and the order is necessary. A system guaranteeing equity all over a country is just not possible unless those who hold power first fulfil their trust obligations, appointing the best of the lot to every job, with no back doors of friendship, connection, recommendation or bribe. Otherwise gangs of the undeserving entrench themselves, and even a ruler genuinely wishing to usher in an era of justice can do nothing about it, for these officials are the very functionaries through which the government acts. The command structure itself has layers, and verse 59 will spell them out. The direct commands, the rejection of shirk and kufr, the worship of the One and the ṣalāh He made fard, are His word outright. The open field, the mubāḥ, is what the Book and sunnah leave unrestricted, rated neither wājib nor ḥarām, and its rules are entrusted to rulers so that systems run.

### The inner reading

After the servant's faith and recognition, no attribute outranks holding in trust, and after unbelief none is uglier than betrayal. Obedience comes forth from holding in trust, disobedience from betrayal, the basis of corruption, the start of every misfortune, the foundation of disobedience. To hold in trust is the religion's pillar, tawḥīd's perfection, and the mark of prophets and angels. The revealed Book describes Gabriel as the one "brought down by the trustworthy spirit" (26:193), and elsewhere as "obeyed, and also trustworthy" (81:21). The daughter of Shuʿayb said of Mūsā (Moses), God's speaking companion, "hire him, surely the best one to hire is the one strong and trustworthy" (28:26), and the king of Egypt said of Yūsuf (Joseph) the sincerely truthful, "surely today with us you are emplaced, established, trusted" (12:54). The trusts of the Book and the sunnah are three: obedience and the religion, which the Lord of the Worlds calls the Trust He offered (33:72), then women, who are held as a trust by men, taken as a trust from God with their private parts made lawful by His word, and third the property left with someone or the secret told to him, "let him who has been entrusted deliver the trust" (2:283). Deliver the trust back to the one who entrusted you. If a man relates some words, pay regard, for they are his trust. And sitting together is a trust: company must be kept on the condition of holding in trust.

### In short

- The verse was revealed over the key of the Kaʿbah, returned to ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah against the requests of al-ʿAbbās and ʿAlī, and its rule is universal, for the righteous and the wicked alike.
- Every office is a trust, and connection-based appointment draws a curse that no fard or nafl worship lifts.
- Judgment is between people, all people, and the weak, like Abū Dharr, are warned off the bench before they are disgraced by it.
- The verse sketches the state: Allah's sovereignty, offices as trusts, rule as deputyship, justice without discrimination.
- Read inwardly, trustworthiness ranks right after faith, the attribute of Gabriel, Mūsā and Yūsuf, and its three trusts are the religion, the wife, and whatever is left or told to you.

<<<VERSE 4:59>>>
The community's chain of command: "O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. Should you disagree on anything, then refer it to Allah and His Messenger, if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day. This is the best and fairest resolution". Verse 58 addressed the holders of power. Verse 59 addresses everyone under it, and one reading of its first addressees names the new believers around ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Obey Allah and obey the Messenger"** opens the chain. Command and obedience really belong to Allah alone, though the practical form of obeying His command divides into parts. The direct commands, the rejection of shirk and kufr, the worship of Allah the One, the belief in the Last Day and in Muḥammad al-Muṣṭafā ﷺ as the Last and True Messenger, and the practice of ṣalāh, fasting, ḥajj and zakāh as obligations, come as the Qur'an's own word. The commands needing explanation are obeyed through the Messenger's ﷺ own explanations, which are also a kind of revelation, so that his word and deed become the interpretation of the divine command, and obedience to him is a constant companion of obedience to Allah throughout the Qur'an. Whoever obeys me obeys Allah, said the Prophet ﷺ, and whoever disobeys me disobeys Allah, in the two Ṣaḥīḥs from Abū Hurayrah.

**"and those in authority among you"** — the ulu'l-amr. Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, among the earliest readers of the Book, applied the term to the scholars and jurists, the ʿulamāʾ and fuqahāʾ, the succeeding deputies of the Prophet ﷺ, in whose hands the proper regulation of religion lies. Another group, including Abū Hurairah's report, takes it to the officials and rulers holding the reins of government. At-Tafsīr al-Mazharī, with the leading commentary that transmits it, includes both categories, for the system of command is inevitably connected with these two.

**"Should you disagree on anything, then refer it to Allah and His Messenger"** commands that whatever areas the people dispute about, major or minor, go back to the Book of Allah and the sunnah of His Messenger, as Mujāhid and several others among the Salaf said, for "in whatsoever you differ, the decision thereof is with Allah" (42:10). Whatever the Book and sunnah testify to is the plain truth, and what is beyond truth save falsehood.

**"if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day"** carries the verdict: whoever does not take the Book and sunnah as judge in disputes has no claim on belief in Allah or the Last Day.

**"This is the best and fairest resolution"** was read by as-Suddī and several others as having a better end and destination, and by Mujāhid as carrying a better reward.

### The occasions

Al-Bukhārī recorded, from Ṣadaqah ibn al-Faḍl, that Ibn ʿAbbās said the verse was revealed about ʿAbdullāh ibn Ḥudhāfah ibn Qays ibn ʿAdī, whom the Messenger of Allah ﷺ sent on a military expedition. The Group collected it with the exception of Ibn Mājah, and at-Tirmidhī called it ḥasan gharīb. Another narration through the chain of Ibn Jurayj, in the records that include ibn Zakariyyā's Ḥāfiẓ, tells of Khālid ibn al-Walīd sent against an Arab clan with ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir along. A Muslim of the clan came by night to ʿAmmār asking whether staying would benefit him, was told to stay, and so instructed his family. In the morning Khālid found no one but him, imprisoned him and seized his property, and ʿAmmār said: let him go, for he is a Muslim and I have given him amnesty. Khālid objected that ʿAmmār gave protection while he was the leader, and ʿAmmār's answer stood: protection is given from you to others even while you are the leader.

Imam Aḥmad recorded from ʿAlī the story that makes the verse's limit vivid. A troop sent under a man from the Anṣār provoked their commander, who called for wood, lit a fire and said: I command you to enter it. The people almost did, but a young man among them held them back: they had run away from the Fire to Allah's Messenger, so let them not rush until they returned to him, and if he commanded, then they could enter. When they told the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, he said: "Had you entered it, you would never have departed from it. Obedience is only in righteousness", in the two Ṣaḥīḥs.

### The limits of obedience

The Muslim is required to hear and obey in what he likes and dislikes, unless commanded to sin, and then there is no hearing or obeying, as Abu Dāwūd recorded from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar, a hadith the two Ṣaḥīḥs carry. ʿUbādah ibn aṣ-Ṣāmit told of the pledge given to Allah's Messenger to hear and obey in activity and rest, ease and difficulty, even if deprived of their due shares, and not to dispute the leadership with its rightful people, and the Prophet ﷺ added: "Except when you witness clear kufr about which you have clear proof from Allah." Another hadith from Anas runs: hear and obey, even if an Ethiopian slave with a head like a raisin is set over you as chief, recorded by al-Bukhārī. Umm al-Ḥusayn heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ during the Farewell Ḥajj give the ruling: a slave appointed over you who rules by Allah's Book is to be heard and obeyed, recorded by Muslim, whose other narration adds the mutilated nose. There is no obedience to anyone in disobedience to Allah, and whoever obeys the commander obeys the Messenger, and whoever disobeys the commander disobeys him.

The commands of the third kind, not explicit in Qur'an or hadith, belong to the mujtahid ʿulamāʾ, who delve into the established texts with the precedents and parallels of the problem and arrive at the rule of conduct within the parameters of the sacred imperatives. And where the Qur'an and sunnah leave a matter open, the mubāḥ, the making and enforcing of rules belongs to rulers and their officials, so that systems run: how many post offices a city needs, what transit serves it, how shelter is provided for a growing population. Obedience to jurists in matters of juristic research, and to officials in administration, is in reality the obedience to Allah's commands, which is why it stands third in the verse, with its own distinct identity. The reverting itself takes two forms, as the proof of ijtihād and qiyās shows: back to the code of commands mandated in the Book and the sunnah, or, where no textually-mandated rule exists, to analogical deduction from their precedents, for the word of the command is general and includes both.

### The inner reading

According to the explanation of deeper knowledge, the one who has it, the ʿārif, has authority over the one who is just starting out. The shaykh has authority over the aspirant, the murīd, and the leader of each group over its members. Of the aspirant it is said that his friend and guardian, the walī, serves his interest better than he serves it himself. Obedience to the Messenger ﷺ is set beside obedience to God, honouring his affair and elevating his status. And when the verse says to refer the quarrel to God and the Messenger, the scholarly language reads the Book and the sunna, but the explanation of God's unity, bayān al-tawḥīd, reads it as committing and entrusting knowledge of the matter to God. Two conflicting inclinations may arise in a believer's heart. Where the reasoning of the scholars is at hand, he weighs what he grasps of their understanding. The master of the heart goes another way: the matter is entrusted to the Real outright, and he attends to what is said within his innermost self, to what has been cast into the heart without any intermediary.

### In short

- Obedience runs to Allah, to His Messenger as the interpreter of His command, and to the scholars and rulers in their proper spheres, and all three are really one obedience.
- The occasion was a military command, and the fire-lit commander learned that obedience is only in righteousness.
- No hearing or obeying in sin, no obedience in clear kufr, and yet obedience even to the maimed slave who rules by the Book.
- Disputes go back to the Book and sunnah, or to the analogical deduction of the mujtahids, and the non-referrer's belief is in question.
- Inwardly, the ʿārif governs the beginner, and the master of the heart settles his quarrels by committing the matter to God.
