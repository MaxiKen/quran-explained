<<<VERSE 5:20>>>
Before calling his people to a difficult duty, Musa (Moses) recalls the gifts they have already received: "O my people! Remember Allah’s favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you, made you sovereign". Prophetic guidance, freedom after oppression and exceptional provisions all call for gratitude. The reminder prepares them to trust the One who has repeatedly helped them.

The account gives a concrete example of the covenant discussed earlier. The Children of Israel had promised obedience, yet their response to the coming command would test whether past blessings had produced lasting faithfulness. Remembering favour must therefore affect what they are prepared to do next.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people"** directs attention to Musa, Allah's servant and messenger to whom He spoke. He is named in the account as the son of 'Imran. His address comes as prophetic counsel, preparing his people for the command to enter the Holy Land.

**"O my people! Remember Allah’s favours upon you"** calls for remembrance with the heart and tongue. Musa begins with what Allah has done for them rather than presenting the task without its background of mercy. Their past deliverance gives a reason for present trust.

**"when He raised prophets from among you"** names the spiritual gift first. Prophets repeatedly arose among them to call people toward Allah and warn of punishment. The wording says prophets were raised from among the people, not that every member of the community was a prophet.

**"made you sovereign"** recalls their release from Pharaoh's domination and their control over their own affairs. Muluk, kings, can describe the rule enjoyed by a people through its rulers. It is also explained more broadly as the independence of a person with a home, household and help, rather than only someone possessing a royal throne.

**"and gave you what He had never given anyone in the world"** refers to exceptional favours among the people of their time. These include both spiritual distinction and material gifts: revelation, leadership, the parting of the sea and the provisions given in the wilderness. Manna and quails are named, while another gloss describes quails and honey.

### Why begin the command with a reminder of blessings?

The people had seen Pharaoh and his army drowned and had been delivered from slavery. The historical explanation describes them as becoming masters of Egypt and then being called to recover the ancestral land in Syria. Allah had already demonstrated power over an enemy far stronger than themselves.

Musa's approach therefore brings the coming obligation into continuity with what they know. They are not being asked to trust an unknown power after receiving no help. The Lord who delivered them also commands the next step.

The wider story will show refusal followed by wandering, and the promise will later be fulfilled under new leadership. Those consequences belong to the later verses. Here the emphasis is on the favour that should have made obedience a grateful response rather than a burden considered in isolation.

### What was exceptional about the succession of prophets?

The explanation traces prophetic guidance through the family of Ibrahim (Abraham). Among the Children of Israel, prophets continued to arise, one following another, until 'Isa (Jesus), the last prophet from that community. The blessing was not merely possession of an old memory. It included repeated living calls to guidance.

A report through A'mash, recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim, gives a thousand prophets in the period from Musa to 'Isa. The account describes the Children of Israel as having received an unusually large number. The figure is retained as reported, not independently verified through an additional chronology.

After 'Isa, Allah sent Muhammad ﷺ, the son of 'Abdullah, from the descendants of Isma'il (Ishmael), Ibrahim's son. He is described as the final and most honoured Prophet. The earlier community's distinction therefore does not mean prophethood or divine favour could never reach another community afterward.

### In what sense could an entire people be kings?

A nation cannot consist entirely of monarchs any more than it can consist entirely of prophets. One explanation therefore uses ordinary collective speech. The rule of a dynasty or people is attributed to the whole group, even though one ruler or a small number actually administers it.

The discussion gives the Umayyads and Abbasids as examples, followed by Ghaznavis, Ghauris, Mughals and the British in India. In each case, people commonly name a rule through its associated people or house. The expression need not mean that every person individually occupied a throne.

An additional political observation is drawn from this. The ruler's authority may be understood as that of the community through its chosen representative. The account describes the people as entitled collectively to appoint and remove their leader. That is the argument made from the collective wording, not a separate constitutional code supplied by the verse.

A second explanation gives malik a wider everyday sense. Someone with property, a home and assistance may live with a kind of household independence. After subjection, possessing those things could make each family seem sovereign within its own life.

Abdur-Razzaq reports Ibn 'Abbas explaining the expression through a servant, wife and house. Al-Hakim's Mustadrak records a wording mentioning a wife and servant, and the qualification that the exceptional favour concerned the people of their own time. Al-Hakim calls its chain sahih according to the standards of the Two Sahihs and notes that they did not collect it.

Qatadah says they were the first people to take servants. A further hadith describes someone who begins the day bodily healthy, secure with his family and supplied with that day's provision as having what amounts to the world's riches gathered for him. The point is that security and sufficiency can be a great form of wealth without a royal title.

### Does their distinction exclude the later Muslim community?

The favour is explained relative to contemporary peoples, including Greeks and Copts. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the Children of Israel receiving scripture, judgment, prophethood and good provision, and being preferred above the nations (45:16).

When they later asked for an object of worship like those of another people, Musa rebuked their ignorance and reminded them of Allah's preference toward them (7:138-140). Distinction should have led to fidelity, not imitation of idolatry.

The Muslim ummah, the community of the final Prophet ﷺ, is also described as favoured. The passage cites its appointment as a just community bearing witness over humanity (2:143) and its description as the best community raised for people (3:110). It refers to widely transmitted reports about that honour without providing additional reports to reproduce here.

The discussion also speaks of that community's more complete law, greater extent of rule, resources and lasting public honour. These are the comparisons made in its account of the communities. They do not require reading Musa's words as a claim about every later people in every age. A blessing unmatched in one period can be followed by a greater blessing in another.

### What responsibility accompanies being a bearer of revelation?

Allah may choose a people to carry His message to others, just as He chooses a person to receive revelation. The Children of Israel held that responsibility in an earlier age. After the final Prophet ﷺ, his followers carry it.

Honour is joined to a test. The explanation connects a community's public distinction with showing the truth of the guidance it bears, but does not make that benefit unconditional. The community must prove its faith and willingness to obey when patience is tried.

The coming refusal therefore matters precisely because the people have received so much. The gifts are not a licence to decline every costly duty. They are reasons to respond faithfully when the command arrives.

### In short

- Musa recalls prophetic guidance, freedom and exceptional provision before asking his people to undertake a difficult command.
- Sovereignty includes collective rule and the security of an independent household, not only individual kingship.
- The exceptional favour concerns the people of their time and does not exclude later favour to another community.
- Carrying revelation brings responsibility. Gratitude must be shown through obedience when faith is tested.

<<<VERSE 5:21>>>
Musa (Moses) now states the command for which he has prepared his people: "O my people! Enter the Holy Land which Allah has destined for you ˹to enter˺." The promise of the land is joined to a duty to enter rather than retreat in fear. Turning back would bring loss instead of the promised success.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O my people! Enter the Holy Land"** commands entry into the purified land. The accounts identify Jerusalem and its surroundings, while other explanations extend the description across the region of Syria. The immediate task is to confront the occupying enemy under the command given through Musa.

**"which Allah has destined for you ˹to enter˺."** Allah has assigned or promised it to them. One explanation describes it as the inheritance promised through their father Isra'il to those among them who believe. Another connects the bequest with Ibrahim (Abraham). A further gloss emphasises that Allah has commanded them to enter it.

**"And do not turn back or else you will become losers.”"** forbids retreat from the duty because of fear. The loss concerns the intended success in this life and their standing in the next. A gloss also mentions the loss of the quails and honey among their provisions.

### Which place is meant?

The names supplied include Baytul-Maqdis, Jerusalem, and the associated names Quds and Eliah. Other explanations mention Ariha, situated between the Jordan and Jerusalem. It is described as an ancient city with reports of great size. One such account gives a thousand districts, each with a thousand gardens. Those numbers are part of the reported description, not independent measurements.

Other identifications include Damascus and Palestine or parts of Jordan. Qatadah calls the whole land of Syria holy. The explanations can therefore describe either the wider region or a particular place within it, rather than necessarily contradicting one another.

The land is called holy because of its association with Allah's prophets. Ka'b al-Ahbar says he read in a revealed book, perhaps the Torah, that Syria was a special treasure containing servants especially dear to Allah. Another narration describes Ibrahim standing on a mountain in Lebanon and being shown the land within his sight as holy. These are reported accounts attached to the explanation, not additional geographical claims established independently here.

### How does the command fit their earlier history?

The account describes Ya'qub (Jacob) and his household leaving their earlier home for Egypt during the time of Yusuf (Joseph). Their descendants remained there until Musa led them out. A powerful people had meanwhile taken control of the land to which they were now directed.

Musa promised victory if they obeyed Allah and entered to confront that enemy. The promise did not mean they could refuse the commanded action and still demand the result. The command and assurance belong together in this particular prophetic episode, not as a new instruction concerning contemporary territorial conflict.

### In short

- Allah's promise is joined to the command to enter, not to permission to remain inactive.
- The Holy Land is explained through Jerusalem and the wider Syrian region associated with the prophets.
- Fearful retreat from the command would turn the opportunity into loss.

<<<VERSE 5:22>>>
The people answer the command by making their obedience depend on the disappearance of the difficulty: "There is an enormously powerful people there, so we will never ˹be able to˺ enter it until they leave." Musa's (Moses') assurance of victory does not persuade them. They will enter only after the enemy has already gone, without undertaking the struggle required of them.

### Phrase by phrase

**"They replied, “O Moses!"** introduces the response of the Children of Israel to their own prophet. It is the answer of people who have received the command and understand that it requires action from them.

**"There is an enormously powerful people there"** describes inhabitants of great bodily size, strength and ferocity. The accounts call them Amalkites and associate them with the remaining people of 'Ad. A brief gloss describes them as violent killers.

**"so we will never ˹be able to˺ enter it until they leave."** expresses refusal through a claim of inability. Their attention is fixed on the enemy's visible strength rather than Allah's promise. They will not enter while those people remain.

**"If they do, then we will enter!”"** offers compliance only after the decisive obstacle has been removed for them. It is not acceptance of the command as given. The command required them to advance despite the enemy's presence.

### What did the reconnaissance party report?

Reports associated with Ibn 'Abbas, 'Ikrimah and Ibn Abi Talhah describe Musa advancing with his people toward Syria and Baytul-Maqdis. After crossing the Jordan, they stopped at Ariha. The twelve chiefs were sent ahead to investigate the people, local conditions and the ground on which the confrontation would take place.

Near the city's gate, an Amalkite man seized the twelve and brought them before the king. The court discussed killing them or punishing them in another way. It finally chose to release them, hoping that their experience would spread terror among their people and prevent an attack.

The chiefs returned and reported the enemy's power to Musa. His confidence was not shaken because he had already received Allah's promise of victory. But he recognised that an alarming public account could make the people retreat before the command was attempted.

He therefore told the chiefs not to spread their report among the people. Ten privately told friends, and the news soon became public. Only two, Yusha' ibn Nun and Kalib ibn Yu'qina, kept the instruction and confined their report to Musa. Their faithful response is developed in the next verse.

### What happened when the news spread?

The people became distraught and protested against the journey. They said it would have been better to drown in the Nile with Pharaoh's people than to be delivered there only to die at the hands of these powerful inhabitants. Their language turned an earlier rescue into a grievance against the next obligation.

The refusal in the verse expresses that reaction. They treated the enemy's strength as decisive even though they had recently witnessed deliverance from Egypt. They wanted the benefit of entry without the test through which they had been commanded to obtain it.

The passage's criticism concerns this disobedient response in the narrated event. It does not turn every descendant of the community into a person responsible for those words. Its lesson is the danger of allowing fear to overthrow a commitment already accepted.

### Should the exaggerated giant stories be accepted?

The discussion expressly rejects the extravagant Israelite tales attached to this episode. Some name the man who seized the chiefs as 'Awj ibn 'Unuq and give him fantastic proportions and powers. Those exaggerations are described as unsupported by reason or the revealed law, a collection of fabrications rather than reliable explanation.

The retained account is more limited. It describes the Amalkites as people of unusual size and strength, associated with the people of 'Ad, and reports one man overpowering the scouts. It does not endorse the additional towering measurements and marvels found in the rejected tales.

That distinction matters. Retelling a story associated with scripture does not require accepting every later embellishment. The reservation belongs beside the story so that a reader can distinguish the reported core from the material expressly dismissed.

### What makes the response a breach rather than simple caution?

Reconnaissance itself was not the problem. Musa had sent the chiefs to learn the conditions. The breach came when their fear overruled the command and assurance he had conveyed. Information meant to prepare the people for obedience became an excuse for abandoning it.

The two faithful chiefs would urge a concrete step rather than denial of the danger. Their example shows that the alternative was not pretending no strong enemy existed. It was to place that danger under the authority of Allah's promise.

### In short

- The people refused to enter while the powerful inhabitants remained, making obedience conditional on effortless safety.
- The scouts' report spread fear after most of them disregarded Musa's instruction to keep it private.
- The exaggerated tales about a giant are explicitly rejected, while the limited account of a formidable enemy is retained.
- The failure was not gathering information, but letting fear cancel a command whose promised outcome came from Allah.

<<<VERSE 5:23>>>
Two men answer fear with trust and a practical step: "Surprise them through the gate. If you do, you will certainly prevail." Allah describes them as blessed and God-fearing. Unlike the people refusing Musa's (Moses') command, they urge obedience to the promise he has conveyed and reliance on Allah for its fulfilment.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Two God-fearing men"** is principally explained as two men who feared Allah and the consequence of disobeying Him. They are identified as Yuwsha' son of Nun, Joshua, and Kalib son of Yufna, Caleb. Other forms supplied are Yusha' ibn Nun and Kalib ibn Yu'qina, or Caleb ben Jephunneh.

**"who had been blessed by Allah"** means He had granted them righteousness, faith and the ability to remain obedient. The description makes their firmness a divine favour rather than proof that they possessed independent moral power.

**"said, “Surprise them through the gate."** They call for a decisive advance into the city instead of remaining outside in fear. The gate of Baytul-Maqdis is the immediate objective. Their counsel turns confidence into action.

**"If you do, you will certainly prevail."** They assure the people that the promised victory will follow obedience. One explanation connects their confidence with assessing the inhabitants as physically impressive but weak in courage. Another grounds it in complete trust in Allah's promise received through Musa.

**"Put your trust in Allah if you are ˹truly˺ believers.”"** makes reliance part of faith. Preparation and the commanded advance are to be undertaken, while the outcome is entrusted to Allah. Trust does not mean waiting for the city to empty itself before acting.

### Who were the two men?

Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, 'Atiyyah, As-Suddi and Ar-Rabi' bin Anas are among those identifying them as Joshua and Caleb. In the reconnaissance account, they were the two chiefs who kept Musa's instruction and did not frighten the people with their report. They told Musa what they had seen but did not spread it among everyone else.

There are other explanations of the wording. One reading takes the expression as indicating men who were feared or respected among their people. A gloss describes them as men who themselves feared the violent inhabitants but were granted certainty. Another says they were two men from those inhabitants who feared Musa and received faith in Allah's oneness.

These alternatives should not erase the principal account of the faithful scouts, nor should that account make the others disappear. They reflect different explanations of who feared whom and of the men's position among the people.

### Why mention both fear and blessing?

One explanation notices that the object of fear is not explicitly named in the phrase. The omission points toward the One who ultimately deserves fear: Allah, the absolute Lord. No created being can independently give benefit or bring harm against His will.

The mention of blessing is equally important. All twelve chiefs had outward faculties, understanding and the company of a prophet. Yet only two remained firm in the reported test. Possessing intelligence and opportunity did not make guidance automatic.

The lesson is not that effort is unnecessary. Deed and effort are conditions of seeking the gift. But success in right guidance remains Allah's favour. An intelligent or capable person should therefore ask Him for guidance rather than become proud of personal resources.

### How do courage and reliance work together?

The two do not ask their people to ignore what has been learned. They propose going forward despite it, because the command comes with a promise. Faith, preparation and reliance form one response rather than competing choices.

Their statement about victory may include insight into the enemy's weakness of heart, or rest entirely on confidence in revelation. In both explanations, the practical counsel is the same: begin the commanded action and trust Allah, instead of allowing fear to make obedience impossible in advance.

### In short

- The two blessed men urge a concrete advance under Allah's promise, not passive waiting.
- Their principal identification is Joshua and Caleb, the faithful scouts, with other interpretations also reported.
- Right guidance is Allah's gift, even where people possess similar abilities and opportunities.
- Faith joins preparation and obedience with reliance on Allah for the result.

<<<VERSE 5:24>>>
The people repeat their refusal more harshly: "O Moses! ˹Still˺ we will never enter as long as they remain there." They then tell Musa (Moses) and his Lord to fight while they stay where they are. Advice from the two faithful men has not overcome their resistance. The moment that required commitment instead reveals their determination not to act.

### Phrase by phrase

**"˹Yet˺ they said, “O Moses! ˹Still˺ we will never enter as long as they remain there."** renews the same condition despite the command and the promised victory. The enemy must leave first. The people will not participate in bringing about the outcome they want.

**"So go—both you and your Lord—and fight"** is an offensive refusal to support their prophet. One explanation understands the intended sense as telling Musa to fight with Allah's help while they withhold theirs. Another gloss takes the address to his master as a reference to Harun (Aaron), with Allah helping the two as He had helped against Pharaoh.

**"we are staying right here!”"** leaves no ambiguity about their own choice. They intend to remain away from the fight. The words became proverbial as an example of abandoning a leader at the moment his followers were required to stand with him.

### Is the statement explained as outright disbelief?

The discussion distinguishes insolent refusal from deliberate mockery of Allah. If the words were intended as mockery, it says, they would be open kufr, disbelief. Yet Musa's continuing presence with them and his later prayers for them in the wilderness are taken as reasons not to assume that intention.

On the explanation preferred there, they mean that Musa should act with his Lord's help because they themselves will not help. That remains deeply hurtful and disobedient without requiring the additional conclusion that the sentence was spoken as a theological denial or jest. The distinction preserves the severity of the breach while attending to the subsequent account.

### How did the Companions answer a similar moment at Badr?

The contrast is drawn with the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ consulting his Companions before Badr. They had missed the caravan led by Abu Sufyan, while an armed Quraysh force of roughly nine hundred to a thousand approached. The Muslims faced a serious confrontation rather than the easier outcome they had first expected.

Abu Bakr spoke well, and other emigrants did likewise. The Prophet ﷺ continued asking for advice because he wanted to hear from the Ansar, who formed the majority. Sa'd bin Mu'adh recognised that and pledged that they would follow even if he entered the sea. They would not refuse battle, and he hoped their conduct would give the Prophet ﷺ satisfaction. The Prophet ﷺ was pleased and encouraged to proceed.

Anas relates another version recorded by Abu Bakr bin Marduwyah, and also by Ahmad, An-Nasa'i and Ibn Hibban. After 'Umar spoke, the Prophet ﷺ again sought their view. The Ansar understood that he was addressing them and declared that they would not repeat the refusal made to Musa. They would follow even to Bark Al-Ghimad near Makkah.

### What did Al-Miqdad say?

Al-Bukhari records 'Abdullah bin Mas'ud recalling Al-Miqdad's response at Badr. He told the Prophet ﷺ that they would not tell him to go with his Lord while they stayed behind. Instead, he should proceed and they would be with him.

The fuller account names him Miqdad ibn al-Aswad and describes him promising to defend the Prophet ﷺ from the front and rear and on both sides. The Prophet ﷺ had been praying before Allah as the powerful enemy approached. Miqdad's pledge delighted him and strengthened the Companions' resolve.

Ibn Mas'ud later expressed his wish that he had enjoyed the honour of making that declaration. The contrast makes the duty clear without a new abstract rule: one community refused its prophet at the test, while these Companions pledged their presence and support.

### In short

- The people insist on remaining inactive until the enemy leaves, despite the command and counsel they have received.
- The offensive words are explained as disobedient refusal, while the discussion distinguishes that from an intention of blasphemous mockery.
- The Companions' pledges before Badr provide the opposite example: they would accompany and defend the Prophet ﷺ rather than leave him to act alone.
- A pledge of obedience is tested when support becomes costly, not only when agreement is easy.
