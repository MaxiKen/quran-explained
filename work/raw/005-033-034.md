<<<VERSE 5:33>>>
The protection of life in the preceding verse is followed by penalties for organised violence: "those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger and spread mischief in the land". The passage concerns armed attacks that destroy public safety through killing, plunder and terror. It sets out grave legal consequences, then reminds offenders that earthly disgrace does not make the Hereafter disappear.

These are punishments administered through legal authority, not instructions for private vengeance. Their explanation requires distinctions between different offences, kinds of punishment and rights. The following verse also supplies an important exception for repentance before capture.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Indeed, the penalty for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger"** describes muharabah, the forcible destruction of peace and security. The immediate victims are human beings, but the attackers openly challenge the law of Allah and His Messenger ﷺ through their armed power.

**"and spread mischief in the land"** includes terrorising travellers, killing and seizing property. The legal discussion concerns an armed group or an individual operating through force, not every ordinary theft or act of pickpocketing. The wider expression of corruption also includes attacks on people's honour and safety.

**"is death, crucifixion, cutting off their hands and feet on opposite sides, or exile from the land."** names four penalties. The word aw, or, is understood in two principal ways. One gives the ruler a choice appropriate to the case. The other assigns a particular punishment to each kind of offence. These positions must remain distinct.

**"This ˹penalty˺ is a disgrace for them in this world"** identifies the public humiliation and punishment attached to the crime. Violent power does not become honour merely because it frightens other people.

**"and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter"** adds the warning of the Fire. How this relates to repentance and to punishment as expiation is discussed through different explanations and hadith reports below.

### What does waging war mean here?

Harb is explained through taking or wresting away, and is set against salm, peace and security. Muharabah therefore concerns more than a fight between two individuals. It is the use of force to overturn public safety and impose the attackers' will upon others.

Muqatalah refers to fighting, whether or not it ends in death and whether or not looting accompanies it. Muharabah emphasises armed intimidation and the destruction of peace. The legal examples include highway robbery, violent rebellion and group attacks upon life, property and honour.

The distinction does not mean a single murder or theft is harmless. Such crimes have their own rules. Here the focus is the additional public danger created when offenders rely on armed strength to defy the law and make people unsafe.

The fighting is called war against Allah and His Messenger because an Islamic government is charged with enforcing their law. Force used to break that law openly challenges the authority it represents. The expression does not suggest that human weapons can physically harm Allah.

### Which events are connected with the revelation?

Al-Bukhari and Muslim record Anas bin Malik's account of eight men from 'Ukl who came to the Prophet ﷺ and pledged Islam. Other versions name 'Ukl or 'Uraynah, and another description calls them the 'Arniyyun. They became ill because Al-Madinah's conditions did not suit them.

The Prophet ﷺ sent them with a shepherd and camels, allowing them to drink the animals' milk and urine. The account says that they recovered, then killed the shepherd and drove off the camels. This is a narrated historical treatment, not medical advice to reproduce it.

They were pursued and captured. Muslim's wording describes the cutting of their hands and feet, the branding of their eyes with heated iron and their being left in the sun until they died. Another version places them in the Harrah area and says their request for water was refused.

A further narration from Anas describes them as cattle people rather than farmers. After going out with the herd, they killed the shepherd in al-Harrah and stole the animals. Its wording describes their eyes being gouged and their remaining in that condition until death. Qatadah says he had been told the verse concerned them. Muslim records this narration through Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna and 'Abd al-A'la from Sa'id ibn Abi 'Arubah.

Other accounts connect the ruling with attacks on people travelling to embrace Islam. One names Hilal Ibn 'Uwaymir's people, who killed travellers from Banu Kinanah and took their belongings. Another report from Ibn 'Abbas describes Abu Burdah Aslami breaking a peace agreement with the Prophet ﷺ by robbing people on their way to Madinah. These are differing reported settings, not details to merge into a single event without explanation.

### Is the ruling limited to those particular people?

'Ikrimah and Al-Hasan Al-Basri are reported by Ibn Jarir as connecting these verses with idolaters. They explain that repentance before capture protects those people from the specified penalty, but does not allow a Muslim who commits murder and violent corruption to erase liability simply by joining the unbelievers.

Abu Dawud and An-Nasa'i also record an account from Ibn 'Abbas through 'Ikrimah concerning idolaters. The supplied wording of that report still speaks of liability for committed crimes. It should not be made to say that every private claim disappears through a change of religious affiliation.

The broader legal explanation treats the verse as general, covering whoever commits the described acts rather than only members of one tribe or religious group. The discussion of repentance in the next verse must then be read together with the distinction between public penalties and the rights of victims.

### Why distinguish three kinds of punishment?

In the Shariah, the revealed law, not every penalty belongs to the same category. A crime can wrong another person while also disobeying Allah. Both rights are involved, but the legal treatment depends on which is considered predominant.

Hudud, the plural of hadd, are fixed penalties in which Allah's right is treated as predominant. Qisas means proportionate retaliation, in which the injured person's right is predominant. Ta'zir refers to penalties whose particular form or measure is left to the relevant judicial or governing authority rather than fixed in the same way by revelation.

Confusing these categories leads to mistaken conclusions about pardon, discretion and proof. A power that a judge possesses in one category cannot simply be assumed in another.

For ta'zir, the authority considers the offence and the circumstances of place and time. The penalty can be lighter or heavier, and pardon may be appropriate. A government may also regulate judicial discretion within the bounds of the law rather than leave every decision entirely to an individual judge.

The fixed penalties are different. In the account given here, a ruler cannot arbitrarily increase, reduce or abolish a hadd once its conditions have been met. A change in time or place does not itself authorise rewriting it.

### Which fixed penalties are named?

The legal classification presented here lists five hudud: those concerning robbery, theft, unlawful sexual intercourse, false accusation of such intercourse, and drinking wine. The first four are described as expressly stated in the Qur'an. The fifth is explained through ijma, the consensus of the Companions, concerning wine.

This is the classification used in this legal discussion, not a reason to treat every kind of criminal penalty as one of these five. Qisas and ta'zir remain distinct categories.

Intercession is also treated differently. A relevant recommendation may be heard in a discretionary matter, but intervention to set aside an established hadd is prohibited. The Prophet ﷺ strongly forbade such intercession in Allah's fixed penalties.

Repentance can remove the sin before Allah without automatically cancelling the established worldly penalty. The exception for highway robbery, when repentance occurs before capture, is especially significant because it differs from the general rule stated here.

### How does strict punishment relate to strict proof?

The account joins the severity of hudud to exacting requirements for establishing the crime. If a required condition is missing, or a legally relevant doubt remains in the proof, the hadd is not applied. The principle is that doubt averts fixed penalties.

That does not necessarily end every legal consequence. A case that does not qualify for a hadd may still warrant a discretionary penalty under the authority's assessment. The absence of the fixed penalty and the absence of any proven wrongdoing are not identical propositions.

The discussion illustrates this with three trustworthy witnesses to unlawful intercourse where the required fourth witness is absent. It says the fixed penalty would not be imposed, while describing a discretionary corporal penalty as possible. This is the particular legal account supplied, not a complete treatment of all evidentiary and accusation rules.

It gives theft as another example. A missing condition or doubt can prevent the cutting penalty without necessarily preventing another lawful penalty for an established offence. These distinctions belong to judicial proceedings, not to personal judgment by onlookers.

### How is qisas different?

Qisas concerns equivalent redress for a killing or injury. Because the victim's right is predominant in this category, the entitled person can waive that right. For homicide, the wali, the victim's legal guardian or representative, may seek the prescribed retaliation after legal proof or forgive it.

This differs from the hadd for theft. A property owner's personal forgiveness does not by itself cancel an established fixed penalty in which Allah's right is predominant. The classification matters even though both offences harm other people.

Nor does pardon of qisas necessarily require the authorities to ignore a continuing public danger. The explanation distinguishes the guardian's surrendered private claim from the government's responsibility to protect others. It allows a further appropriate penalty, including imprisonment for life in the example given, where necessary to address that danger.

The same broad distinction applies to wounds. Proportionate redress belongs to the injured party, while public responsibility and any separate lawful penalty remain matters for the authority. None of this makes retaliation a licence to act without legal determination.

### Are the four penalties choices or different assignments?

One position understands aw as offering the ruler a choice. Ibn 'Abbas is reported through 'Ali bin Abi Talhah as giving the Muslim leader that discretion for an armed offender spreading terror on the roads. Ibn Jarir also records related views from Mujahid, 'Ata', Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib, Ad-Dahhak, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i and Al-Hasan Al-Basri.

Malik and Dawud are also named with the choice interpretation. The ruler considers the offenders' power, the fear they create and the seriousness of the acts rather than treating choice as permission for arbitrary cruelty. One formulation allows one or more of the listed penalties as appropriate.

The grammatical argument compares other uses of or in legal alternatives. The hunting penalty offers an animal brought to the Ka'bah, feeding the poor or an equivalent fast (5:95). The discussions of compensation for shaving because of illness and of expiation for an oath also present alternatives. On this reading, the same conjunction here can indicate choice.

The other principal view is held by Abu Hanifah, Al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, together with a group of Companions and their successors. It understands the conjunction as distributing penalties among different crimes. The options are not interchangeable once the offender's actual conduct is established.

### How are offences matched with penalties in the second view?

Where the attackers kill but take no property, the prescribed punishment is death. Where they both kill and seize property, it is crucifixion. Where property is forcibly taken without killing, the penalty is cutting on opposite sides. Where the attackers have only terrorised people and have not yet killed or plundered, the penalty is exile or its legally explained equivalent.

The report concerning Abu Burdah Aslami says Jibra'il brought a command distributing the penalties in this manner after the treaty was broken. It also mentions pardon for those who embrace Islam. The specific rules of repentance and remaining private claims require the next verse's explanation.

The protection in the homicide example includes a Muslim and a non-Muslim citizen within the Islamic territory. The legal account does not make the latter's killing irrelevant to this offence.

The cutting is explained as the right hand and left foot. One brief gloss adds the opposite pair if the offence is repeated. The descriptions are recorded as the historical legal explanations supplied, not instructions for carrying out violence privately.

There is also disagreement over crucifixion's timing. One account describes suspension while alive followed by lethal injury. The stronger of two views attributed to Al-Shafi'i places public suspension for three days after execution, while another allows it shortly before death. These different procedures should not be presented as one unanimously agreed form.

### Why can the whole participating group be liable?

The emphatic verbal forms, described as belonging to bab al-taf'il, and the plural wording are read as indicating group liability. The person who physically performs the killing or seizure acts with the support and protection of the other participants.

On this explanation, liability is not restricted to the one pair of hands that performed the final act. The participating band made the attack possible through cooperation. This concerns accomplices in the armed crime, not unrelated relatives or people who happen to share an identity with an offender.

The same grammatical discussion connects the penalty with a hadd rather than only private qisas. Consequently, the victims' personal pardon does not alone cancel this public fixed penalty. The exception based on repentance before capture is therefore stated separately.

### What can exile mean?

Several interpretations are given. One understands it as pursuit that drives the offenders from their place of activity until they are captured or escape the Islamic territory. Ibn Jarir records this through several authorities, including Ibn 'Abbas, Anas bin Malik, Al-Layth bin Sa'd and Malik bin Anas.

Another means transfer to a different place under Muslim authority. Sa'id bin Jubayr, Abu Ash-Sha'tha', Muqatil bin Hayyan and others are named among those who do not require expulsion beyond Islamic territory. Other views allow removal outside it.

A further interpretation is imprisonment. 'Umar al-Faruq reasoned that merely moving an active criminal to another town could expose new people to the same danger. Confinement prevents free movement and thus fulfils the purpose of removal. Abu Hanifah adopts this view. A brief gloss speaks of confinement until upright conduct and repentance become evident.

### What about kidnapping, sexual attacks and wounds?

The discussion includes armed attacks upon women, kidnapping and sexual violation under the destruction of public safety. It considers the ruler able to select an appropriate penalty from the four in such cases. Where the separate legal proof required for an unlawful-intercourse penalty is established, it also discusses that distinct hadd.

That account should not be confused with saying that every allegation automatically establishes either penalty. The earlier distinctions concerning legal proof and judicial authority still apply.

Where no one was killed and no property taken but victims were wounded, the account applies the rules of qisas to the injuries. The several dimensions of an attack must therefore be identified rather than forced into one undifferentiated description.

### Does worldly punishment necessarily remove the punishment of the Hereafter?

One explanation says the earthly penalties do not themselves ensure forgiveness in the next life without sincere repentance. Ibn Jarir connects the final warning with those who die without repenting. Their present disgrace is followed by great punishment in Jahannam, Hell.

Other reports emphasise punishment as expiation for Muslims. Muslim records 'Ubadah bin As-Samit describing a pledge taken by the Prophet ﷺ: no association of partners with Allah, theft, adultery, killing children or fabricated accusations. Whoever kept it would receive Allah's reward. Whoever offended and was punished would have that punishment as expiation. For an offence Allah concealed, the final decision belonged to Him, whether to punish or pardon.

Ahmad, Ibn Majah and At-Tirmidhi record a report from 'Ali that Allah is too just to combine two punishments for the same servant's punished offence, and too generous to punish what He has already concealed and forgiven. At-Tirmidhi calls it hasan gharib, good and singly transmitted. Ad-Daraqutni notes that versions attribute it either to the Prophet ﷺ or to Companions, and judges the prophetic version sahih, authentic.

The distinction between the warnings concerning idolaters and the expiation reports concerning Muslims is one way the discussion treats these texts. The repentance-based explanation remains another emphasis. Neither the explicit warnings nor the supplied reports of expiation should be silently omitted.

### Why place these laws beside calls to fear Allah?

External penalties alone cannot govern every hidden intention. The Qur'an joins law to awareness of Allah and the Hereafter, and places the call to seek nearness to Him among these legal passages. It addresses both public order and the heart from which conduct grows.

The stated purpose is not simply to multiply punishment. It is to restrain wrongdoing, protect people and make obedience something a person chooses even when no officer is watching. The next verse keeps open a path of repentance before the offender is overpowered.

### In short

- The verse concerns armed corruption of public safety, with penalties applied by lawful authority rather than private vengeance.
- Jurists differ over whether the four penalties are choices or assignments corresponding to different offences.
- Hudud, qisas and discretionary penalties have different rules concerning proof, pardon and authority.
- The explanations preserve differences over exile, crucifixion, group participation and the relation between earthly punishment and final accountability.
- Severity is joined to exacting proof, moral reform and the following verse's opening for repentance.

<<<VERSE 5:34>>>
The severe penalties are followed by an opening for voluntary return: "As for those who repent before you seize them". The timing matters. These offenders abandon their armed wrongdoing while still outside the authorities' control, rather than merely declaring repentance after defeat. Allah's forgiveness and mercy make genuine reform possible.

The exception concerns the specified public penalty. It must also be read alongside the rights of people who were killed, injured or robbed. Repentance is not a device for keeping another person's property or making an unpaid claim disappear.

### Phrase by phrase

**"As for those who repent before you seize them"** excepts offenders who turn back while they still have the power to continue or evade capture. Their surrender of wrongdoing is voluntary. The explanation contrasts this with a claim made after they have been surrounded and overpowered.

**"then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** gives the reason for accepting the return. Allah forgives those who repent, including those who turn from disbelief and idolatry. Mercy is shown by removing the specified penalty under the conditions stated, not by pretending no wrong occurred.

### Why is repentance before capture treated differently?

A powerful band may be difficult to defeat. Leaving a path back encourages its members to abandon violence without further bloodshed. The severe consequence of participating in the band is balanced by an opportunity for reform before the authorities obtain control.

The explanation also observes that taking life is an extreme punishment. Even where a whole participating group faces liability, persuasion toward repentance remains open. The law does not require authorities to reject a genuine voluntary return merely because the preceding crimes were grave.

This is described as an exception to the general rule for established hudud, fixed penalties. In the account given here, repentance does not ordinarily cancel the worldly penalty for theft or unlawful intercourse after its conditions and legal proof are established, though it can remove the sin's consequence in the Hereafter. Highway robbery has this specially stated provision.

### What rights remain after the public penalty is waived?

The position favoured by Abu Hanifah and the majority distinguishes Allah's right in the fixed public penalty from human claims. Property still available must be returned. Financial loss must be compensated or released by the person entitled to it.

For killing or wounding, the rules of qisas, proportionate redress, still apply. The injured person or the killed person's lawful representative may forgive that private right. Without such satisfaction or pardon, genuine repentance has not fully addressed the harm done to Allah's servants.

A broad explanation describes the Companions as waiving the punishments specified for this armed offence when repentance preceded capture. That should be kept alongside, not substituted for, the more detailed distinction about victims' rights.

The stronger of two views attributed to Al-Shafi'i also preserves consequences based on human claims. Its particular formulation says a person who killed and stole could remain liable to death and cutting on opposite sides, but not crucifixion. The same discussion calls the ineffectiveness of repentance after capture the stronger of his two positions. These are supplied legal differences rather than a single unanimous formula.

### What happened in the case of Harithah bin Badr?

Ash-Sha'bi relates that Harithah bin Badr At-Tamimi committed violent corruption around Al-Basrah. He sought help from Quraysh figures, including 'Abdullah bin Ja'far, Ibn 'Abbas and Al-Hasan bin 'Ali, to obtain safety from 'Ali. Their initial intercession did not obtain it.

Harithah then went to Sa'id bin Qays Al-Hamadani, who sheltered him and asked 'Ali about the verse's exception for repentance before capture. 'Ali issued a document of safety, and Sa'id identified Harithah as its intended beneficiary. Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Jarir record the account.

A shorter version describes Harithah leaving his city in rebellion, then returning repentant and not being subjected by 'Ali to the specified penalty. The point is the application of the revealed exception, not the purchase of immunity through influential acquaintances.

### How was a man from Murad treated?

Ibn Jarir records that a man from Murad approached Abu Musa when he governed Al-Kufah under 'Uthman. After the obligatory prayer, he admitted waging war and spreading corruption, and declared that he had repented before being captured.

Abu Musa publicly acknowledged the claim and instructed people to treat him accordingly. If truthful, he had followed the path of truthful return. If lying, his sins would bring their consequence. After a period of inactivity, the man rebelled again and was killed.

The later failure did not make the earlier invitation to repent meaningless. It shows that public acceptance of a claim is not a declaration that the claimant can never again choose wrong. He remained responsible for what he did afterward.

### How did 'Ali Al-Asadi respond to the promise of mercy?

'Ali Al-Asadi had blocked roads, shed blood and plundered property. Authorities and ordinary people had been unable to catch him. Hearing a recitation calling those who had sinned against themselves not to despair of Allah's mercy, he asked for it to be repeated.

He then put away his sword and travelled repentant to Al-Madinah. He arrived at night, washed and attended the dawn prayer in the Prophet's ﷺ mosque. Sitting near Abu Hurayrah, he was recognised when morning came, and people moved against him.

He declared that he had returned before they had power over him. Abu Hurayrah affirmed his claim, took him by the hand and brought him to Marwan bin Al-Hakam, the governor under Mu'awiyah. He insisted that the exception applied and that the specified punishment should not be imposed.

Ibn Jarir records the account through Musa bin Ishaq Al-Madani. It continues that 'Ali remained faithful to his repentance and later joined a naval expedition against the Romans. When the ships met, he crossed to an opposing vessel. Its occupants fled to the other side, the vessel capsized and all aboard drowned. This is the reported conclusion to his changed life, not a new ruling about combat.

### What does forgiveness require of the offender?

The promise of mercy gives hope strong enough to interrupt an entrenched life of violence. Yet abandoning the practice is only part of the return. Rights taken from other people must be restored or forgiven by their holders.

That is why repentance cannot be reduced to a useful phrase uttered to escape consequences. Its sincerity must appear in changed conduct and in making right what can be made right. Allah's mercy invites the offender to become different, not to continue the same injustice under a new claim.

### In short

- Repentance before capture removes the specified public penalty under the verse's exception.
- The opportunity encourages armed offenders to abandon violence voluntarily and limits further bloodshed.
- Private claims for life, injury and property remain subject to their own rules and the rights of their holders.
- Reports about Harithah, the man from Murad and 'Ali Al-Asadi show different outcomes after the exception was invoked.
- Genuine repentance combines hope in Allah's mercy with changed conduct and responsibility toward those harmed.
