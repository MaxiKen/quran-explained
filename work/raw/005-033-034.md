<<<VERSE 5:33>>>
"Indeed, the penalty for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger and spread mischief in the land is death, crucifixion, cutting off their hands and feet on opposite sides, or exile from the land. This ˹penalty˺ is a disgrace for them in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter"

This verse sets out the punishment for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger and spread mischief in the land. The material explains that the verse describes armed robbery and banditry, where a group uses force to break the law of the land and to make people afraid. The punishment is death, crucifixion, cutting of the hands and feet on opposite sides, or exile. The verse says the penalty is a disgrace in this world and a great punishment in the Hereafter. The verse follows the story of the two sons of Adam and the rule against unjust killing in 5:32, and it moves from the principle to its enforcement.

The material treats this verse as the heart of the Islamic law of crimes against public safety. It explains the three kinds of punishment in the law, the difference between the rights of Allah and the rights of people, and why the punishment for this crime is fixed.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Indeed, the penalty for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger and spread mischief in the land."** The material explains that the word for waging war comes from a root meaning to seize or snatch, and it is the opposite of peace and security. The war in view is the spreading of disorder. Isolated theft or a single killing does not disturb public peace. The verse concerns a powerful and organised group that robs and kills by force and breaks the law of the land. This is the reading of the Muslim jurists. The material says that the phrase "against Allah and His Messenger" is used because a group that breaks the law of Allah with force is at war with the government, and where the government is Islamic, that war is against Allah and His Messenger, even though the victims are ordinary people.

The material explains the difference between two words. Muqatalah is a bloody fight, and Muharabah is group-led intrusion into life, property and honour by force. The verse uses the second word. Muharabah includes highway robbery, rebellion, and attacks on property, honour and life.

**"Is death, crucifixion, cutting off their hands and feet on opposite sides, or exile from the land."** The material explains that these are four punishments, linked by the word "or". The word is read in two ways. Some early authorities and Imam Malik held that the ruler has the choice among the four, depending on the power of the robbers and the gravity of the crime. Abu Hanifah, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal read the word as dividing the punishments among different kinds of crime. They supported this with a report from Ibn 'Abbas, which tells of a peace treaty with Abu Burdah al-Aslami, who broke it by robbing people on their way to Madinah to embrace Islam. The angel Jibril brought the rule that whoever killed and robbed should be crucified, whoever killed but did not rob should be killed, whoever robbed but did not kill should have his hand and foot cut off, and whoever only spread fear should be exiled. Anyone who repents before being taken is excused.

The material also notes that the three punishments of killing, crucifixion and cutting are written in a grammatical form that expresses repetition and intensity. It also notes that the plural form applies to the whole group, even when only one of them committed the crime, because they all shared the plan. The punishment is therefore a Hadd, the right of Allah, not a Qisas that the victim could forgive.

The material also reports the view of 'Umar al-Faruq about exile: a man who is exiled but left free to roam will harass the people of other towns, so it is better to imprison him. Abu Hanifah took this view.

**"This penalty is a disgrace for them in this world."** The material says that the punishment brings humiliation in this life, and that the Hereafter punishment is harsher and more lasting. It also says that the worldly punishment does not remove the Hereafter punishment unless the person sincerely repents.

### The three kinds of punishment in the law

The material explains the law's three kinds of punishment. The Hudud are fixed punishments for crimes that are a right of Allah. There are five: robbery, theft, adultery, false accusation of adultery, and the drinking of wine. The Qisas is retaliation for killing or wounding, which is a right of the person harmed, and so the victim or his guardian can forgive it. The Ta'zir are penalties left to the judge, and they can range from light to severe or be pardoned. A Hadd cannot be waived by a ruler, and it cannot be reduced by repentance once the crime is proved in court, although the Hereafter punishment can be forgiven by sincere repentance. A Hadd is dropped in case of doubt, but the offender is not left unpunished, and the judge may give a lesser penalty.

### Robbery and the rights of people

The material says that a repentance does not remove the rights of the servants of Allah. Anything taken must be returned, and where someone was killed or wounded, the rule of Qisas applies, which the heirs may forgive. Abu Hanifah and most jurists hold that the financial liability must be discharged or forgiven. A robber is considered truly repentant only when he pays for the rights he violated, or they are forgiven.

### Who is included, and the cases the reports give

The material says that the verse applies to a group or individual who robs people and breaks the law by force of arms. It does not include common individual thieves or pickpockets, who fall under the rules for theft. Some reports tie the verse to a particular occasion. One account, from Anas and recorded by Muslim, speaks of a group from the tribes of Ukl and Uraynah who came to Madinah ill, and were told to go out with the Prophet's herd and drink its milk and urine. When they recovered, they killed the shepherd, drove off the camels, and were brought back and punished severely. Another reading names the Arniyyun, who came to Madinah ill, were treated in the same way, killed the shepherd and stole the herd. Qatadah is reported to have said that the verse was revealed about them. The material does not settle the detail, but it treats the case as the setting of the rule.

A further account concerns the people of Hilal bin 'Uwaymir, who killed a group of travellers from the Banu Kinanah who had been on their way to Madinah to declare their Islam. The material says the verse was read by some as a judgement on idolators who killed and looted, and it sets out the same four punishments for each kind of crime.

The material also reports the view of Sa'id bin al-Musayyib, 'Ata', Dawud, al-Hasan al-Basri, ad-Dahhak, an-Nakha'i and Mujahid, who took the choice to be the ruler's, and it notes that the Imam Malik held the same view. Abu Hanifah, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, with a group of Companions and Successors, read the word as dividing the punishments among the crimes.

### The grammar and the group

The material says that the first three punishments in the verse use a verbal form that expresses repetition and intensity, and that the plural applies to the whole group, so that the punishment falls on all, although one member did the act. The material says this shows that the punishment is a Hadd, a right of Allah, and not a retaliation that the victim may forgive. The form also shows that the crime is the group's, since they all acted in the cooperation of their companions.

The material explains the sunnah of the Prophet ﷺ as the basis for these rules, and the consensus of the Companions on the fifth Hadd, the punishment for wine. It says that this consensus is an authority alongside the text.

### The place of exile

The material says that exile can be read as expulsion from the land of Islam, or as expulsion from the place where the crime was committed. It also reports the view of 'Umar al-Faruq that a robber exiled to another town would only harass its people, so it is better to imprison him. The material notes that the Harrah, a place near Madinah, is where the Ukl group were left to die, and it treats their case as the example of how severe the punishment for killing a shepherd and driving off the herd could be.

### In short

- The verse sets out the punishment for armed robbery and rebellion that breaks public peace.
- The four punishments are for the ruler to apply in the way the crime requires, and the material gives the reading of the jurists on that choice.
- The punishment is a disgrace in this world and a greater punishment in the Hereafter, and repentance must include restoring the rights of people.

<<<VERSE 5:34>>>
"As for those who repent before you seize them, then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"

This verse makes an exception to the punishment in 5:33. A robber or rebel who repents before he is taken, while he still has strength and before the authorities have seized him, may be forgiven and the penalty dropped. The verse ends by saying that Allah is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful.

### Phrase by phrase

**"As for those who repent before you seize them."** The material explains that the exception applies only before capture. The robber must abandon the practice while his position of strength still stands, and of his own choice. The material contrasts this with theft and adultery, where a repentant offender who has been proved guilty is not spared the worldly penalty, though his Hereafter punishment may be forgiven.

**"Then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."** The material says that the verse's mercy is the reason for the exception. The severity of the punishment for robbery is matched by a door left open for repentance.

### Why the exception exists

The material gives three reasons. First, the robbery punishment falls on the whole group even when one member committed the crime, so the exception softens it. Second, it is not always easy to overcome a powerful group, so the door of persuasion is left open to induce them to repent. Third, killing is an extreme measure, and the law wants it to happen as rarely as possible, so the effort to reform the robbers continues.

The material reports the account of Ali al-Asadi, who waged war and robbed travellers near Madinah, and who repented after a reciter in a caravan recited a verse that tells the servants of Allah not to despair of His mercy. He put down his sword and went to Madinah. Abu Hurayrah took him to Marwan, the governor, and said he could not be punished. Harithah bin Badr also rebelled and then repented, and 'Ali did not impose the legal punishment on him.

### The limit of the exception

The material notes that the exception covers only the worldly punishment for the robbery. It does not return what was taken. Any valuable thing that was taken and still exists must be returned, and where a person was killed or hurt, the family may forgive, or the offender must submit to retaliation. The material says that repentance without restoring the rights of people is incomplete.

### The views of the jurists on the exception

The material gives the views of the jurists on the extent of the exception. Al-Shafi'i held two opinions on the punishment for a robber who has killed and taken property. The sounder of his views is that the robber is killed and his limbs are cut from opposite sides, and he is not crucified. Another opinion is that the crucifixion should be for three days after death, or before the killing. Al-Shafi'i held that repentance does not help a robber once the authorities have seized him. The material records that Ibn 'Abbas and al-Shafi'i took the view that the four punishments in 5:33 divide the crimes, so that only the killer is killed, the killer and thief is crucified, the thief alone has his limbs cut off, and the one who only causes fear is banished. Banishment is understood by some to include imprisonment.

Abu Hanifah held that where a robber has been caught before he repents, the repentance excuses him from the worldly punishment, but any money he took must be returned, and the financial liability must be discharged or forgiven.

### The exception and the hudud

The material says that the exception in this verse differs from the general law of the hudud. The phrase "for those who repent" is stated without a further clause to say that the penalty is set aside. Some scholars read this as showing that only the worldly punishment fixed by Allah is waived for the repentant robber, and not the rights of people. The material says this is one reading, and that the scholars did not treat the matter at length before it.

### The case of Marwan and Abu Hurairah

The material gives the account in full. Ali al-Asadi was a robber near Madinah. He heard the verse of not despairing of Allah's mercy from a reciter in a caravan he was attacking, asked him to recite it again, and repented. He went to Madinah, sat with Abu Hurairah in the mosque, and when the people came for him, he said he had come in repentance before they had authority over him. Abu Hurairah took his hand to Marwan bin al-Hakam, the governor of Madinah in the time of Mu'awiyah, and said that he could not be punished. Marwan did not punish him. The material notes that the government was already helpless against this robbery, and that everyone was pleased with the outcome.

The material says that a similar case happened with Harithah bin Badr, who rebelled and then repented and came back to Madinah. 'Ali did not impose the legal punishment on him.

### In short

- A robber who repents before capture, while he still has the upper hand, is excused the worldly punishment.
- The exception softens a severe rule, opens the door to repentance, and keeps killing as rare as possible.
- The exception does not remove the duty to return what was taken or to make amends to those who were harmed.
