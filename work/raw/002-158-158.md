The subject of the Kaʿbah began back in verse 124, "And when his Lord put Ibrāhīm to a test", continued with the House being made a place of return, a sanctuary and a centre of worship (verse 125), and then with the prayer of Ibrāhīm (Abraham) asking to be taught the rites of worship (verses 126 to 129), which include the Hajj and the ʿUmrah. The centrality of the House was then expressed by making it the qiblah of every ṣalāh wherever it is performed, and its importance was established by making it the goal of the Hajj and ʿUmrah. Verse 158 now turns to a rite performed beside it. "Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺." The verse concludes: "And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing". Earlier the objection of the disbelievers to the Kaʿbah as the qiblah was removed. Here a doubt of the Muslims themselves is removed. It concerned the propriety of the saʿy, the brisk walk between the two hills, in the Hajj and ʿUmrah of which the Kaʿbah is the hub. The passage is thus closely bound together.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah."**. Ṣafā and Marwah are two hills adjoining the Kaʿbah in Makkah. Shaʿāʾir is the plural of shaʿīrah, a sign, mark or token, so the symbols of Allah are what He has determined to be the marks of His religion, the rites and ceremonies of the Hajj that He has commanded and the landmarks of His clear sacred law. Going between Ṣafā and Marwah is among the acts Allah legislated for the Hajj in the time of Prophet Ibrāhīm. Pilgrims walk briskly between the two hills after the ṭawāf of the Kaʿbah, and the Qur'an elects to call this "going round" them.

**"So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺."**. Literally the Arabic says that there is no sin on whoever makes the Hajj to the House, or the ʿUmrah, in going round them. Hajj means in origin to aim or intend, and in the usage of the Qur'an and hadith it is the act of setting out solely to visit the House of Allah and performing the required rites there. ʿUmrah means in origin a visit, and in the Sharīʿah it is the visit to al-Masjid al-Ḥarām with ṭawāf and saʿy. The verb for going round them has an assimilated letter, and the act is the pacing (saʿy) between the two hills seven times. The verse was revealed when the Muslims were averse to it because the pagan Arabs used to go between the hills, each of which had an idol on top that they used to stroke. Allah's declaring that there is no fault in it comforted those Muslims who had been avoiding the walk for fear that it was an unacceptable custom of the time of ignorance, and made clear that it is an accepted act of the Hajj.

**"And whoever does good willingly"**. Whoever volunteers any good deed not obligatory on him, whether ṭawāf or something else. One view is that this refers to performing ṭawāf more than seven times, another that it means a voluntary ʿUmrah or Hajj, and a third, which al-Rāzī states and which is attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, that it means volunteering good works in general. Allah knows best. A variant reading of the verb has its letters assimilated.

**"Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing"**. He accepts such works and knows your intentions. His reward for a small deed is immense, and He knows exactly what reward is sufficient, so He never gives anyone less than their due: "Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom's weight. And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace" (4:40). He accepts the smallest of your works, and His reward is abundant.

### Why the verse says "no sin"

ʿUrwah asked ʿĀʾishah about this verse and suggested that, by Allah, there would be no sin on a person who did not go between the two hills. She replied that what he had said was the worst of readings, O my nephew. Had that been the meaning, the verse would have read "there is no sin on one who does not go round them". Rather, she said, the verse was revealed about the Anṣār. Before Islam they used to enter iḥrām at Mushallal for the idol Manāt that they worshipped, and those who did so used to hesitate to go between Ṣafā and Marwah. After Islam they asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about this hesitation of theirs from the days of ignorance, and Allah revealed the verse. ʿĀʾishah added that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ made going between the hills part of the Sunnah, and no one should abandon it. The hadith is in the Ṣaḥīḥayn. In another version ʿUrwah later mentioned ʿĀʾishah's statement to Abū Bakr ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Ḥārith ibn Hishām. He said he had not heard this. He had, however, heard learned men say that everyone except those ʿĀʾishah mentioned used to call going between the two hills a practice of the Jāhiliyyah, while others among the Anṣār said they had been commanded to make ṭawāf of the Kaʿbah but not between Ṣafā and Marwah. Abū Bakr ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān concluded that the verse seems to have been revealed about both groups. Al-Bukhārī has a similar narration from Anas, who said: we disliked going between Ṣafā and Marwah because they were shrines of Quraysh in the pre-Islamic period, so we abandoned them in Islam, and then Allah revealed this verse.

The reports on the occasion of revelation come through several chains. One runs through Muṣʿab ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Zubayrī from Mālik from Hishām from his father ʿUrwah from ʿĀʾishah, who said the verse was revealed about the Helpers, who used to make pilgrimage to Manāt near Qudayd and avoided going between Ṣafā and Marwah until they asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about it when Islam came. Al-Bukhārī narrates this from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Yūsuf from Mālik. Another chain, through Yaḥyā and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān from Hishām from his father, has ʿĀʾishah say that it concerned a group of the Helpers who before Islam made pilgrimage to Manāt and were forbidden to go between the hills, and who raised the matter when they went on pilgrimage with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. Muslim narrates it from Abū Bakr ibn Abī Shaybah from Abū Usāmah from Hishām from his father from ʿĀʾishah.

ʿUmar ibn Ḥubshī asked Ibn ʿUmar about the verse and was sent to Ibn ʿAbbās, who explained that each hill had carried an idol, a male figure called Isāf on Ṣafā and a female figure called Nāʾilah on Marwah. According to a claim of the People of the Book, the two had committed adultery inside the Kaʿbah, were turned to stone by Allah, and were set on the two hills so that people might take heed. With the passage of time they came to be worshipped instead of Allah, and the people of the Jāhiliyyah stroked them whenever they passed between the hills. When Islam came and the idols were destroyed, the Muslims disliked going between Ṣafā and Marwah because of them. Al-Shaʿbī reported the same, that Isāf was on Ṣafā and Nāʾilah on Marwah and that people used to touch them, so that after Islam they were hesitant about the walk until this verse came down.

The wording "no sin", then, must be read in the light of the question it answers. One might think the Qur'an merely says that the saʿy is "no sin", which at most would make it one of many permitted acts. But the question was about the idols on the two hills and the fact that the people of the Jāhiliyyah did the saʿy to show devotion to them, which seemed to make the practice forbidden (ḥarām). It is in answer to that doubt that Allah declared it free of sin. Since it is the real and authentic tradition of Ibrāhīm, there is no reason to think it tainted, and the malpractice of a pagan band in the intervening period does not turn what is good in the sight of Allah into a sin. The expression "no sin" therefore does not go against the saʿy being obligatory. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have held that the walk is not obligatory, on the ground that where no sin is incurred the context implies free choice. Al-Shāfiʿī and others, however, considered it a pillar of the Hajj rites, and the Prophet ﷺ made its obligatory character clear.

### The ruling and the Prophet's ﷺ practice

Muslim records a long hadith from Jābir in which the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, having finished the ṭawāf of the House, returned to the corner and kissed the Black Stone, then went out by the door near Ṣafā reciting "Indeed, Ṣafā and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah" and saying that he would begin with what Allah had commanded him to begin with. In al-Nasāʾī's narration he said: begin with what Allah began with, meaning Ṣafā. Imām Aḥmad reports from Ḥabībah bint Abī Tajrah that she saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ going between Ṣafā and Marwah with the people in front of him and he behind them, walking fast, his garment twisted round his knees from the speed, and reciting: perform the saʿy, for Allah has prescribed the saʿy for you. Al-Bayhaqī and others also report this saying. The hadith is used as proof that the saʿy is a pillar (rukn) of the Hajj. It has also been said that it is obligatory (wājib) rather than a pillar, so that whoever omits it, by mistake or deliberately, can make up the shortcoming with a sacrifice (dam). The positions of the schools are that the saʿy is a commendable practice of the Prophet ﷺ according to Imām Aḥmad, an absolute obligation (farḍ) according to Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī, and a necessity (wājib) according to Abū Ḥanīfah, which means that one who abandons it must slaughter a goat in compensation. The details of how the Hajj, the ʿUmrah and the saʿy are performed are readily available in the books of fiqh.

### Hājar's search and the pilgrim's state of heart

Ibn ʿAbbās related, in a hadith mentioned earlier, that the origin of this rite is the going back and forth of Hājar, the wife of Ibrāhīm, between Ṣafā and Marwah in search of water for her son Ismāʿīl (Ishmael). Ibrāhīm, a native of Iraq, had left them at Allah's command in the place now known as Makkah, where there was neither habitation nor water. When Hājar feared her son would die she stood up, begged Allah for help and kept going between the two hills, humble, fearful and meek before Him. After seven unsuccessful attempts she returned to find the spring of Zamzam flowing from the ground. Allah had answered her prayer, relieved her loneliness, ended her plight and given her water that is nourishing food and a cure for sickness. The event is a sign of how Allah looks after His servants. Whoever persists in His service until there is nothing but burning sand beneath his feet will find Allah in His mercy bringing a refreshing spring out of the dry desert. When Muslims make the pilgrimage they repeat Hājar's historic quest between the hills and so commemorate what it means for everyone who struggles in Allah's path.

Whoever performs the saʿy, therefore, should remember his own meekness and humility and his need of Allah to guide his heart, to bring his affairs to success and to forgive his sins. He should want Allah to remove his shortcomings and errors, to guide him to the straight path, to keep him firm on it until he dies, and to change his condition from one of sin to one of perfection and forgiveness, the very providence that was granted to Hājar.

### Honoured for the neighbour's sake

In the devotional reading, these sites and relics, these ruins and remains, are extolled and visited because they are the ruins of the lovers, where the traces still gleam. The passions of houses belong to those who lived in them, and there is no concern or joy in the house itself. Even the dust of the lovers' footprints has tremendous value for those who love them, and the smallest grain of dust on the edge of their path is more precious than the most fragrant musk, simply because the beloved once walked beside it. Ṣafā and Marwah were favoured by being close to the House. The running between them became part of the law just as the circling of the House did, and just as the ṭawāf is one of the main rites of the pilgrimage, so is the running. The neighbour is honoured for the sake of the neighbour.

### In short

- Ṣafā and Marwah are among the marks of Allah's religion, and the saʿy between them belongs to the rites Allah gave Ibrāhīm, continuing the Kaʿbah theme that began at verse 124.
- "No sin" answers the Muslims' fear that the walk was a pagan practice because of the idols Isāf and Nāʾilah. ʿĀʾishah insisted it does not mean the walk is optional, and the Prophet ﷺ made it part of the Sunnah (Ṣaḥīḥayn, al-Bukhārī from Anas).
- The Prophet ﷺ began at Ṣafā "with what Allah began with" (Muslim, al-Nasāʾī) and said Allah had prescribed the saʿy (Aḥmad, al-Bayhaqī). It is a pillar or farḍ for Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī, wājib for Abū Ḥanīfah, and commendable for Aḥmad.
- The rite re-enacts Hājar's search for water, and the pilgrim should perform it with her humility and need, asking Allah for guidance, steadfastness and forgiveness.
- Voluntary good of every kind is accepted and rewarded in full by the Appreciative, All-Knowing (4:40).
