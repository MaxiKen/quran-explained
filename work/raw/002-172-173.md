<<<VERSE 2:172>>>
The previous verses corrected the error of the idolaters who made unlawful what Allah had made good and permissible. Verse 172 now warns the believers against falling into the same error, reminds them of Allah's blessings and teaches them gratitude. "O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺". Allah commands His believing servants to eat from the pure things He has created for them and to thank Him for them, if they are truly His servants.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you."**. You who have faith in Allah and follow His Messenger ﷺ, eat of the lawful things He has given you, what is cultivated on the land and the meat of cattle. The ḥalāl is that which carries no bad consequence, and the ṭayyib, the good thing, is that whose blessing is not attributed to any created thing. When a servant finds food that combines both qualities, it is permitted and good.

**"And give thanks to Allah"**. Thank Him outwardly and inwardly for what He has made lawful and for the overwhelming blessings He has given you. Gratitude includes undivided devotion to Allah and restraint from disobedience. The true meaning of thankfulness is that you do not draw a breath in anything but the pleasure of the Real for as long as the energy from that permitted and good food remains in you.

**"if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺"**. If it is really He whom you worship, and nothing besides Him. One reading takes it as a rebuke to those who thought they were serving Allah by declaring good things unlawful: if you truly intend to worship Allah, do not declare them unlawful, for worshipping Him entails treating them as lawful.

### Lawful food and answered prayer

Pure nourishment helps bring acceptance of prayer and worship. Impure nourishment obstructs that acceptance. Imām Aḥmad records, and Muslim and al-Tirmidhī also, that Abū Hurayrah related the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying: O people, Allah is Ṭayyib, pure and good, and accepts only what is ṭayyib. He has commanded the believers with what He commanded the messengers, saying "O messengers! Eat from what is good and lawful, and act righteously. Indeed, I fully know what you do" (23:51), and "O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you". The Prophet ﷺ then described a man on a long journey, dishevelled and dust-covered, who stretches his hands to the sky crying "O Lord, O Lord", Yet unlawful provision supplies his food, drink and clothes. What has nourished him is unlawful too. How, he asked, could such a man's supplication be answered?

Eating ḥarām promotes evil instincts, kills the taste for worship and makes prayers ineffective. Eating ḥalāl generates inner light, creates a distaste for evil, leads towards high morals and produces a state in which the heart welcomes worship, finds the very thought of sin sickening, and has its prayers answered. That is why Allah told all His prophets to eat from what is good and to do what is righteous (23:51). Living by the ḥalāl helps a prayer to be answered, and living by the ḥarām destroys that chance.

### Gratitude rather than superstition

Human beings should feel thankful and obedient to Allah while eating and drinking, saying in effect that in accordance with His command they eat and drink what He has provided. Such feelings evoke true devotion to the Maker. Under self-made beliefs the psychology changes: attention shifts to the supposed properties of the things consumed, and the very blessing meant to produce thanksgiving produces veneration for material objects instead, until the created is given the position of the Creator. When Allah makes something unlawful, it is not because the thing is sacred but because it is impure or the divine law has testified to its uncleanness, as the next verse shows with carrion, blood, swine and what is sacrificed to idols.

### In short

- Having censured the idolaters for forbidding good things, Allah commands the believers to enjoy the lawful and good things He provides and to thank Him, for that is what worshipping Him alone entails.
- Lawful food is a condition of answered prayer: the Prophet's ﷺ hadith of the dusty traveller shows that one nourished on ḥarām cannot expect his supplication to be heard (Abū Hurayrah, in Aḥmad, Muslim and al-Tirmidhī, cf. 23:51).
- Ḥalāl food brings inner light and love of worship, while ḥarām deadens the heart.
- Gratitude keeps food a blessing from Allah, whereas superstition turns it into an object of veneration.

<<<VERSE 2:173>>>
Verse 173 specifies what Allah has in fact forbidden, so that the prohibited may remain prohibited and nothing else be added to it. "He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah." The verse continues: "But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful". The idolaters used to eat carrion and animals slaughtered in a name other than Allah's while treating other animals as forbidden. The verse corrects both errors at once: the prohibited must never be treated as lawful, and it is an error to declare any animal forbidden beyond those specified.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺"**. "Only" (innamā) is a restrictive particle, so the sense is that Allah has forbidden only the things named and nothing else. Sūrat al-Anʿām states the same thing more plainly: "Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'I do not find in what has been revealed to me anything forbidden to eat except carrion, running blood, swine—which is impure—or a sinful offering in the name of any other than Allah'" (6:145). This raises a problem, since many other things are proved unlawful by other verses and by hadith. The answer is that ḥalāl and ḥarām are not being discussed here in the absolute sense but with reference to the animals that the idolaters of Makkah treated as lawful or unlawful on the basis of their pagan beliefs. The previous verse censured their forbidding of lawful animals, and in contrast this verse tells them that they fail to keep away from the very animals Allah has forbidden while shunning those He has allowed. The restriction is therefore relative, directed against polytheistic beliefs, not absolute. The prohibition concerns consumption, which is the subject of the passage, and the Sunnah adds that whatever is cut from a living animal counts as carrion too.

**"carrion"**. Maytah, the dead animal, is in the terminology of the Sharīʿah any animal not slaughtered according to its requirements, whether it dies of itself or is killed by strangling, a violent blow, a fall, goring, or being partly eaten by a wild beast. Dead creatures of the sea are excluded, as Allah says: "It is lawful for you to hunt and eat seafood, as a provision for you and for travellers" (5:96), and as the hadith of the whale in the Ṣaḥīḥ shows. The Musnad, al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and the Sunan record the Prophet's ﷺ saying about the sea that its water is pure and its dead are permissible. Al-Shāfiʿī, Aḥmad, Ibn Mājah and al-Dāraquṭnī report from Ibn ʿUmar that the Prophet ﷺ said two dead things and two kinds of blood have been made lawful for us: fish and locusts, and liver and spleen. Fish and locusts are therefore ḥalāl without slaughter even if they die on their own or are killed, though a fish that has decomposed and floats on the surface is ḥarām. The subject returns in Sūrat al-Māʾidah.

**"blood"**. Blood is forbidden here absolutely, but 6:145 qualifies it as blood that flows, so the jurists agree that congealed blood such as the liver and the spleen is clean and permissible.

**"swine"**. The flesh of the pig is forbidden whether the animal is slaughtered or not, and this includes its fat, either by implication, because the term laḥm (flesh) covers it, or by analogy. The meat is singled out because it is the part people mostly seek, and every other part is implied.

**"and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah."**. This is whatever is slaughtered in a name other than His, for monuments, idols, divination arrows or the other practices of the Jāhiliyyah. Ihlāl is the raising of the voice, which the Arabs used to do when sacrificing to their gods. Al-Qurṭubī mentions that ʿĀʾishah was asked about what non-Muslims slaughter for their festivals and then send to Muslims as gifts, and she said: do not eat what has been slaughtered for that day, but eat from their vegetables.

**"But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—they will not be sinful."**. Allah permits these things when they are needed for survival and no permissible food is available. The muḍṭarr, the one compelled, is in the terminology of the Sharīʿah a person whose life is in danger. Ordinary pain or need does not qualify. Such a person may eat on two conditions: his aim must be to save his life and not to enjoy the food, and he must eat only as much as will keep him alive, for filling the stomach remains prohibited even then. Mujāhid explained that the permission applies to one who, if he did not eat, would be driven to highway robbery, rebellion against the rulers or some other disobedience, but not to one who eats transgressing the limits, or continually, or in a state of disobedience to Allah, even if he is in dire need. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said the same, and he and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān also explained "not driven by desire" as not believing it to be permissible. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that it means eating the dead animal without continuing to do so, and Qatādah that it means not eating carrion when the lawful is available. The person compelled must eat without taking his fill and without carrying anything away. Al-Shāfiʿī held that the rebel and the transgressor are excluded from the dispensation, and that whoever sets out on a journey in disobedience, such as a fugitive or an unjust tax collector, may not eat any of these things unless he repents.

**"Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"**. Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said this refers to what is eaten out of necessity. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said that Allah is forgiving towards what has been eaten of the unlawful and merciful in allowing the prohibited in times of necessity. He forgives even one who ate more than he strictly needed, and it is from His mercy that He granted this dispensation. Masrūq said that whoever is in dire need but refuses to eat or drink until he dies will enter the Fire, which shows that eating carrion to survive is not merely permitted but required.

### Rulings on carrion

Hunting is governed by the same principle. An animal out of reach of the hunter's knife becomes lawful without slaughter if the hunter, after saying bismillāh, wounds it with a sharp-edged weapon such as an arrow. Mere wounding is not enough. The wound must be inflicted by a sharp edge. An animal shot and killed by a gun before it can be slaughtered is therefore treated like one killed by a blow from a stick or stone, which 5:3 calls mawqūdhah and classes as ḥarām, though if it is slaughtered before it dies it becomes lawful. Some scholars regard the conical bullet as a kind of arrow, but the majority hold that a bullet bores and tears the flesh by explosive force rather than cutting it, so game killed by it is not permissible without slaughter.

Carrion is forbidden absolutely, so everything about it is forbidden: eating it, buying it and selling it. The same applies to all impurities, whose use, sale and benefit are all unlawful, so much so that one may not deliberately feed carrion or anything impure even to an animal, though if it is left somewhere and a dog or cat eats it of its own accord there is no harm. The prohibition appears general here, but 6:145 clarifies that it is the edible parts that are forbidden, so the bones and hair of a dead animal, which are not food, are clean and may be used, and another verse of Sūrat al-Naḥl permits the use of animal hair without any condition of slaughter. The hide carries impurities such as blood and is forbidden until tanned, after which it is permissible. The fat of a dead animal and everything made from it is forbidden, including its sale. Avoiding soap made from animal fat is a good precaution, but since it is seldom possible to be sure that fat from dead animals was used, there is some leeway, and Companions such as Ibn ʿUmar, Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī and Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī ruled that such fat is forbidden only as food and permitted its external use and sale.

According to al-Shāfiʿī and others, the milk and eggs inside an unslaughtered dead animal are impure because they are part of it. Mālik, in one narration, held them pure in themselves but rendered impure by their location. There is a similar difference about cheese made from the milk of dead animals. The popular view is that it is impure, yet the Companions ate the cheeses of the Magians, and al-Qurṭubī commented that since only a tiny part of the dead animal is mixed into it, it is permissible, because a minute impurity does not matter in a large quantity of liquid. Rennet (infaḥah), the lining taken from the stomachs of suckling lambs and kids to curdle milk, is unobjectionable if it comes from an animal slaughtered in the name of Allah. If it comes from an animal not slaughtered Islamically, Abū Ḥanīfah and Mālik consider it clean while Abū Yūsuf, Muḥammad, al-Thawrī and others call it impure. Since cheese made in non-Muslim countries is very likely to contain such rennet, caution favours avoiding it, although the position of Abū Ḥanīfah and Mālik gives leeway. Cheeses that contain pork fat, which may be shown on the wrapper, are absolutely ḥarām and impure. Ibn Mājah reports that Salmān said the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was asked about butter, cheese and fur, and replied: the lawful is what Allah has made lawful in His Book, the forbidden is what He has forbidden in His Book, and what He has not mentioned is part of what He has pardoned.

### Rulings on blood, including transfusion

Since only flowing blood is forbidden, the blood remaining on the flesh after slaughter is clean, by agreement of the jurists, the Companions and their successors and the Ummah in general. By the same analogy the blood of mosquitoes, flies and bedbugs is not unclean, though a significant amount should be washed off. Because eating and drinking blood is forbidden absolutely, its external use, its sale and any income from it are forbidden too.

Human blood is part of the human body and, once outside it, is rated impure, so transfusing it from one person to another might seem ḥarām for two reasons: respect for the human body forbids such use, and blood is a heavy impurity whose use is not permitted. But the concessions the Sharīʿah allows under compulsion and in the treatment of disease lead to another conclusion. Transfusion needs no surgery, since blood is drawn with a syringe and transferred by the same means, which makes it comparable to milk, which forms in one human body and becomes part of another. The Sharīʿah, in view of the infant's need, made milk its first food and obliged mothers to nurse while married, and after divorce made the father responsible for arranging a wet-nurse or paying the mother to continue: "And if they nurse your child, compensate them" (65:6). Milk is even used medically for adults, and the Fatāwā ʿĀlamgīriyyah states that there is no harm in dropping a woman's milk into a man's nose or giving it orally as medicine, a subject treated in al-Mughnī of Ibn Qudāmah in the chapter on hunting (Kitāb al-Ṣayd). Milk is an altered form of blood and shares with it the quality of being part of the human person, the only difference being that milk is clean and blood is not. The first reason against transfusion therefore falls away, and as to impurity, some jurists have permitted the use of blood on medical grounds. The correct position is that transfusion is not permissible under normal conditions but is doubtless permissible under compulsion, that is, where the patient faces death, no effective drug is available and there is a strong likelihood that the transfusion will save his life. The authority is this very verse, which permits saving one's life by eating carrion. Where there is no such compulsion, or other treatments would work, the jurists differ, some permitting and others not.

### Swine and the consecrated animal

Al-Qurṭubī explains that mentioning the "flesh" of swine is not meant to restrict the prohibition to the meat. All parts of the pig, bones, skin, hair and ligaments, are forbidden by consensus of the Muslim community. The word laḥm is used to show that the pig is unlike other prohibited animals, which slaughter purifies even if eating them stays forbidden. The pig's flesh is not purified by slaughter, because it is absolutely impure and unlawful, although the use of its bristles for stitching leather has been permitted in hadith.

The animal dedicated to other than Allah takes three known forms. First, an animal slaughtered to please someone other than Allah with that name called over it is forbidden by consensus. It is carrion, and no benefit may be taken from any part of it, which is what the verse plainly means. Second, an animal slaughtered to please someone other than Allah even though Allah's name was pronounced over it. Many ignorant Muslims do this when they slaughter goats, sheep or even chickens to please elders and leaders while saying bismillāh. The jurists agree that this too is ḥarām and the animal is a carcass, differing only over the reason. Some hold that the verse itself covers this case. The glosses on al-Bayḍāwī state that every animal over which a name other than Allah's was invoked is ḥarām even if slaughtered in Allah's name, and that scholars agree a Muslim who slaughters intending to please other than Allah becomes an apostate, so that his animal is one slaughtered by an apostate. Al-Durr al-Mukhtār, in its chapter on slaughtered animals, says that slaughtering an animal to celebrate the arrival of a dignitary is ḥarām because it falls under what is dedicated to other than Allah even when His name is mentioned, and al-Shāmī concurs. Others consider it a strain on the Arabic to read the phrase as covering this case directly, and forbid it instead by the commonality of cause, the intention of pleasing someone other than Allah. One scholar judged this the soundest and most cautious view. A further verse supports the prohibition: the phrase in 5:3 about "what is sacrificed on altars" (nuṣub), meaning everything falsely worshipped, stands in contrast to "what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah" earlier in the same verse. Since the latter already covers slaughter with a false name pronounced, the former must mean slaughter intended to please idols, which includes animals slaughtered for someone other than Allah even though His name was recited over them. This observation comes from Maulānā Ashraf ʿAlī Thānawī. Al-Qurṭubī takes the same approach in his commentary, noting that it was the Arabs' custom to call aloud the name of the one for whom they slaughtered, and that this was so prevalent that the real cause of the prohibition, their intention to please other than Allah, was identified in the verse with the call itself. He bases this on the rulings of ʿAlī and ʿĀʾishah. In ʿAlī's time Ghālib, the father of the poet Farazdaq, slaughtered a camel, and although there is no report that any name other than Allah's was mentioned over it, ʿAlī ruled that it fell under what is dedicated to other than Allah and was ḥarām, and the Companions accepted his verdict. Al-Qurṭubī also reports, through Yaḥyā ibn Yaḥyā, the teacher of Imām Muslim, a long hadith from ʿĀʾishah in which a woman asked her about non-Arab foster relatives who constantly celebrated festivals and sent gifts, and ʿĀʾishah replied: do not eat what was slaughtered for that day, but eat from the fruit of their trees. In sum, slaughter intended for someone other than Allah is forbidden both by commonality of cause and by 5:3.

Third, an animal released after slitting its ear or branding it, as an object of reverence to someone other than Allah, neither worked nor meant for slaughter. Such animals, the baḥīrah and sāʾibah, are not covered by this verse or by 5:3. The practice of releasing them is ḥarām by the Qur'an, as a later verse shows, but neither the practice nor the false belief makes the animal itself unlawful. To treat it as forbidden would be to endorse the false belief, so it is lawful like any other animal, and by the principles of the law it never leaves its owner's possession, whatever he imagines. If he sells or gives it away, it is lawful for the recipient. People in some countries endow goats or cows in the name of their gods and leave them with temple keepers who may sell them, even to Muslims, and some ignorant Muslims do the same at shrines and graveyards, leaving a goat or a cockerel with keepers who sell it. Whoever buys such livestock or poultry from keepers authorised by the owners may lawfully buy, slaughter, eat or resell it.

A fourth situation concerns things other than animals: food or sweets offered in fulfilment of vows to someone other than Allah, by Hindus in their temples or by ignorant Muslims at shrines. Such a nadhr for other than Allah is ḥarām by the same commonality of cause, so eating, feeding, buying and selling it are all forbidden, as detailed in works of fiqh such as al-Baḥr al-Rāʾiq. The ruling rests on analogy with the animals the Qur'an names expressly.

### Compulsion, and the forbidden as medicine

Even under compulsion the Qur'an does not make these things lawful. It says only that there is no sin on the one who eats, so they remain ḥarām and the sin of using them is forgiven because of necessity. There is a world of difference between making something lawful and forgiving a sin. Had the aim been to make these things lawful under need, a simple exception from the prohibition would have sufficed. Instead the text adds that Allah is Forgiving and Merciful, making the point that what is ḥarām remains ḥarām and its use remains a sin, but the compelled person is forgiven.

The verse also proves that a person whose life is in danger may use what is forbidden as medicine, under conditions hinted at in the text. There must be a state of compulsion with danger to life, not ordinary pain or sickness. No lawful treatment must work or be available. It must be normally certain that the forbidden medicine will save life, just as a couple of morsels of unlawful meat are enough to save the starving, for a medicine that merely seems useful without certainty of cure does not fall under the exception. And, as the verse states, enjoyment must not be the aim and no more than needed may be used. Given these five conditions, any unlawful or impure medicine may be used internally or externally by the consensus of the jurists. Using impure or ḥarām medicine in ordinary illnesses is disputed. Most jurists forbid it short of compulsion, because the Prophet ﷺ, as reported in al-Bukhārī, said that Allah has not placed the cure of the Muslims in what He has forbidden. Some permit it on the strength of the episode of the ʿUraynah tribesmen, reported in all the hadith collections, whom the Prophet ﷺ allowed to drink the milk and urine of camels, which cured them. But that episode admits of several explanations, so the sound original position is that without the conditions of extreme necessity, ḥarām medicine is not permissible in common diseases. Later jurists, considering the flood of unlawful and impure medicines in modern times and people's weakness, have permitted forbidden medicine when no lawful and pure one is effective or available. Al-Durr al-Mukhtār notes the disagreement, says the apparent ruling forbids it as al-Baḥr al-Rāʾiq states in the chapter on fosterage, but reports from al-Ḥāwī al-Qudsī that some scholars permitted the forbidden as medicine when the cure is certain and no alternative exists, like the permission given to one dying of thirst to take a sip of wine. Modern medicines from Europe and America in which alcohol is certainly used as a base or solvent, or other impure ingredients are certainly present, fall under these rules. Where the presence of impure ingredients cannot be ascertained there is a little more leeway, but precaution is best, especially when the need is not pressing. Allah knows best.

### Hunger and the garden

Someone in dire straits who finds both carrion and other people's food that he could take without risking his hands or causing harm may not eat the carrion. Ibn Mājah reports that ʿAbbād ibn Shuraḥbīl al-Ghubarī said that in a year of famine he came to al-Madīnah, entered a garden, rubbed some grain clean and ate it, and put some in his garment. The owner beat him and took his garment. He went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, who told the owner: you did not feed him when he was hungry, or he said starving, nor did you teach him if he was ignorant. He ordered the garment returned and a wasq, around 180 kilograms, or half a wasq, of food given to him. The chain is sufficiently strong and other reports support it, such as the hadith of ʿAmr ibn Shuʿayb from his father from his grandfather that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was asked about hanging clusters of dates and said there was no harm in a needy person taking some in his mouth without putting any in his garment.

In the devotional reading, Allah has forbidden in outward matters these specific things dedicated to other than Him, and He has forbidden to the innermost hearts companionship with anything other than Him, and even witnessing anything other than Him. The one who is "compelled", who has not found the means to annihilation in the realities of the Real, should travel only by way of the law: either effaced in Allah, or standing by means of Allah, or acting for Allah. Otherwise he is a foolish man of no account.

### In short

- Only four things are forbidden here, relative to the idolaters' inventions: carrion, flowing blood, swine, and what is slaughtered for other than Allah (cf. 6:145, 5:3). Fish and locusts, liver and spleen are excepted (Ibn ʿUmar), and sea creatures are lawful (5:96).
- Carrion and all impurities may not be eaten, sold or used, except non-edible parts such as bones, hair and tanned hide. The jurists differ on fat for external use, on milk, eggs and rennet from unslaughtered animals.
- Flowing blood is forbidden in every use, and transfusion is permitted only under life-threatening compulsion, by analogy with milk and on the authority of this verse.
- Every part of the pig is forbidden and slaughter does not purify it. Slaughter for other than Allah is forbidden even when His name is pronounced (ʿAlī, ʿĀʾishah, al-Qurṭubī), while released animals like the baḥīrah remain lawful, and vow offerings to other than Allah are ḥarām by analogy.
- The one whose life is at risk must eat the forbidden, without desire and without excess (Mujāhid, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Masrūq). The sin is forgiven, not the thing made lawful, and the same conditions govern forbidden medicine.
- The starving may take from others' food in need, and the owner who refuses is at fault (ʿAbbād ibn Shuraḥbīl, Ibn Mājah).
