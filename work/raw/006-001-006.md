<<<VERSE 6:1>>>
The surah begins with praise and ends its first sentence with an accusation. "All praise is for Allah Who created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light." One claim, one proof, and then a contrast: in the face of what He has made, "the disbelievers set up equals to their Lord". Nothing is argued at length here. The making of the heavens, the earth, the darkness and the light is put forward as something anyone can see, and the worship given to anything else is shown as a mistake so plain that no evidence is needed to expose it.

The whole surah is built on this line. It is a surah about the oneness of Allah, and it opens the way a house is inspected, at the foundation.

### Phrase by phrase

**"All praise is for Allah"** is al-ḥamdulillāh. Abū Isḥāq al-Asfraʾīnī said that a surah which begins this way is going to be about the oneness of Allah, in its principles and in its details, and the opening is what tells you so. On one reading of the phrase, what belongs to Allah is gratitude and divinity together: people thank Him, and they worship Him, and neither of those two is owed to anyone else. Only the One who made the heavens and the earth out of nothing is described with perfection, praised with noble qualities, and loved. He made the night and the day, and they follow one another without a gap. The night carries darkness and the day carries light, and each of them is a benefit.

Allah opens by lauding Himself. He praises Himself with a laudation that has no beginning, and reports His own self-sufficient brilliance and His single, unshared elevation. This is the praise of a tremendous Lord and a wise maker of things. He exists by His own existence, He is beyond compare by His own attributes, He is vast by His own greatness. His rank never ends, His brilliance stands on its own, His existence is one, His being needs nothing, His face is majestic, His power is complete. Glory be to Him. He is the One, the All-Subjugating (39:4), the high, the overwhelming, the great, the transcendent. One of the great figures of the religion, and a leader of the Ṭarīqah, the path of spiritual training, said in effect: who is worthy of praise except the One whose power made all this. His answer: who is fit to be praised for purity, or named for greatness, except the One who created heaven and earth, made day and night, set the heaven up like a roof, laid the earth out like a cradle, readied the day for people to earn a living, and made the night a time of rest?

**"Who created the heavens and the earth"** singles out those two from all the rest of creation, because for anyone who looks they are the largest thing in sight. The heavens and the earth are everything a person can point at, and both of them were made from nothing. On a reading transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, the two days of creating the heavens were Sunday and Monday, and the two days given to the earth were Tuesday and Wednesday.

**"and made darkness and light"** means every darkness and every light. Ẓulmah is the Arabic for one patch of darkness, and nūr is light. Darkness is put in the plural and light in the singular, for two reasons that both carry weight. The first is that light is the more honored of the two. The second is that darkness has many causes, and each of them is a separate thing, while light reaches us by one road. That very unevenness is counted among the proofs that Allah is one. Some said that darkness and light here stand for faith and disbelief, and that the plainer sense is night and day. The unevenness was also read the other way round, as instruction: light means the correct way and the straight path, and there is one of those, while the layers of darkness mean the roads of error, and those run into the thousands. That is how it was drawn out in al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ.

**"Yet the disbelievers set up equals to their Lord"** is the turn in the sentence. Despite the proof, some of Allah's servants disbelieve, and hand partners and rivals to the Lord who nurtured them. The Meccan idolaters ascribe idols as equals to Him. Some of them claimed a wife for Allah, and some a son, and He is made far above, and removed from, anything they say about Him.

### Is this a statement or an act of worship?

Grammar gives the opening three possible jobs. It may be news, a plain statement that praise does in fact belong to Allah, so that whoever hears it believes it. It may be an act of worship, Allah teaching His servants how to extol Him, as though He were saying: say, all praise is for Allah. Or it may be both at once, and that is the likeliest of the three. The surah's first words would then be doing two things in one breath: telling you what is true about Allah, and showing you what to say about Him.

### Why heaven and earth, and not the rest?

The verse could have listed everything. It names two things and then two more. The heavens and the earth were chosen because they are the most awesome created thing available to an observer, and because a person who agrees that Allah made those two has already conceded the argument. Everything anyone worships instead of Him sits inside one of them.

The same two appear again as an inner picture. On that reading the heaven means the heaven of recognition, which is the hearts of people who come to know Allah, and the earth means the earth of service, which is the souls of people who worship. Allah decorated the visible heaven with the shape of the stars, hung the sun and the moon in it, and made it a place for the people of the earth to look at. He decorated the other heaven in the same way, with the sun of knowledge, the moon of pure oneness and the stars of thoughts, and made it a place for the people of the heaven above to look at. When devils try to overhear, they are driven off from the high heaven by stones thrown at them, and the Exalted Lord said of that: "We have made them stonings for the satans" (67:5). In the same way, whenever satan tries to pour unease into someone, a flash lights up in the heart of the faithful servant from his heaven of recognition and burns the attempt off. That is what the verse about the mindful means when it says that a visit from satan touches them and at once they see things (7:201). And on the flat of the earth He spread seven seas, and in them are use and livelihood for His creatures.

### In short

- The surah opens with a claim and a proof: all praise, and with it all thanks and all worship, belongs to Allah, and what He made is the evidence.
- He praises Himself first to teach His servants how to praise, and because praise is owed to Him whether anyone offers it or not.
- Darkness is plural and light is singular because darkness has many causes and light comes by one road, and because light is the more honored. That is itself counted as proof of Allah's oneness.
- The heavens and the earth are named because they are the largest thing in sight, and because whatever anyone worships besides Allah stands inside them.
- Setting up equals to Allah is the odd thing in the verse, not a competing argument: people who claimed a wife or a son for Him were claiming it against evidence every observer can see.

<<<VERSE 6:2>>>
The surah now turns from the big universe to the small one. "He is the One Who created you from clay, then appointed a term ˹for your death˺ and another known only to Him ˹for your resurrection˺—yet you continue to doubt." Three things are claimed about you: you were made, your life has an end written on it, and there is a second end, fixed with Allah, that nobody else has been told. The verse then expresses astonishment that anyone doubts after that.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He is the One Who created you from clay"** speaks to the whole of mankind at once, and its first meaning is their father. Allah created Adam from clay and then gave him life. Mankind began from him, multiplied, and spread east and west. The route from clay to a person runs through ordinary food, which comes from the damp of the earth, and from food comes the sperm, and from the sperm the human body is made. So "created you from clay" is not a poetic way of talking. It describes a chain that is still running.

A report about that clay goes further. Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī said he heard the Prophet ﷺ say that Allah created Adam from a handful taken out of the whole earth, mixing its ingredients together, and that is why Adam's children come out different from one another in colour, in shape, in character and in habit. One is black, another white, another red. One is hard, another soft, one good-natured, another ill-tempered. This was transmitted with a good chain.

**"then appointed a term ˹for your death˺"** is, on the reading Saʿīd ibn Jubayr gave from Ibn ʿAbbās, the Hereafter, and the verse mentions it first as the term already decreed. Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid took it the other way round, and this is the reading many prefer: the appointed term is the span of this earthly life, and the other term, fixed with Allah, is the term of a person's life up to his death. That second sense fits the verse that comes later in this surah, where Allah takes the souls at night and knows what people did by day, so that an appointed term may be fulfilled (6:60). Much the same was relayed from As-Suddi and ʿAtiyyah, from Muqātil ibn Ḥayyan, from Zayd ibn Aslam and ad-Daḥḥāk, and from Qatādah, al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrimah and Mujāhid, among others.

Whichever way the two terms are assigned, one is a personal end and the other is a collective one. A person's own end is death, and it has a fixed term that he has reached whenever he reaches it. Nobody has been given the knowledge of when that is, though the angels know, and in a way people know it too, since the dying of Adam's children all around them, everywhere and all the time, is common experience. The other end is the end of the whole world, the Qiyamah, the day of Doom, when all mankind and every created thing that serves them come to their close together. That term stands fixed with Allah, and its full knowledge has not been given to any angel or any human being.

**"and another known only to Him"** means exactly that: none but He knows when it will happen. The Qur'an says of the Hour that its knowledge belongs to his Lord alone, and that none can reveal it at its time but He (7:187), and again, when people ask when it will land: you have nothing to say about it, and its end rests with your Lord (79:42-44).

**"yet you continue to doubt"** is addressed to the people of Makkah. As-Suddi took the doubt to be doubt about the coming of the Last Hour. They are uncertain about the Resurrection although they know who began their creation, and One who can begin can certainly bring back. He created this world and fixed a term for its ending. He made living things whose span runs out at death. And the other term is the one after which there is no death and nothing wears away.

### Why your own body proves the Hereafter

The argument in these two verses is a ladder. The first verse showed a big universe made by Allah, and the second says that you are a small universe made the same way. Start from the beginning of a person and follow through to his end, through birth, living and death, across all the internal distances a body contains, and the oneness of Allah turns out to be a fact as plain as anything you can check.

Then comes the doubt, and the doubt is answered out of the same body. This world has a limited age. Life in it cannot be free of grief, pleasantness arrives with unpleasantness attached, and good cannot be separated from evil or evil from good. Given that, a person finds it hard to picture an eternal world that is free of grief altogether, the world the Qur'an describes when it speaks of toil being stripped away (35:34). If the Next World is made out of some other stuff, then it is beyond anyone's reach to imagine. If it is made out of the stuff of this world, then this world looks too poor to produce a world of total perfection.

But the person asking the question is his own answer. A human body is built entirely out of earth's elements, and it has properties that no earth has anywhere. It hears, it speaks, it thinks, it does astonishing things, and the dirt it was made from does none of those. Something that is not earth at all has come out of earth, and everyday experience confirms it. If a living, active person can rise out of clay, and if sweet-smelling flowers and heavy fruit can come out of the same ground, then a world more perfect than this one is not too much to ask from it. So there is no room for the doubt, and using a person's own death as the evidence for the end of the whole world system is the most natural and most logical thing in the world. The verse ends in wonder that anyone keeps doubting a Qiyamah proved like that.

### Why the proof does not land

If the reasoning is that clear, why does it leave people unmoved? Because every one of these proofs arrives behind a curtain of causes. When a sign reaches someone's attention he credits it to the cause he can name and lets the sign itself go unnoticed. He is not inclined to accept it, and instead of answering the call he says that if it really came from Allah, then Allah and His angels would have to be visible to the naked eye. That demand is empty. When Allah and His angels do appear in their glory, the time for preaching the truth and declaring Allah's greatness will be over. It will be the time for settling accounts.

### Clay and spirit

There is an older reading of "from clay" that takes the verse apart at its seams. Adam was two things, clay and spirit. His clay belonged to the world of creation, and his spirit to the world of command. What belongs to creation is this: Allah kneaded Adam's clay in His own hand. What belongs to command is this: "I have blown into him of My spirit" (15:29). And the two verses about him match the two halves of him. "Allah chose Adam" (3:33) belongs to the beauty of the command, while "Adam disobeyed" (20:121) belongs to the drag of the clay.

In him there was a rose garden and a bed of mud at once, and the mud was where the roses grew. Every rose, though, has its thorn. A rose like Ibrāhīm (Abraham) came with a thorn like the rebellious Nimrod. A rose like Mūsā (Moses) came with thorns like Pharaoh and Hāmān. A rose like ʿĪsā (Jesus) came with thorns like the Jews who wronged him. A rose like Muḥammad the Arab came with a thorn like Abū Jahl, and no luck in it. No one's mind has flown far enough to reach the branch of Adam's good fortune, and no one's insight has taken in the brightness of the sun of his clarity.

When Adam was at rest in the highest gardens, he assumed that tent of safety would stay pitched for him forever. Then he was addressed, from the side of the All-Compeller and the court of the Most High: "What, one brought up in ornaments and unable to argue clearly?" That is the question the Qur'an puts in the mouth of the deniers (43:18). And the rebuke that goes with it runs like this. Allah said: I want to make a real man out of you, and you have settled for colour and scent, like a bride contented with dress. How long will you sit still and hope for adornment? Set your aim on the road and walk like a man. Take your hands off the neck of your wife, because your hands are needed for gripping the crocodile of passion. Be the drinking companion of the lion of the Sharīʿah. Withdraw from the qualities you were made of, and travel with discipline for feet and blame for boots, out into the wide country of having nothing. Sit down on a dust heap, be happy with one piece of bread, torn clothes and a ruin, and then you will be a man. Throw your life away, walk the road, stand straight, and be a man, and you will be kept alive once your skirt is emptied of all this rubble.

### In short

- "From clay" is literal: Adam was made of clay, mankind came from him, and every person is still built out of food that grows out of damp earth.
- The two terms are a person's death and the end of the world, and the order of the two was disputed. Either way, one is known to the angels and to experience, and the other to Allah alone.
- Your own body is the answer to the doubt about the Resurrection. Earth, by itself, cannot hear, speak or think, and it produced something that does all three.
- Doubt survives the evidence because evidence arrives through causes, and through a demand that Allah appear visibly, which would end the time for being invited in the first place.
- The verse is read as astonishment: the people who doubt it are the people of Makkah, who had watched the Prophet ﷺ grow up among them.

<<<VERSE 6:3>>>
The third verse closes the argument the first two opened. "He is the Only True God in the heavens and the earth. He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal, and knows whatever you do." After the big universe and the small one, one Being is left standing, worshipped in both of them, and knowing everything done in either. What follows in the surah, from the demands for signs to the weighing of secret motives, grows from those last three clauses.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He is the Only True God in the heavens and the earth"** is read by Ibn ʿAbbās as the consequential outcome of the first two verses. Because He made all that, He alone deserves worship and obedience in the heavens and on the earth. He is the God of the dwellers of heaven and the God of all the dwellers of the earth. He is the One called Allah throughout them all, and He is the One they single out: the inhabitants of the heavens and of the earth believe in His divinity, they name Him Allah, and they ask Him in fear and in hope. Those who disbelieve among jinn and among humankind are the exception. The Qur'an says elsewhere, in the sense the same words carry: "He is the God of those in heaven and those on earth" (43:84), and that He knows every affair, the public and the private.

**"He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal"** covers what you keep inside your chest and what you say out loud to one another, the intention, the statement, and everything you make public as much as everything you hide. Nothing of it is harder for Him than anything else.

**"and knows whatever you do"** is what you earn, every good deed and every bad one. And He will judge you by them.

### Does "in the heavens" mean He is inside them?

These words have been read as a map of how He relates to things, not a place where He is. He is in the heaven by His essence, and everywhere by His knowledge. He is with the spirit by His company and with the soul by His nearness. The soul comes to nothing in Him, and where the spirit disappears He is what is left of it. He is found where people find, and known where people know. Reports about Him do not spoil the ultimate reality, and the reality does not cancel the reports. Say "He established Himself on the Throne" (7:54), because He is upon the Throne in a sitting that befits Him. Recite "He is with you wherever you are" (57:4), because He is with you. He does not occupy a place because He needs one. He displays places out of mercy. He built the Throne for those who are seeking Him, not for those who already know Him. And if someone who knows Him drew one breath without Him, he would have tied the belt of unbelief back on.

The same voice turns the verse into a prayer: you are my tongue's boast in both worlds and my spirit's joy in the seeing that comes tomorrow. Remake what occupies me, with Yourself. I have no spirit left to scatter in front of the finding of You, and no tongue equal to acknowledging your favours. Whoever sees You is hidden inside the seeing, and whoever looks for You is not on the earth and not in the heaven.

### In short

- The verse moves from creator to God: because He made the heavens, the earth, darkness and light, He alone is worshipped in all of them.
- Its knowledge covers hidden intention, spoken word and earned deed, and the three are the ground of the judgement that the surah keeps announcing.
- Only disbelieving jinn and human beings are outside that worship, and they are outside it by choice, not because He is unknown to them.
- The phrases "in the heavens" and "on the Throne" are read alongside "He is with you wherever you are", and explained as nearness and knowledge, not location.

<<<VERSE 6:4>>>
Verse 4 states a fact about these people rather than an argument. "Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it." The word for sign, āyah, covers a miracle, a proof and a verse of the Qur'an, and the verse is a complaint: it is not that the evidence is weak, it is that the people shown it keep turning their faces aside. The chronically heedless human race is being described here, and the fault is its obstinacy against what is true.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord"** means the people of Makkah. The signs are Allah's proofs of His own oneness and of the truth of His noble Messengers, verses sent down in the Qur'an, clear demonstrations, and miracles of the kind that cannot be explained away.

**"they turn away from it"** is the whole of their answer. They do not consider these signs and they do not trouble themselves about them. Whatever is shown them for their guidance, they swing their face away from it and pay it not the least attention. The idolaters ignore a proof or a miracle from their Lord as though it had not arrived. The clear demonstrations of Allah's oneness, and the miracles that prove His messengers truthful, came to them, and after that they still turned aside. They have taken to a way of their own, and that way runs away from evidence.

### What signs were they refusing?

The signs meant here are the kind the Qur'an reports for this Prophet ﷺ and this people. The eclipse of the sun was one, the splitting of the moon was one, and the stars were among them. Each one came, and each one was met with unbelief. A sign that arrives does not oblige anyone to think, and this verse is a description of people who have decided in advance not to, before the next verse reports what that decision ends in.

### In short

- The verse is a complaint, not an invitation to produce more evidence: the people described have signs and refuse to look at them.
- Āyah here means sign, miracle, proof and revealed verse together.
- Turning away is an act of the will. The verse does not say they could not see, it says they could not be bothered.
- Their stubbornness is described as a habit of their own, which is why the punishment in the next verse is presented as something that will meet them on level ground.

<<<VERSE 6:5>>>
""They have indeed rejected the truth when it came to them, so they will soon face the consequences of their ridicule"." Verse 4 said they turned away. Verse 5 says what that amounted to, denial of the truth once it was in front of them, and what it will amount to, being told the news of what they are laughing at now. The middle of the sentence is a warning and a hard threat at once: they will find out that what they denied was true, and they will taste how their own conduct ended.

### Phrase by phrase

**"They have indeed rejected the truth when it came to them"** turns on one word, al-Ḥaqq, the Truth, and that word is left open here to two things: the Qur'an, and the blessed person of the noble Prophet ﷺ. Both came to them, and both were denied, and the two belong together, since the Book arrived in the company of the man who brought it. It was the people of Makkah who denied it, denying the Qur'an and the sign at the same time. They had turned away from clear proofs and demonstrations, and then they went on to something plainer still and denied it.

**"so they will soon face the consequences of their ridicule"** is a threat of punishment. Right now these people are careless about their own fate. They mock the miracles and the commandments of Allah that the Prophet ﷺ brought, and they mock the message that there is a Qiyamah and a Hereafter. Soon the news of that ridicule will come to them, and it will come as the punishment for it. The realities they laughed at will be standing in front of their eyes. There will be a Qiyamah, and there will be a Reckoning in which everyone answers for faith and for deed, and everyone is given the reward or the punishment that matches what was done. Believing and admitting it at that point will not serve them, because that day is not a day for doing anything. It is the day of being paid back.

### The sign they could have checked

The strongest reason for calling this a rejection of the truth is that the truth arrived in a form these people had already inspected for forty years. He lived his whole life among these Arab tribes. They watched him grow through boyhood into a young man, and through his youth into later life. They also knew perfectly well that no human teacher had taught him, to the point that he could not write his own name by himself, and all of Arabia knew him by the title unlettered and unschooled. In forty years he showed no marked interest in poetry, which was the distinction of that age, and no aptitude for formal learning. Then, at the end of his fortieth year, there was a sudden change, and an overnight flowering of what nobody could have expected. His tongue became a spring of insights, realities, knowledge and arts that disarmed the best minds in the world. He challenged every eloquent master of words in Arabia to produce the like of what he had brought, and at the very least one verse like a verse of the Qur'an. Those same opponents were ready to give up life, wealth, honour, children and family, at any moment, to defeat him. On a challenge that would have cost them nothing but effort, they had no courage at all.

So the bare fact that this man was there, and that this Qur'an was with him, was a sign of unanswerable authenticity. Add to it the thousands of miracles and open proofs that came about through him, and no sane person can say that they were invented, and the size of the refusal becomes clear. They denied all of it, and that is what the verse records.

### Three days when the news arrived

The material connects the consequences promised here to days the audience could identify: the day of Badr, the day of Uḥud, and the day of the Confederates. The news of what they mocked came to them on those days, in the form of punishment, and long before the Reckoning they were still refusing to take seriously. This surah was revealed in Makkah, and the sense of the promise is not that the threat is cancelled by an escape period. It is a God-sent piece of luck, and the sense is: act now, for it is by faith alone that a person succeeds in this world and in the world that lasts.

### In short

- The truth they rejected was the Qur'an and the Prophet ﷺ himself, and both came to them in a town where he had lived his whole life under their eyes.
- Forty years of an untaught man and then a sudden flowering of speech no one could answer is presented as a proof in its own right.
- The threat is that the mocked realities will one day be plainly in front of them, at a Reckoning that will be a day of payment rather than a day of action.
- Some of that news landed in this world, on the days of Badr, Uḥud and the Confederates.
- The delay is a mercy with a deadline in it, and the verse reads as an appeal to use the time still left.

<<<VERSE 6:6>>>
Verse 6 changes the evidence from a prophet in their own town to the ruins around them. "Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them? We had made them more established in the land than you. We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet. Then We destroyed them for their sins and replaced them with other peoples." The nations in question were not present for anyone to look at, so the seeing meant here is the seeing of the mind: to ponder, to travel and to read what is left. History is offered as a document, and the verse is an appeal to a reader of documents

### Phrase by phrase

**"Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them?"** asks the people of Makkah whether they were not informed, in this Qur'an, and whether they had not heard of it in their trading journeys to Syria and elsewhere. In Arabic the word behind "how many" here is a statement rather than a question, and its sense is simply: many. A generation, qarn, means a body of people living together in one period, and it is also used for a long stretch of time, put at anything between ten and one hundred years. The events and the reports point to one hundred. The Prophet ﷺ told ʿAbdullāh ibn Busr al-Maʿzinīyy that he would live for one qarn, and when he prayed for a certain child that he live a whole qarn, the boy lived exactly a hundred years. Most scholars take the well-known hadith, the one still recited in the khuṭbah of Jumuʿah all over the world, to mean precisely this: a qarn is a hundred years.

**"We had made them more established in the land than you"** says that Allah gave them sovereignty, room and time in it, more than He has given the people of Makkah. They had more of everything: more wealth, more children, more buildings, wider provision, greater riches and more soldiers. Their place in the land was fixed through strength and plenty. The address shifts here, out of the third person into the second, and that shift is the point of the sentence: whoever is being spoken about is being compared with whoever is being spoken to.

**"We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet"** is rain let loose in torrents, one after another, whenever they needed it, and rivers running under their homes, their fields, their plantations and their trees, with the springs full. That abundance was itself a form of being let go on.

**"Then We destroyed them for their sins"** for the errors and mistakes they committed, and above all for denying the prophets and going against Allah. **"and replaced them with other peoples"** means He raised up after them a different generation, and on one reading a better one, so that He could test them in their turn. Some of those replaced the old sins with the same sins and were destroyed the way their ancestors had been.

### What history is for

A large part of the Qur'an is made of historical accounts that are meant to be read for wisdom, and events of the past are documents full of warning. A wise man used to say that the world is the best book and time is the best teacher. That is why the Qur'an keeps taking the essence of history out and using it as something that warns and advises. It does not do what the usual story-telling does, where the event is the point and the end. It sets part of a narrative where the subject and the moment of its message need it, and it tells another part of the same story somewhere else, on another occasion, for another purpose. A report is not an end in itself. What matters is the outcome of what happened and the practical thing it asks of you, so a person takes what his own aim requires, moves ahead, and looks at his circumstances in that light, learning the lesson that matters from what went before and correcting himself.

Speaking generally, people have refused to give world history any serious part in running their lives. They use it as an intellectual pastime, something that helps them sleep or fills spare time. So this verse puts the question to them twice, as if to say: have they not seen, and do they not know Allah's way of destroying communities that did wrong?

### What that spectacle says about power

The last clause is not a footnote about replacement. It says that Allah's power was not simply the power to wipe out nations of strength and authority in a single flash. It was there, on the ashes of their destruction, making other peoples rise and fill the same places, as though nothing had happened there and no one were missing from the scene. That is an observable thing, in its own way, in every period. Thousands die every day and no vacuum is left anywhere, and we never stop to notice that the people who belonged to a place no longer belong to it.

One who stood on the plain of ʿArafat, with something like a million men and women spread around him, was struck by a thought. Seventy or eighty years before that day, not one person in that entire assembly had existed. And on that same plain, seventy or eighty years earlier, roughly that many human beings had stood, and today there is no trace of any of them. Apply the same picture to any gathering you have ever been in. Think about its past and its future, and see what you are left with: somebody telling you to wake up to what is finally true of everything. Praised be Allah, the best of creators.

### Why this is aimed at Makkah

The point of the comparison is that the people of Makkah did not have the strength of the people of ʿĀd and of Thamūd, the legendary nations, and they did not have the wealth of the people of Syria and Yaman, and yet the nations who had both were finished. So the lesson these generations teach is worse for the people of Makkah than it was for them, and the verse spells out why. Those old nations perished and became a tale and a story, and the new ones were tested and destroyed for doing what their predecessors had done. Beware of the same end, for you are not dearer to Allah than those nations were, and the Messenger you are defying is dearer to Allah than the messengers they defied. That makes you more likely to be punished, and only Allah's mercy and kindness keeps it off.

### In short

- Seeing the fate of past nations means pondering them, since nobody alive could watch them fall, and what they have to go on is travelling and memory.
- A qarn is a people of one period, and in the reports about it a hundred years, which is how most scholars take the hadith read in the Friday sermon.
- Those nations were given more land, wealth, children, buildings, provision and soldiers than the Meccans, and none of it stood against punishment for their sins.
- New peoples were raised on the site of the destroyed ones, which shows that Allah's power is as much replacement as destruction, and that is what makes history a warning instead of a mystery.
- Qur'anic history is not story for its own sake. Its purpose is the outcome of the event and the correction it asks of the person reading it.
