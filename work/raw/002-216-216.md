<<<VERSE 2:216>>>
Verse 216 moves from spending possessions to risking life. "Fighting has been made obligatory upon you ˹believers˺, though you dislike it." The verse acknowledges that burden, then contrasts limited human judgment with Allah's knowledge of what truly benefits people.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Fighting has been made obligatory upon you ˹believers˺,"** The explanation concerns jihād against enemies transgressing against Islam. Its legal discussion distinguishes a collective obligation from circumstances making it binding on individuals.

**"though you dislike it."** Fighting naturally burdens the heart. It involves travel, struggle, wounds and death, and risks both life and wealth. Acknowledging that reluctance does not remove the command.

**"Perhaps you dislike something which is good for you"** Apparent loss can lead to victory, reward or martyrdom. Human assessment of benefit often fails to see the outcome.

**"and like something which is bad for you."** Staying away may seem comfortable but lead to defeat, subjugation and loss. The principle also extends to other choices where desire hides harm.

**"Allah knows and you do not know"** Allah knows the consequences in this life and the Hereafter. His command, rather than personal liking, must guide the believer's judgment and conduct.

### What occasions are mentioned?

One early report connects the verse with Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad and their associates. It relates the command to a general summons with the Prophet ﷺ.

The same account places verse 217 with ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh and his companions. They killed ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī on what they reckoned to be the final afternoon of Jumādā al-Ukhirah, day 30. They had not seen the moon of Rajab. Quraysh reproached them for fighting during a sacred month. The fuller discussion of that incident belongs to the next verse.

### How is the collective obligation explained?

Al-Zuhrī says that everyone has a duty of support according to need. Those who stay behind must supply help when required and march when summoned. If their participation is unnecessary, they remain behind.

The Ṣaḥīḥ records a warning about dying without having fought or sincerely intended to fight, describing such a death as Jāhiliyyah. At al-Fatḥ, the conquest of Makkah, the Prophet ﷺ said migration from Makkah to al-Madīnah had ended. Jihād and intention remained, and those summoned were to march.

The wording of the verse might initially suggest an absolute duty on every Muslim. Other verses and sayings qualify that impression. In ordinary conditions it is farḍ ʿalā al-kifāyah, a collective obligation, rather than farḍ ʿayn on each individual. When a sufficient group discharges it, the others are released. If nobody does so in a country or period, all bear responsibility for abandoning it.

A hadith speaks of a group continuing this duty until the Day of Judgment. The discussion also cites (4:95). There, those who strive have higher ranks, but a good reward is promised to those with valid reasons for remaining. These include compelling excuses and engagement in other religious service. Such a promise supports the distinction from an unconditional individual duty.

The division between those marching and those acquiring religious knowledge in (9:122) makes the same point. Both forms of service remain possible within a collective duty.

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record a man asking to join the Prophet's ﷺ jihād. His parents were alive, so he was directed to serve them and earn reward through that service. Those not needed by a group already carrying out the duty can attend to other religious responsibilities.

### When does the duty become individual?

The Imām may issue a general summons because of pressing need. Then the duty becomes binding on all, as illustrated by the rebuke for clinging to the land in (9:38).

The discussion also considers invasion of an Islamic country. If the defenders are too few or weak, responsibility reaches neighbouring Muslims. If they too cannot repel the attack, it extends farther and may ultimately bind every individual Muslim.

The majority of jurists and hadith scholars therefore distinguish these circumstances from the normal collective obligation. While it remains farḍ kifāyah, children need parental permission to go, and a debtor must first clear the debt. When a general call or aggressive encirclement makes it farḍ ʿayn, those conditions no longer apply. The account includes the permissions of parents, a husband and a creditor in that distinction.

### Why can reluctance misjudge benefit?

The explanation lists possible outcomes of fighting: defeat of the enemy, victory and dominance, possession of lands, wealth and offspring, booty, martyrdom and reward. These belong to its historical account of the command and its consequences.

Even an intelligent person can confuse benefit with harm. Looking back may reveal that something eagerly pursued was damaging, while something avoided proved helpful. What seemed loss in Allah's way can be disclosed as gain and lasting peace.

Conversely, avoiding the duty can lead to the enemy taking land and government, impoverishment and subjugation. It can also forfeit spoils, martyrdom and reward. The desiring soul may reject the obligation that brings happiness and seek what brings destruction.

### How does the principle reach ordinary conduct?

People may prefer unrestricted freedom when Allah's limits are better for them. They may welcome flattering friends instead of critics who kindly expose faults. Saving face before people can tempt them to defy truth, although real honour comes from upholding it before Allah.

A religion promising Paradise without sacrifice may be attractive. The explanation instead stresses struggle and sacrifice, and warns against becoming absorbed in the present while neglecting life beyond death.

The inward reading distinguishes the lower self from the heart. Discipline defers the self's comforts, while nearness brings the heart immediate consolation. Happiness requires resisting the self's opposition and following the heart's approach to Allah. Trust in the Real's promise of ease is preferable to the self's fearful forecasts.

### In short

- The legal discussion ordinarily treats jihād as a collective obligation, qualified by need and other religious duties.
- A general summons or an invasion beyond the defenders' capacity can make the duty individual.
- Permission and debt requirements differ between those circumstances.
- Natural reluctance and apparent advantage cannot replace Allah's knowledge as the standard of obedience.
