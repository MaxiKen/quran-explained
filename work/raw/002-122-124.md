<<<VERSE 2:122>>>
Verse 122 closes the long address to the Jews that began many verses earlier, and it closes it with the very words that opened it. "O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others". A similar verse has already come near the beginning of the surah. The whole account of the Israelites' conduct, their favours, their lapses and their punishments, was framed by this one statement. It stood at the start as the thesis, the verses in between were the detailed demonstration, and now it returns as the summary.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O Children of Israel!"** The Children of Yaʿqūb (Jacob), who is Israel. There is a point in the form of address. Allah's way with the people of Mūsā (Moses) is to call them by their proper name, "O Children of Israel", whereas He addresses the community of Muḥammad ﷺ by a title of honour, "O you who believe".

**"Remember My favours upon you"**. The blessings, sacred and worldly, that were given to their forefathers: deliverance from Pharaoh and his people, and all the bounties and benefits that followed. They are told to recall how Allah blessed them in their daily life and in their religion. Among those favours is the knowledge they were given of the unlettered Prophet, who is described in their Books by his characteristics, his name, the good news of his coming and the description of his Ummah (community). The reminder is also a warning against concealing that knowledge.

**"and how I honoured you above the others"**. Over the people of their own time, with prophethood and authority, and, on one reading, with Islam itself, the religion of submission to Allah. The Children of Israel were chosen for a special task: to call other nations to Allah and to impress on them that they would answer to their Lord for their actions. To help them in that task Allah raised countless prophets from among them, Ibrāhīm (Abraham) and Yaʿqūb before them, then Yūsuf (Joseph), Mūsā, Dāwūd (David), Sulaymān (Solomon), Zakariyyā (Zachariah), Yaḥyā (John the Baptist) and ʿĪsā (Jesus), to name only some.

### Why the opening statement is repeated

Over the centuries the community degenerated. It took its privileged position, which was due solely to the lofty task entrusted to it, as a sign of ethnic superiority, and so it lost its right to represent the religion of Allah on earth. The coming of the Arab Prophet ﷺ marked the replacement of the Children of Israel by the Children of Ismāʿīl (Ishmael) as the people charged with carrying Allah's word to the nations. The genuinely pious among the Israelites soon realised that what Muḥammad ﷺ taught came from the same origin as the message of the earlier prophets. Those who denied him did so out of prejudice, refusing to accept that any nation but their own could be chosen. The verse is repeated here to stress how much depends on following the unlettered Prophet, and to tell them not to envy their cousins, the Arabs, because the Final Messenger is an Arab. Envy must not drive them to oppose him, deny him or hold back from following him.

There is also a reason of method. A basic, general statement is easy to keep alive in the mind, and because it applies readily to particular cases it helps the details to be remembered too. Good speakers and writers state their basic ideas briefly before a long discussion and repeat them at the end by way of summary. The statement here, which encourages the Israelites to return to the Straight Path by reminding them of Allah's blessings, and (in the next verse) warns them by depicting the Day of Judgement, is of exactly that kind, repeated so that the two ideas sink deep.

### In short

- The verse repeats the statement that opened the address to the Israelites, so that the whole account is framed by a reminder of favour and a warning of judgement.
- Their favours included rescue from Pharaoh, a line of prophets, authority, and knowledge of the Prophet ﷺ described in their Books.
- They were preferred over the people of their time for a task, not for their race. Having turned the task into a claim of superiority, they were replaced by the Children of Ismāʿīl, and they are told not to let envy of the Arabs keep them from the Prophet ﷺ.

<<<VERSE 2:123>>>
Verse 123 completes the repeated statement with its warning half. "And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given". Where verse 122 recalled favours, this verse depicts the Day of Judgement, so that the Israelites are both encouraged back to the Straight Path and warned of what their lapses lead to.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And guard yourselves against the Day"**. Fear the torment of that Day, the Day of Return, and protect yourselves from it by following Allah's instructions and keeping away from what He has forbidden.

**"when no soul will be of any help to another"**. No soul will be requited for another or ward anything off from it. This has been understood in several ways: no unbelieving soul will be able to drive anything away from another unbelieving soul, or no righteous soul from another righteous soul, or no father will be able to turn Allah's punishment away from his son and no son from his father.

**"No ransom will be taken"**. No compensation or ransom will be accepted from a soul, however great the payment offered.

**"no intercession accepted"**. No intercessor will intercede for it, not a close angel, not a sent messenger, not a righteous servant, and no plea on its behalf will benefit it, however high the position of the one who pleads.

**"and no help will be given"**. They will not be defended against Allah's punishment or kept from what they are about to face. Apart from Allah, no helper will be left to any soul on that Day.

### Why the Israelites needed this warning

In this world a person can sometimes bear another's burden. Intercession is sometimes accepted, a man can sometimes free himself by paying compensation, and a helper is often at hand in a difficult situation. In the next world none of these mitigating factors will come into play. Allah's justice will apply to everyone equally. The Israelites, who thought that their descent guaranteed them salvation, are warned through the Arab Prophet ﷺ that in the next world value attaches only to true faith borne out by earnest deeds, and that no ethnic group holds a monopoly over the Hereafter.

### Whose intercession, and for whom

As for the enemies of Allah, nothing will be accepted from them, no payment and no plea. As for His friends, the Prophet ﷺ said: "Protect yourself from the Fire even if it be by half a date" given in charity. The intercession of the intercessors will not benefit the disbeliever, for that has been settled for each community with its prophet. The believers, on the other hand, will benefit in particular from the intercession of the Prophet ﷺ. On that Day everyone will be saying "My soul, my soul", but our Prophet ﷺ will say "My community, my community".

### In short

- The Day of Judgement allows none of the escapes of this world: no one can stand in for another, no ransom, no intercession for the deniers, no helper.
- The verse answers the Israelites' belief that belonging to a chosen people would save them. Only faith proved by deeds will count.
- Intercession is denied to the disbelievers, while the believers will have the Prophet's ﷺ intercession, and he will be pleading for his community when everyone else pleads for himself.

<<<VERSE 2:124>>>
With verse 124 the surah turns from the history of the Israelites to the man they, the Christians and the Arab idolaters all claimed as their father. "˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”" Allah tells of the honour of Ibrāhīm (Abraham), His close friend (al-Khalīl), whom He made an imām, a leader and model for people to follow, because of how he conducted himself and held to tawḥīd, the oneness of Allah. The honour came when he carried out Allah's commands and prohibitions. The verse says, in effect: Muḥammad, remind the idolaters and the People of the Book, who pretend to follow the religion of Ibrāhīm while in reality they do not, that you and your followers are his true followers, and remind them of the commands with which Allah tested him.

The passage that begins here answers the Jews in an indirect way. They were proud of descending from Yaʿqūb (Jacob) and Ibrāhīm, believed that as the chosen people they alone could lead humanity, and held that prophethood could not go to anyone outside their race. The story of Ibrāhīm and his elder son Ismāʿīl (Ishmael) suggests several answers. Only a person who is not unjust, who has passed the trial Allah set him, can guide humanity, and the Jews did not meet that condition. The Kaʿbah, which the Muslims face in ṣalāh (prayer) and which the Jews would not accept, was built by Ibrāhīm, so the Muslims' qiblah (direction of prayer) is his. The way of Islam is the way of Ibrāhīm, and the Muslims alone really follow him. Ibrāhīm himself prayed for the Last Prophet to be sent, so whoever wants to follow him must accept Muḥammad ﷺ. And Ibrāhīm had two sons, Ismāʿīl and Isḥāq (Isaac), and prayed for Allah's grace on both (as Genesis, chapter 17, also records), so that the superiority once enjoyed by the children of Isḥāq has passed to the children of Ismāʿīl. Whoever wants a share in Allah's grace must therefore accept the last and now the only valid form of the Abrahamic way. Ibrāhīm is the patriarch of both the Arabs and the Jews. He was granted leadership only after very severe tests, and the rule applied to him applies to every generation: a share in Allah's covenant goes to those who prove themselves worthy, and those who do not will meet the fate of any other offender, whatever nation they belong to.

### Phrase by phrase

**"˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord"**. The name is read Ibrāhīm, and in another reading Ibrāhām. The trial raises an obvious question: a trial tests someone's aptitude for a task, but Allah knows everything that exists inside out, so what was its purpose? The clue is the word Rabb, Lord. Of all the Divine Names the verse uses the one that denotes bringing a thing to its perfection gradually, stage by stage. The trial was not punishment for a crime, nor a way of discovering a hidden aptitude. It was part of the nurturing of a prophet, by which his inherent qualities were made to show themselves to the world so that he could be led, step by step, to the station his Lord had already chosen for him. Although Allah's knowledge and will choose a man for prophethood, he does not assume that station until his worthiness has been shown openly for people and angels to witness, just as happened with Ādam (Adam) before the angels earlier in this surah. In the Arabic the object, Ibrāhīm, is placed before the subject, his Lord, a word order that signals his glorious rank among the prophets.

**"with ˹certain˺ commandments"**. Literally "with words", kalimāt. Here it means laws, commands and prohibitions, which is how most scholars understood it. The word "words" is used in the Qur'an in more than one way: for what Allah has willed, as when Maryam "testified to the words of her Lord" (66:12), for His law, as in "The Word of your Lord has been perfected in truth and justice" (6:115), and for truthful news or a just command or prohibition. Here it is the last sense: the orders Ibrāhīm was charged with. One gloss takes it as ten traits he was commanded to fulfil, five concerning the head and five the body. Another view says it means every single word with which he prayed to Allah. These trials were not academic tests of mental capacity or theory. They tested readiness to obey Allah and steadfastness in submitting to His commands, which shows that what has value in Allah's sight is deeds, inward and outward, not theoretical hair-splitting.

**"which he fulfilled"**. He carried out all of Allah's orders, performing them thoroughly and completely. He was truthful and obedient to Allah's legislation, which is why the Qur'an says of "Abraham, who ˹perfectly˺ fulfilled ˹his covenant˺" (53:37), or, in another rendering, who paid his debt in full. That is the measure of his success in the trials. Elsewhere he is called a model of excellence, devoted to Allah and upright, not a polytheist (16:120-123). The Prophet ﷺ is told to say that his Lord has guided him to the Straight Path, the faith of Ibrāhīm the upright (6:161), and Ibrāhīm "was neither a Jew nor a Christian" but one who submitted in uprightness (3:67-68).

**"Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.”"**. The word is imām, which lexically means a leader, chief or guide: a vicegerent and exemplar in religion whose conduct and path people imitate. Since the verse concerns Ibrāhīm, the word means above all a prophet, though it includes leadership in the general sense, and also the title of patriarch that the Jews and Christians give him. The honour was a reward for his good deeds, for keeping the commands and avoiding the prohibitions. Prophethood cannot be won by personal effort, yet a prophet must display his perfection in the qualities that tested Ibrāhīm, and people of lower rank must possess them in their own degree to deserve leadership of any kind. The Qur'an says plainly: "We raised from among them leaders, guiding by Our command, when they patiently endured and firmly believed in Our signs" (32:24). That verse sums up all those qualities in two words, ṣabr (patience), the perfection of practice, and yaqīn (certainty), the perfection of knowledge.

**"Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?”"**. Make leaders from among my descendants too, whom people will follow. Allah accepted the prayer. The proof is in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt: "reserved prophethood and revelation for his descendants" (29:27). So every prophet Allah sent after Ibrāhīm came from his offspring, and every Book Allah revealed was revealed to them.

**"Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”"**. The covenant is prophethood and leadership, My promise to you and the honour and mercy I bestowed on you. Allah told him that there would be unjust people among his descendants, polytheists and wrongdoers, and that they would not benefit from His promise. They would neither be imāms nor be imitated, even though they are children of Allah's Khalīl. The righteous among his children are the ones who profit from his prayer. The clause implies that the covenant reaches only those who are not wrongdoers. One reading restricts the wrongdoers to the disbelievers among them. Another view holds that the covenant will not include the wrongdoers in the Hereafter, though in this life it does include them. Ibn Jarīr said the verse shows that the unjust are not to be leaders of people. Ibn Khuwayz Mindād al-Mālikī went further: an unjust man does not qualify to be a khalīfah, a ruler, a giver of religious verdicts, a witness, or even a narrator of hadith. To hold the station of guide and leader is, in a way, to be a vicegerent of Allah, and that rank cannot be given to a rebel against Him. It follows that Muslims, in so far as they have a choice, should not appoint as their ruler or representative a man who rebels against Allah or disobeys Him.

### What were the words he was tested with?

Scholars differ about this, and several opinions are attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās himself. ʿAbd ar-Razzāq related from him that Allah tested Ibrāhīm with the rites of Hajj, and Abū Isḥāq reported the same. ʿAbd ar-Razzāq also related from Ibn ʿAbbās that He tested him with ṭahārah, bodily purity: five things on the head and five on the body. On the head: rinsing the mouth, drawing water into the nose and expelling it, using the siwāk (tooth-stick), parting the hair and cutting the moustache. On the body: trimming the nails, plucking the underarm hair, shaving the pubic hair, circumcision and washing with water after answering the call of nature. Ibn Abī Ḥātim said that a similar statement was reported from Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, Mujāhid, ash-Shaʿbī, an-Nakhaʿī, Abū Ṣāliḥ, Abū al-Jald and others. Imam Muslim narrated something similar from ʿĀʾishah, that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that ten things belong to the fiṭrah (the natural constitution): trimming the moustache, growing the beard, using the siwāk, drawing water into the nose and expelling it, cutting the nails, washing between the fingers, plucking the underarm hair, shaving the pubic hair and washing with water after relieving oneself, and the narrator said he had forgotten the tenth, which he thought was rinsing the mouth. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from Abū Hurayrah that the Prophet ﷺ said five things are of the fiṭrah: circumcision, shaving the pubic hair, trimming the moustache, cutting the nails and plucking the underarm hair, this being Muslim's wording. These ten characteristics of the fiṭrah have been made permanent injunctions for all later communities too, and the Last Prophet ﷺ insistently commanded his followers to keep them.

Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq reported a different list from Ibn ʿAbbās: the words Allah tested Ibrāhīm with, and which he carried out, were leaving his disbelieving people when Allah commanded it, disputing with Nimrod, the king of Babylon, about Allah, being patient when he was thrown into the fire despite how traumatic that was, migrating from his homeland at Allah's command, bearing patiently the material cost of hosting guests by Allah's order, and Allah's command to slaughter his son. When he had been tested with all of this and was ready for the major test, Allah said to him "Submit", and he answered "I submit to the Lord of all worlds" (2:131), even though this meant defying his people and standing apart from them.

Some said the injunctions were ten, others thirty, but there is no real conflict between these views, since everything mentioned in this connection served in one way or another as a test, and this is what Ibn Jarīr and other leading scholars held to be the truth of the matter. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that thirty elements make up the whole of Islam: ten are mentioned in Sūrah 9 (al-Barāʾah, also called at-Tawbah), in the verse that lists those who repent, worship, praise, fast, bow, prostrate, enjoin good, forbid evil and keep Allah's limits (9:112), ten in Sūrah 33 (al-Aḥzāb), in the verse about the men and women who submit, believe, obey, are truthful, patient, humble, charitable, fasting, chaste and much in remembrance of Allah (33:35), and ten in Sūrah 23 (al-Muʾminūn), in its opening description of the believers who will prosper (23:1-11). Ibrāhīm was tried in these too, and he fulfilled them all with equal faithfulness. In the devotional tradition it is related from al-Ḥasan that Allah tried him with the stars, the moon and the sun, and he spoke beautiful words in that, because he knew his Lord was constant and never ceasing, and that He tried him with the sacrifice of his son, and he was patient and did not fall short.

### The great trials, in order

Because Allah intended to raise Ibrāhīm to an especially exalted station and to give him the title Khalīlullāh, the Friend of Allah, his trials were very severe. Not only his people but his own family were sunk in idol-worship. Against their creed and customs he was given ad-Dīn al-Ḥanīf, the pure religion, and sent to bring them back to the Straight Path. He obeyed without flinching, waged war on idolatry and called them to worship the One God alone. This drew down on him the anger of his people and of their king Namrūd (Nimrod), who decided to burn him alive. Seeking only the pleasure of his Lord, he let himself be thrown onto the pyre. Having passed that test, he heard Allah's command: "O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham!" (21:69). The command was addressed to fire as such, not to one particular fire, so every fire in the world grew cold, Nimrod's among them. Since extreme cold is as painful and deadly as heat, and Hell itself has a region of extreme cold called Zamharīr, Allah in His grace added the word salāman, "and safe".

The second trial was the command to leave his homeland and migrate to Syria with his family. Then he was told to leave even that country, and he set out at once with his wife Hājirah (Hagar) and his infant son Ismāʿīl, led by the archangel Jibrāʾīl (Gabriel). Whenever they passed through fertile land he wished to settle there, and each time the angel told him Allah did not want it. At last they reached the barren desert that was to be the site of Makkah, where the Kaʿbah would be built, and there he was told to stop. Then came a harder trial: he was ordered to leave his wife and son in that desert and return to Syria. So completely had the Friend of Allah given up his own will that he let no moment pass between the command and its execution. He started back without telling his wife. When she saw him going she called after him and got no reply, not even when she asked why he was abandoning them in so vast and lonely a place. But she was the wife of the Friend of Allah and could see how matters stood, so she asked whether he had received a divine command. Only then did he answer that it was so. She said calmly: go, then. The Lord who commanded you to part from us will Himself look after us and will not allow us to be destroyed.

She sat back in the desert, full of trust and peace, with the infant on her lap. In time thirst, her own and above all her suckling child's, drove her to go looking for water. She climbed up and down the hills of aṣ-Ṣafā and al-Marwah seven times and came back without success. It is to commemorate this that running seven times between the two hills was made an obligatory rite of Hajj and ʿUmrah. When she returned to her son, Allah's mercy came in the form of Jibrāʾīl, who made a spring of fresh water gush from the parched ground, the spring now called Zamzam. Within a day or two the water drew animals, and the animals drew men. Little by little the necessities of life became available, and the future city of Makkah began to take shape. Under divine permission Ibrāhīm came now and then to see how his wife and son were faring.

Then came the greatest trial of all. The boy had grown up in those unpromising conditions, deprived of constant fatherly care, and now the father was commanded to slaughter him with his own hand: "Then when the boy reached the age to work with him, Abraham said, “O my dear son! I have seen in a dream that I ˹must˺ sacrifice you. So tell me what you think.” He replied, “O my dear father! Do as you are commanded. Allah willing, you will find me steadfast.”" (37:102). Ibrāhīm took his son to the wilderness of Minā and, as far as his own part went, carried out the command. But Allah never meant the son to be slaughtered, only the father to be tested. The words of the Qur'an show that in his dream he had seen the act of slaughtering, not its completion, and that much he performed. Perhaps this is why the revelation came as a dream picturing the act rather than as a spoken order to kill his son. Allah therefore praised him: "You have already fulfilled the vision" (37:105). In return for this total submission Allah sent down a ram from heaven to be sacrificed in Ismāʿīl's place, and the yearly sacrifice of animals became an act of worship in memory of Ibrāhīm's way. The demand made of him was the hardest sacrifice there is, his son's life, and when Allah intervened before the blow fell it was because Ibrāhīm had already shown his perfect willingness to give whatever Allah asked, however great or terrible. One who is ready to make such soul-searing sacrifices for Allah's cause shows the utmost dedication to it, and it is only just and natural that he should lead his people. Besides these rigorous trials a number of further restrictions were laid on him in the form of injunctions, which he kept with the same devotion.

### Why the covenant excludes wrongdoers

The word ẓālim, wrongdoer, carries a further lesson stated very explicitly: every prophet is wholly sinless, as much before he becomes a prophet as after. Certain expressions in the Qur'an that seem to suggest otherwise, for example in the case of Ādam, are used metaphorically, not in the literal or technical sense of sin. To read them as technical sin is a grave doctrinal error, and insisting on it opens the way to further errors.

A note has also been made about a modern misreading. Since the Second World War the writings of the Danish thinker Kierkegaard, and particularly his book "Fear and Trembling", which treats Ibrāhīm's trial in the manner of a psychological novel, have fascinated many in the West and are said to have set off several existentialist philosophies and much modern Protestant theology. Three objections are raised. Ibrāhīm was, by the admission of Jews and Christians too, a prophet, not the average sensual man who is the subject of novels, psychology and that theology. He did not merely pass through emotional stress, a problem of conscience or a crisis of identity, the fear and trembling of the title, but was equally tried in faithfully observing divine injunctions. And when he knew Allah had chosen him he did not become silent, secretive and lonely, as the novelist's fancy would have it, but proclaimed the fact, without which he could not have functioned as a prophet at all. It is even said that the Friend of Allah would not take his daily meal until he had found a guest to share it. In short, prophethood is a degree of reality that can be understood only partially, and only with the help of the Divine Books, and it is not a field where human fantasy may roam at will.

### The inner reading of the trial

Al-Ḥasan's words about the stars, the moon and the sun are taken up in the devotional reading. The shining stars and the brilliant sun were adorned, and Ibrāhīm was tested, "so that the one tried would know, not that the one trying him was ignorant": to show him what came from him and how he walked on the road of servanthood. He came through it virtuous, fortunate and felicitous. When he said "This is my Lord" (6:76), it is said that there is an ellipsis: he meant, these estranged people say that this is my God, but it is not, for it is one of the low things that are taken down, and "I do not love the low ones". Well done, Ibrāhīm. It rose up from below, and he knew that the Lord is high, beyond His servants, and when it set he turned from it, saying that what sets is not worthy of Godhood.

The lords of realisation voice another allusion. From the first, Ibrāhīm's clay was mixed with the water of bosom friendship, his secret core burned with the fire of passion, his spirit lit with the love of eternity, and an ocean of passion stirred within him. Then at dawn, when the passionate take their morning draught, when drunkards shout for joy and the heart-lost raise their uproar, he opened his eyes, still giddy from the wine of friendship and the drunkenness of passion, and said "This is my Lord". As the lovers say: your image has so filled my eyes that wherever I look I fancy it is you. Drunkenness and passion are both paths of trial. Passion alone threw Yūsuf (Joseph) of Canaan where it did, and drunkenness alone did what it did to Mūsā (Moses) of ʿImrān, and in Ibrāhīm the two were joined. It is no wonder, then, that heart-lost drunkenness should make him gaze at the moon and the stars and say "This is my Lord". A drunkard does not know what he says, for if he knew, how would he be drunk? The one who says "I am drunk" does not know drunkenness.

As for his trial by the sacrifice of his child, it came because he gazed on the beauty of Ismāʿīl and began to pay regard to him, and the sword of that beauty wounded his heart. The command came: O Bosom Friend, did We preserve you from Āzar and the idols of Āzar so that you could gaze on Ismāʿīl's face? The stamp of bosom friendship with Me cannot be combined with glancing at others, whether at Āzar's carvings or at Ismāʿīl's face. Any talk that keeps you back from the road, call it unbelief or faith, and any picture that holds you back from the Friend, ugly or beautiful, is the same. Soon a sword was placed in his hand with the words: sacrifice Ismāʿīl, for two friends will not fit in one heart. With two qiblahs no one walks straight on the road of tawḥīd: it is either the Friend's approval or your own caprice. The outward story of the sacrifice is known and famous. In the tongue of allusion it means: cut off your heart's attachment to your child with the sword of truthfulness, for truthfulness is Allah's sword in His earth, and it cuts whatever it is laid on. Ibrāhīm heard the command and with that sword cut his heart off from his child, separating the love of Ismāʿīl from it. Then the call came, "You have already fulfilled the vision" (37:105). His state was saying: I separated from creatures to sharpen my love for You, and I made my child an orphan in order to see You.

### In short

- Ibrāhīm was tested by his Lord with commands, which he carried out completely (53:37), and as a reward was made an imām, a prophet and model for people to follow.
- The trials were a nurturing by the Rabb, not a punishment or an enquiry, and they showed his worthiness openly, as Ādam's had been shown before the angels.
- The "words" are explained as the rites of Hajj, the ten traits of the fiṭrah, the thirty qualities of 9:112, 33:35 and 23:1-11, or the great trials of leaving his people, Nimrod, the fire (21:69), migration, hospitality and the sacrifice of his son (37:102, 37:105), and there is no real conflict between these lists.
- Leadership was promised to his descendants (29:27), but never to wrongdoers, which bars the unjust from being imām, ruler, giver of verdicts, witness or narrator, and shows that prophets are sinless.
- The passage answers the Jews' claim to inherited privilege: Ibrāhīm is the father of Arabs and Jews alike, and the covenant goes only to those who prove worthy. Inwardly, his trials taught that two friends will not fit in one heart.
