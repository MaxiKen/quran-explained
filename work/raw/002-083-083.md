<<<VERSE 2:83>>>
Verse 83 reads out the terms of the covenant itself. "And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel", it begins, and then lists what they had promised: to worship none but Allah, to be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy, to speak kindly to people, to establish prayer and to pay alms-tax. The verdict follows: "But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away", except for a few, "and were indifferent".

### Phrase by phrase

**"And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel"**. Allah reminds the Children of Israel of the commandments He gave them in the Torah, of the covenants He took from them to abide by those commands, and of how they knowingly and deliberately turned away from all of it. The promise was made between Allah and the Israelites, and they accepted its terms.

**"Worship none but Allah"**. Declare Him the only true God and ascribe no partner to Him. Allah commanded them to worship Him alone and associate nothing with Him, just as He has commanded all His creatures, for this is the purpose for which He created them. He never sent a messenger without revealing to him that there is no god but He, so worship Him (21:25), and He sent a messenger to every community saying, worship Allah and shun false gods (16:36). This is the highest and most important of all rights, Allah's right to be worshipped alone without partners. The Arabic is phrased as a statement, "you shall not worship any but Allah (lā taʿbudūna illā Llāha)", a form that carries the force of a prohibition. A variant reading has the third person, "they shall not worship", and another has the plain command, "worship not".

**"be kind to parents"**. After Allah's own right comes the right of His creatures, and first among them the right of the parents. Allah habitually mentions the parents' rights alongside His own. He says, be grateful to Me and to your parents (31:14), and He has decreed that you worship none but Him and honour your parents (17:23), continuing on to the command to give close relatives their due (17:26). Ibn Masʿūd asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ which deed is best, and he answered, prayer performed on time. Ibn Masʿūd asked what came next, and he said, kindness to one's parents. He asked again, and the answer was jihād in the cause of Allah. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record this.

**"relatives"**. Keep contact with your kin. Kinship is joined to the parents here because the duty flows outward from them.

**"orphans"**. The young who have no fathers to fend for them. Be kind to them.

**"and the needy"**. Al-masākīn is the plural of miskīn, one who cannot find what he needs to spend on himself and his family. Be generous to them. These categories are treated more fully in Sūrat an-Nisāʾ, where this Ummah is given the same commands: worship Allah and associate none with Him, and be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor and the rest (4:36).

**"speak kindly to people"**. Say good words to people and be lenient with them. This includes commanding good and forbidding evil, and being truthful about the status of Muḥammad ﷺ, for one early gloss reads it as "speak the truth concerning Muḥammad". Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī explained that the good word means commanding good and forbidding evil, together with patience and forgiveness, and that the good speech Allah commands embraces every kind of behaviour that pleases Him. Imām Aḥmad narrated from Abū Dharr that the Prophet ﷺ said: do not belittle any form of righteousness, and if you find no good deed to do except meeting your brother with a smiling face, then do that. Muslim records this hadith in his Ṣaḥīḥ, and at-Tirmidhī, who graded it ṣaḥīḥ. A variant reading, ḥusnan for ḥasanan, uses the verbal noun as an intensified description, speak goodness itself. Allah commands His servants to speak well to people after commanding them to treat people well, so that two categories of manners are covered, good speech and good action. Tell people to do what is right and avoid what is wrong, but without harshness or severity.

**"establish prayer; and pay alms-tax"**. Allah then stresses His own worship and the doing of good by ordaining ṣalāh and zakāh: perfect the five daily prayers, and give from your wealth what is due to the poor, to those who have a right to it.

**"But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent"**. They accepted all this and then turned away, refusing to fulfil these obligations, knowingly and intentionally abandoning them. The address is in the second person, but the forefathers are meant, and the present generation rejects it as their forefathers did. The "few" were among those forefathers, and it is said that the phrase refers to ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām and his companions. "Indifferent" means denying the covenant and setting it aside. Of these very commands, this Ummah has practised what no nation before it practised, and all praise is due to Allah.

### Who were the few?

The few who kept the pledge were those who acted on the Sharīʿah of Mūsā (Moses) as long as it was in force, and who, once it was abrogated, followed the Sharīʿah of Muḥammad ﷺ. The verse also sets out the basic elements common to Islam and all the earlier Sharīʿahs: tawḥīd, the oneness of Allah, service to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy, gentleness in speaking to every human being, and ṣalāh and zakāh.

### Speaking gently to everyone

The command to speak kindly asks for a gentle tone and an open-hearted manner towards others, whether they are good or evil, pious or impious, orthodox or wayward, followers of the Sunnah or people given to innovation. In religious matters, however, one must not hide the truth to please people or win their approval. When Allah sent Mūsā (Moses) and Hārūn (Aaron) to Pharaoh with His signs (20:42), He instructed them to use gentle and soft words, and no one who addresses another person today is superior to Mūsā, nor is anyone addressed viler than Pharaoh. Ṭalḥah ibn ʿUmar once told ʿAṭāʾ, the great master of exegesis and hadith, that he could see people of unorthodox beliefs around him, and that being short-tempered he dealt harshly with such people when they came to him. ʿAṭāʾ told him not to behave that way, recited this verse, and said that Allah has commanded us to speak politely to people. If Jews and Christians are all to be treated like this, he asked, would the command not apply to a Muslim, whatever kind of man he is? Al-Qurṭubī preserves the exchange.

### Kindness to the weak as a test

A modern reflection draws out the order of the duties. Man's first duty to God is to worship Him without ascribing partners to Him. His second is kindness to others, and kindness begins at home with parents and family, extends to neighbours and relatives, and finally reaches everyone one meets. The only proper way to deal with people is with justice and goodwill. The real test comes in one's dealings with the orphans and the needy, that is, with the weaker members of society. The strong are guaranteed respectful treatment by their very strength, but where the weak are concerned no such incentive exists, so one must take special care to behave well toward them. Those who are good to people without worldly power or resources do it for God's sake alone, since no other motive is available. There are many reasons why people feel little inclination to be kind to the weak. Benefactors often regard themselves as superior, and when they are not shown the deference they consider their due they treat the less fortunate with disdain. The thought of a permanent liability is often so irksome that they turn rude in order to shake off a burdensome obligation. Such an attitude leaves no room for the idea that the weak also have their self-respect. When the benefactor feels slighted, he writes the beneficiary off as unworthy and incompetent, and that thinking shows itself in many ways.

### In short

- The covenant's terms were worship of Allah alone, kindness to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy, good speech, prayer and zakāh, the shared core of every Sharīʿah (compare 4:36).
- Allah's right comes first, then the parents' right, which the Qur'an pairs with His own (31:14, 17:23), and which the Prophet ﷺ ranked right after timely prayer.
- Good speech covers commanding good, forbidding evil, truthfulness about the Prophet ﷺ, and even a smiling face, as Abū Dharr's hadith teaches.
- The Israelites turned from the covenant, all but a few such as ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām and his companions, while this Ummah has kept these commands as no nation before it.
