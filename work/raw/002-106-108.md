<<<VERSE 2:106>>>
Verse 106 answers an objection that the Jews, and some idolaters with them, had begun to press on the Muslims. "If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything". It is the Qur'an's own statement of the principle of abrogation (naskh), and the foundation of a whole branch of Islamic learning.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If We ever abrogate a verse"**. At first the Muslims prayed facing the Bayt al-Maqdis in Jerusalem, and later Allah commanded them to face the Kaʿbah. Certain other rulings too were withdrawn altogether or replaced by others. The Jews, and some of the idolaters, seized on this to taunt the Muslims, saying that the changes were the Prophet's ﷺ own work and not Allah's. The Quraysh said: "Muḥammad, why do you command us to do something and then forbid it?" The disbelievers mocked that one day Muḥammad ﷺ enjoined something on his Companions and the next day forbade it. The aim was to plant doubt in Muslim minds about whether the Qur'an was revealed by Allah at all. Their argument ran like this: if everything Allah reveals is good, as the Muslims say, why should one ruling be replaced by another? That would mean one of the two was good and the other bad, and no divine revelation can be bad, so the Qur'an cannot be a revealed book. The campaign became so intense that some simple-minded Muslims were affected and started raising the question with the Prophet ﷺ. The People of the Book regarded prophethood as their exclusive right. They could not conceive of a prophet among another people and tried every means of discrediting the Arab Prophet ﷺ. One theological objection concerned abrogation. Did replacing a command imply a mistake in Allah's earlier revelation?

The verse refutes the whole line of reasoning. Abrogation does not mean replacing good with bad or bad with good. Everything Allah reveals to His prophets is good, and what is abrogated is as good as what abrogates it. Naskh has two lexical meanings, to write or copy, and to abolish or repeal. All exegetes agree that the second sense, repeal, applies here. In Qur'anic and ḥadīth terminology, naskh therefore means establishing a ruling in place of an earlier one. The later ruling may simply cancel its predecessor or replace it with another regulation. Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, glossed the phrase as "whatever verse We abrogate". Mujāhid said "whatever verse We erase", and in another narration, related from the companions of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, "We keep the words but change the meaning", which Abū al-ʿĀliyah and Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī also said. As-Suddī said "We erase it", and some took it to mean erasing and lifting the wording itself, as happened with the former verses "the married man and the married woman who commit adultery, stone them", and "if the son of Ādam had two valleys of gold he would seek a third". Ibn Jarīr defined it as repealing the ruling of a verse, making the permitted forbidden or the forbidden permitted. Naskh applies only to commands, prohibitions, permissions and the like, never to narratives. Whether the wording is withdrawn, or the ruling, or both, it is called naskh. The abrogation may be of the verse together with its recitation, or of its ruling only while the recitation continues.

**"or cause it to be forgotten"**. The word is read in two ways. Read nunsiʾhā, from "to postpone", it means that Allah defers a verse, withholding its ruling or its recitation, or keeps it in the Preserved Tablet. ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr, Mujāhid and ʿAṭāʾ said it means "We delay it", that is, we leave it unabrogated, and ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī, as-Suddī and ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas said "We delay repealing it". Ibn ʿAbbās glossed the pair as "whatever verse We repeal or leave standing unchanged", and the companions of Ibn Masʿūd read it as "We keep its wording and change its ruling". Read nunsihā, from "to forget", it means "We make you forget it, erasing it from your heart". Qatādah said that Allah made His Prophet ﷺ forget what He willed and abrogated what He willed. At times Allah did cause the Prophet ﷺ and the Companions to forget a verse entirely, and several instances are related, the purpose usually being to repeal a particular regulation. Between them the two phrases cover every form in which a verse can be abrogated.

**"We replace it with a better or similar one"**. "Better" refers to the benefit of those addressed. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as a ruling more beneficial and easier for you. As-Suddī said a better verse, or one like the one repealed. Qatādah said a verse that is easier, or that permits, commands or forbids. The replacement is more beneficial for Allah's servants either because it is lighter to carry out or because it carries greater reward, or it is the like of the first in obligation and reward. Allah sends Jibrīl (Gabriel) with what is more profitable and easier to act on, or equal in reward, benefit and practice, more beneficial for the moment or for later. If Allah chooses to withdraw the ruling of a verse while keeping the verse in the Qur'an, or to remove a verse from people's memories altogether, there is nothing objectionable in it. He alone knows the wisdom of His choice, He makes such changes for people's good, and He always sends another verse or ruling better than or at least equal to what He withdrew.

**"Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything"**. The question is an affirmation: you know, O Prophet, that Allah has power over all things, including abrogating and substituting verses, over what is abrogated and what is left standing. He does as He wills and rules as He wishes. Being all-powerful and all-knowing, He has the authority to change His commands as He pleases, and He knows what is good for people at any given time and changes His commands accordingly.

### Why abrogation happens

Allah is reminding His servants that He alone owns His creatures and does with them as He wills. His is the supreme authority and all creation is His. His will governs creation and the distribution of happiness and misery, health and sickness, success and failure. He judges His servants and determines what is permitted or forbidden. Nobody can overturn His judgment. He is not questioned about His acts, although His servants are questioned about theirs. Through naskh He tests His servants' obedience to His Messengers. He commands something containing a benefit known to Him, and then in His wisdom forbids it, so that perfect obedience lies in holding to His commands, following His Messengers, believing whatever they bring, carrying out their orders and avoiding what they prohibit.

Making laws and repealing them in favour of new ones is routine in human governments. Human laws are changed sometimes because the lawmakers misjudged the situation when they framed the law and must correct their mistake, and sometimes because a law that suited conditions when it was made no longer suits them after unforeseen change. Neither of these can apply to divine commands. There is a third kind. A lawmaker makes a law knowing full well that circumstances will change so that it will no longer be suitable, and when the change he foresaw arrives he replaces it with the new law he had in mind from the start. A physician knows the patient's present condition requires one medicine. After two days, the anticipated change will require another. The initial prescription and its later replacement are therefore planned together. He could hand the patient written instructions for the whole course with every change marked, but that would overburden an already weak patient and risk harm through error or misunderstanding. This third kind is the only abrogation that can occur, and has occurred, in divine commands and divine Books. Every new Sharīʿah and revealed Book has abrogated many rulings of the earlier one, and within a single Sharīʿah too a law has often been in force for a time and then replaced by divine wisdom with another. Aḥādīth related by Muslim say that there was never a prophethood that did not abrogate some rulings. The principle is not hard to grasp. Only certain Jews, malicious and ignorant, mixed up the divine abrogation of rulings with the two kinds of repeal to which human laws are subject, and these two verses were revealed in reply to their taunts.

### Has abrogation actually occurred?

Some of the Muʿtazilah, apparently wishing to deny the enemies of Islam any occasion for such taunts, tried to explain the whole question away. Abrogation of divine commands is rationally possible, they argued, but it has not actually happened in the Qur'an: no verse abrogates another (nāsikh) and none is abrogated (mansūkh). This view is attributed to Abū Muslim al-Iṣfahānī, and the scholars at large have always rejected it. The people of all revealed laws, it has been said, agree on both the validity of abrogation and its actual occurrence. Only the Jews, apart from their ʿĪsawiyyah sect, denied its possibility, and only Abū Muslim denied its occurrence while granting its possibility. Imām al-Qurṭubī wrote that understanding abrogation is essential, that great benefits flow from it which no scholar can do without, and that none but the ignorant and the dull deny it. He related an illuminating incident. The fourth Caliph (Khalīfah) ʿAlī saw a man preaching in the mosque and asked what he was doing. Told that he was preaching, ʿAlī said that he was doing nothing of the kind, only announcing to the people who he was and whose son he was so that they would know and remember him. He called the man over and asked whether he knew which rulings were abrogated and which had abrogated them. When the man admitted he did not, ʿAlī expelled him from the mosque and forbade him ever to preach there. Numerous sayings of the Companions and their Successors affirm actual abrogation in Qur'anic and ḥadīth rulings. Ibn Jarīr and ad-Durr al-Manthūr gather some with evidence for their soundness. Less strongly attested reports are still more numerous. Hence the consensus of the scholars on naskh, with the exception of Abū Muslim and a few other Muʿtazilah whose position Imām ar-Rāzī exposed in detail.

The word naskh also needs to be used with care. Technically it means changing a ruling and putting another in its place, but the change may be the total repeal of one ruling and its replacement, such as fixing the Kaʿbah as the qiblah instead of the Bayt al-Maqdis, or it may be the retention of a ruling with added conditions and provisos. The scholars of the earliest period used the word in this broad sense, covering total repeal and partial modification alike, which is why they listed some five hundred verses as abrogated. Later scholars counted as naskh only a change that cannot in any way be reconciled with the earlier ruling, which greatly reduces the number. As-Suyūṭī, for instance, counts only twenty such verses. Later still Shāh Walīyullāh, reconciling abrogated rulings with the earlier ones wherever possible, brought the number down to five, the cases where the later ruling cannot be squared with the earlier without far-fetched interpretation. This effort is highly commendable, since the basic presumption behind a ruling is its permanence and abrogation runs against that presumption, so it is improper to assume abrogation in a verse whose ruling can reasonably be shown to be still in force.

The argument rejects a modernist inference from these reduced counts. Reduction does not make abrogation a defect that scholars spent fourteen centuries trying to remove. Shāh Walīyullāh's count of five is not presented as a final discovery on that supposed course. Nor does it imply that later thinkers should bring the number to zero. May Allah forgive the reproduction of such a blasphemy. Such an approach serves neither Islam nor the Qur'an, cannot erase the profound investigations of the Companions, their Successors and the scholars of fourteen hundred years, and will not silence the enemies of Islam. All it does is hand a weapon to present-day detractors and rebels, who would say that what the scholars of the Ummah maintained for fourteen centuries has at last been proved wrong. Open that door and every kind of disorder enters, and every ruling of the Sharīʿah falls under suspicion, with no guarantee that the results of this "modern" research will not themselves prove wrong tomorrow. Certain recent writings revive Abū Muslim's argument through the word mā. They read it as conditional "if" rather than relative "whatever". In their interpretation, "if We abrogate a verse" expresses only a supposition. They compare it with hypothetical sentences introduced by law: "Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted" (21:22). Another example is "If the Most Compassionate ˹really˺ had offspring, I would be the first worshipper" (43:81). On this basis they argue that abrogation is possible but never occurred. Such writers show little intimate knowledge of Arabic grammar, for a condition expressed by mā is very different from the imaginary situation introduced by law. More to the point, it is on the strength of this very verse that the Companions affirmed the occurrence of abrogation and cited many instances, as did their Successors and all the reliable exegetes, and in the face of such unanimity the new interpretation cannot be accepted. Even Shāh Walīyullāh, while reducing the number, never dreamed of denying the fact. All authoritative scholars from the Companions to the present, including without exception the scholars of Deoband, have affirmed both the possibility and the actual occurrence of abrogation. The detailed rules of naskh are too many and too intricate for a commentary and belong to the books of legal theory.

### The allusion of the mystics

The devotional reading hears in the verse a promise to the Prophet ﷺ himself: We never make you advance beyond the station of servanthood without setting you down in the courtyards of freedom, and never remove one of the attributes of mortal nature from you without making you abide through one of the marks that bear witness to the Divinity. It addresses him as the paragon of the horizons, the messenger to jinn and men, the quintessence of predetermination, the luminous full moon, the totality of perfection, the qiblah of prosperity, the one whose branch of union is in bloom and whose star of exaltedness never stops shining, the one whose good fortune has passed beyond the clouds of being. What others wear as crowns, he wears as sandals, and the sandals thrown from his steed's feet became the crowns of sultans. Even the stations beyond which he is advanced, which are the beautiful deeds of all the friends of God, count as ugly deeds for him while he remains in them, and when he passes beyond them he is to ask forgiveness for them. So the Prophet ﷺ, al-Muṣṭafā, said: "My heart becomes clouded, and I ask forgiveness of Allah seventy times a day." Abū Bakr aṣ-Ṣiddīq said, "Would that I could witness the thing for which God's Messenger asked forgiveness!" Others read the verse as a rule of the spiritual life: the servant is never moved out of one state without being given what lies above and beyond it. In the image, a removed trace of worship gives way to a light of servanthood. That light may in turn give way to a moon of servitude. The servant rises through these stages until an attraction from the Real seizes him. One such attraction outweighs all the deeds of jinn and men.

### In short

- The verse answers the Jews' and idolaters' taunt that changing rulings, such as the qiblah, proved the Qur'an was Muḥammad's ﷺ own work. Abrogation never replaces good with bad, since both the abrogated and the abrogating rulings are from Allah and good.
- Naskh means repealing a ruling, with or without its wording, and "cause it to be forgotten" covers deferral and actual forgetting, so that every form of abrogation is included. The replacement is always better for people or equal to the old.
- Divine abrogation is like a physician's planned change of medicine, foreseen from the start, not a correction of error, and every prophethood abrogated some earlier rulings.
- The Companions, their Successors and the scholars unanimously affirm that abrogation has actually occurred. Only Abū Muslim al-Iṣfahānī and a few Muʿtazilah denied it, and recent attempts to revive their view by reading "if" for "whatever" misread the grammar. The count of abrogated verses ranges from five hundred in the broad early sense to twenty (as-Suyūṭī) or five (Shāh Walīyullāh) in the strict sense.
- Allah's power over all things includes the power to replace His commands, and for the mystic each abrogated station gives way to a higher one.

<<<VERSE 2:107>>>
Verse 107 grounds the right to abrogate in the right of ownership. "Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah". The One who owns everything may command His subjects as He sees fit, and those who object will find no one to shield them from the consequences.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah"**. O Prophet, you know that the heavens and the earth and all their stores belong to Allah, and that His rule in them is whatever He wills. He commands His servants with whatever He wills because He knows best what is good for them, instructs and forbids as He pleases, establishes the sacred law as He wishes and abolishes what He wishes. Imām Abū Jaʿfar ibn Jarīr paraphrased: "Do you not know, Muḥammad, that I alone own the heavens and the earth and decide in them whatever I will? I forbid what I will, change and repeal whichever of My earlier rulings I will, whenever I will, and I keep in force whatever I will." He added that although Allah addressed this statement of His greatness to His Prophet ﷺ, He was also giving the lie to the Jews, who denied that any ruling of the Torah could be abrogated. The Jews likewise denied the prophethood of ʿĪsā (Jesus) and of Muḥammad ﷺ because they disliked what they brought from Allah, including the change of certain rulings of the Torah by His command. Allah therefore declares His ownership of the heavens and earth and all authority within them. His subjects are His creatures, required to obey His commands and prohibitions. He can establish, repeal or retain any ruling as He wills.

**"and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah"**. Addressed to the Jews, this means that apart from Allah they have no one close enough to benefit or protect them from His torment and no one able to prevent their punishment. For believers, Allah alone is the protector who cares for them. No other helper can keep harm away. He is the Walī, who guards and protects everyone, and His power encompasses everything. As friend, Allah keeps people's good in view when He lays down commands. As helper, He protects the obedient against their enemies. Their blessings in the next world outweigh the harm suffered here. Their enemies' apparent dominance therefore cannot determine their ultimate condition.

### Abrogation in the earlier scriptures

The Jews' refusal to admit abrogation is nothing but unbelief and rebellion. Sound reason does not deny that Allah's commands can be abrogated, since He decrees what He wills just as He does what He wills. And abrogation did occur in the earlier Books and laws. Allah permitted Ādam to marry his daughters to his sons and later forbade it. After the Ark came to rest He permitted Nūḥ (Noah) to eat every kind of animal and later prohibited some foods. Marrying two sisters at once was permitted to Isrāʾīl and his children and later forbidden in the Torah. Allah commanded Ibrāhīm (Abraham) to sacrifice his son and withdrew the command before it was carried out. He commanded the Children of Israel to kill those who had worshipped the calf and then lifted the command so that they would not all be wiped out. There are many other examples which the Jews themselves admit yet ignore. It is well known, too, that their Books foretold Muḥammad ﷺ and commanded them to follow him, so that by their own scriptures they were bound to follow him and no good deed of theirs would be accepted unless it conformed to his Law. He brought another Book, the Qur'an, the last revelation from Allah. These two verses therefore refute the denial of abrogation and expose the unbelief behind it. The discussion rejects the claim that sound reason makes abrogation impossible. Some also alleged that divine texts excluded it. The argument treats these objections as ignorance and arrogance.

### From kingdom to essence

The devotional reading finds in the verse the stages of the spiritual journey. God's way with His friends is to draw them from witnessing what He possesses (milk) to beholding His kingdom (mulk), and from there to witnessing His truth (ḥaqq). Next they are moved on from seeing His signs (āyāt) to seeing His attributes (ṣifāt), and at last from the attributes to the witnessing of the essence (dhāt) itself.

### In short

- Because the whole kingdom of the heavens and the earth is Allah's, He commands, forbids, repeals and retains as He wills, and His subjects have only to hear and obey.
- Ibn Jarīr: the statement, though addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, rebuts the Jews who denied that the Torah's rulings could be abrogated and rejected ʿĪsā and Muḥammad ﷺ for changing them.
- The earlier scriptures themselves record abrogations, from the marriages of Ādam's children and the food of Nūḥ to the sacrifice of Ibrāhīm's son and the killing of the calf-worshippers.
- Apart from Allah no one can protect from His punishment or help against His enemies, so the believer's only guardian is Allah Himself.

<<<VERSE 2:108>>>
Verse 108 warns the believers against a habit that had ruined earlier communities. "Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger"**. Allah forbids the believers to pester the Prophet ﷺ with questions about matters that have not yet arisen, as He says elsewhere: "O believers! Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you. But if you inquire about what is being revealed in the Quran, it will be made clear to you" (5:101). Ask about a matter once it has been revealed and it will be explained, but do not ask about what has not happened, for it may become forbidden on account of your asking. The Ṣaḥīḥ records that the greatest offender among the Muslims is the one who asks about something not forbidden and it becomes forbidden because of his question. That is why the Messenger of Allah ﷺ disliked the question put to him about a husband who finds another man with his wife, who would be making public a grave matter if he spoke and staying silent about a grave matter if he did not, until Allah later revealed the ruling of liʿān, the mutual oaths of the spouses (24:6-9). The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ forbade hearsay, "it was said" and "he said", the wasting of wealth, and asking too many questions. Muslim records that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Leave me alone so long as I leave you alone. Those before you were destroyed only by their excessive questioning and their disputing with their prophets. So when I command you something, do as much of it as you can, and when I forbid you something, keep away from it." He said this after telling the Companions that Allah had commanded them to perform ḥajj. A man asked, "Every year, Messenger of Allah?" The Prophet ﷺ did not answer, and the man repeated the question three times, until the Prophet ﷺ said: "No. Had I said yes it would have become obligatory, and you would not have been able to do it." Anas ibn Mālik said that they were forbidden to question the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and so were delighted whenever a bedouin came and asked him while they listened.

Not all the questions in view were sincere. Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq related from Ibn ʿAbbās that Rāfiʿ ibn Ḥuraymilah or Wahb ibn Zayd said: "Muḥammad, bring us a Book sent down from heaven that we can read, and make rivers flow for us, and then we will follow you and believe in you." Allah sent down this verse in answer. Ibn ʿAbbās also said that it was revealed about ʿAbdullāh ibn Abī Umayyah and a group of the Quraysh who said: "Muḥammad, turn aṣ-Ṣafā into gold, widen the land of Makkah for us and make rivers gush out all through it, and if you do, we will believe in you." The Makkans asked him to enlarge their city and make aṣ-Ṣafā solid gold. Other reports add that the Jews and the idolaters put all sorts of demands to him. Some said, "Bring us a whole Book from heaven as Mūsā (Moses) brought the Torah." ʿAbdullāh ibn Umayyah al-Makhzūmī said, "Bring us a Book from heaven that reads: from the Lord of the Worlds to Ibn Abī Umayyah, know that I have sent Muḥammad to mankind." Others said, "We will not believe in you until you bring the angels as guarantors." Hostility to the Prophet ﷺ had become so habitual to the Jews that they were forever making insolent demands, once asking him to produce the whole Qur'an at a single stroke, as the Torah had been revealed.

**"as Moses was asked before?"**. Before Muḥammad's ﷺ time the Children of Israel asked Mūsā to let them see their Lord, to hear His speech, and other things of that kind. The Qur'an records in Sūrat an-Nisāʾ that they said, "Show us Allah openly." Allah here censures those who question the Messenger of Allah ﷺ merely to be difficult, just as the Israelites questioned Mūsā out of stubbornness, rejection and rebellion. In such cases the Jews' intention was never to seek guidance, resolve doubts or strengthen faith, but only to cast aspersions on a prophet or call Allah's wisdom into question. Such demands are improper because there is a reason for everything Allah does, but divine wisdom alone knows it. The creature has no right to dictate the precise form his Creator's acts should take. He should not even ask why and wherefore, but accept and submit to the Divine Will. Read as addressed to the Muslims, the verse warns them against making improper demands of the Prophet ﷺ, and against resisting and quarrelling with the revelation as the people of Mūsā did with their prophet.

**"But whoever trades belief for disbelief"**. Whoever prefers disbelief to faith, taking the one in place of the other by declining to reflect on the clear proofs already given and demanding others instead. The verse brands this behaviour as kufr.

**"has truly strayed from the Right Way"**. He has left the straight path for the path of ignorance and misguidance and relinquished the way of guidance. The word for "right" (sawāʾ) basically means the middle, so he has lost the balanced, even road. This is the state of those who turned away from accepting and obeying the prophets and kept putting needless questions to them in defiance and disbelief, of whom Allah said: "Have you not seen those ˹disbelievers˺ who meet Allah’s favours with ingratitude and lead their own people to their doom? In Hell they will burn. What an evil place for settlement" (14:28-29). Abū al-ʿĀliyah summed it up: they exchanged comfort for hardship.

### Awe in the Messenger's presence

The Children of Israel wearied Mūsā, and the Muslims were forbidden to follow their precedent. They were commanded to be mindful of the Messenger's ﷺ modesty as far as they could, so that in his presence they kept an awed silence, as Allah said of him: "support and honour him" (48:9). Beauty of conduct (adab) in outward dealings is the pattern for beauty of conduct with God within. Needless questioning is thus not a small matter of manners. Those before us perished by it, rulings were made harder by it, and the verse places it on the road that leads from belief to disbelief.

### In short

- Believers must not press the Prophet ﷺ with needless questions or demands for signs, as the Israelites pressed Mūsā to show them Allah openly.
- The occasion was the demands of Rāfiʿ ibn Ḥuraymilah or Wahb ibn Zayd and of ʿAbdullāh ibn Abī Umayyah and the Quraysh for a Book from heaven, rivers, and aṣ-Ṣafā turned to gold.
- Excessive questioning destroyed earlier nations and can turn a permitted thing into a forbidden one, so the Prophet ﷺ said, "leave me alone so long as I leave you alone", and the Companions welcomed the bedouin who asked on their behalf (5:101).
- To demand new proofs instead of reflecting on those given is to trade faith for disbelief and to lose the middle road (14:28-29), and reverent silence before the Messenger ﷺ (48:9) is the model of adab with God.
