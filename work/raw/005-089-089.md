<<<VERSE 5:89>>>
The subject moves from declaring halal, that is lawful, things unlawful to the words used to do it: "Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths, but He will hold you accountable for deliberate oaths." Statements making lawful things forbidden are sometimes sworn, so the ruling on oaths follows.

The verse distinguishes oaths that carry no consequence from oaths that do, and then sets out the expiation in detail. It closes by telling believers to guard their oaths and by describing this clarification as a reason for gratitude.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths"** concerns laghw, what is not counted. It is explained as a word of oath slipping from the tongue without intention, such as saying no by Allah or yes by Allah, and as swearing about something assumed true that turns out to be false.

**"but He will hold you accountable for deliberate oaths."** concerns oaths intended in the heart. The accountability described here is worldly, taking the form of expiation, since the wording is set against the oaths a person has bound himself with.

**"The penalty for a broken oath is to feed ten poor people from what you normally feed your own family"** names the first option. The poor meant are those in need, unable to find the necessities of life. The measure is discussed below.

**"or to clothe them, or to free a bondsperson."** adds two further options. All three are expiations for a deliberate oath, and whichever a person chooses suffices by consensus.

**"But if none of this is affordable, then you must fast three days."** provides for the person who cannot bear the cost. He lacks the means to feed ten poor people, clothe them or free a slave, so his expiation is fasting.

**"This is the penalty for breaking your oaths."** identifies this as the legal way to atone for a deliberate oath that has been broken.

**"So be mindful of your oaths."** is explained in three ways, given below.

**"This is how Allah makes things clear to you, so perhaps you will be grateful"** describes Allah expounding His commands and prohibitions as He has expounded the expiation of oaths, so that people may be grateful for the clarification. Another explanation describes the laws as making clear what is lawful and what is unlawful, so that people may thank Allah for teaching them what they did not know.

### What are the three kinds of oath?

The first is yamin ghamus, the disastrous oath. It is swearing falsely about a past event with knowledge, as when a person did something, knows he did it, and then swears that he did not. Jurists call it a grave major sin whose curse falls in this life and the next.

No kaffarah, expiation, is required for it. Taubah, repentance, and istighfar, seeking Allah's forgiveness, are what it requires. Ghamus literally means that which drowns, and this oath drowns a person in sin and its curse, a disaster for his present and future life.

The second is yamin laghw, the ineffectual oath. A person declares on oath that a past event was true on the basis of his knowledge, while it was in fact false. He may have been told that someone had arrived and sworn to it in trust, only to learn that the opposite was the case.

The same category covers an oath word such as by, or I swear, said without intention. This kind of oath brings neither expiation nor sin.

The third is al-yamin al-mun'aqidah, the binding oath, in which a person swears that he will or will not do something in the future. If it is broken, expiation becomes necessary. In some situations breaking it also brings sin, and in others it does not.

### How do the two verses on oaths relate?

In Al-Baqarah the statement is that Allah does not hold people accountable for what is not countable among their oaths, but holds them accountable for what their hearts have designed (2:225). There, laghw means an oath slipping out without intention or volition, or an oath about something assumed true that turns out false.

Set against it is the oath containing an intentional lie, which is yamin ghamus. So laghw brings no sin, while the sin belongs to the intentional lie.

The sin mentioned in Al-Baqarah is therefore the sin incurred in the Hereafter for disobeying a divine injunction. The ruling in this verse concerns the present life, where expiation is required. Allah does not oblige amends for what is not countable, and He does oblige expiation for a binding oath about the future that is then broken.

The subject of unintentional oaths was treated in Al-Baqarah and is not repeated here at length.

### What measure of food is required?

The wording speaks of the awsat, the middle or average, of what people feed their families. Ibn 'Abbas, Sa'id bin Jubayr and 'Ikrimah explain it as the average scale of family food. 'Ata' Al-Khurasani reads it as the best of what people feed their families.

One explanation gives it as bread and the seasoning served at lunch and dinner. Another describes the midmost food a family consumes, neither better nor worse, at one mudd measure for each needy person. A third gives the average food of a person's city at half a sa' for each poor person.

The legal discussion develops two ways of discharging this duty, since the word used can mean either feeding someone or giving him food. The person paying expiation may choose either.

He may invite ten poor people and feed them. Then he must satisfy ten poor people with both of the day's meals, giving them to their fill from the middling food he and his family normally eat at home. One account describes this as two average meals for ten poor persons.

Or he may place the food in their ownership. In that case he gives each of the ten one fitrah amount per person, which is one and three quarter kilograms of wheat, or its value in cash. Fasting suffices only when a person cannot take any of the three options.

### What clothing is required?

Each of the ten is to be clothed with something suitable to pray in, whether the poor person is male or female. Al-'Awfi reports Ibn 'Abbas giving a robe or garment for each of the ten.

Mujahid says the least of clothing intended is a garment, and the most is whatever a person wishes. Abu Malik, Hammad bin Abi Sulayman, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i, Tawus, 'Ata', Abu Ja'far Al-Baqir and Al-Hasan give a garment for each.

Other explanations describe clothing that covers nakedness, such as a shawl, a gown or a loin cloth, and covering described as what genuinely counts as clothing, for instance a shirt, a turban or a waist wrap. One account specifies covering sufficient to clothe them properly, meaning a wrap for the lower body together with one for the upper. Another describes what society counts as a suit of clothing.

Al-Shafi'i is cited for the position that it is not sufficient to give all these items to a single needy person.

### Which slave is to be freed?

The freeing is explained as freeing a believing slave. A lengthy hadith appears in Malik's Muwatta', in the Sahih of Muslim and in the Musnad of Ash-Shafi'i. It reports that 'Umar bin Al-Hakam As-Sulami had to free a slave as atonement and brought a black slave girl before the Messenger ﷺ.

He asked her where Allah is, and she answered that He is above the heavens. He asked who he was, and she answered that he is the Messenger of Allah. He then told 'Umar to free her, because she is a believer.

One gloss restricts the general wording in the same way as in the expiation for killing or for repudiation through zihar, so that the slave freed must be a believer.

### Must the options be followed in order?

No. Allah mentioned the easiest option first and the harder ones after it, since feeding is easier than giving clothing and clothing is easier than freeing a slave. That ordering does not oblige a person to attempt them in sequence.

The position that following the order is not obligatory is attributed to al-Shāfi'ī, and the wider statement is that whichever option a person chooses suffices by consensus. The same jurist is also cited in the ruling on clothing given above.

### Must the three fasts be consecutive?

Ubayy bin Ka'b, Ibn Mas'ud and his students are reported as reading the verse with the addition of three consecutive days. Even if that wording is not transmitted as part of the Qur'an through mass narration, it stands as an explanation by Companions and carries the ruling of something related from the Prophet ﷺ.

On that basis Abu Hanifah and some other imams hold that the three fasts of the expiation of an oath must be consecutive. Another gloss likewise gives a three day fast consecutively.

### Can expiation be paid before the oath is broken?

No. Abu Hanifah and other imams explain the closing words as meaning that when a sworn statement about the future is violated, its expiation is what has been described. The expiation must be given after the oath is broken.

The reason is that the cause making expiation necessary lies in the breaking. As long as the oath is unbroken, no expiation is required. Just as there is no salah before its time and no Ramadan fast before the month of Ramadan, so the expiation of an oath cannot be given before the oath breaks.

One account puts the same point positively. If breaking the oath is the better course, a person should break it and then give the atonement.

### What does guarding one's oaths mean?

Three explanations are given. Ibn Jarir explains it as not leaving broken oaths without paying their expiation.

Another says that if a person has sworn to something, he should not break his oath without a legal or physical necessity. A third, given in Mazhari, says a person should not hasten to make a sworn statement at all. He should guard his oath as though keeping it in reserve and should not swear casually except under severe compulsion.

One gloss adds that oaths should not be broken unless it is for a righteous deed or to set right between people, as stated in the verse of Surat al-Baqara (2:225). Another warns against oaths taken falsely in Allah's name as well as against breaking them.

### What is the real renunciation this passage points to?

The discussion connects the verse with what preceded it. Real monasticism in Islam consists of the fear of Allah and gratitude to Him.

Fear of Allah makes a person abstain from what He has forbidden. When desire awakens for something haram, forbidden, that fear restrains him from satisfying it. When anger rises to the point of wanting to kill, it stays his hand. His heart may urge an unrestricted life, but fear of Allah's wrath keeps him inside the prescribed limits.

Gratitude works the same way from the other side. When a grateful person receives health, wealth, position, goods and a share of popularity, they do not lead him into self-importance or boasting. He takes everything as Allah's gift and concedes that all he enjoys is by grace, and he is overwhelmed by gratitude and humility.

These are the emotions that join a person to Allah. Through fearing Him and being grateful he comes close to Him. Keeping away from material things is certainly a virtue, but the distancing has to be a mental process and not merely a physical one.

### What does the inward reading make of oaths?

It observes that the chevaliers of the Tariqah, when ecstasy overpowers them, will at times take oaths that renew the covenant and confirm the compact. Their words take a form like this: by what is due to You, I shall look at nothing besides You, speak to nothing besides You, and never abandon Your covenant.

Measured against tawhid, the affirmation of Allah's oneness, oaths of that kind count as idle talk. They show negligence in the witnessing of unity. What standing has a servant to assign himself value, to imagine that he is somebody, or to treat his own words as weighty enough for swearing an oath to his Lord?

The servant's part is to receive Allah's rulings with a fine approval. If Allah calls him near or pushes him away, he neither objects nor withdraws. Talk of the realities of joining and parting is not for him. He takes whatever is given and accepts whatever arrives. In truth Allah is the One whose kindness reaches perfection, and He is the One who decides and disposes in every condition.

The Pir of the Tariqah is quoted praying to the One closer to us than ourselves and more lovingly kind to us than ourselves, who caresses us without us by His generosity rather than because of our worthiness or our works. The load exceeds what we can carry, the practice is not ours to perform and the favour lies past our power. Our own doing has only harmed us, while what You have done endures for us. Whatever You have done standing in our place, You did by Yourself and not on our account.

People of knowledge are familiar with expiation in the Shariah as the freeing of slaves and as feeding and clothing the poor. In the language of allusion the path has its own threefold expiation. The spirit is spent in ecstasy, the heart in sincerity of intention, and the soul in unceasing effort.

Whoever cannot do that should abstain and fast from what is forbidden and blameworthy. The inward reading therefore mirrors the three options and the fasting that replaces them, without cancelling the outward ruling.

### In short

- Oaths are of three kinds, and only the binding oath about the future requires expiation when broken.
- The expiation is feeding, clothing or freeing a believing slave, in any order, with three fasts for whoever cannot afford them.
- Measures differ in the explanations, from two filling meals to stated amounts of wheat, and the fasts are held by some to be consecutive.
- Expiation cannot be paid before the oath is broken, and guarding oaths includes not swearing casually and not leaving a broken oath unexpiated.
- True renunciation is fear of Allah and gratitude to Him, an inward distancing rather than a merely physical one.
