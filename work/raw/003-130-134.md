<<<VERSE 3:130>>>
> “O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper” (3:130)

This verse forbids riba, interest or usury, and calls believers to taqwa. The warning against multiplying a debt describes a familiar abusive practice. It does not mean that a smaller rate of interest is permissible.

### Phrase by phrase

**“O believers!”** addresses those who have accepted faith and are accountable for their dealings. Faith is not only prayer and private devotion. It must guide how people borrow, lend, trade, and treat those who are in need.

**“Do not consume interest”** prohibits taking an increase on a loan because of the passage of time. In pre-Islamic practice, a creditor might demand payment at the agreed date and, if the debtor could not pay, extend the term in exchange for a larger debt. The amount could keep growing until the original loan was multiplied. The verse confronts this exploitation of a person’s hardship.

The phrase **“multiplying it many times over”** describes how such a debt could compound. It is not a threshold that makes only exorbitant interest forbidden. The prohibition is not restricted to interest that has doubled or compounded repeatedly. A person should not infer that a smaller percentage is lawful merely because it has not yet multiplied.

The prohibition also does not condemn every form of earning or trade. A fair exchange is different from demanding an increase on a debt simply because a borrower needs more time. The essential concern here is the unjust gain secured through a creditor’s power over a debtor.

**“And be mindful of Allah”** calls believers to keep His limits in view when money is involved. Taqwa means resisting a profitable transaction when it depends on another person’s vulnerability. The phrase addresses both the lender who takes the increase and the community that allows such injustice to become ordinary.

**“so you may prosper”** redefines success. It is not merely a growing balance or a larger return. Prosperity includes a livelihood gained lawfully, a just society, and a good end with Allah. Elsewhere the Qur’an encourages giving wealth in charity (2:274), and promises forgiveness and reward to those who repent and do good (3:135-136). Surah al-Baqarah also treats the prohibition of riba in detail. Wealth is a trust to use responsibly, not a license to profit from distress.

### In short

- Riba is forbidden, including interest that has not multiplied.
- The verse confronts debt practices that exploit a borrower’s hardship.
- True prosperity is joined to taqwa and just dealings.

<<<VERSE 3:131>>>
> “Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers” (3:131)

This verse warns believers of the Fire and urges them to avoid the wrong that leads to it. It follows the prohibition of riba with a serious reminder that financial injustice is not a minor matter before Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Guard yourselves”** means take the means of protection: obey Allah, abandon what He forbids, and repent when you have done wrong. The warning does not call believers to despair. The next verse directs them to obedience, and the following verses describe forgiveness, Paradise, generosity, and good character.

The context is the prohibition of riba. A person must not use wealth or contracts to harm another and then imagine that outward worship cancels the rights of the people. Taqwa includes both devotion to Allah and justice in dealings. If someone has taken what is not rightfully theirs, repentance includes ending the wrong and restoring the other person’s due where possible.

**“against the Fire”** names the consequence of persistent rejection and wrongdoing. The Arabic word Jahannam is used for Hell. The warning is meant to awaken responsibility, not to make people pronounce that a particular individual is condemned. Allah knows people’s intentions, their repentance, and the final state in which they die.

**“prepared for the disbelievers”** reminds the believers of the grave end of rejecting Allah. It is not a declaration that every sinner is beyond mercy. The Qur’an repeatedly calls people back to repentance, and the nearby passage promises forgiveness to those who remember Allah and seek pardon (3:135-136). Surah al-Baqarah also discusses the prohibition of riba. The proper response to a warning is to change one’s conduct and hope in Allah’s mercy.

The Qur’an also invites people to race toward forgiveness and Paradise (57:21). That invitation balances fear with hope. Believers should neither feel secure while persisting in injustice nor despair when they have sinned and can still turn back to Allah.

### In short

- The warning calls believers to protect themselves through obedience and repentance.
- Riba and other injustices carry serious moral consequences.
- The verse warns without authorizing anyone to declare another person’s final fate.

<<<VERSE 3:132>>>
> “Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy” (3:132)

The verse joins obedience to Allah with obedience to His Messenger ﷺ. The path to mercy is not a claim of faith alone, but a life shaped by revelation, the Prophet’s teaching, and care for others.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Obey Allah”** means to follow what Allah has commanded and avoid what He has forbidden. This includes the worship and ethical duties that the Qur’an names, as well as the detailed guidance explained by the Messenger. Obedience is not merely repeating words. It is letting divine guidance govern conduct, including financial dealings, family life, worship, and the treatment of neighbors.

The passage has just prohibited riba. The command therefore asks believers to put that prohibition into practice rather than seek a loophole that keeps an unjust increase under another name. The Qur’an also forbids consuming one another’s property by false means (2:188). The principle applies broadly to dealings that deceive, exploit, or deprive people of what is rightfully theirs.

**“and the Messenger”** means Muhammad ﷺ, whom Allah sent to recite the revelation, teach its meaning, purify the community, and model its application. The Qur’an says that whoever obeys the Messenger obeys Allah (4:80). It also says that the Messenger does not speak from personal desire in delivering the revelation (53:3), and commands believers to take what he gives them and refrain from what he forbids (59:7). His teaching is not a rival to the Qur’an. It conveys how the revelation is to be lived.

The Qur’an sometimes gives a command in concise terms and the Messenger demonstrates its form. It commands salah, for example, and refers to standing and bowing, but the complete sequence and practice are learned from the Prophet. The same is true of zakah: the obligation is stated, while the Messenger’s teaching explains how it is applied to different kinds of wealth. The companions learned by observing him and passing on his instruction to the Ummah.

The Messenger also clarified the Qur’an’s guidance for daily life. The verse about not consuming one another’s property falsely (2:188) establishes a principle, while Prophetic teaching helps the community distinguish fair transactions from deception and harm. This is part of understanding Islamic law: the Qur’an remains the foundation, and the Sunnah explains and exemplifies its direction. The Messenger’s responsibility includes teaching wisdom and clarifying revelation (16:44).

A report transmitted by At-Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud, Ibn Majah, and Ahmad warns of people who would reject a Prophetic command simply because they do not find its details written word for word in the Qur’an. Al-Bayhaqi also records this teaching. The report reinforces the verse’s central point: obedience to the Messenger is part of obedience to Allah. It does not mean that every statement attributed to the Prophet is accepted without examination. Hadith reports are assessed according to their chains and reliability before they are used as evidence.

The Qur’an says, “obey Allah and the Messenger,” and repeatedly joins both commands. Surah Al-Nisa states that whoever obeys the Messenger obeys Allah (4:80). Faith in Allah’s oneness is not separated from following the Messenger who conveyed His guidance. Taqwa, awareness of Allah, is therefore shown by how believers follow revelation, not only by what they say about belief. In salah the worshipper stands and performs ruku, bowing, as part of the practice taught by the Prophet.

**“so you may be shown mercy”** tells believers that obedience is a means by which they seek Allah’s mercy. They do not earn mercy as a wage or control Allah by their actions. They follow His guidance, ask for forgiveness when they fall short, and hope in His generosity. Obedience is also not blind loyalty to every person claiming authority. The verse names Allah and His Messenger, whose guidance is the measure by which other claims are judged.

### The connection between law and mercy

Some may imagine that rules restrict freedom and that mercy means having no rules. This verse joins them: obedience is a way to receive mercy. The prohibition of riba protects people from a debt that grows by exploiting hardship. The duties of salah and zakah cultivate remembrance of Allah and care for the community. A rule is not merely a limit. It can preserve people from harm and make justice possible.

The verse also rejects the separation of inner faith from outward conduct. Iman includes trust in Allah, but it also includes taking the Messenger’s teaching seriously. The Messenger taught how to pray, pay zakah, and treat others. His example gives the community a practical way to live by the Qur’an rather than reducing religion to personal interpretation.

This does not remove the responsibility to think carefully. Scholars study the Qur’an, the hadith, language, and context to understand a ruling. They may differ in application, but disagreement should be handled with humility and sound evidence. A person should not use the words “obedience” or “tradition” to justify harm, nor dismiss authentic Prophetic guidance because it is unfamiliar.

The promise of mercy is also hopeful for those who have failed. A believer who has taken part in riba or another wrong can stop, seek forgiveness, and repair harm. The Qur’an later says that those who wrong themselves should remember Allah and ask for forgiveness (3:135), and that whoever does evil then seeks Allah’s forgiveness will find Him forgiving and merciful (4:110). Obedience is a continuing return, not a claim that a person never errs.

### In short

- Obedience to the Messenger is part of obedience to Allah.
- The Prophet’s teaching explains how Qur’anic commands are lived in worship and daily conduct.
- Following guidance and repenting from failure are means of seeking Allah’s mercy.

<<<VERSE 3:133>>>
> “And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺” (3:133)

The verse urges believers to hurry toward the means of forgiveness and to compete in good deeds, with Paradise as their hope. Its vastness conveys the generosity of Allah’s promise, while its preparation for the mindful calls for a life of taqwa.

### Phrase by phrase

**“And hasten”** means do not delay repentance or good action. The command is not to race others in boasting, wealth, or status. It is to use the time one has to obey Allah, help people, and repair what has been damaged. The Qur’an elsewhere calls people to compete in good deeds (2:148, 5:48) and to seek what is lasting rather than temporary display.

The early explanations of the word “hasten” differ in emphasis but point toward the same broad response. Ali understood it as fulfilling obligations, Ibn Abbas as Islam, and Abu al-Aliyah as Hijrah. Anas ibn Malik was reported to connect it with the first call of Allahu akbar in salah. Saʿid ibn Jubayr emphasized complete obedience, Dahhak mentioned jihad, and ʿIkrimah repentance. These are not rival definitions that narrow the verse to a single act. They show that the believer hastens toward Allah through many forms of sincere obedience.

**“towards forgiveness from your Lord”** points to repentance and good deeds that Allah accepts. Forgiveness is mentioned before Paradise, reminding believers that entry into the Garden is by Allah’s mercy. Deeds matter, but they are not a price that obliges Allah to reward someone. Even the ability to do good is a favor from Him. A believer acts sincerely and asks Allah to cover faults and accept the effort.

**“and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth”** gives an image of extraordinary spaciousness. Human beings cannot measure the unseen by worldly geography. The comparison communicates that Paradise is vast beyond ordinary human conception. Another verse describes its couches lined with brocade (55:54), and the Qur’an again urges people to race toward forgiveness and a Garden of such breadth (57:21).

A hadith reported by Al-Bukhari describes Al-Firdaws as the highest and best part of Paradise, with rivers originating from it and the Throne above it. The Prophet ﷺ taught believers to ask Allah for Al-Firdaws. This does not turn Paradise into a prize obtained through calculation. It teaches believers to hope boldly in Allah’s bounty.

A man once asked Abu Hurayrah about the Fire in relation to Paradise being as wide as the heavens and earth. The report recorded by Al-Bazzar gives the Prophet’s analogy of night and day: when night covers one place, day still exists elsewhere. The unseen is not limited to what a person sees from one position. The report invites humility about matters beyond human perception.

**“prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”** means Paradise is promised to the muttaqin, those who cultivate taqwa. Taqwa is not perfection without mistakes. The verses that follow describe people who spend generously, restrain anger, forgive others, and ask Allah to forgive them when they sin. A God-conscious person returns to Allah and tries to embody these qualities.

### What does it mean to compete?

Competition in good is compatible with humility. Believers may encourage one another to give, pray, learn, and serve, but they do not envy qualities outside their control or treat another person’s success as their own loss. The Qur’an cautions against coveting what Allah has given to others, while praising effort in deeds that a person can choose (4:32). It speaks of aspiring to what is excellent (83:26).

Forgiveness is named before Paradise because no amount of human effort can make Allah’s grace unnecessary. The Prophet ﷺ taught people to act uprightly, take a balanced course, and hope for good news from Allah. Deeds are the path of obedience, but mercy is the gift by which a servant reaches the Garden. This keeps the believer from both laziness and self-admiration.

The phrase **“as vast as the heavens and the earth”** is also a reassurance that Allah’s reward is not cramped by competition. There is room in His generosity for those who turn to Him sincerely. A believer can wish for Al-Firdaws without resenting another person’s good fortune. Their task is to hasten toward what pleases Allah and leave the reward to Him.

### Different forms of hastening toward Allah

The explanations from the early community describe a range of good actions. Ali spoke of fulfilling obligations, Ibn Abbas of Islam, Abu al-Aliyah of Hijrah, and Anas ibn Malik of al-Takbir al-Uula, the first declaration of Allahu akbar in salah. Saʿid ibn Jubayr emphasized complete obedience, Dahhak mentioned jihad, and ʿIkrimah repentance. The list brings together worship, migration for faith, struggle in the path of Allah, and turning back after sin. It does not make one deed a substitute for the others.

A spiritual reflection distinguishes several ways people hasten. The ʿābidūn, worshippers, move forward through acts of obedience. The ʿārifūn, those seeking deeper recognition of Allah, move through sincere aspiration and drawing near. Those who have disobeyed, the ʿāṣūn, can hasten through remorse and repentance. Each person begins from a different state, but each is called to turn toward Allah rather than delay.

The verse says to seek forgiveness before Paradise. This order teaches that the Garden is reached through Allah’s grace, not bought by a person’s deeds. A hadith reported by Al-Bukhari and Muslim teaches that no one enters Paradise solely because of deeds, even the Prophet, unless Allah covers the person with mercy. A report discussed in Al-Targhib wa al-Tarhib carries the same reminder: strive, take a balanced course, and hope in Allah’s favor. Effort remains necessary, but it never makes the servant independent of the Lord.

When He describes Paradise as wide as the heavens and earth, the Qur’an uses the greatest scale people can readily imagine. Some commentators also consider whether the Arabic word for “width” may carry the sense of value or price. Abu Muslim is reported to have said that if the heavens and earth were a price, they would still indicate Paradise’s incomparable worth. Al-Itazi discusses this interpretation in al-Tafsir al-Kabir. The more direct reading remains that Paradise’s expanse is beyond ordinary measure.

The dimensions of Paradise are not a physical map by which people can locate it. The believers accept the promise while recognizing the limits of human knowledge. Discussion of Allah’s ṣifāt, His attributes, should likewise avoid measuring the Creator by the dimensions of created things. When He mentions forgiveness, the Qur’an does not describe it as having a width or a price. Allah’s mercy is not a created object that people can measure.

The Prophet’s instruction to ask for Al-Firdaws shows the balance between hope and effort. The believer asks Allah for the highest reward, acts with sincerity, and does not become arrogant about their deeds. The call to hasten is practical: repent today, fulfil obligations, and choose what is good while life remains.

### In short

- Hasten toward repentance, obligations, and other good deeds.
- Paradise is beyond human measurement and is sought through Allah’s mercy.
- Taqwa combines hope in Allah with sincere effort and good conduct.

<<<VERSE 3:134>>>
> “˹They are˺ those who donate in prosperity and adversity, control their anger, and pardon others. And Allah loves the good-doers” (3:134)

This verse describes several qualities of the muttaqin: they give in both ease and hardship, restrain anger, and forgive people. These are not isolated gestures. Together they show a person who benefits others, refuses to let anger govern them, and seeks the higher standard of ihsan, excellence.

### Phrase by phrase

**“those who donate in prosperity and adversity”** means that giving is not reserved for people with surplus wealth. The believer gives according to ability in times of plenty and times of difficulty. A wealthy person may give a substantial amount, while a poor person may give a little, share food, offer time, or help through useful effort. The value lies in sincerity and what the person can reasonably give, not in comparing one gift with another.

The Qur’an also describes believers giving by night and day, privately and publicly (2:274). It does not require giving to be a performance. Sometimes a public gift encourages others, while a private one protects sincerity or the recipient’s dignity. In either case, the giver remembers that provision comes from Allah and that wealth is a trust.

Prosperity can distract a person through comfort and the desire to keep accumulating. Adversity can make a person fear that any giving will leave them with too little. The verse describes people who remember Allah in both states. They do not use poverty as an excuse to feel worthless, nor abundance as a reason to become careless. Their generosity is proportionate to their means. They do not neglect dependants, incur harmful debt, or give in order to control the recipient.

A report from Aʾishah describes giving even a single grape when that was what she had available. The Prophet ﷺ taught believers to protect themselves from the Fire even by giving half a date in charity, and not to dismiss a person who asks, even if the response is small. The point is that a gift need not be impressive to be sincere. A kind word, a portion of food, a ride, a skill, or a little time may be meaningful when offered for Allah’s sake.

Spending in Allah’s way can include effort and service, not money alone. A person may use their knowledge, labor, time, or influence to benefit others. Such service should be lawful and genuinely helpful. It should not be used to make the recipient feel indebted or to claim superiority over them. The giver is not the owner of another person’s gratitude.

**“control their anger”** means they restrain the impulse to retaliate or speak abusively when provoked. Anger may arise naturally. The verse praises a person who does not let that feeling dictate unjust conduct. Control can mean pausing, remaining silent, leaving a harmful situation, or responding firmly without cruelty. It does not require someone to accept ongoing abuse or abandon a lawful way of seeking protection.

The Prophet ﷺ explained that the truly strong person is not merely the one who can overpower others physically, but the one who controls himself when angry. Al-Bukhari and Muslim report this teaching. The strength described is inward discipline: refusing to let a moment of rage cause lasting harm.

A hadith from Ibn Abbas describes the virtue of giving a debtor time or forgiving the debt, and relates this to protection from the heat of Jahannam. Ahmad recorded the report. Another report from Muʿadh bin Anas says that a person who suppresses anger while able to act will be called before creation and given a choice of reward. Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah transmit it, and At-Tirmidhi graded it hasan gharib. These narrations encourage restraint, but they do not mean a victim must stay in danger or that injustice should be hidden. A person may seek justice without vindictiveness.

**“and pardon others”** means to release a personal grievance when doing so is wise and safe. Forgiveness differs from pretending no wrong occurred. A person may forgive while still setting a boundary, asking for restitution, or reporting harm. The verse praises those who can relinquish personal vengeance, not those who enable further abuse.

The Qur’an elsewhere tells the Prophet to take the path of pardon (7:199). Another passage teaches that repelling evil with what is better may turn an enemy into a close friend (41:34). This is a high moral possibility, not an automatic result. Some people may respond to kindness, while others may continue to harm. The believer’s task is to seek what is just and beneficial, not to guarantee another person’s reaction.

Reports tell of ʿAli Zayn al-Abidin restraining his anger when a servant accidentally spilled water on him. When she recited the verse about those who control anger, he held back. When she continued with the words about pardoning people, he forgave her. When she recited the phrase about Allah loving the good-doers, he freed her. The story illustrates the progression in the verse: restraining anger, forgiving, then choosing a generous response. It should not be used to pressure workers or servants to tolerate mistreatment. The responsibility for kindness belongs especially to those with power.

**“And Allah loves the good-doers”** means that Allah loves those who go beyond merely withholding harm and actively choose excellence. Ihsan includes worshipping Allah with awareness and treating people with goodness. A person may pardon an offender, offer help to someone who has little, or repair a relationship where reconciliation is safe. Goodness is not weakness. It is the deliberate choice of a better response while remaining within justice.

### The social meaning of these qualities

The verse begins with spending because generosity protects the rights of people and helps meet real needs. It then turns to anger, a feeling that can lead someone to violate those rights, and to pardon, which can end cycles of retaliation. It culminates in ihsan, the active practice of goodness. A community shaped by these qualities is less likely to be governed by greed, grudges, or revenge.

This conduct benefits the giver as well as the recipient. Giving loosens the hold of wealth on the heart. Restraining anger prevents an impulsive act from becoming a lasting injustice. Forgiveness can release a person from being consumed by resentment. These benefits do not mean that a victim is at fault for feeling anger or that forgiveness should be demanded from them. The verse praises voluntary moral excellence, not coerced silence.

People can learn these qualities from trustworthy examples. The opening of the Qur’an asks for guidance to the straight path of those Allah has blessed (1:6), and elsewhere believers are told to remain with the truthful (9:119). Examples must be evaluated by their honesty, mercy, justice, and fidelity to revelation, not by reputation alone. The Prophet’s teaching provides a model for combining worship with mercy toward people.

The rights of Allah and the rights of people are both serious. A person who falls short in worship can turn to Allah in repentance, but a person who has taken another’s property or caused harm may also need to restore what was taken or seek the injured person’s pardon. The Qur’an calls the community to repentance and reminds it that Allah accepts it (9:104). It also says that whoever does wrong and then asks Allah for forgiveness will find Him forgiving and merciful (4:110). Seeking Allah’s forgiveness should not become an excuse to ignore another person’s rights.

The end of the verse says Allah loves the muhsinin, the people who act with excellence. The Prophet ﷺ taught that ihsan in worship is to serve Allah as though one sees Him, knowing that He sees the servant. In dealings with others, ihsan means giving what is due and, where wise, responding with more goodness than the minimum. It does not mean allowing injustice to continue. Allah’s love is the highest hope of the person who gives, restrains anger, and pardons sincerely.

### Giving without waste, and forgiving without enabling harm

Generosity is not measured by the amount a person gives at one time. It is a settled habit of sharing within one’s means. A person who spends beyond their capacity, neglects dependants, or gives to display status has not followed the balance of this verse. The Qur’an warns that extravagance leads people toward Satan’s way (17:27). In another passage, people are cautioned not to withhold wealth out of fear that Allah’s provision will run out (47:37). The mindful person avoids both waste and miserliness.

A devotional report about the Prophet, Muṣṭafā, says that Ruh al-Qudus inspired him that no soul would die before receiving its provision. It urges people to seek provision lawfully and not let its delay push them into disobedience. This reminder is not a reason to stop working. It encourages trust in Allah while staying within the Shariah, the revealed moral and legal path. Wealth belongs ultimately to Allah, al-Malik, the Sovereign Owner, and people are accountable for how they earn and spend it.

An account recorded by Imam al-Razi in al-Tafsir al-Kabir describes people giving gold and silver when encouraged to donate, while a poor man brought date-flakes because he had little else. Another person offered to give up his sense of honor by resolving not to retaliate when insulted. These are illustrative reports, not a measure by which the worth of one person can be compared with another. They show that generosity may take the form of money, food, restraint, or service.

Al-Baihaqi relates an account about ʿAli Zayn al-Abidin while a servant was helping him with wudu. When water spilled on him, he controlled his anger, then forgave her, and finally freed her after she reminded him of Allah’s love for the good-doers. The story illustrates a progression from controlling anger to pardon and ihsan. It does not make the person who spilled the water responsible for an unjust reaction, nor does it require servants or workers to accept abuse.

A well-known story about Abu Hanifah describes a man insulting him in public. Abu Hanifah is said to have returned later with a gift, telling the man that his insults had given him an opportunity to show patience. The man reportedly repented. The account illustrates how a generous response may soften hostility, but not every wrongdoer will change. The believer can pardon when it is safe and beneficial, while still asking for justice when needed.

The later verse 138 describes these teachings as guidance and a reminder for the mindful. The traits here are therefore not a checklist for judging other people. They are a mirror for the believer’s own conduct. One might say, “I am generous,” but the more important question is whether those around them experience practical help, safety from their anger, and fairness when a disagreement occurs.

### In short

- Give according to your means in both ease and hardship.
- Restrain anger without denying the right to safety or justice.
- Forgive when wise and safe, and go further by choosing ihsan.
