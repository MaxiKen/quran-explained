<<<VERSE 3:169>>>
> “Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for—” (3:169)

This verse comforts the believers after Uhud and corrects the assumption that those killed in Allah’s cause have been lost without reward. It affirms a real life and provision with Allah, while leaving the exact nature of that life beyond what people can fully know.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead”** does not deny the bodily death witnessed by the companions and the families who buried their loved ones. It denies that martyrdom is annihilation or a pointless end. Those who gave their lives in obedience to Allah have entered a state whose reality differs from ordinary earthly life.

The verse refers especially to those killed at Uhud, though its promise is not limited to that battle. The companions had seen bodies carried from the battlefield, and some families were mourning fathers, sons, and brothers. The Qur’an speaks to that grief without treating sacrifice as meaningless. The martyrs’ death is real in this world, but it does not define their continuing existence before Allah.

**“In fact, they are alive with their Lord”** affirms an honourable life in the unseen realm. The word barzakh is used for the interval between earthly death and resurrection. This verse gives the martyrs a special distinction within that unseen life: they are alive with their Lord and receive provision. It does not invite us to pretend that we know every detail of their state.

Reports in the hadith describe martyrs’ souls as being placed in green birds, moving freely in Paradise and returning to lamps beneath the Throne. One report says they wished to return to earthly life to be killed again in Allah’s cause, after tasting the honour and delight Allah had granted them. Another report describes their desire that their surviving brothers learn of their reward and not grieve as though the martyrs had been deprived. These accounts explain the verse through images suited to what the Prophet ﷺ taught, while the unseen reality remains known fully to Allah.

A report from Masruq says that he asked Abdullah about the verse and was told that the Prophet ﷺ had answered the companions’ question about it. Reports also come through Anas and Abu Saʿid. Anas’s account of the Ansar killed at Biʾr Maʿunah includes a supplication in qunut and a revealed message that Allah was pleased with them and they were pleased with Him. Such reports connect the verse’s assurance to the Sunnah, the Prophet’s teaching and practice.

A report through Ibn Abbas describes the martyrs of Uhud as souls in green birds that visit the rivers and fruits of Paradise and return to lamps in the shade of the Throne. The companions in that report wished that those left behind knew their state. Another report tells of Jabir bin Abdullah’s father asking to be returned to life to fight again and asking that the news be conveyed to his family. These narrations echo the verse’s assurance of life and provision, but they do not require us to claim that every detail of the unseen has been disclosed.

**“well provided for”** means that Allah grants them sustenance and honour. The Qur’anic description uses provision as a sign of life, not as a metaphor for an empty memory among the living. Their reward is not simply the esteem later generations give them. Allah Himself sustains them in a reality beyond ordinary perception.

The verse therefore describes more than the general continuation of the soul after death. Believers and disbelievers alike pass into the barzakh, but the martyrs receive a distinctive honour and provision. The precise form of this life is not something the living can measure by their experience of food, travel, or bodily sensation. It is enough to affirm what revelation says and avoid inventing details beyond it.

### Reports and the limits of what we know

Several well-known reports explain this teaching. Muslim records the account connected with Masruq and Abdullah. Al-Bukhari and Muslim transmit reports about the companions who answered the call after Uhud. Reports associated with Ahmad describe the souls of the martyrs as birds in Paradise. Al-Bayhaqi transmits an explanation from Ibn Abbas about the grace and bounty that followed the later expedition. Ibn Jarir also transmits accounts concerning the events after Uhud. These reports provide a fuller picture of the comfort given to the companions, while their wordings and details are not all identical.

The narrations should be read with care. Some describe souls wandering through Paradise, others mention a river or a green tent, and still others emphasize the martyrs’ provision and reunion with those who follow. The reports do not require one to flatten every description into a single physical map. The common point is that martyrdom is not deprivation: Allah grants the martyrs life, sustenance, joy, and a closeness that people in this world cannot fully picture.

There are different ranks and conditions among martyrs, and the broad promise should not be used to make careless judgments about a particular person. The verse teaches what Allah has promised to those killed in His cause. It does not give human beings access to every individual’s hidden intention. Sincere intention matters, and Allah alone knows it completely.

This assurance also applies beyond the battlefield of Uhud. The mention of Badr and Uhud in the reports gives the first audience a direct setting, but the Qur’anic wording is general. A believer who dies faithfully in Allah’s cause is not erased. This should encourage courage and steadfastness, not a desire for death or a disregard for life. The Prophet ﷺ taught the value of life and lawful effort. The promise here honours those who were killed, not those who seek harm without right or wisdom.

### The comfort offered to the living

The verse asks the bereaved not to measure their loved ones’ fate only by the body they saw or the absence they feel. Grief is not forbidden, and the companions’ sadness at Uhud was real. What is rejected is the claim that the martyrs have been abandoned or that their sacrifice has no outcome. They are with their Lord, receiving what He provides.

The martyrs’ joy in the next verse also includes concern for those who remain. Their hope that their brothers learn the truth is not a rebuke of grief. It is reassurance that their deaths did not destroy the bond of faith. The living are invited to continue in faith and good deeds, trusting Allah with the unseen future.

Some spiritual teachers use the verse’s language of life to speak about the heart that lives through remembrance of Allah. Such reflection can remind a person that worldly life is not the only measure of life. A teaching within the Tariqah tradition, associated with Shiblī, says that when a servant is wholly devoted to God, God sustains that servant’s spiritual life. This is mystical language about spiritual dependence, not a claim that a person literally becomes divine. It must not replace the verse’s direct statement about those killed in Allah’s cause, nor blur the distinction between a spiritual metaphor and the promised life of the martyrs.

Early authorities also connect the passage with the martyrs of Uhud. Ad-Dahhak is among those reported to identify them as its immediate setting. A report about Jabir bin Abdullah’s father is transmitted by Ibn Marduwyah, while Az-Zuhri appears in a separate narration about the soul of a believer. Muhammad bin Ishaq explains the martyrs’ joy in the promise that Allah fulfilled for them, a point developed in the verses that follow. Their deaths occurred in jihad, yet the promise of life and provision is Allah’s gift, not an invitation to seek death. The first audience had travelled from Makkah to Madinah and had recently endured the battle at Uhud, so the assurance answered an immediate loss.

### In short

- The martyrs have a real life with Allah after bodily death.
- Allah provides for them in a way whose full reality is unseen to us.
- The promise honours sacrifice without inviting people to seek death or invent details about the unseen.

<<<VERSE 3:170>>>
> “rejoicing in Allah’s bounties and being delighted for those yet to join them. There will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve” (3:170)

The martyrs rejoice in the gifts Allah has given them and in the good news about the believers who will later join them. Their joy is not only personal. They are glad that their brothers and sisters in faith will also receive Allah’s favour.

### Phrase by phrase

**“rejoicing in Allah’s bounties”** describes their happiness in the provision and honour already granted to them. They are not waiting in deprivation or regret. What Allah has given them has made them joyful.

**“and being delighted for those yet to join them”** points to the believers still living in the world. The martyrs learn that faithful believers who later die will be reunited with them and share in Allah’s reward. The hope of reunion makes them glad for their companions, rather than jealous or possessive of the blessings they already have.

Their joy also shows that death has not severed the bond of faith. The martyrs remember those who remain and wish them well. A report from Ibn Abbas says they asked that their living brothers be told of their state, so that they would not abandon the struggle in Allah’s cause or believe that the martyrs had been lost. The verse therefore turns their joy into reassurance for the grieving community.

**“There will be no fear for them”** means they will not dread what lies ahead in the Hereafter. **“nor will they grieve”** means they will not be burdened by sorrow over what they left behind in the world. Their earthly families may grieve, but the martyrs themselves have been given security and contentment with Allah.

These words do not mean that no believer ever feels fear or grief in worldly life. The verse describes the martyrs’ state with their Lord. It gives the living hope that the Hereafter is not defined by the fear and sorrow that accompany worldly trials.

### Reports of the martyrs’ joy

A report carried by Abu Dawud through Ibn Abbas says that after Uhud the Holy Prophet ﷺ described how Allah Almighty placed the spirits of the martyrs in green birds, which drank from the streams and ate the fruits of Paradise before returning to their places beneath the Throne, the ʿarsh. They wished their grieving companions knew how they were being honoured. The report joins the image of provision in verse 169 to the joy and concern for those left behind in this verse.

Al-Suddi explains that a martyr is given news when a close friend or relative is about to join them, and that the arrival of the loved one brings joy like a reunion after long separation. The report should be understood as a description of the unseen, not as a claim that the living can know when any particular person will die. Another narration says that the companions of Biʾr Maʿunah were remembered in qunut, with a message that Allah was pleased with them and they were pleased with Him. This language of remembrance connects the martyrs’ joy with Allah’s acceptance.

Muhammad bin Ishaq explains their happiness as joy in the fulfilment of Allah’s promise and the immense reward they received. Zayd bin Aslam extends the assurance to believers generally, martyrs and others. This does not erase the martyrs’ special honour. It shows that the same divine generosity sustains the faithful, whether or not they were killed in jihad. Ikrimah’s report about Quraysh turning back toward Makkah after Uhud gives the immediate historical backdrop: the companions feared another assault, while the martyrs had already received the joy promised by Allah.

### In short

- The martyrs rejoice in Allah’s gifts and in the promise of reunion with faithful believers.
- Their joy includes concern for those still living.
- Allah grants them security from fear and grief in the life to come.

<<<VERSE 3:171>>>
> “They are joyful for receiving Allah’s grace and bounty, and that Allah does not deny the reward of the believers” (3:171)

This verse completes the description of the martyrs’ joy. They rejoice not only in what they have received, but also in Allah’s assurance that the reward of the believers is not lost.

### Phrase by phrase

**“They are joyful for receiving Allah’s grace and bounty”** reiterates that their happiness comes from Allah’s generosity. Grace is the favour He gives, and bounty is the increase and abundance He grants beyond what people could claim as their due. Their reward is not a payment imposed on Allah by human effort. It is His gracious gift.

**“and that Allah does not deny the reward of the believers”** makes the promise broader than the martyrs alone. Allah will not waste the efforts of those who believe and act faithfully. The verse does not say that every believer receives an identical worldly outcome. It assures the community that sincere faith and good deeds are not forgotten, even when people see suffering or an apparent defeat.

The martyrs’ joy is grounded in certainty about Allah’s justice and generosity. They know that the believers who remain will be rewarded in the way Allah chooses. That assurance can strengthen the living without turning faith into a bargain for immediate success.

### Reward beyond the battlefield

Muhammad bin Ishaq explains that the martyrs rejoice because Allah’s promise has been fulfilled for them. Zayd bin Aslam says the assurance that Allah does not waste the believers’ reward includes all believers, martyrs and others. Al-Suddi’s account of martyrs receiving news of a loved one’s approach gives one image of that reunion. These explanations do not make the reward identical for everyone. They show that no sincere effort is lost with Allah.

The hadith report transmitted by Abu Dawud through Ibn Abbas describes the Holy Prophet ﷺ telling the companions after Uhud that Allah Almighty gave the martyrs provision and a place beneath the ʿarsh. Their rejoicing for those who have not joined them is connected to faith, not merely shared history. The people who fought at Uhud had come from different homes, including Makkah and Madinah, but their bond was the belief that Allah would honour the faithful. A remembrance recited by the companions killed at Biʾr Maʿunah similarly speaks of Allah’s pleasure and their own contentment.

A later spiritual anecdote from Sarī Saqaṭī reflects on accepting trials, but it should not be mistaken for a command to seek suffering. His image can illustrate that a person’s loyalty is tested by hardship. The Qur’anic assurance remains that Allah does not waste the reward of believers who act with faith, ihsan, and taqwa. The purpose of jihad in this passage is obedience and protection of the community under the Prophet’s direction, not pursuit of pain for its own sake.

### In short

- The martyrs rejoice in Allah’s generous favour.
- Allah does not let the sincere reward of believers go to waste.
- A worldly loss does not prove that a person’s faithful effort has been rejected.

<<<VERSE 3:172>>>
> “˹As for˺ those who responded to the call of Allah and His Messenger after their injury, those of them who did good and were mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have a great reward” (3:172)

After the loss at Uhud, the Prophet ﷺ called the companions to pursue the Quraysh force toward Hamra al-Asad. Many were still wounded, yet those who had fought at Uhud answered the call. Their response showed steadfastness, good conduct, and mindfulness of Allah. Their willingness to resume jihad after injury arose from obedience, not from indifference to life.

### Phrase by phrase

**“those who responded to the call of Allah and His Messenger”** begins with alladhīna, “those who,” and refers to the companions who accepted the Prophet’s instruction to go out again after Uhud. The call came from the Messenger, but the verse attributes it to Allah as well. Obedience to the Prophet’s instruction in this matter was obedience to Allah.

The Quraysh force led by Abu Sufyan had withdrawn from Uhud and then considered returning to attack Madinah. The Prophet ﷺ learned of the danger and called the companions to pursue them. The Muslims did not wait for their wounds to disappear or assume that their previous losses made further action pointless. They went out to show that the community remained present and prepared.

**“after their injury”** emphasizes the difficulty of their response. The companions had been wounded in the previous day’s battle. Some could barely walk without support. Their injuries were real, but they did not use them as a pretext to reject the Prophet’s call. Their courage was joined to obedience and discipline, not to a belief that they were invulnerable.

Reports give differing numbers for the expedition. One account speaks of seventy men responding to the call, while another speaks of two hundred. The reports may describe different stages or groups, but the central point is that a body of companions who had been at Uhud went out while still hurt. Jabir bin Abdullah was allowed to join although he had not taken part in the battle, because he wished to accompany the Prophet and had family responsibilities that had kept him from Uhud.

The expedition reached Hamra al-Asad, several miles from Madinah. The Quraysh did not return to fight. The companions’ march demonstrated that the Muslims had not been broken by the losses at Uhud. It also gave the community a chance to respond to the danger rather than merely speculate about the enemy’s intentions.

**“those of them who did good”** describes the companions’ ihsan, excellence in action. It is not enough to perform an outward act while neglecting sincerity, justice, or care. Their obedience after Uhud was accompanied by a desire to act rightly before Allah.

**“and were mindful of Allah”** refers to taqwa, awareness of Allah that guides a person away from disobedience. The companions did not treat bravery as an excuse to ignore Allah’s limits. Their courage and their restraint belonged together.

The verse does not imply that only a fraction of those who responded were good or mindful. The expression “those of them” can describe the group being praised, rather than divide the companions into the righteous and unrighteous. Their readiness to obey the call is presented together with good conduct and taqwa.

**“will have a great reward”** promises Allah’s reward for their response. The reward is not only the fact that they returned safely. It includes Allah’s pleasure and what He has prepared in the Hereafter. Their wounds did not diminish the value of their obedience.

### The account of Hamra al-Asad

The battle of Uhud had left the Muslims wounded and grieving. The Quraysh, however, had not completed their campaign against Madinah. Reports say Abu Sufyan and his men reconsidered their retreat and discussed returning. The Prophet ﷺ called those who had been present at Uhud to pursue them. In some accounts, seventy volunteers answered. In another, two hundred went out. A report from Aʿishah says that her nephew ʿUrwah’s father, Abu Bakr, and Az-Zubayr were among those who volunteered. The shared lesson is the companions’ prompt response despite injury.

Other reports describe messengers or travellers bringing alarming news about the Quraysh force. One account says that Nuʿaym ibn Masʿud al-Ashjaʿi told the Muslims that Abu Sufyan had gathered a larger force. Another account describes Maʿbad al-Khuzaʿi, a non-Muslim ally of the Prophet, meeting Abu Sufyan and warning him that the Muslims had regrouped at Hamra al-Asad. These reports differ in their details about who carried which message, but they agree that the Muslims’ decision to march affected the enemy’s confidence.

The reports also describe the companions continuing with their wounds. Their willingness did not mean that injury was unimportant or that commanders should ignore people’s limits. The story praises those who chose to answer a call that they were able to meet. It does not establish a rule that an injured person must always ignore medical need.

A further report connects the expedition with the market season. The companions traded at Hamra al-Asad and returned with profit. This detail helps explain the “grace and bounty” mentioned in the next verse: they received safety from battle and material benefit, while their highest reward was Allah’s pleasure.

### The march to Hamra al-Asad

The expedition is known as the march to Hamra al-Asad, a place outside Madinah. Some reports describe the destination as Badr al-Sughra, the lesser Badr, because the Muslims and Quraysh had agreed to meet there later. The immediate account in this passage focuses on the pursuit after Uhud. Ibn Jarir transmits a report that the Prophet ﷺ learned the Quraysh might return, then called the companions who had fought at Uhud to go out. A report in Al-Bukhari also records the companions volunteering despite wounds.

The accounts differ on whether seventy men or two hundred answered. These figures may refer to different stages of the mobilization, and the essential point is shared: the Prophet called, and the wounded companions responded. The companions’ injuries from Uhud had not healed. Some were helped as they walked. Jabir bin Abdullah was permitted to accompany them although he had missed the earlier battle. Their action was not a second battle in the full sense. The enemy turned away, and the march displayed the Muslims’ resolve.

Reports name Maʿbad al-Khuzaʿi, a man from the Khuzaʿah tribe, as a well-wisher of the Muslims who met Abu Sufyan on the road toward Makkah. He warned Abu Sufyan that the Muslim force at Hamra al-Asad remained strong. Another account speaks of Nuʿaym ibn Masʿud bringing a threat to the Muslims. The details differ, so the lesson should not depend on assigning every message to a single person. The Quraysh had left Uhud, then considered returning. The Muslims acted on the threat, and their action made the enemy reconsider.

Ikrimah’s account likewise describes Quraysh discussing a return after Uhud, while the Prophet prepared the companions to meet them. The immediate expedition is called Hamra al-Asad in the main narrative. A later agreed meeting is sometimes called Badr al-Sughra. Distinguishing these details prevents the reader from confusing the march with the earlier battle of Badr or assuming that the Muslims fought another battle at Hamra al-Asad.

### Ihsan and taqwa together

The verse joins ihsan and taqwa. Ihsan means striving to do what is good with excellence and sincerity. In the well-known hadith of Jibril, the Prophet ﷺ described worshipping Allah as though one sees Him, while knowing that even if one does not see Him, Allah sees the worshipper. This definition brings private intention into public action.

Taqwa is often explained as guarding oneself from sin out of awareness of Allah. A report about Ubayy ibn Kaʿb and Umar ibn al-Khattab compares it to walking carefully through a thorny path, lifting one’s clothing and avoiding the hazards. The image conveys vigilance: a God-conscious person does not stride carelessly through choices that could lead to disobedience. Abu al-Dardaʾ is also reported to have said that taqwa is the best gain a person can carry away from this world.

This combination matters because courage by itself is not a guarantee of spiritual reward. A person might perform a brave deed for reputation, revenge, or worldly advantage. The verse praises courage that is joined to obedience, ihsan, and taqwa. The companions sought Allah’s pleasure and followed the Prophet’s call.

The Prophet’s instruction to pursue the Quraysh is not quoted as a separate sentence in the Qur’an, yet the verse praises those who responded to the call of Allah and His Messenger. This makes clear that the Messenger’s guidance is not merely a report of scripture. His authoritative instruction directs the believers in how to act, within the bounds Allah has set.

### Trust and practical action

The companions’ march also illustrates that trust in Allah does not mean refusing to use available means. They went out, gathered together, and faced the danger as a community. Reliance on Allah accompanied preparation and action. The later words “Allah is sufficient for us” do not mean that a person should sit idle when there is a responsible step to take.

The call after Uhud was a particular command to a particular community under the Prophet’s leadership. It should not be generalized into permission for individuals to wage violence or to disregard lawful authority and the safety of others. The lesson is obedience, courage, and prudent collective action in the circumstances addressed by revelation.

### In short

- The companions answered the Prophet’s call after Uhud despite their wounds.
- Their courage was joined to ihsan and taqwa, not a claim of invulnerability.
- Trust in Allah is compatible with responsible preparation and action.

<<<VERSE 3:173>>>
> “Those who were warned, “Your enemies have mobilized their forces against you, so fear them,” the warning only made them grow stronger in faith and they replied, “Allah ˹alone˺ is sufficient ˹as an aid˺ for us and ˹He˺ is the best Protector.”” (3:173)

The companions heard that the Quraysh might return from Makkah with a larger force. Instead of giving in to panic, they grew stronger in faith and entrusted the outcome to Allah. Their reliance did not stop them from marching out after Uhud in jihad. It gave them resolve to act.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Those who were warned, ‘Your enemies have mobilized their forces against you, so fear them’”** refers to the warning that Quraysh had gathered to attack. Reports identify Abu Sufyan as the leader of the opposing force. One account names Nuʿaym ibn Masʿud al-Ashjaʿi, from the tribe of al-Ashja, as the person who delivered the warning. The warning was meant to make the wounded Muslims abandon the pursuit.

Other accounts describe a caravan or travellers carrying alarming talk into Madinah, or Maʿbad al-Khuzaʿi warning Abu Sufyan that the Muslims had regrouped. The reports preserve different details around the episode. The verse focuses on the effect of the warning on the believers: it did not frighten them into abandoning their trust in Allah.

**“the warning only made them grow stronger in faith”** does not mean that they denied the danger or had no human fear. It means the news increased their conviction and reliance. They understood that the enemy had assembled, but they also knew that the enemy’s numbers did not control the final outcome.

**“and they replied, ‘Allah alone is sufficient for us as an aid’”** expresses tawakkul, reliance upon Allah. The believers acknowledged that Allah was enough to support them, not that they themselves could guarantee victory. The wording does not prohibit seeking help from people or taking sound precautions. It places ultimate reliance in Allah.

**“and He is the best Protector”** means that Allah is the one to whom the matter is entrusted. The believers could organize, march, and make decisions, while knowing that their plans could not replace Allah’s protection. Their trust was neither passive nor boastful.

### Trust that acts

The companions did not interpret reliance as permission to remain at home while expecting Allah to remove the threat without any effort. The Prophet ﷺ gathered those who could go, prepared them, and led them toward Hamra al-Asad. They used the means available to them, then entrusted the outcome to Allah.

A familiar saying captures the principle: tie your camel, then trust in Allah. It reflects the Sunnah of combining reliance with responsible preparation. The saying is a reminder of action, not a replacement for revelation. Neglecting an available safeguard and calling that neglect “trust” is not what this verse praises. At the same time, using every available means does not guarantee control over the outcome. A believer acts responsibly on the path of Allah and accepts that the final result belongs to Him.

The phrase “Allah is sufficient for us” is also associated with Ibrahim when he was thrown into the fire. The companions used it when warned about the enemy after Uhud, recalling the earlier test at Badr in which Quraysh had also threatened the believers. In both situations, the statement expresses trust under pressure. It is not a magical formula detached from faith and conduct. The word alladhīna, “those who,” introduces the believers who answered the warning with faith rather than panic.

The Qur’an elsewhere instructs believers to fight the allies of Satan (4:76), describes Satan’s party as ultimately losing (58:19), and says that Allah has decreed victory for His messengers and those who believe (58:21). It promises help to those who help Allah’s cause (22:40, 47:7) and declares that Allah will support His messengers and believers (40:51-52). These passages ground confidence in Allah without promising that every believer will avoid every injury or worldly loss.

### Stronger faith, not overconfidence

The companions’ response was not a claim that they were invincible or that they knew exactly what would happen. They did not say that the Quraysh had no power. They said that Allah was sufficient, and they went forward with the mission before them. Their faith strengthened their resolve without turning into contempt for danger.

The verse offers a pattern for facing intimidating news. A report of an opponent’s strength may be accurate, exaggerated, or intended to manipulate. A believer should consider the facts and make prudent decisions, rather than be governed by fear. Reliance on Allah gives courage to face reality. It does not require denying reality. A spiritual interpretation describes Ṭawāliʿ, the first glimmers of insight, as opening the heart to greater certainty when circumstances seem confusing. This is an inward reflection on the verse, not a substitute for assessing the situation and acting wisely.

### In short

- The threat of a renewed attack strengthened the companions’ trust in Allah.
- Tawakkul means relying on Allah while taking responsible action.
- “Allah is sufficient for us” expresses humility and confidence, not a guarantee of worldly invulnerability.

<<<VERSE 3:174>>>
> “So they returned with Allah’s favours and grace, suffering no harm. For they sought to please Allah. And surely Allah is ˹the˺ Lord of infinite bounty” (3:174)

The companions returned from Hamra al-Asad safely. They had faced the threat after Uhud, but no battle took place. They also gained material benefit through trade, and the highest favour was that they had acted seeking Allah’s pleasure.

### Phrase by phrase

**“So they returned with Allah’s favours and grace”** describes the outcome of the expedition. The companions came back to Madinah with safety and with Allah’s favour. Reports also mention profit from trading at the market during the journey. The verse’s words allow for both the material benefit and the broader blessing of protection.

**“suffering no harm”** means that the expedition did not bring the feared battle or new casualties. The companions had been wounded at Uhud, so this phrase does not erase their earlier injuries. It describes their return from this later march without the harm they feared from the Quraysh force.

**“For they sought to please Allah”** identifies their purpose. They answered the Prophet’s call and went out despite their wounds, seeking Allah’s approval. Their safety and profit were blessings, but the verse does not say that safety or money was the only reason for their action.

**“And surely Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty”** means His favour is not exhausted by one reward or limited to worldly gains. He grants safety, provision, spiritual reward, and His pleasure according to His wisdom.

### What the expedition brought

Some reports explain “grace” as the safety of the companions and “bounty” as the profit they earned from a passing caravan during the days of the hajj season. They bought and sold at the market near Hamra al-Asad. Another account describes the bounty more generally as reward or even booty. These explanations differ in how specifically they identify the benefit, but they share the point that Allah brought the Muslims back with good rather than the destruction feared by their enemies.

Hamra al-Asad lies outside Madinah. The companions’ willingness to go there showed that the loss at Uhud had not ended their ability to respond. The Quraysh did not return to attack, and the Muslims did not suffer new casualties. They returned having demonstrated resolve and having followed the Prophet ﷺ.

This outcome should not be turned into a formula that every faithful expedition ends without injury. The companions had already been wounded, and the Qur’an has acknowledged those losses. Here, Allah’s favour took the form of protection on this particular journey, alongside reward for obedience.

### What fear of Allah means

The verse’s mention of seeking Allah’s pleasure leads naturally to the next command to fear Him rather than Satan’s followers. Reverence for Allah is not a life of paralysis. It means avoiding what displeases Him while pursuing a good purpose with discipline. The believers’ march after Uhud was a form of jihad under the Prophet’s direction, not a private search for conflict.

Abu Sufyan’s retreat toward Makkah and the Muslims’ return to Madinah marked the close of this particular danger. Reports place their trading near the route associated with Badr, while the account of the pursuit names Hamra al-Asad. The blessing included both the absence of another battle and the provision they gained. Neither the market profit nor the protected return was the sole measure of success. Their intention to please Allah gave the action its spiritual weight.

### In short

- The companions returned safely from Hamra al-Asad.
- They received Allah’s favour and, according to reports, profit from trade.
- Their highest aim was Allah’s pleasure, and His bounty exceeds worldly gain.

<<<VERSE 3:175>>>
> “That ˹warning˺ was only ˹from˺ Satan, trying to prompt you to fear his followers. So do not fear them; fear Me if you are ˹true˺ believers” (3:175)

The verse explains that Satan uses the threat of his followers to make believers afraid and abandon what is right. Allah commands them not to let that fear overrule their faith, and to remain mindful of Him.

### Phrase by phrase

**“That warning was only from Satan”** refers to the intimidation that the Quraysh had gathered a stronger force and that the believers should fear them. Satan’s influence can work through people who spread panic or through thoughts that exaggerate an enemy’s strength. The verse does not mean every warning about danger is false. It identifies the purpose of a threat used to frighten believers away from their duty.

Some interpretations identify Nuʿaym ibn Masʿud as the one who delivered the warning and describe him as acting on Satan’s behalf. The Qur’anic point is broader than naming one messenger: Satan tries to use human fear to weaken faith and disrupt obedience.

**“trying to prompt you to fear his followers”** means Satan wants believers to treat his allies as if they control the outcome. The phrase can also be understood as Satan terrifying people through the power of his followers. The absent object is understood from the context: he tries to make the believers fear them. Either way, fear becomes a tool for pulling people away from trust in Allah.

**“So do not fear them”** does not prohibit ordinary caution or wise assessment of danger. The companions had just been told that the Quraysh might return, and the Prophet ﷺ organized a response. The command rejects terror that causes a person to abandon Allah’s guidance or accept intimidation as final authority.

**“fear Me if you are true believers”** directs the believers to fear Allah in the sense of reverence, accountability, and obedience. They should be more concerned about disobeying Allah than about the threats of His enemies. Fear of Allah is not despair or paralysis. It helps a believer avoid wrongdoing and act with courage and care.

The Qur’an elsewhere says that those above the angels fear their Lord (16:50). In this context, fearing Allah means taking His command seriously, not refusing to act. A person who fears Allah can still feel concern for physical safety, protect others, and seek help. The distinction is between prudent caution and surrendering one’s faith to intimidation.

### Fear, means, and reliance

This verse completes the lesson begun with the companions’ statement that Allah is sufficient for them. They were not told to ignore the enemy or to make reckless choices. They marched under the Prophet’s leadership and trusted Allah with the result. Faith refuses to let an opponent’s threats become the ultimate measure of what is possible.

The Qur’an says that Allah is sufficient for His servant even when people try to frighten him with other powers (39:36), and it directs those who trust to rely upon Him (39:38). It also reminds believers that Satan’s plot is weak (4:76) and that his party will lose (58:19). These verses do not promise a life without hardship. They place hardship within a wider reality in which Allah’s authority and final judgment prevail.

The command also guards against a different error: fearing Allah should not be used as an excuse to mistreat people or to manufacture danger. The companions faced a real threat in a specific situation under the Prophet’s leadership. The warning concerned the Makkan force led by Abu Sufyan, coming from the direction of Makkah, and was meant to make the Muslims fear Satan’s helpers. The general lesson for believers is to seek Allah’s pleasure, avoid wrongdoing, examine threats calmly, and take lawful steps to protect life.

A report about spiritual fear describes Abu ʿAli al-Daqqaq visiting Abu Bakr ibn al-Fuwarrak while he was ill. Abu ʿAli thought his host was crying from fear of death and tried to comfort him. Abu Bakr replied that he was concerned about accountability after death, not about dying itself. This anecdote illustrates the verse’s distinction: fear of Allah is concern about disobeying Him and meeting Him with one’s deeds. It is not panic at the fact of death, and it does not mean surrendering to the threat of an enemy. The believers can fear Allah, rely on Him, and still take responsible steps in jihad when lawfully called.

### In short

- Satan exploits fear of his followers to weaken faith and resolve.
- The command to fear Allah means reverence and obedience, not panic or recklessness.
- Believers can take prudent measures while placing their ultimate trust in Allah.
