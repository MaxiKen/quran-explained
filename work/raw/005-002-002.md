<<<VERSE 5:2>>>
This verse gives the believers two instructions that work together. The first is to protect the things Allah has made sacred: "O believers! Do not violate Allah’s rituals", the sacred months, the animals brought as offerings, the garlands hung on them, and the people travelling to the Sacred House to seek their Lord's favour. The second is to refuse to answer an injustice with another injustice. The verse says that the hatred of a people who once blocked the believers from the Sacred Mosque must not drive them into transgression. Instead, believers must help one another in what is good and God-fearing, and never in sin and aggression. The verse ends with the reminder to be mindful of Allah, because His punishment is severe.

The verse follows directly from the one before it, which asked believers to honour their obligations. This verse spells out two parts of that obligation: respect for the signs of Allah, and justice even toward enemies. It belongs to the same period as the events of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, when the Muslims were stopped from performing 'Umrah, and to the period after, when some of them wanted to take revenge.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers!"** is the same address as in the previous verse. It turns to people who have already accepted faith and asks them to act on that acceptance.

**"Do not violate Allah’s rituals"** translates the Arabic word sha'ā'ir, which the material explains as the plural of sha'īrah, a mark, sign or symbol. The things called sha'ā'ir are visible acts that point to something beyond themselves. Ibn 'Abbas said they are the rites of Hajj. Mujahid said they are As-Safa and Al-Marwah and the sacrificial animal. Other reports give a wider meaning. Al-Hasan al-Basri and 'Ata' are quoted as giving the clearest and most general reading: the sha'ā'ir of Allah are all the obligations whose limits the Shari'ah has set. Al-Jassas is said to have treated their words as a summary of all the other readings. On that reading, violating the signs of Allah has three forms. One is to abandon the obligations altogether. A second is to keep some of them and drop others. A third is to cross the limits that have been set. The verse forbids all three. The material also points out that the Qur'an gives the same instruction in another place: whoever exalts the signs of Allah shows the fear of Allah in the heart [22:32].

**"the sacred months"** refers to the four months during which fighting was forbidden: Dhul-Qa'dah, Dhul-Hijjah, Muharram and Rajab. The material adds that the rule against fighting in these months was later changed. Some reports say the verse on the garlands was abrogated, and Ibn Abi Hatim recorded that Ibn 'Abbas said the same about another verse of the surah. The scholars the material quotes agree that the ban on fighting in the sacred months was abrogated. Even so, the sacred months remain a reason to avoid sin, as 2:217 makes clear when it calls fighting in them a great transgression.

**"the sacrificial animals"** are the Hady, animals brought to the Sacred House to be slaughtered. The verse forbids preventing them from reaching the Sacred Area, and forbids seizing them. The material notes that verse 5:3 lists the sacrifice made on stone altars, An-Nusub, as forbidden, because such an animal can no longer be slaughtered properly. The material also forbids using such an animal for riding or milking when it has been set aside as an offering. Muqatil bin Hayyan said that "do not breach their sanctity" is the meaning of the instruction about the garlands.

**"the garlands"** are the markings placed on the animals' necks to show that they are offerings. The material records that in the period before Islam people hung animal hair and pelts on themselves when they travelled outside the sacred months, and that the idolaters of the Sacred Area hung tree-stems from the Sacred Area so that they could travel safely. Those garlands are not to be violated.

**"nor those ˹pilgrims˺ on their way to the Sacred House"** protects the people going to the Sacred House. The material says the verse means that no one heading to the House with the intention of seeking Allah's pleasure may be fought, stopped, or frightened away. Mujahid, 'Ata', Abu al-'Aliyah, Qatadah and several others took "the bounty of their Lord" to mean trade, which the pilgrims were allowed to carry on during Hajj. Ibn 'Abbas took the word "pleasure" to mean that the pilgrimage itself was done to seek Allah's pleasure. The Sacred Mosque mentioned here is al-Masjid al-Haram at Makkah.

**"seeking their Lord’s bounty and pleasure"** names the purpose of such people. Trade is named as one way of seeking provision, and the intention to please Allah is named as the reason for the journey. The material connects this to a particular man, al-Hutam, who had driven away the livestock of Madinah. The verse came down, and when the Muslims wanted to attack him on his way to the House, they were held back.

**"When pilgrimage has ended, you are allowed to hunt."** The earlier verse banned hunting while in Ihram. This phrase lifts that ban once the pilgrim has left the state of Ihram. The material says the command is a permission and not a requirement. It also notes that the rule applies after leaving the Sanctuary, so that hunting inside the Sacred Area remains forbidden.

**"Do not let the hatred of a people who once barred you from the Sacred Mosque provoke you to transgress."** This is the central instruction of the verse. The reference is to the people of Makkah who stopped the believers at Hudaybiyyah. The material says that the Muslims returned from Hudaybiyyah sad and angry. Now that they were in a position of strength, some of them wanted to repay that treatment by stopping the Makkans from reaching the House. The verse forbids this because it is injustice, and Islam does not allow injustice to be avenged with more injustice. The material treats this as a rule that holds in every circumstance.

**"Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness"** is the positive instruction. The Arabic words are Al-Birr, which the material says means doing good deeds, and At-Taqwa, which means keeping away from what is evil. Ibn Jarir and others explained that believers must help one another in good actions and in avoiding what is wrong.

**"and do not cooperate in sin and transgression."** The material defines sin, ithm, as disobedience in matters of rights and in acts of worship alike. Transgression, 'udwan, means crossing the limits set by Allah, which is injustice and aggression. Ibn Jarir put it this way: sin is leaving aside what Allah has ordained, and transgression is going past the bounds He has set, both in religion and in how a person deals with himself and with others.

**"And be mindful of Allah."** This is the closing command. The material explains that taqwā means fearing Allah in what He has commanded and forbidden. It makes the ground of all the earlier instructions clear: a believer keeps the covenant, respects the signs and acts justly because of the fear of Allah.

**"Surely Allah is severe in punishment."** The verse ends with a warning. Those who abstain from what Allah has commanded will face His punishment, and the material understands this as the reason to avoid the transgressions just described.

### The story behind the verse

The material gives a detailed background, and it is worth knowing because it shows what the verse was addressing. In the sixth year after the Hijrah, the Prophet ﷺ set out with more than a thousand Companions to perform 'Umrah. The people of Makkah refused to let them enter and imposed hard conditions in a treaty: the Muslims were to return that year and come back the next year without weapons, to stay only three days and to leave after 'Umrah. The Muslims accepted this out of obedience, and they performed the missed 'Umrah in the seventh year. The treaty left seeds of anger among the Companions.

The material then tells of al-Hutam, whose name is given as Hatim ibn Hind, one of the chiefs of Makkah. Some reports name him as Sharih ibn Dubay'ah al-Kindi. He came to Madinah to trade, then came to the Prophet ﷺ claiming he wished to become Muslim, but he said he had chiefs whose agreement he needed. The Prophet ﷺ had already told his Companions that a man was coming who would speak with the words of the devil. When the man left, the Prophet ﷺ described him as coming with the face of a disbeliever and going away with the steps of a traitor. The man then drove off the camels that were grazing outside Madinah. In the seventh year, when the Muslims were going to perform the missed 'Umrah, they heard someone chanting the talbiyah at a distance. It was al-Hutam, with the stolen animals wearing garlands as offerings. Some Companions wanted to attack him and take back the animals. Ibn Jarir and al-Suddi, quoting 'Ikrimah, related that the verse was revealed about this man and this moment, and it forbade the Muslims to act against him.

The material also gives a second setting, which it presents as the later one. After the conquest of Makkah in the eighth year, the Makkans were free and kept their pagan practices of Hajj and 'Umrah. Some Companions wanted to take revenge for Hudaybiyyah by stopping them. The verse was used to forbid this. The material explains that, whatever the setting, the lesson is the same.

### What the verse says about hunting after Ihram

The material explains that the first verse of the surah banned hunting during Ihram. Here the limit of that prohibition is set. Ihram ends, and the hunting that was forbidden becomes lawful again. Some scholars took this as an instruction that lifts a prohibition, which is different from a new command. The material also points to the rule that hunting within the Sacred Area stays forbidden, because the permission applies only to pilgrims who leave the Sanctuary.

One reading takes the permission as plain, and another reads it as the end of a state of prohibition, so that the believer is free again. None of the readings treats the permission as a duty.

### The disagreement about abrogation

The material contains more than one view about whether parts of this verse were changed. One reading says that the Makkan pagans falsely claimed that the verse about sacred things was still binding, and it points to the verse of repudiation in Surat At-Tawbah as the one that settled the matter. Ibn Abi Hatim recorded that Ibn 'Abbas said the verse on the garlands was abrogated, along with another verse of the surah. The consensus cited about the sacred months is that the ban on fighting in them was lifted. The material presents these as different claims and does not reduce them to one settled answer.

### Justice toward enemies

The material is clear that the verse forbids revenge that wrongs people who have done nothing new. The Qur'an, the material says, teaches that friend and foe are to be weighed on the same scale of justice [5:8], which is nearer to piety. Believers are commanded to do justice as an obligation, however hostile the enemy and however great the harm suffered. The material presents this as one of the distinctive qualities of Islam, which does not answer one wrong with another but chooses justice in return. Another reading explains the verse in the same way: the people who had blocked the believers were not to be blocked in return, and the pilgrims of one clan were not to be attacked because of the crimes of another.

Ibn Abi Hatim recorded a report from Zayd bin Aslam about the setting at Al-Hudaybiyyah, which the material also gives. The Companions wanted to block a group of idolaters on their way to 'Umrah, because the idolaters had blocked them, and then this verse came down. Ibn 'Abbas and others explained that "Shana'an" means enmity and hatred. The lesson is that hatred must not cause injustice, because justice is owed in every situation.

### Helping one another in righteousness

This part of the verse has a broad reach in the material. Ibn Jarir, Ibn Abi Hatim and others took it as a rule that governs the whole community. Believers help one another in what is righteous and God-fearing, and they must not help one another in sin and aggression.

The material draws a clear limit. The verse does not say that help should go only to fellow Muslims and not to others. It makes righteousness and the fear of Allah the basis of cooperation, and so it applies also among Muslims. Help must not be given to a Muslim brother who is acting against the truth or moving toward injustice. Instead, the believer should hold his hand back from wrong, because that is the truest help. A report found in the Sahih collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim, through Anas, says that the Prophet ﷺ told his Companions to help their brother whether he was just or unjust. They asked how one helps an oppressor. He said: stop him from doing injustice, and that is helping him. Ahmad recorded a similar report, and al-Bukhari recorded it through Hushaym.

The material also gives related reports about the rewards of guidance. A believer who mixes with people and bears their harm with patience earns more reward than one who keeps away from them. Muslim recorded that whoever calls to guidance earns a reward like that of everyone who follows him, without any loss to their reward, and whoever calls to error carries a burden like that of everyone who follows him, without any reduction in their burden. Tabarani is cited for the report that whoever joins an unjust person to help him leaves the fold of Islam. Scholars in the material explain that this is why the righteous of earlier times refused to hold office in the courts of unjust rulers. A further report, which the material introduces with the verse "I will never be a helper for the criminals" [28:17], says that on the Day of Judgment a call will go out for the oppressors and their helpers, even those who only prepared the pens and inkpots of the unjust.

### Cooperation as the order of the world

The material gives a long reflection on the verse's second half. It argues that human life depends on cooperation. No one can grow, process and transport his own food, make his own clothes, or build his own house. Each person needs hundreds and thousands of others, and this mutual need keeps the world going. The material says that Allah put into each person the inclination and liking for the work for which He created him, so that people take up their work willingly, without legal compulsion, and earn their living through it. The result is that most needs are met for a modest price. The material gives the examples of a hotel meal and a taxi, where the food and the vehicle each bring together the work of many people from many countries.

The material then contrasts this with systems that take the choice of work away from people through force. Such systems, it says, cost the freedom of the people involved and did not prove economical. The same argument holds that cooperation has a darker side. If it is used for theft, robbery, killing and destruction, the same power of cooperation can damage the whole order. For that reason, people in the past formed groups and nations based on ideology and kinship for their own security.

### The formation of separate nations

The material, quoting 'Abd al-Karim al-Shahristani, gives an account of how people came to think of themselves as separate nations. At first, when the population was small, people living in the four directions regarded themselves as four nations. Later, nationalism grew on the basis of lineage, family and tribe. Arabia was organised on this basis, and tribes went to war over these ties. The material says that a similar division of castes still persisted in parts of India. It then describes how modern nations were formed on the basis of region, language and homeland, and how this division also reached the Muslim world.

The material then gives the Qur'an's answer. The surah an-Nisa' says that all human beings come from one father and one mother. At his last Hajj the Prophet ﷺ declared that no Arab is better than a non-Arab, and no white person is better than a black person. Superiority depends only on taqwā. The Qur'an calls believers brothers [49:10], and it draws the line of kinship at faith rather than blood. Abu Jahl and Abu Lahab were cut off from the Prophet ﷺ, while Bilal from Ethiopia and Suhayb from Byzantium were joined to him. The verse "He it is who created you, then some of you are disbelievers and some are believers" [64:2] is read as the basic division of mankind. The material says that at Badr, Uhud, the Ahzab and Hunayn, close relatives who stayed away from Allah and His Messenger found that their bond had broken.

### Why the verse is a warning about crime

The material adds a practical warning. Those who see a crime and keep silent, or refuse to give true witness when a crime needs to be proven, are helping the criminal. The material says this is a serious sin and a clear disobedience of the command to help one another in righteousness. Ibn Abi Hatim and other reports carry the same warning about helping the unjust. The material also says that real change does not come from parades or training alone. It comes through education in righteousness and the fear of Allah, which is the way to make people promote good and oppose evil.

### In short

- The verse protects the signs of Allah, including the sacred months, the offerings, the garlands and the pilgrims, and forbids all three forms of violating them.
- It forbids revenge against people who once blocked the believers, because injustice must not be answered with injustice.
- It sets righteousness and the fear of Allah as the basis for all cooperation, including cooperation among Muslims.
- The material links it to the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, the case of al-Hutam, and the wider principle of justice toward enemies.
- It ends with a warning: be mindful of Allah, because His punishment is severe.
