<<<VERSE 5:2>>>
Allah commands believers to respect what He has made sacred and to remain just even toward people who have wronged them. The verse joins these duties in a rule for common action: "Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness, and do not cooperate in sin and transgression." Shared anger cannot make wrongdoing right. Religious commitment must govern both the purpose of an action and the help given to carry it out.

The immediate setting concerns pilgrimage, sacred months, offerings and people travelling to the Sacred House. Muslims had themselves been prevented from reaching the Sacred Mosque. Their hurt was real, but it did not give them permission to violate sacred things or retaliate unjustly. The covenant to obey Allah includes restraint when obedience is difficult, not only when feelings are calm.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! Do not violate Allah’s rituals ˹of pilgrimage˺"** calls those who believe in Allah and follow His Messenger ﷺ to honour the things He has marked out. Sha'a'ir is the plural of sha'irah, a sign or symbol. Here it includes religious rites and especially the rites of pilgrimage. Ibn 'Abbas explains it through Hajj. Mujahid mentions the offering and the places As-Safa and Al-Marwah. Another explanation includes Allah's prohibitions: they must not be treated as though they were permissions.

**"the sacred months"** requires respect for the times Allah has made inviolable. The months named are Rajab, Muharram, Dhu al-Hijjah and Dhu al-Qa'dah. The immediate instruction forbids fighting in them and reinforces the duty to avoid sin. The discussion of whether the fighting restriction remained in force needs to be distinguished from the honour of the months themselves.

**"the sacrificial animals"** refers to the hady, animals brought as offerings to the Sacred House. They must not be seized or prevented from reaching the place where they are to be sacrificed. Their dedication is not to be ignored merely because someone wants to use or take them.

**"the ˹offerings decorated with˺ garlands"** singles out the visible marks identifying sacrificial animals. Qala'id is the plural of qiladah, a garland or neckband. Such marks protect the offering by making its purpose known. The prohibition includes interference with the marked animals and, in one explanation, with those who put on the garlands.

**"nor those ˹pilgrims˺ on their way to the Sacred House"** protects people travelling toward Allah's House. They must not be attacked, frightened, obstructed or caused pain on their way. The command concerns the dignity of the journey, not a claim that every traveller's beliefs are correct.

**"seeking their Lord’s bounty and pleasure."** Bounty is explained as provision obtained through trade. Pleasure refers to the purpose of pilgrimage and drawing near to Allah. Ibn 'Abbas gives that explanation of pleasure. The description includes what pagan pilgrims themselves claimed to seek, even though their claim to Allah's approval was mistaken because of their disbelief.

**"When pilgrimage has ended, you are allowed to hunt."** Release from ihram, the sacred state of pilgrimage, removes its prohibition on hunting. The command gives permission rather than an obligation to hunt. A fuller explanation also requires departure from the Sanctuary, whose restrictions remain distinct from ihram. Another gloss associates the release with the conclusion of the days of Tashriq.

**"Do not let the hatred of a people who once barred you from the Sacred Mosque provoke you to transgress."** Shana'an means hatred or hostility, as Ibn 'Abbas and others explain. The word is also read shan'an. Neither reading changes the warning: resentment must not drive believers into aggression, killing or other injustice. Being wronged does not cancel Allah's limits.

**"Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness"** commands help in al-birr, doing good, and at-taqwa, keeping away from evil out of awareness of Allah. The two expressions join positive obedience with restraint from what He forbids. Cooperation is judged by its object, not simply by the identity of those asking for help.

**"and do not cooperate in sin and transgression."** Ithm includes sin and disobedience, whether in worship or in duties toward people. 'Udwan is crossing a limit, including injustice and aggression. Ibn Jarir explains ithm here as neglecting Allah's commands, and transgression as exceeding the boundaries He has set for religion and human dealings. The form ta'awanu is shortened from tata'awanu by dropping one of its initial ta letters. It still expresses mutual help.

**"And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment"** brings every part of the command back to accountability. Fear of Him means holding to obedience and avoiding disobedience. His punishment is a serious warning to anyone who refuses His commands or treats His restrictions lightly.

### What happened at Hudaybiyah?

In the sixth year after the Hijrah, the migration, the Prophet ﷺ set out for 'Umrah with more than a thousand Companions. They entered ihram intending worship, not an attack. At Hudaybiyah, near Makkah, he sent word explaining their purpose and asking that they be allowed to perform the pilgrimage.

The Makkans refused and required difficult conditions for a treaty. The Muslims were to leave ihram and return without performing the 'Umrah for which they had come. They could return the following year without arms, remain for only three days, perform their rites and depart. Other conditions also weighed heavily on the Companions. They nevertheless obeyed the Prophet ﷺ and returned peacefully.

The missed pilgrimage was performed in Dhu al-Qa'dah of the seventh year, with the treaty's conditions observed. The earlier refusal had left pain and resentment. That emotional setting helps explain why the verse insists that even a serious injury must not become permission for a new injustice.

Zayd ibn Aslam reports another scene at Hudaybiyah, recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim. While the Muslims were distressed at being denied entry, a group of idolaters coming from the east passed by on their way to 'Umrah. Some Companions proposed stopping them as their fellow idolaters had stopped the Muslims. The instruction not to violate the pilgrims' sanctity answered that proposal. Those passing pilgrims were not to be attacked because other people had barred the Muslims.

A further account looks to the conquest of Makkah in Ramadan of the eighth year after the Hijrah. The Prophet ﷺ released the Makkans without taking revenge. They continued their practices, including their pagan pilgrimage customs. Some Companions considered retaliating now that power had changed hands. Why should people who had stopped a rightful Muslim pilgrimage be allowed to carry out their own rites?

The answer is that religious obligations do not change into their opposite when power changes hands. Muslims must honour Allah's signs even when an enemy has failed to do so. The background accounts are related through 'Ikrimah and al-Suddi by Ibn Jarir. They explain the temptation to retaliation without making it legitimate.

### Who was the man with the stolen animals?

Another account concerns a man called al-Hutam, also named Hatim ibn Hind or Al-Hutam bin Hind Al-Bakri in the reports. One narration from Ibn 'Abbas identifies him as Sharih ibn Dubay'ah al-Kindi, from al-Yamamah. A related gloss names Shurayh Ibn Dabi'ah and the pilgrims of the clan of Bakr Ibn Wa'il. These are the forms of the names supplied in the accounts.

He came to Madinah with trading goods. After selling them, he left his baggage and attendant outside and visited the Prophet ﷺ alone. He appeared interested in Islam. Asked what he called people to, the Prophet ﷺ explained worship of Allah alone, the establishment of prayer and payment of the poor due.

The man replied that these things were good but that he had chiefs he must consult. Perhaps he would embrace Islam and return with them. His apparent interest was deceptive. Before his arrival, the Prophet ﷺ had warned his Companions that a man would come speaking with the devil's tongue. After the visitor left, he described him as having arrived in disbelief and departed treacherously, not as a Muslim.

Outside Madinah the man drove away livestock belonging to its people. They learned of the theft too late to catch him. Later he appeared among pilgrims, with the stolen animals now garlanded as offerings for the Ka'bah. The Muslims recognised the man and the animals and wanted to attack him, kill him and recover what he had taken.

The accounts place this encounter differently. One associates it with the journey of Hudaybiyah. Another places it during the seventh-year journey to make up the missed 'Umrah, when the Companions heard the talbiyah, the pilgrimage response, being recited. The common point is the instruction that the offerings and the pilgrims must not be violated in the proposed act of revenge.

The fact that the man had committed theft does not make theft harmless. The issue here is the retaliation the Muslims were contemplating in this sacred setting. Allah's signs were to be respected even where those presenting the offerings professed a religion other than Islam. Anger at the offender was not permission to disregard that command.

### How are Allah's signs violated?

A sign is something perceptible that points beyond itself. Religious signs are recognised practices that express religious belonging. Prayer, the adhan or call to prayer, Hajj, circumcision and the beard worn according to the Sunnah are given as examples. The Sunnah is the Prophet's ﷺ established way.

Hasan al-Basri and 'Ata' explain Allah's signs broadly as the obligations whose limits have been set by the revealed law. Al-Jassas regards that explanation as gathering the different meanings together. On this understanding, the warning is not confined to a few objects. It concerns the way Allah's appointed duties and boundaries are treated.

Their sanctity can be violated in three ways. A person may reject an obligation altogether. He may obey only selected parts, leaving the required act incomplete. Or he may push past its appointed boundary. The command rules out all three. Choosing what is convenient while disregarding the rest is not complete observance.

The Qur'an connects honouring Allah's signs with piety within the heart (22:32). Outward conduct therefore matters because it expresses something inward. Respect is not simply a visible performance detached from obedience.

Pilgrimage gives particular examples. Restrictions apply to a person in ihram, including the wearing of ordinary clothing as described in the legal gloss. The Sanctuary also has restrictions, including hunting. A pilgrim must not confuse release from one restriction with release from every restriction attached to a place or rite.

### What remains sacred about the months?

The prohibition of fighting in the sacred month is reinforced by the declaration that fighting then is a grave transgression (2:217). Avoiding other sins receives added emphasis in a sacred time too. A holy period is not honoured merely by naming it while acting carelessly within it.

Abu Bakrah reports that the Prophet ﷺ addressed the matter during the Farewell Hajj. Al-Bukhari records the report in his Sahih. The Prophet ﷺ explained that the ordering of time had returned to its condition when Allah created the heavens and earth. The year contains twelve months, with four sacred ones.

Three follow one another: Dhu al-Qa'dah leads into Dhu al-Hijjah and then Muharram. Rajab stands separately. The report identifies it as the Rajab recognised by Mudar, lying between Jumada Ath-Thaniyah and Sha'ban. This testimony is used to affirm the continuing sacred character of these months.

There is also a legal account that describes the prohibition on fighting during them as later abrogated, a view attributed to an overwhelming agreement of scholars. That statement and the continuing sanctity of the months address related but distinguishable matters. The months retain their honoured character even within the account that treats a particular fighting restriction as superseded. The different legal statements should not be silently reduced to one.

### Why mark and protect the offerings?

The hady is an offering brought to the Sacred House, Al-Ka'bah. Its dedication must be respected. Taking it by force or stopping it from reaching the Haram, the sacred precinct, violates that purpose. Another legal explanation also prohibits diverting the animal to ordinary use, such as riding it or taking its milk instead of preserving its dedication to sacrifice.

Garlands identify the animal visibly. They may be made from material taken from trees near the Sanctuary. Someone who might otherwise interfere can see that this is an offering and leave it alone. The sign thus protects the act of worship toward which the animal is being brought.

The instruction is also explained as a warning not to abandon bringing offerings and marking them. These practices honour Allah's signs. Their visibility may encourage others to do the same. A person who leads another toward good receives a reward corresponding to the good done, without taking away the other person's reward.

The Prophet's ﷺ own preparation for Hajj illustrates care for the offering. He spent the night at Dhu al-Hulayfah, also called Wadi Al-'Aqiq. The next morning he visited his wives, who numbered nine at that time. He performed ghusl, a full ritual bath, used perfume and prayed two rak'ahs, two units of prayer. He then garlanded the sacrificial animals and announced his intention for Hajj and 'Umrah.

His offerings included more than sixty camels. They were healthy and well chosen, rather than inferior animals disposed of reluctantly. The connection with honouring Allah's signs is direct: reverence appears in the quality of what is presented, not only in the claim that it has been presented.

Muqatil bin Hayyan gives a further background to garlands, reported by Ibn Abi Hatim. Before Islam, travellers used animal hair or skins as marks of safe passage outside the sacred months. People from the Sacred House's precinct used material from its trees. The marks signalled a claim to protection and were not to be casually violated.

### Were all the protections left unchanged?

Ibn Abi Hatim records from Ibn 'Abbas that two provisions in this surah were abrogated. One is the provision concerning the garlands. The other is the permission given to the Prophet ﷺ either to judge between those who approached him or to turn away. The broader introductory discussion of the surah's final rulings must be read alongside this report about particular provisions.

Another explanation states that the protection here for pagan pilgrims seeking the House was superseded by the declaration of dissociation in Surah al-Tawba. The printed reference attached to that statement is malformed, so no precise verse number is supplied here. The account is preserved without assigning it a reference from memory.

These qualifications concern the history of particular rulings. They do not make hostility a source of law. The prohibition of unjust aggression and the obligation to honour Allah's commands remain the governing principles in the verse's explanation.

### Why mention trade alongside pilgrimage?

People coming to the Sacred House may seek provision through commerce as well as seek Allah's approval through worship. The reference to bounty is therefore not treated as a denial of their pilgrimage purpose. Another cited verse allows seeking the Lord's bounty during pilgrimage by trading.

Among those who explain bounty as trade are Qatadah and Mujahid, together with 'Ata' and Abu Al-'Aliyah. The explanation is also given by Muqatil bin Hayyan, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Mutarrif bin 'Abdullah and 'Abdullah bin 'Ubayd bin 'Umayr. Ibn 'Abbas explains the accompanying reference to pleasure through the purpose of Hajj.

The two aims must not be confused. Provision names what travellers seek through their commerce. Allah's pleasure names their religious intention. Where the pilgrims are idolaters, the description states what they seek or claim, not that their beliefs have therefore been approved.

### Does the command to hunt make hunting obligatory?

The prohibition in ihram is temporary. Once a person leaves that condition, hunting returns to its permitted status, subject also to the limits of the Sanctuary. The words do not require every former pilgrim to hunt.

This illustrates a wider question about a command given after a prohibition. One explanation says that such a command restores the earlier ruling. If an act had been obligatory, the prior obligation returns. If it had been recommended or merely permitted, that earlier status returns instead.

On this reasoning, neither of two blanket rules is adequate: that every command after a prohibition makes the act obligatory, or that every such command makes it merely optional. The earlier ruling and the evidence matter. Here the result is permission to hunt, not a fresh duty to do so.

### How far must justice extend toward an enemy?

The verse names a particularly painful wrong: being prevented from the Sacred Mosque. Yet it forbids using that injury as an excuse for aggression. The later command to remain just despite hatred states the same standard (5:8). Justice is due to everyone, not only to friends.

A person may appear obedient while circumstances are easy, then abandon restraint when opposed or humiliated. He begins to think only of defeating and degrading the opponent. That change reveals why the command is difficult. The test is not whether justice is admired in calm moments, but whether it governs action when anger offers an excuse to discard it.

Supporting the resentful person in oppression would be cooperation in sin. Supporting what is right may instead require refusing someone powerful, prestigious or personally close. The worldly advantages of joining that person do not alter the duty. Success or failure in this hard choice bears on a person's final outcome.

The Muslims' earlier restraint at Hudaybiyah and their later possession of power make the lesson concrete. Having suffered exclusion did not authorise them to repeat injustice against others. Allah's command, rather than a balance of grievances, determines what they may do.

### Why does human life need cooperation?

No individual can provide everything required for life alone. Food involves growing, gathering, processing and preparing. Clothing involves producing fibre, making cloth and turning it into something wearable. Transport and housing also depend on work that no solitary person can perform in full. Intelligence, wealth and strength do not remove this dependence.

The rich need people whose labour and skill make their property useful. Workers need the earnings that their work brings. Traders depend on buyers and buyers on traders. A householder needs builders and people trained in different crafts, while those workers need the demand for their skills. Mutual need joins people whose lives would otherwise fall apart.

The dependence continues at death. Others prepare the body and arrange burial. Afterward, prayers for forgiveness and acts whose reward reaches the deceased still involve the living. Cooperation is not limited to earning and consuming during one's lifetime.

This arrangement is presented as part of Allah's wisdom. Different inclinations draw people toward different tasks. A person takes up a craft or service as a means of livelihood, and that individual purpose also meets someone else's need. The system does not have to wait for every participant to possess extraordinary selflessness.

Nor could a written rule alone produce all the willing effort required. Laws may exist while bribery, favouritism, neglect and indifference govern actual practice. Assigning everyone a trade by government order would not by itself make people content with it. The natural distribution of interests and abilities supplies something compulsion cannot easily replace.

A meal in a hotel illustrates the hidden breadth of cooperation. Ingredients and seasonings may come from many lands. Furniture, dishes and utensils depend on other workers. The people managing, cooking and serving contribute their own parts. A small mouthful is the end of a process involving people, animals and machines far beyond the dining room.

Transport offers another illustration. Someone who cannot walk the distance, or lacks the time, finds a bus or a hired vehicle. Its parts, assembly and operation involve workers from many places. Paying the fare gives access to all that prior work. Money would be of little use if the cooperative system that makes goods and services available did not exist.

### What is the warning about compelled labour?

The social argument contrasts this pattern with the compulsory assignment of work in socialist countries. It describes the killing and imprisonment of thousands and the forcing of survivors into work as though they were machine parts. It concedes that production sometimes increased, but judges the loss of freedom too costly.

This is the historical economic argument being made, not an independent assessment of every economic arrangement. Its central contrast is between work accepted through inclination and need, and work imposed through coercion. People may actively seek difficult or humble employment when they choose it. The same people may try to escape if ordered into it by force.

The explanation therefore describes people as both free and constrained: free in taking up their work, yet directed by the inclinations and needs through which society's work is distributed. The constraint is not experienced in the same way as an external command backed by force. This distinction is part of the argument for recognising Allah's wisdom in mutual dependence.

### Can cooperation itself become destructive?

Yes. Cooperation magnifies what people undertake together, whether good or evil. Organised theft, robbery, killing and destruction can damage society more severely than isolated wrongdoing. A capacity necessary for human survival becomes dangerous when its object is wrong.

The response has often been to organise groups for protection against other groups. Shared identity then becomes the basis on which people promise assistance. But unless justice governs that assistance, the group may defend its own wrongdoers and treat outsiders as people without rights.

'Abd al-Karim al-Shahristani gives an account of early divisions in Al-Milal wa al-Nihal. He describes four initial groupings by direction: north and south, east and west. With growing populations came divisions through family, ancestry and tribe. In Arabia, such ties could define solidarity and become grounds for war. Banu Tamim, Banu Khuza'ah and Banu Hashim are examples of distinct tribal groupings.

The discussion also points to caste distinctions in India as an example of inherited ranking. It then criticises the replacement of genealogical divisions by modern European forms of territorial and linguistic nationalism. The objection is not that people have different languages or homes. It is that these differences become the final rule for loyalty and cooperation.

Muslims are included in the criticism. Wider labels such as Turks, Arabs, Sindhis and Iraqis are followed by further divisions, including Syrians, Egyptians, Najdis, Hijazis, Bangalis and Panjabis. Regional and provincial loyalties can enter government and ordinary relationships so deeply that people cease to ask whether the action they are supporting is right.

### What replaces racial or tribal superiority?

The opening of Surah al-Nisa' recalls humanity's descent from one father and mother. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ stated in his final Hajj address that neither Arab ancestry nor white skin gives superiority over non-Arab ancestry or black skin. The criterion is taqwa, awareness of Allah expressed in obedience.

The declaration of believers' brotherhood makes that principle a bond (49:10). People from Ethiopia, Byzantium and Turkey stand with Arab believers, including those of Qurayshi and Hashmi descent. Shared faith joins people whom ancestry would keep apart. Bilal from Ethiopia and Suhayb from Byzantium belong within this bond, while the family relationship of Abu Lahab and Abu Jahl does not give them the same religious loyalty to the Prophet ﷺ.

The Qur'an also describes Allah creating people who then divide into believers and disbelievers (64:2). The battles of Badr, Uhud, Ahzab and Hunayn are given as settings where religious allegiance could stand across a blood relationship. Even brothers could find themselves on opposing sides. The point is that ancestry did not override the demands of faith in those conflicts.

Yet the verse's rule for cooperation is not simply "help Muslims and refuse everyone else". Its stated criteria are goodness and piety. Even a Muslim brother must not receive help in falsehood or aggression. Conversely, the invitation to cooperate in good is presented as a basis on which people can act together, rather than as a licence for collective selfishness.

### How can an oppressor be helped?

Anas bin Malik reports that the Prophet ﷺ instructed believers to help their brother whether he was wronged or committing wrong. The Companions understood helping a victim. They asked how helping an oppressor could be right. He explained that restraining him from oppression is the help he needs.

Ahmad and Al-Bukhari record this teaching. It is also cited here with attribution to Al-Bukhari and Muslim. The meaning is not permission to assist both sides indiscriminately. The victim needs protection from injustice, while the oppressor needs to be stopped from causing it. Both forms of help serve justice.

Preventing someone's wrongdoing protects his present life and his future before Allah. Joining him in it may feel loyal, but it helps to ruin him. True brotherhood can therefore require refusal, correction and restraint rather than agreement.

Another report recorded by Ahmad describes the believer who lives among people and patiently bears their annoyance as earning more reward than the believer who withdraws and does not bear it. Cooperation requires patience with others. Refusing complicity in wrongdoing is not the same as abandoning all social responsibility.

### What reward or blame follows from influencing others?

A report attributed to the Prophet ﷺ and cited through al-Bazzar says that directing another person toward good earns a reward like that of doing the good oneself. The benefit of guidance extends beyond the immediate act of speaking.

Muslim records the teaching that someone who calls others toward guidance receives a reward corresponding to theirs, without reducing their reward. Someone who calls toward error carries a burden corresponding to the wrongdoing that follows, without relieving those who commit it. The teaching is also cited with attribution to Al-Bukhari in the discussion. Its consequences continue until the Day of Resurrection.

These reports explain why cooperation cannot be treated as morally neutral. An organiser, encourager or helper may not perform the final act personally, but still helps bring it about. The result may be shared reward or shared blame.

A report cited through Tabarani gives an especially severe warning: joining an unjust person to assist his injustice is described as going outside Islam's fold. This is a warning as transmitted, not an independent judgment upon a named person. It underlies the caution of righteous elders who avoided service in the courts of oppressive rulers because of the help such service could give to wrongdoing.

The declaration refusing to be a helper of criminals provides another point of reference (28:17). In its discussion appears a report of a call on the Day of Judgment for oppressors and their assistants. Even those who prepared their pens and inkpots are included. The report pictures them confined in a steel coffin and thrown into Hell. Its force lies in the warning that apparently small services can sustain grave injustice.

### Why is this not only a duty for governments?

The command aims to make each person an active supporter of good and a barrier against wrongdoing. Fear of Allah should operate whether another human being is watching or not. The Companions and their Successors are presented as examples of the social effect of such formation.

A comparison is made with civil defence. A country threatened by war may train its population to help protect it. Yet public life also needs people trained to promote good and resist crime. Marches and drills alone cannot cultivate that character. Education has to shape judgment, honesty, sympathy and responsibility.

The social criticism here is that institutions neglect spiritual and moral formation while relying on legal machinery to repair the damage. If a whole population abandons concern for the lawful and unlawful, police cannot supply the missing conscience on their own. Those in power also have a duty to adopt righteousness rather than expect public order while disregarding it themselves.

The argument names theft, violent robbery, sexual offences, killing and destruction as consequences that legal systems struggle to contain when moral responsibility collapses. Its proposed remedy is not merely another regulation. It is the adoption of goodness and taqwa as aims of public and private life.

Ordinary people must not treat the removal of crime as someone else's responsibility. Concealing a criminal's wrongdoing or withholding truthful testimony can assist the crime. Such conduct violates the command to cooperate in good and avoid cooperation in sin. The witness and the bystander therefore have responsibilities alongside the ruler and the official.

### In short

- Allah's rites, sacred times, offerings and pilgrims must be treated according to His commands, not according to personal resentment.
- Being barred from the Sacred Mosque did not authorise unjust retaliation. Justice binds believers even when an enemy has acted unjustly.
- Cooperation sustains human life, but can also magnify crime. Its proper basis is goodness and restraint from evil, not race, tribe, territory or blind group loyalty.
- Helping an oppressor means stopping the oppression. Influencing or assisting others can bring shared reward or shared blame.
- Responsibility reaches individuals as well as governments. Truthful testimony, moral education and refusal to enable wrongdoing are all part of cooperating in righteousness.
