<<<VERSE 2:243>>>
The verse makes fear of death answerable to Allah's power: "Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death?" Their flight did not secure life. Allah caused them to die and then restored them. The event is presented as a sign of resurrection and a warning against imagining that human plans can overrule the appointed end of life.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen"** The incident belonged to a time before the Prophet ﷺ. Seeing here means knowing with insight, described as ru'yah al-qalb, vision of the heart. The question invites amazement and attention. Revelation makes the event certain, as though it stood before the listener's eyes.

**"those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death?"** They are identified as a group among the Children of Israel. The most extended account describes escape from plague. Another describes fighters fleeing because they feared being killed. Reports differ about their number. The verse itself gives thousands, not a single exact figure.

**"Allah said to them, “Die!” then He gave them life."** Neither their numbers nor their change of location prevented the decree. The command may be understood as direct or conveyed through angels. Their restoration shows that the One who causes death can bring bodies and souls together again. The discussion connects the effectiveness of His command with (36:82).

**"Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful"** Restoring the group was a favour to them. Telling later people of the event is also a favour, because it supplies a lesson and evidence. Yet many receive worldly and religious benefits without acknowledging their Giver.

### What details are given about the group?

Ibn 'Abbas identifies their settlement as Dawardan. 'Ali bin 'Asim describes it as a village several miles from Wasit in Iraq. Waki' bin Jarrah transmits an account giving four thousand people who sought a land free of death. Other reports give eight, ten, thirty, forty or seventy thousand. One grammatical explanation treats the plural uluf as a form of multitude pointing to at least ten thousand. That explanation must remain distinct from reports giving lower numbers.

The extended story describes an epidemic in a place whose conditions had become harmful. The people fled to an open, fertile valley between mountains. Two angels, at opposite ends, gave a cry, and all died together. The neighbouring people could not readily shroud and bury so many. The area was enclosed with walls or branches, and the bodies eventually decayed to bones.

Hizqil (Ezekiel), a prophet among the Children of Israel, later passed the place. He learned what had happened and asked Allah to restore them. He was directed to call the scattered bones together. The skeletons reassembled. A further command clothed them with flesh, nerves and skin. Then the souls were commanded to return to their former bodies. The people stood alive, looked around in amazement and praised Allah, affirming that none besides Him deserved worship.

One account says the return occurred after eight days, while another says eight days or longer. Another describes a long interval and full decomposition. The details are transmitted accounts with differences, not one undisputed chronology. A further report says the traces of death remained, so their garments became shrouds, with an effect continuing among descendants. Its unusual character is not a reason to remove it or to raise its certainty beyond a reported explanation.

### What does the reassembling of bones show?

The event supports bodily resurrection, not merely survival of a memory or an abstract influence. Bones, flesh and souls are restored by Allah's command. The account also reflects on the response of apparently lifeless things to His direction. It connects that response with (20:50), which speaks of Allah giving each thing its form and guidance.

A poem attributed here to Maulana Rumi pictures dust, air, water and fire as dead to ordinary human perception but alive before Allah. The lesson concerns their obedience within the capacities He gives them. Human inability to recognise that response does not limit His command.

The sign is generous guidance, yet seeing a sign does not automatically produce gratitude. An inward reading says that those without sharpened insight into tawhid, Allah's oneness, may fail to benefit even from direct evidence in their own bodies. Strong faith in the unseen can recognise the truth without demanding that every believer personally witness a restoration of the dead.

### What does the teaching about plague require?

Trust in decree is not an instruction to ignore the Prophet's ﷺ rules of precaution. Ahmad records an authentic account from Ibn 'Abbas of 'Umar bin Al-Khattab travelling towards Ash-Sham, Syria. At Sargh he met Abu 'Ubaydah bin Jarrah and other commanders, who reported plague in the region. The epidemic is described as beginning at 'Amawas near Baytul-Maqdis, with many Companions and later believers dying in it.

'Umar consulted those present. 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf, who had been away on business, then supplied the prophetic instruction: do not enter a land when plague is known to be there, and do not leave an affected land in flight from it. 'Umar thanked Allah and turned back. The account also appears in the Sahihayn, Al-Bukhari and Muslim.

When Abu 'Ubaydah questioned whether this was flight from destiny, 'Umar answered that they were moving from Allah's decree to Allah's decree. He was obeying an instruction within that decree, not claiming power to cancel it. The exchange, also discussed through al-Qurtubi, rejects a false opposition between trust and prescribed precaution.

Another wording describes plague as a punishment sent upon earlier peoples, with a remainder that withdraws and returns. The instruction still forbids entering an affected land or fleeing one solely to escape the disease. This is the religious teaching given in the material, not a substitution of an invented account of its origin.

### What wisdom is given for those restrictions?

Avoiding entry protects people from foreseeable harm. Taking care of life is itself a duty. It also guards against the mistaken conclusion that someone's death could have been prevented merely by changing location when the appointed term had arrived. Precaution and belief in decree are meant to stand together.

Restraining panic-driven departure has collective benefits. Wealthier people might leave those unable to travel alone, terrified and without anyone to care for the sick or bury the dead. Travel can also burden people already carrying illness. A sick traveller may have neither care nor safety on the road. Ibn al-Madini quotes a warning that one who runs from an epidemic does not remain safe. Finally, those carrying the disease may spread it elsewhere.

'A'ishah al-Siddiqah asked the Prophet ﷺ about plague. Al-Bukhari records through Yahya ibn Ya'mur his explanation that what had been punishment became mercy for believers who remained patiently in their locality, knowing that only what Allah had decreed could reach them. Such a person receives reward equal to a martyr. The related teaching describes death from plague as shahadah, martyrdom. Its reward is connected with patience and trust, not with deliberately seeking illness.

The wording about leaving in flight also produces exceptions in the legal explanation. Departure for another pressing need is not treated as the same act. A change of climate accompanied by firm recognition that destiny cannot be escaped is also excepted there. Entry for a necessary purpose with the same belief is described as permissible. These are the qualifications the material gives, and must not be omitted from the restriction.

### How does this prepare the next command?

The story introduces courage in Allah's cause and then spending for it in (2:245). It denies that flight guarantees survival. The related warnings in (3:168) and (4:77-78) concern refusing struggle in the belief that staying away controls the time of death. Their application is developed under the next verse rather than repeated at length here.

Another historical reading connects the passage with the Israelites' defeat by the Philistines. More than four thousand were slain, fear spread, and they lost their settled confidence for twenty years. Samuel called them to leave foreign gods, return wholeheartedly to their Lord and serve Him alone. The account cites the biblical passage in 1 Samuel 7:3, followed by 7:10, where thunder confounded their enemies. These are biblical references in that account, not Qur'anic citations with those numbers.

This reading pictures abandoning trust as the moral death of a nation and returning to trust as its regeneration. It sits alongside the bodily resurrection account rather than removing it. Both warn against treating fear itself as wise deliverance.

### In short

- Flight cannot overrule Allah's appointed term of life, and restoration of the dead shows His power to resurrect.
- The reported details differ in number, setting and duration and should not be presented as one fixed chronology.
- Trust in decree accompanies the prophetic restrictions and qualifications concerning plague, not neglect of precaution.
- Signs call for gratitude, while courage and generosity are developed in the following verses.

<<<VERSE 2:244>>>
The command follows the warning that flight cannot guarantee life: "Fight in the cause of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing." Fear of death must not be used to abandon a duty Allah has imposed. His hearing reaches what people say, and His knowledge reaches the motives and deeds behind their declarations.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Fight in the cause of Allah,"** The purpose is obedience to Allah, supporting His path and upholding His religion. The command is not described as fighting simply for personal possessions or prestige. One explanation addresses the earlier people after they had been restored to life. Another directs the believers hearing the passage to fulfil their duty.

**"and know that Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing"** Allah hears commitments, excuses and cries of pain. He knows intention, action and the condition of those carrying out the command. He will recompense obedience and knows the wrongdoing of someone who refuses what is required.

### Why does the earlier story matter here?

The people who fled could not secure life by running. The following command applies that lesson to jihad, struggle and fighting in Allah's cause. Entering the struggle is not itself control over the moment of death, and refusing it does not extend the appointed term. The time of death and the provision allotted to a person remain under Allah's decree.

This is why the passage first gives a striking account and then issues the command. It is meant to loosen the hold of a particular excuse: the assumption that obedience is a journey into death while disobedience purchases safety. The warning is directed against desertion out of fear, with special exempted circumstances acknowledged as treated elsewhere. No extra conditions or unmentioned exceptions are invented here.

In (3:168), those who stayed behind claim that the slain would have lived had they listened to them. The answer challenges them to avert death from themselves if that confidence is true. The related account in (4:77-78) concerns reluctance once fighting is prescribed and the reminder that death reaches people even in fortified places. These passages support the same correction, not a claim that worldly dangers do not exist.

### What circumstances faced the Muslims?

The Muslims left Makkah for Madinah under severe persecution. There they could live according to their religion, but opponents continued armed attacks intended to uproot them. Compared with their enemies, they had fewer people and fewer resources. Their disadvantage could discourage them from meeting the attack.

The historical lesson therefore concerns facing a real threat without letting fear itself become defeat. The separate account of the Israelites and the Philistines recalls Samuel's summons to renewed trust. Its references to 1 Samuel 7:3 and 7:10 belong to that biblical account. Returning to sincere service, rather than surrendering to despair, is its moral emphasis.

The passage links iman, faith, with obedience under pressure. A declaration of trust that collapses whenever obedience is costly does not fulfil the same purpose. At the same time, the preceding discussion of 'Umar, 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf and Abu 'Ubaydah bin Jarrah shows that carrying out a protective prophetic instruction is itself obedience. Courage must not be confused with ignoring the rule one has been given.

### What does Khalid's final illness illustrate?

Abu Sulayman, Khalid bin Al-Walid, had spent his life in battles and was known as the Sword of Allah. Near death he lamented that there was scarcely a part of his body without a mark from an arrow, spear or sword, yet he was dying in bed rather than as a martyr in battle.

One version compares his death in bed to a camel's, another to a donkey's. Both convey the bitterness he felt at not obtaining the end he had hoped for. He rebuked cowardice and wished that cowards would find no rest in their assumption that avoiding battle secured life.

The illustration does not claim that every fighter survives or that every person who stays home dies immediately. It shows the failure of a simplistic equation between exposure to battle and control over one's death. The much-wounded commander could still die away from the battlefield. The appointed end remained Allah's, not the enemy's or the fighter's to determine.

### How does Allah's hearing comfort those who suffer?

An inward reading hears the closing names as reassurance. If pain brings a cry, Allah hears it. He knows the person's condition and sees what is being endured. The command is not issued by One unaware of its cost.

A poetic saying contrasts those who want only ease with a person who wishes to bring the complaint to Allah, finding comfort in being heard. This is not a recommendation to seek unnecessary pain. It explains how knowledge of Allah's hearing can ease pain that comes while fulfilling a duty.

The next verse joins expenditure of wealth to the giving of life and effort. Its good loan in (2:245), and the image of multiplied spending in (2:261), develop the practical support of the cause. Courage and generosity belong to the same orientation towards Allah rather than being unrelated virtues.

### In short

- The command concerns fighting in obedience to Allah and for His cause, not personal prestige.
- Avoiding a prescribed struggle cannot guarantee escape from death.
- Khalid's death away from battle illustrates that the appointed end is not controlled by the amount of danger someone has faced.
- Allah hears both commitments and cries of pain and knows the intention behind action.

<<<VERSE 2:245>>>
Spending sincerely for Allah is pictured as a loan with a secure and multiplied return: "Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over?" Allah needs nothing from His servants. The image honours their giving and assures them of reward. Provision is in His hands, and their final return to Him makes generosity an investment beyond immediate worldly gain.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Who will lend to Allah a good loan"** Qard means a loan. Here it describes good deeds and spending in Allah's cause, willingly and for Him alone. Since everything already belongs to Allah, the language does not imply that He lacks resources. Another application concerns lending to people in need, whom the giver helps for Allah's sake.

**"which Allah will multiply many times over?"** The promised reward exceeds the amount given. Two verbal readings convey the multiplication. Explanations mention tenfold, seven hundredfold or more, while another gives a thousandfold return. The likeness in (2:261) further illustrates abundant reward. These expressions of increase are not collapsed into one fixed rate for every deed.

**"It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺."** Allah restricts or expands provision according to wisdom and justice. Both conditions can be tests. One explanation includes health alongside wealth. The believer should give without imagining that anxious hoarding is the final controller of resources.

**"And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned"** At the Resurrection, deeds receive their outcome. A charitable loss in immediate possession can therefore be a gain in the lasting account. The point is not a promise that every giver will instantly become richer in this world.

### Why use the language of a loan?

A loan is expected to be returned. The figure expresses confidence in recompense. A saying about Allah's descent in the last third of the night asks who will supplicate so that He may answer and who will lend to One who is neither needy nor unjust. An inward presentation describes His extending His hands in generous invitation. The report is described as sound, but no additional collector is attached where none is given.

Yahya Mu'adh wonders how someone can still cling to wealth when the Lord of the Throne invites a loan. The inward response extends beyond money: the heart and spirit belong to Allah and should not be reserved for another ultimate aim. The giver trusts both His ability and His justice.

Actual loans to people also carry merit. Ibn Majah records a saying that a Muslim lending to another Muslim receives reward like giving charity twice. Sadaqah means charitable giving. The teaching gives help in an hour of need a place within the verse's invitation.

A further report describes the growth of even a single date given for Allah until its reward exceeds Mount Uhud. Small material size does not restrict what Allah may make of a sincere deed.

### How did people react to the invitation?

Ibn al-'Arabi describes three responses. Some mocked it by saying that Allah was poor and they were rich. The answer in (3:181) records and condemns that statement. Others did not say the same thing but were held back by attachment and greed, becoming unable to give. A third group responded promptly with the best of what they possessed.

Abu al-Dahdah, from al-Ansar, the Helpers, exemplifies the third response. He asked the Prophet ﷺ why Allah invited a loan when He was free of need. The Prophet ﷺ explained the promised entry to Paradise through giving. Abu al-Dahdah offered his two date farms, his only property.

The Prophet ﷺ directed him to dedicate one as a waqf, an endowment for Allah's cause, and keep the other for his family. He gave the better farm, which contained six hundred date palms. His wife welcomed what he had done. The Prophet ﷺ spoke of abundant date trees and spacious dwellings awaiting him in Paradise. The account preserves both wholehearted generosity and provision for dependants. Giving everything away was not required as proof of sincerity.

The name also appears as Abu'l-Dahdah or Abu'l-Dahdahah in another explanation of the verse's setting. The variations identify the reported account rather than justify merging unrelated details from outside it.

### What distinction separates generous repayment from interest?

Returning more than was borrowed can be good conduct when no increase was made a condition of the loan. A prophetic saying praises the person who repays obligations well. That voluntary generosity is distinguished from a required premium.

If the lender stipulates an increase, the material calls it riba, interest, and haram, unlawful. The verse's promised increase from Allah does not authorise extracting a stipulated increase from a needy borrower. Divine reward and a human loan contract are not the same arrangement.

### What story illustrates generosity in the household of 'Ali?

An allusive account describes 'Ali Murtaza finding Hasan and Husayn crying before Fatima Zahra because they were hungry. She had put plain water in a pot to give the children hope that food was cooking. 'Ali sold a stored cloak for six dirhams and bought food.

Afterwards a nomad offered him a camel on credit for sixty dirhams, allowing payment when spoils or a treasury gift arrived. Another bought the camel from him for 120 dirhams. 'Ali planned to settle the original sixty and use the remainder. While looking for the first seller, he met the Prophet ﷺ, called Mustafa in the account.

The Prophet ﷺ told him that Gabriel had sold the camel, Michael had bought it and it was a camel from Paradise. The account connects this unexpected return with an earlier kindness to a poor person and the verse's good loan. It is transmitted as an illustrative story, without an added collector or grade or an invented intervening act to fill the account's gap.

The name Abu'l-Hasan appears as an address to 'Ali in the story. Its theme is relief given through a transaction rather than treating the hungry family as forgotten. The account's specific amounts remain part of the illustration, not a formula guaranteeing the same return to every lender.

### How are rich and poor both honoured?

The rich are honoured by the invitation to lend, an image of friendship. The poor are honoured because the loan is sought for their benefit. An inward reading even calls that second honour greater: someone may ask a loan from a person who is not a close friend, but asking for another person's sake expresses that person's dearness.

This makes the poor person more than an occasion for the wealthy person's reward. The invitation itself marks care for the person in need. Material setbacks can open a door to generosity and a blessing otherwise not reached. The verse asks the believer to see more than deprivation in a costly act undertaken for Allah.

The surrounding passage joins this spending with effort in His cause. The reminders in (3:168) and (4:77-78) reject fear-driven confidence in controlling death, while (2:261) points towards abundant recompense for wealth given sincerely. The shared lesson is trust expressed in action, not only claimed in words.

### In short

- The good loan is sincere spending and good action, not a suggestion that Allah is needy.
- Allah promises multiplied reward and controls the expansion and restriction of provision.
- Helping people through loans is meritorious, but a stipulated premium is distinguished from voluntary generous repayment.
- Abu al-Dahdah's account joins giving the better property with retaining support for family.
- Both giver and recipient are honoured within the invitation.

<<<VERSE 2:246>>>
A confident promise is tested when it becomes a duty: "Appoint for us a king, ˹and˺ we will fight in the cause of Allah." The chiefs of the Children of Israel asked their prophet for leadership after defeat and displacement. He questioned whether they would actually fight if commanded. They insisted that they would, but most withdrew when the order came. Allah knew the difference between their declaration and their conduct.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Have you not seen those chiefs of the Children of Israel after Moses?"** The chiefs are an assembly or council in a period after Musa (Moses). The question brings a revealed account to the hearer's attention. Mujahid identifies their prophet as Shamwil (Samuel).

**"They said to one of their prophets, “Appoint for us a king, ˹and˺ we will fight in the cause of Allah.”"** They wanted a leader to unite them, direct the army and decide common affairs. Their existing kingship had ended, while enemies controlled territory and people.

**"He said, “Are you not going to cower if ordered to fight?”"** Their prophet doubted that the promised determination would survive an actual obligation. Two readings of the relevant verb are mentioned, 'asaytum and 'asitum. The interrogative exposes an expected failure rather than simply encouraging their boast.

**"They replied, “How could we refuse to fight in the cause of Allah, while we were driven out of our homes and ˹separated from˺ our children?”"** They pointed to lost homes and children taken captive or killed. Jalut (Goliath) and his men are named in that explanation. The losses gave them a powerful reason to want their situation changed, but did not themselves guarantee obedience.

**"But when they were ordered to fight, they fled, except for a few of them."** Most abandoned the undertaking they had demanded. One account gives the faithful few as 313 men. Another identifies them with the people who later crossed the river with Talut (Saul). The details of that test belong to the following narrative.

**"And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers"** Allah knows the breach of their promise and refusal of His instruction. A strong public claim cannot conceal failure when the duty is presented.

### What history leads to the request?

Wahb bin Munabbih describes the Israelites remaining upright for a time after Musa, then introducing religious changes and, among some, worship of idols. Prophets continued commanding righteous deeds and ruling according to the Torah. Persistent wrongdoing was followed by military defeat, deaths, captivity and loss of land.

They had previously possessed the Torah and the Tabut, the inherited chest or Ark, associated with their victories and passed down since Musa. An enemy king captured both, and few remained who had memorised the Torah. The account describes prophecy becoming scarce among the tribes.

Only a pregnant woman remained in the line of Lavi (Levi) through which prophecy had appeared. Her husband had been killed. The people housed her in hope of a boy who could become their prophet. She repeatedly prayed for a son. The boy was named Shamwil, explained as Allah having heard her pleas. Another report gives Sham'un (Simeon), with a similar meaning.

He grew in righteousness, received revelation and called the people to Allah and tawhid, His oneness. They then asked him to appoint a king so that they could fight under one command. The loss of both leadership and religious fidelity forms the background, not merely a lack of military equipment.

Another account places renewed subjugation roughly three hundred years after Musa and lasting about a quarter-century before the desire for restored strength. The Amalekites are named in the explanation of their domination. These are the chronological and identifying details as reported, not a separate reconstruction of history.

### What does the loss of the Ark mean here?

The chest was associated with barakah, blessing, and the legacy of earlier prophets. Its capture symbolised the collapse the people wanted to reverse. The shared account describes Musa and other prophets placing it at the front of the army. The later story of its return is reserved for the verse that gives that sign, rather than repeated here.

The restoration of religious direction was therefore essential to the effort. The prophet was not simply hired to endorse a military programme. He called the people back to the Lord whose commands they had abandoned.

### Why did their stated reasons fail to produce resolve?

An inward reading sees them approaching the undertaking as their own chosen project. Once it became a binding command, laziness, desertion and indifference replaced their earlier confidence. Demanding a duty is easier than bearing it.

Their outward speech centred on defending their possessions, homes and children. These losses were real, but the reflection questions whether their motive was purely Allah's right. Had they emphasised that He was their Protector and that His command bound them, their resolve might have been different. The suggestion is qualified as a possible explanation, not a claim to know every individual's hidden motive.

The following choice of king tests them again. Selfishness can make people seek collective leadership while privately wanting the highest place for themselves. The narrative distinguishes an actual willingness to obey from an enthusiasm that lasts only while obedience agrees with one's preferences.

### In short

- The chiefs requested leadership and promised to fight after loss and captivity.
- Their prophet warned that a demanded undertaking could still be abandoned when made obligatory.
- Most withdrew, while a few kept their promise.
- The history connects military decline with religious departure and calls for sincerity deeper than anger over personal losses.

<<<VERSE 2:247>>>
Their request for a leader was answered, but not on the terms they expected: "Allah has appointed Saul to be your king." Talut (Saul) lacked the ancestry and wealth they thought entitled them to rule. Their prophet directed them instead to Allah's choice, Talut's knowledge and his strength. Leadership was not a possession the chiefs could reserve for their own families.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Their prophet told them, “Allah has appointed Saul to be your king.”"** Shamwil (Samuel) identified Allah as the appointing authority. He did not claim to have selected Talut merely on personal preference. The people were to unite under the appointed leader.

**"They protested, “How can he be our king when some of us are more deserving of kingship than he, and he has not been blessed with vast riches?”"** They relied on royal descent and wealth. Talut was not from the families associated with kings or prophets, and lacked the resources they expected a ruler to use in maintaining his kingdom and army.

**"He replied, “Allah has chosen him over you and blessed him with knowledge and stature."** Talut's qualifications included knowledge, especially of warfare, bodily strength, patience in combat and strength of character. One account calls him the most knowledgeable, handsome and complete in character among them. Another inward reading takes stature as substantial qualities of the self rather than physical size alone.

**"Allah grants kingship to whoever He wills."** Sovereignty belongs to Allah. His choice is governed by wisdom, not constrained by the chiefs' family claims. They answer to Him, rather than making His action depend on their approval.

**"And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”"** His gifts are wide enough to grant authority beyond the families that have possessed it. He knows who is suited to rule. Poverty does not make His provision or knowledge insufficient.

### What did the chiefs assume about Talut?

One account describes him as a soldier. Other reports say he carried water, dyed skins, worked as a tanner or was a shepherd. These are differing descriptions of his ordinary livelihood. They agree in denying the wealth and royal background on which the chiefs insisted.

Kingship had been associated with the offspring of Yahudha (Judah), while another explanation notes Talut's membership in Benjamin, the smallest tribe of Israel. The chiefs treated those differences as barriers even after their prophet announced Allah's appointment. Their complaint implied that the prophet himself had made the choice, which is why he emphasised its divine origin.

The dispute was therefore not simply a careful inquiry about skills. The answer already supplied real qualifications. The objection held to inherited prestige and possession as though they exhausted a person's fitness to lead.

### What qualities does the answer place first?

Knowledge must accompany authority. Talut understood warfare and could direct those who had asked for a common command. Strength included endurance and patience under combat, not just an impressive appearance. The explanation describes a ruler needing a strong soul as well as a strong body.

An inward interpretation uses nafs, the self, to explain this substantial strength. It contrasts a person with real qualities against the saying about a name without a body: someone bearing a title but lacking substance. On that reading, the praise is not simply a measurement of height.

The different emphases should remain together. The transmitted description includes actual tallness and commanding physique, while the allusion makes clear that outward size without inward merit would not be enough.

### What does their objection reveal?

Repeated controversy over every matter shows a community's deterioration. Some desired to be king themselves rather than seek the people's good. They asked for unity but resisted an answer that denied their personal precedence.

A believer should reason from realities rather than prejudice and petty considerations. Broad-minded judgment recognises competence outside expected social rank. The account of the Ark's return provides a further extraordinary confirmation of Talut's appointment. its details belong to the next verse. Its barakah, blessing, is not explained as something the chiefs control by wealth.

The inward criticism is that they looked only with the eye of worldly standing and forgot Allah's freedom of choice. His knowledge saw merit where they saw poverty. The closing names answer both failures: His bounty is not confined by their resources, and His knowledge is not confined by their assumptions.

### In short

- Talut's appointment over the Israelites, the Children of Israel, came from Allah, not merely the prophet's personal preference.
- The chiefs judged by lineage and wealth, while the reply emphasised knowledge, strength and character.
- The accounts differ about his livelihood but agree that ordinary status did not disqualify him.
- Leadership must be considered through real fitness and collective benefit rather than selfish claims to prestige.
