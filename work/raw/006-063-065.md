<<<VERSE 6:63>>>
Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea? He ˹alone˺ you call upon with humility, openly and secretly: “If You rescue us from this, we will be ever grateful.”"

The passage has just stated Allah's knowledge and his power in the abstract. Now it takes one case, the case of a person in genuine danger, and asks what he actually does.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Say"** The question is put to the disbelievers of Makkah, and it is a question whose answer the asker already knows. Their own behaviour supplies it.

**"Who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea"** The word translated here as the darkest times is the plural of darkness, and the plural is needed because English has no plural for the noun. Darkness comes in several kinds: the dark of night, the dark of a rain cloud, the dark of a dust storm, and the dark under the waves of the sea. All of them are included. Another way of putting the phrase is the hardships and tumults of travel by land and by sea.

**"He alone you call upon with humility, openly and secretly"** In that situation they forget their idols and call on nobody but Allah. At times the confession of helplessness comes out loud, and at times it stays in the heart of hearts, where no one else can hear it. One reading takes the pairing to mean that they call on him in public and in private, humbling themselves, and that the supplication is made with no partner mixed into it. In the surah of the Night Journey the whole scene is set out. He drives the ships through the sea so that people may seek his bounty, and when harm strikes them on the water the partners they called on vanish and only he is left, and when he brings them safely to land they turn away, since man is by nature ungrateful. Then comes the question: do they feel safe from his causing a side of the land to swallow them, or from his sending a storm of stones against them, or safe from his sending them back to sea a second time, sending a hurricane of wind and drowning them for their disbelief (17:66-69)? The same moment is put more briefly in the surah of the Ants (17:67).

**"If You rescue us from this, we will be ever grateful"** The promise is made in the middle of the danger: if he brings them out, they will be among the thankful, they will take him as the real rescuer, and they will not ascribe any partner to him. The verb in this clause is read two ways in the reports, so that the sentence can be taken as a direct address, rescue us, or as a report about them, if he rescues them. The surah of Jonas sets the whole scene out, the ship, the fair wind, the joy, then the stormy wind and the waves closing in from every side, and the moment when a man thinks he is surrounded, and then calls on Allah with faith made pure for him alone, saying: "If you deliver us from this, we shall truly be of the grateful" (10:22). And in the surah of the Ants the question is asked in its plainest form: is not he who guides you in the darknesses of the land and the sea, and who sends the winds ahead of his mercy, better than your gods? Is there any god with Allah? (27:63)

### Why the question works

The argument is not that the idolaters were honest in distress. It is that distress strips a person, and that what he then says is the truth he hides the rest of the time. No other living creature meets as much trouble as human beings do, and the reason is that a person is meant to be put through hard conditions so that the artificial and unreal thoughts are driven out of his mind and he can see his own nature. Whenever he confronts a major trouble he begins to call on Allah single-mindedly. All the man-made veils are swept away. He realises that in this world he is completely humble and helpless, and that power belongs to Allah alone. Then the trouble lifts. He goes back to his neglect and his recklessness, and he is the same man he was before it came.

The verse quotes them at the moment when they are most lucid, because that is the moment that will be brought into court later. Their own words at the mast, in the dark, are the evidence against them in the sunlight.

### The lesson runs the other way as well

A lesson with the rasp of a lash is in it for believers, and it is this: those people were prompt. They turned to Allah the instant they had nothing else to turn to, and we are not, despite claiming faith in his absolute power. Our attention is fixed on the material support we hope will get us out of trouble. We do not take idols, icons and images as our saviours, and yet the many support systems, logistics, mechanised rescue squads on land, at sea and in the air, and the backup of instruments close by and far away, have become no less than idols for us. We are so impressed with them and so taken up with them that we barely spare a thought for Allah and his perfect power.

The other verses about the same scene add what is missing from the idols' column. It is Allah who sends the winds as heralds of his mercy, and it is out of his mercy that night and day were made, and not one of the partners they call on has ever answered a prayer made in a storm.

### In short

- The verse asks a question about behaviour rather than belief, because the answer is visible in what the idolaters themselves do in danger.
- The darknesses are many, and the plural is deliberate: night, rain cloud, dust storm and the sea under the waves are all included.
- In distress a person is briefly a monotheist, and the verse quotes him at that moment to use his own words against his later shirk.
- The same passage carries a rebuke for believers, whose material supports have quietly taken the place of idols.

<<<VERSE 6:64>>>
Say, “˹Only˺ Allah rescues you from this and any other distress, yet you associate others with Him ˹in worship˺.”

One sentence takes back what the previous verse put on the table, and then names what they did with it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Say, Allah rescues you from this"** Allah himself takes the initiative here, since the answer to the question was never going to be denied. Their own experience had already given it: nothing that they worshipped came up to help them in that hour, and only Allah did.

**"and any other distress"** The deliverance is not limited to the storm. Every distress, grief and turmoil of the same kind is in his hands, and so is the removal of hardships, troubles and anxieties generally. This is stated in other words in the surah of prostrating, where it is said that he will make them taste the lesser punishment before the greater one, so that they may return (32:21). And in surah Ash-Shura: "The hardship that reaches you is an outcome of your evil deeds, while many of them Allah forgives" (42:30).

**"yet you associate others with him"** The people of Makkah are the ones addressed. In times of comfort they go back to shirk and to the worship of false gods, which is the strange betrayal and the fatal ignorance the verse stands on. It is one thing to forget in ease. It is another to be shown an open sign, to be given relief after distress, and then to put the partners back where they were.

### The shapes that shirk takes

Shirk is, at bottom, reposing one's faith in some entity other than Allah, just as tawhid is directing a person's entire faith to him alone. The worship of deities and of other phenomena is one form of it. Another is ingratitude instead of gratitude, which is also called shirk in the wording here, since they promised thanks and did not pay it. The most common form is subtler: a man makes a deity of himself and comes to have faith only in his own efforts. When he is vain about what he has achieved, his trust is in his own work. When he thinks his earnings were generated by him, his faith is in his own talents. When he overlooks another person's right, he assumes that nobody can call him to account for it, and when he dares to oppress someone, he assumes that he has full power over that person and that nothing will stop him. All of this is vanity and pride, and pride, before Allah, is the worst kind of shirk, because indulging in it amounts to putting oneself in the position of God.

### What the hardships mean, and what they do not

The hardships and accidents that meet people here are mostly the result of their own evil deeds, and they are a mild sampling of the punishment of the Hereafter. Seen from that angle they are in a way a mercy, since through them heedless people are given a jolt, so that they may take the occasion to survey their evil deeds and think about how not to fall into them again, and so stay safe from the greater and harsher punishment hereafter. A saying of the Prophet puts the scale of it in a hard frame. He said, by the one in whose hand his life lies, that an ordinary scratch from a piece of wood, a faltering step, or an itching in a vein, are all after-effects of some sin, while the sins that Allah forgives are many. Al-Baydawi explains the difference this leaves: the diseases and calamities of criminals and sinners are traces of their sins, while those of people protected from sin exist to test their patience and fortitude and to raise them to higher ranks in Paradise. Everyone else is not free of sin either, so what people meet is a consequence in one form or another.

The conclusion is not that medicine, treatment and rescue work are useless. The point is that a person should believe in Allah as the prime mover and maker of things and happenings, and should use the means at the same time, taking them for nothing but his blessings, since every means and instrument is his creation, serving human beings under his command and will. Fire, air, water, dust and every force on the face of the earth are subservient to that command. Unless he wills it, fire cannot burn, water cannot extinguish, medicine cannot benefit, and food cannot hurt. What is being warned against is the attitude in which the means are trusted instead of the one who made them.

### In short

- Allah answers the question of the previous verse himself, and widens it from one storm to every distress.
- The Qur'an reads relief as evidence, so that going back to partners after being rescued is not a lapse of memory but a betrayal.
- Shirk includes ingratitude, and, in its commonest form, a person's faith in himself.
- Hardships are a lesser punishment and a summons to return, and the remedy is turning to Allah with repentance and a firm resolve, while still using every lawful means.

<<<VERSE 6:65>>>
Say, “He ˹alone˺ has the power to unleash upon you a torment from above or below you or split you into ˹conflicting˺ factions and make you taste the violence of one another.” See how We vary the signs, so perhaps they will comprehend

This is the widest of the three claims in the passage, and it is the one the Prophet himself reacted to out loud.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He alone has the power to unleash upon you a torment from above"** To punish a criminal Allah needs no police, no army and no helper of the kind a mortal ruler needs. The punishment from above is what comes down: stones, pebbles, wind, fire, flood, and, on one reading of the early commentators, cruel rulers and merciless officials set over a people.

**"or below you"** That is, from beneath your feet: the earth caving in and swallowing, water forcing up out of the ground, drowning, and, on the same reading, the dishonesty, idling and treachery of one's own subordinates and servants.

**"or split you into conflicting factions"** The third kind does not come from either direction. It spreads out from within, and it is the one the verse then glosses: confusion in party strife, a nation breaking up into parties and groups that confront each other. The word used for confusion comes from a root that means to hide or to cover up, and it is in that sense that clothing covers the body, and that a meaning can be hidden when what is said is not open and clear. The word for a faction is a plural form meaning adherence, following, being a partisan of somebody, as in the verse: and Ibrahim was of the company of Noah (37:83). In ordinary usage it means a group that gathers for a purpose and whose members help one another to get it, which is what a party or a faction is.

**"and make you taste the violence of one another"** Some of them will experience torture and murder at the hands of others, on the explanation given by Ibn Abbas and others. The fighting is itself named as a punishment, not as an accident that happened to fall out of the situation.

**"See how We vary the signs, so perhaps they will comprehend"** The signs are made clear, plain and duly explained, so that people understand them, and so that they realise that what they themselves follow is falsehood and what has been brought to them is the truth. The passage about the leaf, the grain and the soul ends here with an appeal to comprehension, because nothing in it is meant to stay abstract.

### Three punishments, and the warning inside the grammar

The three kinds are laid out as punishment from above, punishment from beneath, and punishment that spreads from within. The noun for punishment is left indefinite, and it is admitted by the rules of Arabic grammar that an indefinite noun in a warning of this sort signals that there can be sub-divisions and forms inside each kind. So the list is not closed. It gives directions, and the past supplies the samples.

From above: the flood on the people of Noah. The wind storm that overtook the people of Ad. The stones rained on the people of Lot. Blood and frogs sent upon the Israelites. And the birds that pelted the people of the Elephant with pebbles when they marched on Makkah, leaving them like chaff that has been chewed out.

From beneath: the water under the ground forcing its way up for the people of Noah, so that they were caught by two punishments at once. The drowning of Pharaoh's people. And Qarun, who sank down into the earth with his legendary treasures, as though the ground swallowed him.

### What the Prophet said when this verse was read out

Jabir bin Abdullah reported what happened when the words came down. At the phrase about a torment from above, the Messenger of Allah said: "I seek refuge with your face." At the phrase about a torment from beneath your feet he said the same again. At the phrase about being covered with confusion in party strife, and about tasting the violence of one another, he said: "This is easier, and this is lighter." Al-Bukhari recorded this hadith, and recorded it again in the book of divine oneness. An-Nasa'i recorded it in the book on interpretation. Another account reverses the order. It puts the first two terrors as the lighter ones, and places the flight to Allah's countenance at the last clause.

The same request and the same answer come in two longer reports. Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas said that they were with the Prophet and passed the mosque of Bani Mu'awiyah. He went in and offered two rak'ahs of prayer, and they prayed behind him, and then he supplicated to his Lord at length. When he finished he said that he had asked his Lord for three things. He asked that his community not be destroyed by drowning, and was given it. He asked that they not be destroyed by famine and hunger, and was given it. He asked that they not be made to taste violence from one another, and was not given it. Ahmad recorded it, and Muslim recorded it in the book on trials, though al-Bukhari did not.

A second report comes through Ahmad from Khabbab bin al-Aratt, who was at Badr with the Prophet. He met him one night while the Prophet was praying until dawn. When he finished, Khabbab told him that he had never seen him perform a prayer like that one before. He answered: "Yes, it was a prayer of eagerness and fear. I asked my Lord for three things, and he gave me two and refused me the third." Then he spelled them out. "I asked him not to destroy us with what he destroyed the nations before us, and he gave me that." "I asked him not to let our enemies prevail over us, and he gave me that." "I asked him not to cover us with confusion in party strife, and he refused." An-Nasa'i recorded it, and Ibn Hibban in his authentic collection, and At-Tirmidhi in the book on trials in his compendium. At-Tirmidhi graded it hasan sahih.

A third version, from Ibn Umar, names another of the three requests, that no enemy be set upon the ummah to destroy them. That one was answered. The request about infighting was the one he was stopped from making.

The reports prove a distinction. The kinds of punishment that came on earlier communities from above them and from beneath them, and wiped them out, will not come in that form on this community. One punishment will keep visiting it during its life in this world, and that is its infighting and the mutual confrontation of its factions and parties. This is why the Prophet forbade his community so emphatically from dividing into sects and parties and from challenging and fighting one another, and why, on every occasion he could, he put the fear of Allah in people by warning that if a punishment has to come on Muslims within this life it will come because of nothing else. A report drawn from Zayd ibn Aslam by Ibn Abi Hatim gives the words he used when this verse was revealed: "Do not, after me, become like disbelievers, striking at one another's necks."

### Rulers, servants, and the effect of sin

The reading of Abdullah ibn Abbas and Mujahid turns the two directions into something nearer home. Punishment from above means cruel rulers and merciless officials put over a people, and punishment from beneath means one's own subordinates and servants turning into traitors, idlers and thieves. Sayings of the Prophet confirm that reading. In the compendium Mishkat, citing the branches of faith recorded by al-Baihaqi, one is reported as: "As are your deeds, good or bad, so shall be your rulers and officials set upon you." The sense is that a good and obedient people get merciful and just rulers, and a people whose deeds are evil find merciless and unjust rulers set over them. A longer divine saying is quoted in the same compendium from a collection on the saints, in which Allah says: "I am Allah. There is no god worthy of worship but me. I am the master of kings, and I am the sovereign. The hearts of kings are in my hands." And then: "When my servants obey me, I pour mercy into the hearts of their kings and officials. When they disobey me, I harden their rulers against them, so that they taste every kind of evil punishment." The lesson drawn from it is practical: "Do not waste your energy speaking ill of rulers. Turn to Allah and correct your deeds, and I will put your affairs right." A report from A'ishah in Abu Dawud and An-Nasa'i runs the same way. When Allah wishes well for a ruler, he gives him a good minister and deputy, who reminds him if he forgets and helps him when he acts rightly. And when a ruler's ruin is willed, wicked men are put around him as his ministers.

So hardships suffered at the hands of those above are a punishment from above, and those inflicted through those below are a punishment from beneath. Neither is a stray accident. Both fall under a divine law about deeds. Sufyan Ath-Thawri said that when a sin is committed by him he sees its effect on his own servant, and even on the horse he rides and the donkey he uses for his things. He can feel the change in their temper, because they all start disobeying him. Rumi makes the same point in his Mathnawi. Allah puts a person under an apparent punishment, pain caused by cruel rulers or faithless subordinates in this world, in order to turn his attention towards himself. The hope is that he will be alerted, will start trying to make his deeds good, and will thereby be saved from the much greater punishment of the Hereafter. The remedy for both directions is therefore identical. Everyone looks back and examines what he has done, leaves the paths of error, and stops being disobedient, and then the conditions that remove the hardship are created. To try to remove such things by material means alone is self-deception, and it is an experience people keep having.

### Difference that punishes, and difference that is a mercy

Not every difference is the punishment the verse describes. The difference that is named as a deprivation of mercy is difference in the principles and articles of belief, or difference born of self-interest, wanton desires and fancies. What is not included is the difference of opinion that rests on ijtihad, exercised inside the light of the Qur'an and the sunnah. That is the difference which has continued in subsidiary questions among the jurists of this community since the time of the companions and the generation after them. Its frame of reference is restricted to the Qur'an, the sunnah and ijma, the consensus of scholars. Everyone involved intends to obey and to act on the injunctions in them. The disagreement arises because solutions have to be deduced from wording that is condensed or ambiguous. This kind of difference has been called rahmah, mercy, in a narration reported in the small compendium al-Jami al-Saghir, with references going back through al-Baihaqi and Imam al-Haramayn: "The difference of my community is a mercy." It is made particular to this community for a reason. Difference among scholars who uphold nothing but the truth, and among jurists who are unshakeably mindful of Allah, is governed by those principles. It is done with a true intention and for Allah's good pleasure, so it is never motivated by self-interest or by a wish for recognition, office or money. Al-Munawi, commenting on that compendium, gives the comparison: the different approaches of this community's jurists have the standing that the different law-systems of the blessed prophets in past ages had, since they were all, though they differed, nothing but the commands of Allah.

An illustration from an ordinary street is offered as the test case. A main road is marked out into lanes for the convenience of those who use it, with one lane for buses, another for cars and vans, and another for cyclists and pedestrians. Outwardly this is a division. But since everybody is headed in one direction, and each lane leads to the destination, the difference of routes is functionally useful rather than harmful, and it opens up space and mercy. So too with the approaches of the great imams of ijtihad: the approach one of them takes is weightier in his own sight, and he does not call the approach of another false. The leading imams and the jurists agree that an approach taken by any of them is not thereby false, and that it is not permissible for anyone to call the people who follow it sinners. Anyone who looks at the relationships between the jurists of the companions and the successors, and at the four leading imams, will find excellent working relations, full respect and recognition, despite real differences of technical, intellectual and juristic approach. A hadith from the Prophet, brought through various chains of narration, is quoted as the gloss on being covered with confusion in party strife: "This community will divide into seventy-three groups, all of them in the fire except one." Rancour, hostility and infighting were out of the question, and the same spirit continued among the followers of the main schools for as long as they held to sound knowledge and an honest attitude.

A common complaint is asked about here as well. When the scholars differ, where does one go? The answer is that the matter is as simple as it is in any other trade. A sick person whose case the physicians disagree about is not left hopeless. People find the doctor with the experience and the skill they trust and put the case in his hands, and nobody goes about speaking ill of the other doctors in the town. A client does the same when lawyers differ, and does not malign the rest of the profession. The rule should hold for the layperson too: when the rulings of the scholars differ, make the effort to find the one who is best in knowledge and in the fear of Allah, and act on his advice, and do not spend the time finding fault with the rest. Ibn al-Qaiyyim records this in his book on the signs of those who give rulings. He adds that once a person has chosen an expert and acted on the ruling of someone he considers the best in knowledge and honesty, he is free of blame in the sight of Allah. It is not his job to decide which of the scholars who give rulings has erred. If the one who gave the ruling did err, the responsibility is his own.

And not every agreement is praiseworthy either. If thieves, robbers and rebels joined hands and formed a union of their own, every reasonable person would call that union fatal for society, while police action against it, or public protest, would be called beneficial. So the problem is not difference, and it is not acting on one opinion rather than another. The problems start when others are suspected and slandered, which is the product of ignorance, dishonesty and an excess of self-serving desire. At that point the merciful difference turns into the punishing kind, and a community splits into parties, fights itself, and does the impossible thing of killing its own members, while insult is passed off as the defence of a religious position. Religion has nothing to do with that excess. In the reports collected by al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah it is cited as the cause of nations going astray, and it is what the two verses in the family of Imran were warning against: "Hold fast, all of you, to the cord of Allah, and be not divided" (3:103), and "do not be like those who became divided and fell into disputes" (3:105). The exception is stated in the surah of Hud: "They continue in their differences, except those whom Allah has blessed with mercy" (11:118). On that verse the whole discussion turns, since the point is that difference without mercy is the punishment, and the mercy is what keeps a difference from becoming one.

### The community's one bond, and how it was lost

The essence of all these verses and reports is that a difference which turns into confrontation is ill-fated and blameworthy, and that the root of most of what a community suffers lies in it. What is being described as a punishment is not merely the fighting itself but the loss of what held people together. The pivot of unity for this ummah was one sentence, the testimony that there is no god worthy of worship but Allah and that Muhammad is his messenger. Anyone who had faith in it, in any language, of any colour, of any lineage, was a brother or a sister to everyone else. Mountains and rivers were no handicap, and being Arab, Egyptian, Syrian, Turkish, Indian or Chinese did not matter, since such labels existed for the sake of identity and introduction and nothing more. In our day, intrigue backed by sustained effort has divided the people of that sentence again into racial, linguistic and regional nationalities, and each of those has gone on splitting itself into further factions. A people whose hallmark was that they forgave, forewent and sacrificed even for others now contains people ready to sacrifice the most precious rights for the cheapest and meanest of gains. That is the difference born of self-interest and fancies, and it is a cash punishment, paid here, not only deferred.

Within its bounds, difference is not a disease but an enlargement of a question. Honest minds cannot meet over a difficult problem without differing, and uniformity of opinion is conceivable only among people who have not understood it, or among those who will agree to anything to accommodate a pressure group, and who would sign an opinion against their own conscience. This is why the universe the verse points at is itself full of lawful difference: things differ in shape, form, colour, smell, property and benefit, countless living organisms differ, and human beings differ in temperament, occupation, skill and way of living, and those differences are the charm of living and the open avenue of countless benefits. The same Allah who varies the signs in this surah varies his creation, and asks for comprehension rather than uniformity.

### In short

- The three terrors named are the ones from above, the ones from beneath, and the confusion of faction, and the indefinite wording leaves room for many forms of each.
- The Prophet's own reaction is recorded at each clause, and the reports agree that the third was the one he was not granted protection from.
- On the reading of Ibn Abbas and Mujahid, unjust rulers and faithless subordinates are themselves the punishment, which is why the remedy offered is correction of deeds rather than complaint.
- Division into factions is a punishment because it replaces a bond that was one sentence, while difference of opinion inside the limits of the Qur'an, the sunnah and consensus is described as mercy.
- The layperson's duty when scholars differ is to find the one he trusts most in knowledge and mindfulness and follow him, and to leave the rest alone.
