<<<VERSE 2:74>>>
Verse 74 delivers the verdict on everything the Israelites have just been reminded of. After the covenant, the mountain, the Sabbath and the dead man raised to life, "your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder", the verse says, and then it turns the comparison against them: "for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah". It closes with a warning: "And Allah is never unaware of what you do".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Even then"** means after all that: after the murdered man was brought back to life and named his killer, and after the other signs that came before it. The Children of Israel witnessed tremendous signs, including the raising of the dead, and yet this is what followed. Al-ʿAwfī reported Ibn ʿAbbās describing the scene. When the dead man was struck with a piece of the cow, he stood up more alive than he had ever been. Asked who had killed him, he said that his nephews had, and then he died again. His nephews, once Allah had taken his life a second time, swore by Allah that they had not killed him, denying the truth while they knew it. Of that denial Allah said that their hearts became like stones, or harder.

**"your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder"**. Their hearts stiffened against accepting the truth and became like stones that never soften, dry and barren. A string of punishments and rescues should have taught the Israelites humility and obedience. Instead their hearts grew harder against divine guidance, and that insensibility led them into further misdeeds. With the passing of time their hearts became unlikely to accept any admonition, even after the miracles they had seen, and harder than stones, with no hope of softening. One commentary observes that they were harder than rock because rock changes and is transformed, as the rest of the verse describes, whereas they never changed. Because of this, Allah forbade the believers to imitate the Jews, asking whether the time had not come for believers' hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and the truth He revealed, and not to be like those given the Scripture before, who were left so long that their hearts grew hard (57:16). The people who had received the Torah and the Gospel are the warning example.

**"for some rocks gush rivers"**. Springs and rivers sometimes burst out of stone. One early commentary adds that Allah is not heedless of what they hide of their transgressions.

**"others split, spilling water"**. Some stones crack open and water comes from them even when there is no spring or river nearby. The verb yashshaqqaq has one letter assimilated into the next.

**"while others are humbled in awe of Allah"**. Some stones come tumbling down from the mountain heights out of fear of Allah, while the hearts of these people are unmoved, unstirred and unhumbled. Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq related Ibn ʿAbbās's explanation of the three kinds of stone along these same lines.

**"And Allah is never unaware of what you do"**. He will not leave your transgressions unpunished. He gives you respite until your appointed time comes. The verse warns the Israelites that Allah knows everything they have been doing and will hold them to account for it. A variant reading has "what they do", shifting the address to the third person.

### Do stones really fear Allah?

Some have claimed that the humbling of the stones is a metaphor. Ar-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī and other leading scholars said there is no need for such an explanation, because Allah creates this quality of humility in stones. The Qur'an speaks in this way often. Allah offered the trust (al-amānah) to the heavens, the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it out of fear (33:72). The seven heavens, the earth and all within them glorify Him, though we cannot comprehend their glorification (17:44). The stars and the trees bow down (55:6). The shadows of everything He has created incline to the right and the left in humble submission to Him (16:48). When He said to the heaven and the earth, "Submit, willingly or unwillingly", they both answered that they submit willingly (41:11). Had this Qur'an been sent down on a mountain, it would have been seen humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah (59:21). On the Day of Judgement the skins of the wicked will speak against them, because Allah makes all things speak (41:21). The Sunnah says the same. The Ṣaḥīḥ records the Prophet ﷺ saying of Mount Uḥud that it is a mountain that loves us and that we love. The yearning of the palm stump for the Prophet ﷺ, when he left it for the pulpit, is confirmed in authentic narrations. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records the Prophet ﷺ saying that he knows a stone in Makkah that used to greet him with salām before he was sent, and that he still recognises it. And he said of the Black Stone that on the Day of Resurrection it will testify for those who kissed it. There are several other texts to the same effect.

Scholars approach the same point from reason. The first two states of stone, giving much water and giving a little, are matters of everyday observation. The third, falling in fear of Allah, may raise a doubt, since the capacity to feel fear seems to require reason and sensibility, and stones are supposed to have neither. But reason is not a necessary condition of fear, for animals feel fear without possessing reason. Sensibility is required, yet no rational argument rules out sensibility in minerals. Sensibility depends on life, and minerals may well possess a subtle kind of life of which man is unaware. Scientists have in recent times found signs of life and sensibility in minerals too. In any case an explicit statement of the Qur'an carries an authority that no physical science or rational argument can overturn. Nor does the verse claim that the fear of Allah is the only reason a stone ever falls. It says this is so of some stones, so other falls may have purely physical causes while this is one cause among them.

### The force of "or even harder"

The scholars of Arabic agree that "or" here does not express doubt, but they differ on what it does express. Some say it means "and", so that the hearts are as hard as stone and harder, as when the Qur'an says not to obey "a sinner or a disbeliever" (76:24), or speaks of "ending excuses or giving warnings" (77:6). Others say it means "rather", so that the hearts are as hard as stone, or rather harder, as when a group feared people "as Allah should be feared, or even more" (4:77), or when Yūnus was sent to "a hundred thousand or more" (37:147), or when the distance was "two arms-lengths or even less" (53:9). A third group says that the hearts are of two kinds, some as hard as stone and some harder than stone, and Ibn Jarīr, while allowing the other readings, preferred this one: some of their hearts were like stone and some were harder. In support, this is like the two parables earlier in the sūrah, where the hypocrites are first compared to a man who lit a fire (2:17) and then "or like a rainstorm from the sky" (2:19), and like the parable in Sūrat an-Nūr, where the deeds of the disbelievers are compared to a mirage (24:39) and then "or like the darkness in a deep sea" (24:40). In each case the meaning is that some of them resemble the first example and others the second. Allah knows best.

### The order of the three stones

The three kinds of stone are placed in a deliberate order. In the highest degree stand the stones whose responsiveness is so strong that rivers gush from them, giving sustenance to beasts and men. Against these the hearts of the Jews are so hard that they cannot feel sympathy for fellow human beings even in suffering and pain, and so cannot wish them well. In the second degree come the stones that serve Allah's creatures, but less abundantly. Their hearts are harder even than these. In the lowest degree are the stones that benefit no one but can at least feel the fear of Allah. The Jews are said to lack even this minimum of sensitivity.

A modern commentator reads the same three stones as portraits of people. Streams run down the rocks of a mountainside and gather into rivers. That is the person in whose heart the fear of God sits like water in the bosom of a mountain, and flows from his eyes as tears. Some rocks look like dry boulders, but when they crack they reveal pools of water beneath, wells from which people drink. That is the person who seems far from his Lord until a calamity shakes his soul and he hurries back to God with his eyes streaming. And sometimes great slabs of rock come crashing down a mountainside. That is the person who has treated another unjustly, and who will not yield to any human being, but who submits at once, in all humility, the moment he is reminded of God's command, because his quarrel has become God's affair. Everything in nature carries a message, for God made the world as a source of admonition and remembrance to show man how to bring his life into line with the divine will. Throughout the universe that message is spoken in the language of silent examples. In the Qur'an it is set down in words. The same writer traces the hardening to its root. People who hunt for loopholes in God's commands grow steadily more insensitive. God is the name of the supreme Being, and a living faith in Him shakes the soul so deeply that talk gives way to stunned silence. When the heart hardens, however, people start to manipulate the divine scriptures as they are used to manipulating human writings, and this brazenness increases their insensitivity in turn, until their hearts are no longer moved before the might of their Lord and their souls no longer stir at the thought of God. They become as rigid as the most impervious rock.

### Two kinds of hardness

The devotional reading distinguishes the hardness of the ignorant from a very different hardness found in the recognisers of God. In the ignorant, hardness of heart is unkindness, want of mercy and distance from the path of the Real. In the recognisers, the purified and the limpid, hardness is strength of heart, stability, perfection of knowledge and clarity. Abū Bakr used to point to it in himself. When he saw someone weeping and writhing at the recitation of the Qur'an he would say that he had been like that too, until hearts became hard. This hardness is an allusion to the perfected state of the recognisers and the majesty of the rank of the sincerely truthful. At the beginning of the work, when desire is new, beginners shout, wail, cry and weep, because passion has not yet fully established its rule. When the work reaches perfection, the clarity of recognition grows strong and passion rules completely. Then the shouting and wailing are set aside and joy and revelry take their place. The tongue of their state says: when my passion for that beauty was new, my neighbours could not sleep at night for my weeping, and now I weep less because my passion has grown, for when the fire takes hold there is little smoke. The same reading notes that Allah ranked hard stones above cruel hearts. Water flows from stone, stone grows soft, and stone falls into the desert in fear of God, but the cruel heart of an estranged man neither groans in fear of God nor weeps in remorse, and neither mercy nor tenderness finds a way in. A story is told of one of God's prophets who, crossing a desert, saw a small stone from which flowed a quantity of water far beyond the stone's own measure, and the account breaks off there.

### In short

- After the raising of the dead man, and the nephews' denial of what they had seen, the Israelites' hearts hardened past the hardness of rock, the example the believers are warned against (57:16).
- Rocks give rivers, or crack and yield water, or fall in awe of Allah. Their hearts did none of these.
- The fear of stones is real, not a metaphor, as ar-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī held and as the Qur'an and hadith repeatedly show, from the trust refused by the mountains (33:72) to Uḥud's love and the greeting stone of Makkah.
- "Or even harder" has been read as "and harder", as "rather, harder", or as describing two kinds of hearts, which Ibn Jarīr preferred, on the pattern of 2:17 and 2:19, and 24:39 and 24:40.
- The three stones also picture three kinds of believer, while the hardness of the realised knower, as Abū Bakr described it, is strength rather than cruelty.
