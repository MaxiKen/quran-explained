<<<VERSE 4:3>>>
The verse before this one guarded the property of orphans. This one guards the orphan girls themselves, and then opens into the marriage law of the Qur'an: "If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession". The verse moves in three steps. It warns guardians away from marrying orphan girls for gain. It permits marriage to other women, up to four. And it commands a man who cannot be just between wives to stop at one. The closing words, "This way you are less likely to commit injustice", are the measure of the whole permission.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺"** sets the test before the act. The verse does not wait for the wrong to be done. A guardian who suspects in himself the temptation to skimp on an orphan girl's dowry or her upkeep must drop the idea of marrying her and look elsewhere. The same standard reaches further than guardians. Just as people feared being unfair with the wealth of orphans, they were told to fear being unfair with women, for women stand with orphans in weakness and lack of power. Whoever cannot fulfil a wife's rights should not marry her.

**"then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four"** opens a wide door and then frames it. Women other than the orphan girls under a man's care are many, and the believer is not restricted to one of them. The permission names a ceiling, not a target. Read with the practice of the Prophet ﷺ, it sets the limit of marriage at four wives at one time, and no more.

**"But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice"** names the condition on which the whole permission rests. The justice in question is the kind a man can deliver: equal spending on maintenance, and an equal share of nights and turns with each wife.

**"then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession"** gives two exits for the man who fears he cannot be fair. He keeps to a single wife. Or he stays with what his right hand owns, since bondwomen do not carry the same rights as wives. With them equal apportionment is not owed, and even the waiting period does not apply, so falling short of parity there carries no sin as it would between free wives. One caution belongs here. The ownership the verse speaks of can only come about through its own narrow conditions, conditions that generally do not exist in our time. No one may live with a woman outside marriage by simply declaring her his property. That claim is invalid and plainly unlawful.

**"This way you are less likely to commit injustice"** closes the verse with a word of nearness and a word of tilt. The word for "more likely" comes from a root meaning nearness: this arrangement brings a man nearer to safety. The word for injustice here comes from a root meaning to lean, an inclination that grows until it becomes outright wrongdoing. Following the verse, one wife or a bondwoman, keeps a man away from that lean. It does not by itself make him innocent of every wrong, for reasons the verse itself points to at its end.

### The abuse the verse answers

In the days before Islam the guardian of an orphaned girl held all the power and none of the accountability. A guardian would notice a girl in his charge who had good looks or valuable property. He would then marry her himself, or arrange her marriage to his son. He fixed the dower himself, and it was usually the lowest possible. He kept her in whatever manner suited him, for who was there to object? Her father, the one man who would have weighed every aspect of her future happiness in a marriage and made sure she was well provided for, was in the grave.

The Qur'an answers this with the command that every excuse, device and stratagem used to get at an orphan's property is impermissible. The rights of the orphan must be protected honestly. The verse came down over cases of exactly this kind. In the report of Muslim, ʿĀʾishah said it was revealed about a custodian in whose care was a female orphan with some wealth and no one to defend her. Out of greed for her money the custodian refused to give her in marriage, and he harmed her and treated her badly. The verse tells such a man to marry other women, lawful to him, and leave her alone.

Al-Bukhārī records ʿĀʾishah's account of a case that arose in the lifetime of the Prophet ﷺ. A man had an orphaned girl in his guardianship who held a share in his fruit farm. He married the girl, though he did not really desire her, and instead of giving her a dower from his own pocket he took possession of her very share in the farm. Her money was mixed with his, and he kept her portion from her. The verse was revealed about his case. ʿUrwah ibn az-Zubayr asked ʿĀʾishah what the verse means, and she answered: it concerns the orphan girl who shares her guardian's house and his property. Her wealth and her beauty may tempt him to marry her without giving her the adequate dower that another suitor would have given. Such guardians were therefore barred from marrying them except with full justice and the most suitable dower. Otherwise they were told to marry other women.

That was not the end of the question. People went on asking the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about marrying orphan girls, and another verse came down: "They ask your instruction concerning the women" (4:127). There the words "yet whom you desire to marry" describe the guardian who does not want to marry the orphan girl in his care precisely because she has neither wealth nor beauty. A guardian who would not touch a poor plain girl but reaches for a rich pretty one is not choosing a wife. He is choosing property.

Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Qatādah, ar-Rabīʿ, ad-Daḥḥāk and as-Suddī read the verse against the habits of the time. People had become careful about the wealth of orphans after the earlier command, yet they took liberties with women and married whoever they liked, sometimes treating them fairly and sometimes not. When they asked about the orphans and the verse about their property came down, this verse came down with it: as you fear to deal unfairly with the orphan, so fear to deal unfairly with women. Marry only as many as you can fulfil the rights of. This is the view of Ibn ʿAbbās in the narration of al-Wālibī.

### Why the limit is four

The permission "two, three, or four" was read against a verse about angels: "Who made the angels messengers with wings, two or three or four" (35:1). That verse does not mean no angel has more than four wings, for there is proof that some do. The marriage verse is different. It specifies what men are allowed, as Ibn ʿAbbās and the majority of scholars stated. Had more than four wives been permitted, the verse would have said so. And the Prophet ﷺ applied the number as a hard limit in real cases.

A man named Ghilān ibn Salamah ath-Thaqafī had ten wives when he accepted Islam, and they accepted it with him. The Prophet ﷺ told him: "Choose any four of them, and divorce the rest." Ash-Shāfiʿī, at-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah, ad-Dāraquṭnī and al-Bayhaqī recorded the hadith up to those words, and only Aḥmad collected the full version. That version carries the rest of the story. In the reign of ʿUmar, Ghilān divorced his remaining wives as well and divided his money among his children. ʿUmar concluded that the devil, from what he overhears, had brought Ghilān word of his coming death, and he told him so. Then he gave him a choice: take back your wives and your money, or I will seize all of it and order your grave to be stoned, as the grave of Abū Righal was stoned. Abū Righal belonged to Thamūd, and he had been safe from their punishment only while he stayed within the sacred area. The moment he stepped out of it, the torment took him as it had taken them. Ghilān complied.

The argument from these reports is a fortiori. If the Prophet ﷺ did not let a new Muslim keep more than four of the wives he already had, then marrying more than four from the start is even more clearly barred. Two more cases seal it. Qays ibn al-Ḥārith al-Asadī accepted Islam with eight wives and mentioned it to the Prophet ﷺ, who told him: "Keep four of them and divorce the rest." Abu Daud records it. And Nawfal ibn Muʿāwiyah ad-Daylamī accepted Islam having five women in his nikah and was told to divorce one, in a report from the Musnad of Imam al-Shāfī, found in the Mishkāt with reference to Sharḥ al-Sunnah. The consistent practice of the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions shows what the verse means: joining more than four women in marriage at one time is ḥarām (forbidden).

### Plurality of wives before Islam

More than one wife at a time was permitted in every religion of the world before Islam, and no law had drawn a line. The custom ran through Arabia, India, Iran, Egypt and Babylon. The Jews knew it, the Christians knew it, the Hindus and Aryans knew it, and the Zoroastrians knew it. The Vedic teachings allowed an unlimited number of wives at once, in tens, in thirteens, even in twenty sevens, and Shri Krishna, a greatly revered figure among Hindus, is said to have had hundreds. Among Christian writers there has always been a school, never the loudest, that defended plurality from the Bible as a blessing rather than a blot, and names such as Davenport, Father Nixon, John Milton and Isac Taylor have argued it.

The modern West set out to do away with plural marriage, and the result, on this reading, was not fewer women in men's lives but more of them outside marriage: a spread of kept mistresses and girlfriends with no bond at all. The euphemisms multiply, "relationship", "affair", "consenting adults", "partner", while the thing itself remains. A society that forbids a man to marry two women and winks at his taking two lovers has said no to marriage and yes to adultery, which is a strange place to stand. There is also the plain arithmetic of certain times and places, where women outnumber men. Where lawful plurality is closed, the alternative is not chastity but prostitution, or so the argument runs.

None of this is a defence of what Arabia actually did with the permission. Before Islam men took as many wives as they liked, some nine or ten, and Qays ibn al-Harth alone had eight. They satisfied greed first and discovered justice never. Wives lived like prisoners held in the chains of marriage. The darling of one season became the forgotten woman of the next, and the very idea of standing rights for wives did not exist. It was the Qur'an that stepped into this and stopped a great injustice: four at most, and equality made a binding duty, with a stern warning against contravention.

### What "of your choice" means

The words "of your choice" translate an Arabic expression whose literal sense is "what you like". Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Ibn Jubair and Ibn Mālik read it as meaning what is lawful: women who are permitted to you. Others kept the literal sense, women you like. There is no quarrel between the two. Together they say: women you are drawn to by natural inclination, who are also lawful for you under the rules of the sharīʿah.

### The marriage of orphan girls who are minors

The word for orphans in this verse covers orphaned girls, and in the terminology of the sharīʿah a yatīm is a boy or girl who has not yet reached puberty. From this the jurists drew a ruling: the guardian of an orphaned girl does have the authority to give her in marriage while she is still a minor, but only on condition that the decision is made with wisdom and her future welfare in view. Without that condition the door opens to abuses seen in some communities, where a grown girl is married to a child with no regard for the gap in their ages and no inquiry into the boy's circumstances or character.

An orphaned girl who has reached puberty has, without question, the right to choose her husband freely. In practice, though, young women are often too modest and shy to speak out on their own marriages, even when the right is theirs, and they accept in good faith what their guardians arrange. That very trust makes the guardian's duty heavier. He must not use it to step on her rights.

The verse therefore lays down a rule of total vigilance over the marital rights of orphaned girls. Enforcement is not left to government alone, as ordinary statutes are. The command is addressed to conscience first: fear Allah, and if you see injustice in yourself where an orphan girl is concerned, marry elsewhere. To that, the verse adds the ordinary arm of the state. The agencies charged with enforcement must watch that no such violation occurs in society, and where it does, they must use the force of law to see the rights fulfilled.

### The Prophet's ﷺ own marriages

Any talk of the four-wife permission invites the question of the Prophet's ﷺ own marriages, and the material answers it at length. His purpose in life was to carry the call of Islam, purify people and teach the Qur'an, and he taught by deed as much as by word. There is no corner of human life that did not need his guidance, from the congregational prayer to the treatment of wives, the upbringing of children and the etiquettes of the call of nature. What he did inside the walls of his house, how he dealt with his wives, how he answered the women who came to ask: all of it is recorded in the books of hadith, and much of it reached the community through his wives themselves.

That last point is the heart of the matter. His marriages created the setting in which the community could learn its religion from the inside of his household. Sayyidah ʿĀʾishah alone is the one from whom some two thousand and ten narrations are reported, covering the explanation of Qur'anic injunctions, questions of law and answer, morals and manners, and the Prophet's ﷺ life itself, and through such reports the Muslim ummah received guidance no other setting could have supplied. From Sayyidah Umm Salmah come three hundred and seventy eight. Ibn al-Qaiyyim observed that if the rulings Umm Salmah gave after the Prophet's ﷺ death were collected, they would make a treatise by themselves. ʿĀʾishah taught as the beacon light of religious knowledge for forty eight years after his death, and her students alone number around two hundred.

The calumny that his marriages served desire is answered by the plain record of his life, which his enemies watched for fifty years and never caught. Before the eyes of the Quraysh of Makkah he married, at twenty five, a widow older than himself who had lost two husbands and had children, and he stayed with no one but her for twenty five years. In those years he would leave home for a month at a time and sit in the cave of Hira, given to the remembrance of his Lord. Every one of his other marriages came after the age of fifty. His enemies called him magician, sorcerer, poet, madman, liar, fabricator. They used every arrow in the quiver. Not one of them, not once, said a word casting doubt on his chastity, because there was nothing to say.

The chronology strips the charge of everything. Khadijah was his only wife until he was fifty. Then came his marriages to Saudah and ʿĀʾishah, and Saudah came to his house while ʿĀʾishah, being young, stayed at her father's until the second year after the Hijrah, when she joined him in Madinah. He was fifty four then, with two wives. Hafsah came a year later. Then Zainab bint Khuzaymah, who died eighteen months after the marriage, or three months by one report. Umm Salmah he married in year four, Zainab bint Jahsh in year five, when he was fifty eight and had four wives, though he could have taken four the moment the permission was revealed, and never did. Juwayriyyah came in year six, Umm Habibah and Safiyyah in year seven, and Maymunah later in the same year. In sum: one wife until fifty four, four at fifty eight, and the rest within two or three years after that, in old age. Of all his wives, only ʿĀʾishah aṣ-Ṣiddīqah came to him a virgin. All the others were widows, with the single exception of Zainab bint Jahsh, and some had already lost two husbands. His Companions would have laid down their lives at his word. Had desire been the motive, nothing stopped him from marrying only virgins, or from changing wives every month or two. He never did.

And there is a verse to close the argument. Muḥammad al-Muṣṭafā ﷺ was a true prophet of Allah, and a prophet does not follow his own desires. What he does, he does by the leave and will of Allah, and once prophethood is granted, that is the whole account. But for the one who insists on the smear, there is a fact to face: the Prophet ﷺ proclaimed against himself a restriction never laid on another Muslim, "It is not lawful for you to take more wives after this" (33:52). A man chasing desire does not close his own doors.

Three of these marriages show their purpose in detail. Umm Salmah had lost her husband Abū Salmah, and she came to the Prophet ﷺ a widow with her children, whom he raised in his house with love and concern, demonstrating by his own conduct how stepchildren are to be brought up. Had none of his wives brought children, this whole area of guidance would have gone undemonstrated. Her son ʿUmar ibn Abī Salmah said he grew up in the lap of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. Once, eating with him, the boy kept dunking his hand all over the bowl. The Prophet ﷺ told him: "Say the name of Allah, eat with your right hand, and eat from what is in front of you." Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record it.

Juwayriyyah came to him as a prisoner of jihād, fallen to the share of Thābit ibn Qays or his cousin. She made an agreement with her master to buy her freedom for a sum, then came to the Prophet ﷺ asking his help with the payment. He said to her: "Shall I not tell you something much better than this? That I pay on your behalf and marry you?" She agreed with pleasure, and he paid and married her. Then came something no law could have produced. Hundreds of her people had passed into the ownership of the Companions as prisoners. When the Companions learned that the Prophet ﷺ had married her, they freed their slaves, one after another, unable to keep in bondage people now joined to the Prophet's ﷺ household by marriage. ʿĀʾishah said of it: "One hundred families of Banū al-Muṣṭaliq became free because of his marriage with Juwayriyyah. I know of no other woman who proved a greater blessing for her people."

Umm Habibah had accepted Islam in Makkah in the early days and migrated to Ethiopia with her husband. There her husband turned Christian and died soon after. The Prophet ﷺ proposed to her through the Negus, the emperor of Ethiopia, who gave her in marriage to him there. She was the daughter of Abū Sufyān, at that time the leader of the camp whose declared aim was hostility to Islam. When Abū Sufyān learned of the marriage, the words escaped him involuntarily: "He is a man of valour. His nose cannot be cut off." A man of honour is not easily disgraced, and there, under his own nose, his daughter had become the Prophet's ﷺ wife. The marriage worked as psychological warfare: it watered down the ambitions of the leader of the unbelievers, and the political gain for Islam and the Muslims can hardly be denied. God's far-sighted and wise Messenger, in all likelihood, had that in sight. Those who want the fuller picture will find it in the treatise devoted to the plurality of his marriages, and still more in the books of hadith and the Prophet's ﷺ life read from their own sources rather than from the books of those who set out to trap Muslims with golden snares.

### Justice between wives

The permission ends where injustice begins. More than one wife is allowed and fitting only on condition that the equality required by the sharīʿah can be maintained and the rights of all the wives fulfilled. A man who cannot do that must keep to one wife, or to what his right hand owns. Whoever thinks of marrying more than one should first look hard at himself, weigh his means and his character, and ask honestly whether he can treat several wives justly without failing any of them. If the strong likelihood is that he cannot, then stepping into a second marriage is a thoughtless plunge into grave sin. Human condition being what it is, one wife is enough for most.

The law behind it is exact. A marriage beyond four wives contracted in a single offer and acceptance is void from the start, since no one has the right to more than four. Within the limit of four, the marriages are valid, but it is obligatory, wājib, to maintain justice and equality between the wives, and falling short is a grave sin. A wife whose rights are infringed can take the matter to an Islamic court and obtain redress.

The Prophet ﷺ pressed the duty with warnings and lived it to the outer edge. In a hadith he said that a man with two wives who cannot fulfil their rights equally and justly will be raised on the Day of Doom with one shoulder drooping. He himself treated his wives with full equality in everything within his control, even in matters where equality was not mandatory, and then he prayed:

> O Allah, this is my equalizing in what I control. So do not hold me to account for what You control and I do not.

For the equality demanded of a man runs only through what is in his power: the spending on each wife's upkeep, and the parity of nights and turns. The tilt of the heart is not in his power, and no one is taken to account for what is not his choice, on one binding condition: the heart's tilt must never leak into the things he does control. The Qur'an says plainly that perfect equality between wives is beyond a man: "You will never be able to do perfect justice between wives even if it is your ardent desire" (4:129). Love cannot be portioned by rule. But even that involuntary tilt is not left uncorrected, for the same passage forbids the full tilt: a man who loves one wife more may not therefore turn from the other in total indifference. If something even an infallible Messenger cannot claim, the heart's exact equality, is written as a duty, it is because the duty covers only the part a servant can reach.

### The apparent contradiction with 4:129

Some readers set the two verses side by side and stumble. This verse commands one wife if perfect justice is feared impossible. The later verse declares perfect justice impossible outright. Does it not follow that more than one wife is forbidden altogether?

The answer lies in what each verse is measuring. The first verse speaks of justice in matters of choice: spending, maintenance, turns, the things a man does or refuses to do. That justice is commanded, and discrepancy in it is a grave sin. The second verse speaks of love and the heart's inclination, which no man commands. Had Allah meant the verses to abolish plural marriage, the details would be pointless. Why say "marry women of your choice, two, three, or four" at all? Why say "if you fear you will not be just", when on that reading the injustice would be certain and fear would have no meaning? And the words and deeds of the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions, generation after generation, show more than one wife, up to four, was never treated as forbidden. The two verses contradict nothing. They divide the field: the hand is commanded to equality, the heart is excused its preference, and neither verse voids the other.

### Why "closer" and not "safe"

The verse ends by saying one wife is "closer" to not doing injustice, and the choice of word was itself discussed. A man with one wife has no second wife to wrong. Why not say the arrangement makes him safe outright? Because there are men who inflict every kind of injustice on even one wife. Not marrying a second time does not by itself cure a man of injustice. What the single marriage does is remove the likeliest occasion and bring him nearer to fairness. Complete deliverance from wrong comes only when the one wife's rights are given in full, generously, her shortcomings forgiven and her difficult ways borne with patience. That is the standard for one wife as much as for four.

### In short

- A guardian who fears he cannot give an orphan girl her full rights, her dower and her due, must not marry her. He should marry other women, who are many.
- Marriage is permitted up to four wives at one time, no more. The Prophet ﷺ had new Muslims with ten, eight and five wives keep four, or release one, and that practice fixed the limit.
- Plurality of wives was unlimited in every religion before Islam and unrestricted in Arabia. The Qur'an capped it at four and made equal treatment a binding duty.
- The orphan girl who is a minor may be given in marriage by her guardian only on the strength of wisdom and her future welfare. A grown orphan girl chooses for herself, and her trust makes the guardian's duty heavier, not lighter.
- The Prophet's ﷺ marriages came after fifty, mostly to widows, and served the teaching of the community, the freeing of prisoners, ties with opposing camps, and the raising of stepchildren. ʿĀʾishah alone transmitted some two thousand and ten reports.
- Justice between wives is obligatory in spending and turns, which a man controls. The heart's tilt he does not control, is not punished for, and may not act on. If he cannot keep the justice he controls, the rule is one wife.

<<<VERSE 4:4>>>
The previous verse removed one injustice against women, the injustice of marriage without justice. This one takes up a particular right, the mahr (dower): "Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience". The dower is the payment the husband owes the bride herself. The verse commands that it be given, and that it be given gladly, and then names the one case in which a husband may take any of it back: when the wife, truly of her own will, hands it over.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously"** states an obligation from Allah, and the adverb carries half the law. The dower is the wife's right, and the husband is to pay it himself, into her hands, as one gives a gift, with a good heart. The word translated "graciously" is niḥlah, which in Arabic usage means giving something cheerfully. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥā reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that the niḥlah of this verse is the dowry itself. Ibn Isḥāq narrated from az-Zuhrī, from ʿUrwah, from ʿĀʾishah, that niḥlah means obligatory. Muqātil, Qatādah and Ibn Jurayj said the same, and Ibn Jurayj added the sense of specified. Ibn Zayd said that in Arabic niḥlah refers to what is necessary, so the command is: do not marry unless you give the wife something that is her right. After the Prophet ﷺ no one may marry a woman without the required dowry, nor by giving false promises about one. Since every obligatory right is to be discharged cheerfully, the dower is no exception: give it, and give it in good cheer.

**"But if they waive some of it willingly"** opens the single exception. The words "of their own accord" are pinned to the women themselves, not to circumstance. It is their own selves that must be pleased to let the husband have some of the dowry.

**"then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience"** permits what the earlier hesitation had questioned. Some people had been uneasy about a husband taking back any part of the dower, and the verse was revealed in answer to them. If the waiver is genuine, taking what is given is lawful, with no blame and no harm in the Hereafter. The two words of the ending are worth their weight. One, haniʾan, describes what comes without labour or pain, the way good food is eaten, digested easily and turned into health. The other, mariʾan, from a root meaning to be wholesome, says nearly the same, and the two are so close that some translators render both with one word. What is taken freely from a willing wife sits well. What is squeezed from her does not.

### The three old injustices

Before Islam, the dower was wronged in three ways, and the verse answers all three.

First, the dower was simply taken by the girl's guardians instead of given to her. The command is addressed to husbands so that they themselves put the dower in the wife's hands, and to guardians so that whatever passes through them on the way reaches the girl intact and untouched for personal use without her permission.

Second, the dower was paid, but sourly, as a penalty is paid, by a man with no way out. The word niḥlah cuts that attitude out of the transaction. What is owed by obligation is still to be given with cheer.

Third, husbands used the wife's powerlessness to press her into forgoing the dower altogether. The verse answers that exacting a waiver by pressure achieves nothing. Nothing is forgiven that way. A waiver counts only when the wife herself elects it, out of free choice in its most genuine sense, whether she forgoes part of the dower before taking it or returns part after having it in hand. Then, and only then, it is permissible and correct for the husband.

These abuses peaked in the Jāhiliyyah, and the shame is that traces of them survive among Muslims in some tribes and regions to this day. Before Islam men used to marry without giving any dowry at all. Every such practice is to be left behind.

### What counts as willing

The restriction to the wife's own sweet will is not decoration. It rests on a principle the Prophet ﷺ stated once and for all:

> Beware, do no injustice. A person's property is not lawful for another unless it be through his sweet will.

From that one rule many details flow. Consider the woman of our own time who doubts she will ever see her dower. She expects that asking for it will displease everyone, so, like it or not, she lets it go. That kind of forgiveness is not to be trusted. The real touchstone, as Ashraf Thanavī used to teach, is to place the dower in the wife's hands first, as its owner. What she then returns to the husband, freely and without pressure, is truly given. The same test governs inheritance. It is common enough, after a father's or mother's death, for sons to take over the whole property and leave the girls their share only on paper. If such brothers feel a twinge of conscience, the most they do is go and excuse themselves before their sisters, who, knowing they will not be paid anyway, forgive against their will. That forgiveness is worthless, and withholding the share is open usurpation. The widow's share, and especially the stepmother's, is treated the same way. The one exception is a forgiveness that is genuinely glad.

The text says the whole self must be willing, not merely the heart. Men who pay bribes or interest do so after calculation and with apparent gain, but no one would call that glad giving. Probe such a man's conscience and his self would flatly refuse. That is why good cheer and sweet will are given the deciding role. The rule runs beyond family. Donations sought for mosques or religious schools are lawful only when the giver gives freely and gladly. A donation squeezed out by tribal, social or legal pressure may not be accepted or used. It goes back to the giver.

### Why the dower is called ṣadaqah

The verse calls the dower ṣaduqāt, the plural of ṣaduqah. The words ṣaduqah and ṣudaq both signify the dower of women, and the name is a small sermon. The root ṣadaqa signifies truth, and the dower shows the true attitude of the husband toward his wife, which made the name fit. Mulla ʿAlī Qārī notes this in his commentary on the Mishkāt.

### The food of the generous

Read inwardly, the ending of the verse says something about people at table. What the generous set before others is wholesome, because they feed from good-heartedness. What the miserly serve is spoiled food, for they look only to themselves and feed out of obligation. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The food of the generous is a remedy, while the food of the miser is a disease." What a man takes from a wife's free gift is of the first kind. What he grinds out of her is of the second.

### In short

- The dower is the wife's right, owed by the husband personally, and it is to be paid gladly, not as a ransom. Niḥlah, the word for this gladness, was explained by Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿĀʾishah and others as meaning the dowry itself, and as obligatory and specified.
- No marriage is valid without the required dower, and false promises about it do not count.
- A waiver of the dower is valid only when the wife's own self, not just her resignation, gives it freely. Pressure-produced forgiveness forgives nothing.
- The same rule governs property of every kind: nothing of another person's is lawful without their glad consent, which is why shares of inheritance and pressured donations must also be returned or honoured.
- Taken from a willing wife, what is given back is wholesome enjoyment. The food of the generous is a remedy, the food of the miser a disease.
