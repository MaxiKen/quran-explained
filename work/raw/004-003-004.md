The previous verse warned guardians against eating up an orphan's property. This verse deals with the same danger from another side, the marriage of orphan girls: "If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice."

Before Islam, a guardian could pick out an orphan girl in his care who was beautiful or owned valuable property, marry her himself or to his son, fix whatever dower he liked and keep her wealth as an extra gain. The verse shuts that door. The Qur'an makes it clear that every excuse, device or trick for taking an orphan's property is forbidden, and that guardians must protect an orphan's rights honestly. The verse also does something larger. It limits the number of wives a man may have at one time to four, and it ties even that permission to one condition, justice.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺"**. Here "orphans" means orphan girls. Under the Sharīʿah (the revealed law), the word yatīm covers any child, boy or girl, who has not reached puberty. The fear named in the verse is the fear of acting unjustly toward the girl in one's care. Some explanations spell it out. A guardian who fears that he will not give her a dowry suitable for women of her standing, or that he will not treat her well, should not marry her. He should marry other women instead, because there are plenty of them and Allah has not restricted him. Other explanations link the fear to women in general. If you fear that you will not keep an orphan's wealth safe, you should fear just as much that you will fail to deal fairly with women, in their upkeep and in the share of time they are owed. Both readings are discussed below.

**"then marry other women of your choice"**. The Arabic is mā ṭāba lakum, literally "what seems good to you". Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Ibn Jubair and Ibn Mālik explained it as mā ḥalla, meaning women who are lawful for you. Others take the plain sense, the women you like. There is no conflict between the two. It can mean women whom you like by natural inclination and who are also lawful for you under the Sharīʿah. Grammatically, the word mā here stands for man, "those who". The words leave out the orphan girls in this situation. You may marry as many women as you wish, other than the orphan girls whose rights you fear to neglect.

**"two, three, or four"** means that each man may marry two, three or four, and may not go beyond that. It is a permission, not a command. A later section explains why the limit is four.

**"But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession"**. The verse says: if you fear that you cannot be just among your wives if you marry more than one, then marry only one, or be content with the female captives and servants you possess. Treating those women equally is not obligatory but recommended. If a man does so, that is good, and if he does not, there is no harm in it. Other explanations give the reason that these women do not have the same rights as wives. A man does not owe them an equal share of his nights, and they do not have to observe a waiting period.

One caution is needed. The Qur'an's phrase about a bondwoman whom one owns has very special conditions, and those conditions generally do not exist today. To start living with a woman outside marriage on the claim that she is one's "bondwoman" is a false claim. It is plainly ḥarām, forbidden.

**"This way you are less likely to commit injustice"**. The Arabic verb is taʿūlū, from a root meaning to tilt or incline. The sense is: from doing injustice. Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, as-Suddī, Qatādah, ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, ash-Shaʿbī, an-Nakhaʿī, Abū Rāzīn, Abū Mālik, al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrimah, Mujāhid, ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿAbbās all said in reports collected by Ibn Abī Ḥātim that taʿūlū means to deviate from justice. Ibn Abī Ḥātim, Ibn Mardawayh and Ibn Ḥibbān, in his Ṣaḥīḥ, also recorded a report that ʿĀʾishah said the Prophet ﷺ himself gave this meaning. But Ibn Abī Ḥātim said that his father judged that attribution to the Prophet ﷺ to be a mistake, and that the words belong to ʿĀʾishah. Other explanations say the meaning is that you will not lean toward some wives at the expense of others, or go beyond the limits in providing for four wives and sharing your time among them.

### What the verse is about: orphan girls and their guardians

ʿĀʾishah explained the verse to her nephew ʿUrwah ibn az-Zubayr, in a report recorded by al-Bukhārī. She said it concerns an orphan girl raised in her guardian's household, sharing in his property. Her wealth and her beauty tempt him to marry her without paying the dowry that another suitor might have paid. Guardians were therefore forbidden to marry such girls unless they treated them justly and gave them the best dowry. Otherwise they were ordered to marry other women.

Al-Bukhārī also recorded her account of a particular case. A man was caring for an orphan girl and married her, although he did not want her. Her money was mixed with his, and he kept her share from her. The verse was revealed about him. A second report from ʿĀʾishah in al-Bukhārī gives the facts a little differently. Someone had an orphan girl in his care, and she owned a share in his fruit farm. He married her, and instead of giving her a dowry and gifts from his own pocket, he took her share of the farm into his own hands. Then the verse came down. Its sense is that if a man fears he cannot be just to a girl in his care after marrying her, he should not marry her. He should marry other women of his choice.

Muslim recorded a report of ʿĀʾishah's words from Abū Kurayb, who had it from Abū Usāmah, who had it from Hishām. She said the verse was revealed about any guardian who has a wealthy female orphan with no one to defend her rights. He will not marry her to anyone because he is greedy for her money, and he harms her and treats her badly. So Allah says: if you fear you will not be fair to the orphans, marry other women who appeal to you, provided they are lawful for you, and leave this girl alone.

ʿĀʾishah added that people later asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ again about marrying orphan girls, and Allah revealed the verse that begins "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding women" (4:127). She said that its words about women "whom you desire to marry" refer to a guardian who has no wish to marry the orphan girl under his care because she has neither wealth nor beauty. Guardians were forbidden to marry the orphan girls who had both property and beauty unless they were just to them, since they generally avoid marrying the ones who have neither.

This is how things stood before Islam. Guardians picked out the orphan girls with good looks or valuable property and married them, or arranged for their sons to marry them. They fixed a dower of their own choosing, usually the lowest, and kept the girls in whatever way they liked, since they were the guardians and caretakers. Their fathers were not there to look after their rights. A father would have given his daughter in marriage to a suitable man only after thinking carefully about everything a daughter faces in married life, and he would have made sure she stayed happy and well looked after.

### The other early explanation

Another group of early scholars read the link between orphans and marriage differently. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Qatādah, ar-Rabīʿ, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and as-Suddī said that people used to be wary of taking orphans' wealth, but they took liberties with women and married whomever they pleased. Sometimes they were fair to their wives and sometimes they were not. When they asked about the orphans, the verse "Give orphans their wealth" was revealed, and Allah then also revealed this verse. It says: just as you fear being unfair to the orphan, you should fear being unfair to women. So marry only as many as you can fulfil the rights of. In weakness and helplessness, women are like orphans. Ibn ʿAbbās held the same opinion, in the report of al-Wālibī.

In the same spirit it is said that those who fear failing to preserve orphans' wealth should also fear failing to treat women fairly in their upkeep and in the share of time they are owed. This was needed because men took as many wives as they pleased, sometimes nine or ten. One example is Qays ibn al-Ḥārith, who had eight. Allah put a stop to this and barred them from marrying more than four.

### Can a guardian marry off a minor girl?

The verse is also taken to prove that the guardian of an orphan girl may give her in marriage while she is still a minor, before puberty. The condition is that his decision rests on wisdom and on her future welfare. This guards against unfair practices found in some communities, where a grown girl is married to a child, with no regard for the difference in their ages and no inquiry into the boy's circumstances or character.

An orphan girl who has reached puberty is entitled to choose her husband freely, as an adult. Yet girls in traditionally Muslim societies tend to be modest and shy. They usually do not speak out strongly about their own marriage, even though they are adults with the right to choose. They are likely to accept in good faith what their guardians arrange. This makes it the guardians' bounden duty to stay clear of anything that violates the rights of orphan girls.

In short, the verse requires total vigilance over the marriage rights of orphan girls. Even so, the duty of enforcing it is not placed directly on the government, as laws made by governments usually are. People themselves are told to fear Allah and do what is right. If they sense that they might be unjust in marrying an orphan girl, they must give up the idea and marry someone else, since the choice is wide. On top of that divine motive, the agencies of government have the responsibility to make sure such violations do not occur in society. If they do occur, the government must use the force of law to see that the rights are fulfilled.

### Plural marriage before Islam, and what the Qur'an changed

That one man could have more than one wife was accepted in every religion of the world before Islam. The custom existed in Arabia, India, Iran, Egypt, Babylon and many other lands. No one can deny that it has a natural basis, even now. People in modern Europe broke with the practice of their forebears and made plural marriage impermissible. The result was a multiplication of concubines and girlfriends kept outside marriage. Even in the West a steady line of opinion, though not a strong one, has seen the benefit of plural marriage. Davenport, a well-known Christian writer, concluded from the Bible that plural wives are not only allowed but a source of blessing from God. Father Nixon, John Milton and Isaac Taylor strongly supported the same view. Vedic teachings, it is said, allow an unlimited number of wives at one time, even ten, thirteen or twenty-seven. Shri Krishna, highly respected among Hindus, had hundreds of wives.

A law or religion that aims to guard against adultery needs this permission. It is also a remedy where women outnumber men in some places. Without it, mistresses and prostitutes multiply. This is why nations that do not allow plural wives end up with rampant adultery, and calling it "free sex" does not change what it is. Today Europe and America look down on what they call polygamy and ban plural wives. Yet they let a man take up with as many women as he can find, under the cover of "friendship", with endless words invented to avoid naming it: "relationship", "affair", "consenting adults", "union", "partner". Saying no to marriage and yes to adultery is strange indeed.

So the custom of taking many wives was common before Islam, with no limit set anywhere. The history of nations and faiths shows that no religion or law drew a line here, not the Jews, not the Christians, not the Hindus and Aryans, not the Zoroastrians.

In the early period of Islam, the custom went on without limit. Some people took too many wives out of greed. Later they could not be fair to all of them, and the wives lived like prisoners in the chains of marriage. Fair treatment hardly existed. Everything depended on personal choice or whim, so the favourite of today could be forgotten tomorrow. Concern for the wives' standing rights was very far away.

The Qur'an put an end to this great injustice. It limited the number of wives by declaring that keeping more than four women in marriage is ḥarām. It also gave a stern warning against breaking the divine command that the rights of women married at the same time must be met equally and faithfully.

### Why four, and no more

The verse says "two, three, or four", and scholars concluded that four is the limit. Ibn ʿAbbās and most scholars said so, because the verse states what men are allowed, and if more than four were allowed the verse would have said it. Someone might point to another verse that describes the angels as having wings "two, three, or four" (35:1). That wording does not mean that no angel has more than four wings, since there is evidence that some have more. For marriage, though, the verse fixes what is allowed.

The practice of the Prophet ﷺ confirms it. Aḥmad recorded, through Sālim from his father, that Ghilān ibn Salamah ath-Thaqafī, whom another report calls Ghaylān ibn Aslamah, had ten wives when he became Muslim, and that they had all embraced Islam with him. The Prophet ﷺ told him:

> "Choose any four of them and divorce the rest."

Ash-Shāfiʿī, at-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah, ad-Dāraquṭnī and al-Bayhaqī collected this hadith as far as the words "Choose any four of them". Only Aḥmad has the full version. In Mishkāt (page 274) it is recorded with at-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah as its references.

The reasoning is plain. Had marrying more than four at once been allowed, the Prophet ﷺ would have let Ghilān keep every one of his wives, since they all accepted Islam together. Instead he told him to keep four and divorce the rest. This shows that a man may not keep more than four wives at a time in any circumstances. If that is the rule for men who already had more than four when they became Muslim, it applies even more strongly to marrying more than four.

Qays ibn al-Ḥārith al-Asadī gave a similar account. "When I embraced Islam, I had eight wives," he said. "I mentioned this to the Prophet ﷺ, and he said, 'Keep four of them and divorce the rest.'" Abū Daud recorded it. According to a report in the Musnad of ash-Shāfiʿī, Nawfal ibn Muʿāwiyah ad-Daylamī had five wives in his nikāḥ (marriage) when he became Muslim, and the Prophet ﷺ asked him to divorce one. This also appears in Mishkāt (page 274), with Sharḥ as-Sunnah as its reference. Together, the Prophet's ﷺ practice and that of his Companions make the meaning of the verse very clear. Joining more than four women in marriage is ḥarām.

Ghilān's story goes on. During the caliphate of ʿUmar, Ghilān divorced the wives he had kept and divided his money among his children. ʿUmar's reaction is reported in two ways. In one, he said to him:

> "I think the devil has conveyed to your heart the news of your approaching death, from what he hears by eavesdropping. It may be that you will not live much longer. By Allah, you will take back your wives and your money, or I will take everything and order that your grave be stoned, as is done with the grave of Abū Righāl."

Abū Righāl belonged to Thamūd. He escaped their punishment for as long as he stayed in the Sacred Area, and suffered it as they did once he left. In the other report, ʿUmar summoned Ghilān and said, "You have done this to keep these women from your inheritance, and that is a gross injustice. Revoke the divorces right now and take your property back from your sons. If you do not, beware of a severe punishment." The two reports agree in the point that matters: women's rights may not be taken away by a trick.

In law, if a man contracts marriage with more than four women in one agreement, that marriage is void from the start, because no one has the right to more than four wives. Marriages within the limit of four are valid.

### Justice between wives

The verse allows up to four wives, and in the same breath it warns: "But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one." So having more than one wife is lawful and fitting only on the condition that equality among all of them can be kept, as the Sharīʿah requires, and that every wife's rights can be fully met. A man who cannot do this should keep to one wife. The wrongs of multiple marriage in the days before Islam, with no thought for wives' rights, had made a mockery of this part of human life. So the Qur'an told people: if you cannot do perfect justice between wives, restrict yourself to one, or make do with a bondwoman you own.

The outcome is this. The Qur'an permits four wives, and a marriage within that limit is valid. But when a man has more than one wife, it becomes obligatory (wājib) to keep justice and equality among them, and doing otherwise is a grave sin. Anyone who thinks of taking a second wife should first look at all the factors around him and, above all, look into himself. He should weigh and consider honestly whether he has the ability to treat all his wives equally and justly, without the least violation of their rights. If it is strongly likely that he will fall short, then stepping into a second marriage is a thoughtless plunge into a grave sin. He should stay away from it. Given the human condition, living with one wife should be seen as quite enough.

There is a legal side too. If a wife's rights are violated, she can bring the matter before an Islamic court and receive redress.

Another explanation puts the same point from the side of accountability. Allah gave free men the right to marry four women at one time, and He made justice between them a duty. A servant must therefore respect what is obligatory. If he knows he can keep this obligation properly, his choice is lawful. If he knows he will fall short, he should not do it, because he is accountable for what is obligatory.

The Prophet ﷺ stressed full equality and justice between wives, and he gave stern warnings to those who do otherwise. He also showed the ideal in his own conduct. He went to the very edge of equality even in matters where equality is not required. He said:

> "Whoever has two wives and does not treat them equally and justly will be raised on the Day of Judgment with one of his shoulders hanging down."

This is recorded in Mishkāt.

The equality required covers what is in a man's control, such as spending on each wife and sharing nights equally. It does not cover what is out of his control, such as the natural inclination of his heart, which may lean toward one of them. There is no accountability for that, because it is not a matter of choice. The binding condition is that this inclination must not affect the things he can control. The Prophet ﷺ treated his wives with full equality in everything he controlled, and yet he pleaded with his Lord:

> "O Allah, this is my share in what I control. Do not hold me to account for what You control and I do not."

If even a Messenger who was protected from error could not do more, no one else can claim to. This is why another verse mentions this matter that lies outside a man's control: "You will never be able to maintain ˹emotional˺ justice between your wives—no matter how keen you are" (4:129). Love and the heart's inclination are beyond a person's power, so perfect equality in what comes from the heart cannot be reached. Yet even this involuntary part is not left unchecked. The same verse goes on: "So do not totally incline towards one leaving the other in suspense." If you love one wife more, nothing can be done about it. But total neglect of the other wife is not allowed even then. The justice and equality in "if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice" are the justice in matters of choice and will. Any shortfall there is a great sin. So much so that a man who sees the danger of falling into it has been told not to marry more than one woman.

### Does this verse contradict 4:129?

Some people lose sight of these details and fall into a strange error. They set this verse beside 4:129 and become confused. This verse says, "If you fear you will not be just, then marry one." The other says plainly that justice between wives is impossible. So they conclude that having more than one wife should not be allowed at all.

They should ask themselves a question. If Allah meant to put a cap on plural marriage through these verses, why go into all these details? Why would the Qur'an say "marry other women of your choice, two, three or four"? And what would the words "if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice" mean, since in that case injustice would be certain and the fear would have no point? Besides, the words and deeds of the Prophet ﷺ and the Companions, and their consistent practice, prove that having more than one wife, up to four, was never prevented in Islam.

The truth is as already stated. This verse speaks of justice and equality in what a man can do by choice. The other verse points to his inability to control unequal feeling in love and emotional inclination. The two verses do not contradict each other, and they do not prove that plural marriage is forbidden outright.

### What "less likely to commit injustice" teaches

The word adnā, "nearer", comes from a root meaning nearness, and taʿūlū means leaning or inclining. Here it means an impermissible leaning that ends in injustice and wrongdoing. The sense is: what you have been told in this verse, to keep to one wife or to make do with a bondwoman if you cannot be just, is something that will help keep you from injustice if you follow it. It cuts down the chance of further oppression and transgression. Others say the phrase means that the outcome is nearer to your not being unjust, in not leaning toward some at the cost of others.

A question arises. If a man has only one wife, it seems that injustice has no room at all. Why does the verse say "nearer to" and not that it will make him completely safe from injustice? The answer is that the word "nearer" is there for the people who would not hesitate to do all kinds of injustice even to one wife. For them, having only one wife is not enough to block injustice. In that situation the danger of injustice does shrink, and a man comes closer to justice. But complete deliverance from injustice and oppression will come only when the rights of the one wife are fully given and she is treated fairly and generously, with her shortcomings forgiven and her crooked ways met with patience.

### Why the Prophet ﷺ had more than four wives

A question follows naturally from a verse that limits other men to four. Why did the Prophet ﷺ have more? The answer starts with the purpose of his life. He was mercy and blessing in person. The main purpose of his life in this world was to spread the call of Islam, to purify people and to teach the Qur'an. By his words and by his deeds he made the teachings of Islam shine across the world. There is no part of human life that does not need a prophet's guidance. From setting up the congregational prayer to keeping good relations with wives, raising children and even the manners of answering the call of nature, he left spoken and practical instructions that are preserved in the books of hadith. These tell in great detail what he did inside the walls of his home, how he dealt with his wives and how he answered the questions of the women who came to his house to ask. Such questions number in the hundreds. His wives were the means through which the Muslim ummah received this guidance, and no other setting could easily have provided it. That need for education, teaching and the spreading of the message made it all the more necessary that he marry the wives he did.

Think of ʿĀʾishah. About two thousand and ten narrations are reported from her. They cover a wide range: the explanation of the Qur'an's rulings, legal and religious questions with their answers, ethics and etiquette, and the Sīrah, the life of the Prophet ﷺ. From Umm Salmah the reports number up to three hundred and seventy-eight. Ḥāfiẓ Ibn al-Qayyim said that if the rulings Umm Salmah gave after the Prophet's ﷺ death were collected, they could make a treatise of their own. ʿĀʾishah's rank in hadith, in jurisprudence and in rulings is too well known to need repeating. Her students alone number around two hundred. For forty-eight years after the Prophet ﷺ died she was a beacon of religious knowledge, and she did much to carry his mission forward. These two wives are only examples. Many hadiths come from his other wives as well, and the benefit of this teaching reached the community through them.

### What his life says about the charge of desire

The Prophet ﷺ had the highest of aims. He came in the golden line of the great prophets. He saw the whole world as one unit and thought about mending every human relationship, between individuals, within families and between nations. A modern person who is driven by his own desires finds it hard even to imagine such ideals. At most he thinks of everyone else by his own example. Some critics, among the atheists and orientalists of the West, are like this. They are comfortable with their own distortion, and so they misjudge the unquestionable character of the Prophet ﷺ. By their standards of "civilised" thinking, his many marriages were only an expression of sexual desire. Anyone who is sane and fair would not read his marriages that way if he looked even briefly at his life.

In front of the Quraysh of Makkah, he led a life without blame. At twenty-five he married an older widow with children, whose two earlier husbands had died, and he lived with her for the next twenty-five years. During those years he would sometimes leave home for as long as a month and stay alone in the Cave of Ḥirāʾ, giving his time to the remembrance of his Lord. All his later marriages came after he was past fifty. The people of Makkah saw the first fifty years of his life, his youth above all, and not even an enemy ever found anything to point to that could cast doubt on his purity. His enemies tried every arrow they had. They called him a magician, a sorcerer, a poet, a madman, a liar and a fabricator. But they never said one word about his innocent life, or about any crookedness in his private conduct. So why would a man who spent fifty years in such righteousness, abstaining from the pleasures of life, be driven to marry again and again? A person with the least sense of justice will find no reason other than those given here.

Consider how the marriages actually came about. From the age of twenty-five to fifty, Khadījah lived with him as his only wife. After she died, he married Sawdah and ʿĀʾishah. Sawdah came to live with him, while ʿĀʾishah stayed with her father because she was still young. Some years later, in the second year after the Hijrah, ʿĀʾishah came to live with him in Madīnah. He was then in his fifty-fourth year, with two wives. What is called plural marriage begins at this point. A year later he married Ḥafṣah. Some months after that, Zaynab bint Khuzaymah came into his nikāḥ, but she died about a year and a half later. By another report she was his wife for just three months. Then he married Umm Salmah in the fourth year after the Hijrah and Zaynab bint Jaḥsh in the fifth, when he was fifty-eight. At that fairly advanced age, he had four wives. He could have had four right after the Qur'an gave Muslims the permission, but he never did. Later he married Juwayriyyah in the sixth year, Umm Ḥabībah in the seventh, Ṣafiyyah in the seventh, and Maymūnah later in the same year.

So he lived with one wife until the age of fifty-four, twenty-five years with Khadījah and four or five with Sawdah. At fifty-eight he had four wives, and the remaining wives joined him within two or three years.

Of all his wives, only one, ʿĀʾishah aṣ-Ṣiddīqah, the Mother of the Faithful, married him as a virgin. All the others were widows, except Zaynab bint Jaḥsh, and some had been married twice before and lost their husbands. This plural marriage came in his later years. His Companions, men and women, loved him dearly and would have given their lives at his word. If he had been so inclined, he would have married only virgins. Nothing stopped him from keeping a wife for a month or two and then taking new ones in her place. He never did this.

One more fact deserves mention. Muḥammad al-Muṣṭafā ﷺ was a true prophet of Allah, and prophets do not live for worldly appetite. He does not chase his own wishes. Everything he does is done by Allah's permission and will. Anyone who accepts that he is a prophet has already answered such objections. But take a person who refuses to accept him as a prophet and goes further, saying that he allowed himself many wives to gratify his desire. That person can be asked why the Prophet ﷺ himself announced, openly, the restriction on himself in this matter: "It is not lawful for you ˹O Prophet˺ to marry more women after this" (33:52). That he declared such a limit against himself shows that whatever he did was with the leave and will of his Lord. The educational benefits that reached the Muslim community through his plural marriages, together with the injunctions of Islam that came with them, are so many and so detailed that they cannot be listed here, even in the briefest form. Those who study the books of hadith will see the truth of this.

### Three marriages and what they taught

Umm Salmah. The Prophet ﷺ married her after her husband, Abū Salmah, had died. She came to live in his house with her children from her earlier marriage, and he took care of the children and brought them up with love and concern. In this way he showed through his own conduct how stepchildren should be raised with love and care. She was the only one of his wives who came with children. Had none of them been like her, this part of raising stepchildren would have gone unshown, and the Muslim community would have lacked guidance in it. Her son ʿUmar ibn Abī Salmah grew up in the Messenger's ﷺ care. He recalled that once, when they were eating together, his hand roamed all over the bowl. The Prophet ﷺ told him:

> "Say the name of Allah, eat with your right hand, and eat from what is in front of you."

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim recorded this, as cited in Mishkāt.

Juwayriyyah. She came as a prisoner taken in jihād. Like the other prisoners, she was distributed and fell to the share of Thābit ibn Qays or his cousin. She reached an agreement with her master that she would pay him a sum of money and he would free her. After this she came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked for financial help. He said, "Shall I not tell you something better than this? I will pay on your behalf and marry you." She agreed gladly. He paid what was due and married her. The result was unusually kind. Hundreds of Juwayriyyah's people had passed into the Companions' ownership, since all of them had arrived as prisoners. When the Companions learned that the Prophet ﷺ had married her, they all released their slaves out of respect for him. They felt that these people were now tied to the Prophet's ﷺ family by marriage, and they could not dare to keep them as slaves. ʿĀʾishah said of this: "Because of his marriage with Juwayriyyah, one hundred families from Banū al-Muṣṭaliq became free. I know of no other woman who proved a greater blessing for her people."

Umm Ḥabībah. She became Muslim in Makkah in the early days of Islam, with her husband. Both migrated to Ethiopia with a group of men and women. After they arrived, her husband became a Christian, and a few days later he died. The Prophet ﷺ proposed to her through the good offices of the Najāshī (the Negus, emperor of Ethiopia), she accepted, and in Ethiopia the Najāshī gave her in marriage to the Prophet ﷺ. It is notable that Umm Ḥabībah was the daughter of Abū Sufyān. At that time he led the party whose main aim in life was hostility to Islam. They looked constantly for excuses to hurt the Muslims and to torment the Prophet of Allah, and given the chance they would not have hesitated to do away with him. When Abū Sufyān heard of this marriage, he said without thinking, "He is a man of valour. His nose cannot be cut off." The sense was that the Prophet ﷺ is a man of honour and not easily disgraced. They were doing all they could to disgrace him, and meanwhile, right under their noses, their own daughter had become his wife.

In effect, this marriage became a tool of psychological warfare. It dampened the hopes that the disbelievers' leader held against the Muslims. That it brought political gain to Islam and the Muslims can hardly be denied, given how important it was. Allah's farsighted and wise Messenger probably had that gain in view.

Beyond this short summary, those with deeper knowledge of the Sīrah will find still more wisdom in his plural marriages. Mawlānā Ashraf Thānawī's treatise on the subject, Kathrat al-Azwāj li-Ṣāḥib al-Miʿrāj, is useful. These details are given to remove a trap laid by critics. It is sad but true that the trap catches a lot of educated Muslims who have never learned the Sīrah of the noble Prophet ﷺ or the history of Islam from authentic works. They fall into the spider's web and do not come out, because what they know of Islam comes from books written by orientalists who are hostile to it.

### In short

- The verse forbids guardians to marry an orphan girl in order to get at her property. If a guardian fears he will not be just to her or give her a proper dowry, he must marry other women. Another view says that just as one fears injustice to orphans, one must fear injustice to women, so one marries only as many as one can be fair to.
- Before Islam there was no limit on wives, and wives suffered for it. The Qur'an limited the number to four, and the Prophet's ﷺ command to Ghilān ibn Salamah, Qays ibn al-Ḥārith and Nawfal ibn Muʿāwiyah to keep four and divorce the rest shows that more than four is ḥarām.
- More than one wife is lawful only for a man who can truly be just. Justice is required in what a man controls, like spending and nights, and not in the heart's inclination, but he must not totally lean away from the other wife (4:129). A man who fears he will fall short must marry one woman only.
- The Prophet's ﷺ marriages served the teaching of the community, and his own life, with a single wife for twenty-five years and plural marriage only after fifty, answers the charge of desire.
- The phrase "less likely to commit injustice" means that keeping to one wife, or to one's bondwomen, is the safer way, though full deliverance from injustice comes only from giving even one wife her full rights with patience and generosity.

<<<VERSE 4:4>>>
The previous verse set out to remove the injustice done to women through unlimited marriages. This verse takes up one particular right of women and the injustice practised over it, the right to the dower: "Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience." The dower, called mahr, belongs to the woman herself. It is an obligation on the husband, to be paid with a good heart and kept as hers unless she freely chooses otherwise. Before Islam, men used to marry without giving their wives any dowry at all.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries"**. "Dowries" translates ṣaduqāt, the plural of ṣaduqah. The words ṣaduqah and ṣudāq both mean the dower of a woman. Another name for the same thing is mahr, plural muhūr. Mullā ʿAlī Qārī explains in the Mirqāt, his commentary on the Mishkāt, that the dower is called by this name because its root, ṣadaqa, carries the sense of truth. A dower shows the true attitude of a husband toward his wife, so the name fits. The command is an obligation from Allah on the husbands, and one short explanation renders it simply: give women their dowries with good will.

The command is addressed to the husbands, so that they hand the dower to their wives themselves and not to someone else. It is also addressed to the guardians of the girls. If a guardian happens to receive the dower meant for a girl, he must give it straight to her, and he must not put any of it to his own use without her permission.

**"graciously"** translates niḥlah. Ibn ʿAbbās said, in the report of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, that it refers to the dowry. ʿĀʾishah said that it means "obligatory". Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq reported this from az-Zuhrī, who had it from ʿUrwah. Muqātil, Qatādah and Ibn Jurayj also said it means obligatory, and Ibn Jurayj added the word "specified". Ibn Zayd said that niḥlah, in Arabic, means what is required. The command is therefore that a man must not marry unless he gives his wife what is hers by right. After the Prophet ﷺ, no one may marry a woman without the dowry that is due, or by making false promises about the dowry he plans to give. Others explain the word as a verbal noun that means a present given out of the kindness of one's heart.

In Arabic usage, niḥlah means giving something cheerfully, and this matters. A husband must pay his wife's dowry willingly, in the way a person hands over a present he is glad to give. Every obligatory right is to be paid cheerfully as a rule, and the dower is no exception. Give it in good cheer.

**"But if they waive some of it willingly"**. If the wife gives her husband part or all of the dowry with a good heart, he is allowed to take it, since it is lawful for him in that case. Other explanations say: if they make it lawful for you to take some of the dowry, or if they themselves are happy for you to take a part of it and they give it. The words "of their own accord" in the Arabic pick out the women as the ones whose own pleasure is meant. Another short explanation says: if they gladly give up part of the dowry, of their own free will.

**"then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience"**. The Arabic words are hanīʾan and marīʾan. They are modifiers. Hanīʾan, from hanaʾa, means something received without effort and pain. Said of food, it means good food that goes down and is digested without trouble, and then nourishes the body. Marīʾan, from maraʾa, "to be wholesome", is used in nearly the same sense, and some translators have used one word, "wholesome", for both. The sense is that the husband may eat it as good, wholesome food, with no harm in it for him in the Hereafter. This was revealed in answer to those who disliked the idea of consuming it. Other explanations say there is no blame or sin on him.

### Three wrongs the verse corrects

In Arabia before Islam, the dower was wronged in several ways. The verse addresses all of them. The same wrongs, sadly, still survive in some tribes and regions among Muslims, and every Muslim must keep clear of them.

The first was that the dower, the right of the girl being married, was not given to her at all. Her guardians took it from the husband directly. This was plain injustice. The command "give women their dowries" aims to end it, and it is addressed to husbands and to guardians, as explained above.

The second was in the attitude of the giver. A man who realised he had no way out would pay it sourly and unwillingly, as though paying a penalty. The word niḥlah removes this wrong. It tells the husband to give the dower as something the woman is owed, and cheerfully.

The third was pressure. Many husbands took advantage of the wife's weakness and forced her to forgive her dower. This was no real forgiveness. The husband simply went about free of any worry about the dower, which in his mind had been forgiven. The verse ends this with "But if they waive some of it willingly". It means that if the women freely give up some of it out of their own pleasure, you may have it and enjoy it. Pressing an unwilling wife into forgiving her dower achieves nothing. Nothing is actually forgiven that way. But if a wife, out of her own genuine free choice, gives up a part of the dower or returns it to her husband after receiving it, that is permitted for the husband and valid.

### Why the wife's consent must be real

The restriction "willingly" has a secret of its own. Under the standard rule of the Sharīʿah, no one may take even a small share of another person's property unless it is given gladly. The Prophet ﷺ laid down this rule when he said:

> "Beware, do no injustice. A person's property is not lawful for another unless it comes with his own free will."

This is recorded in Mishkāt. It is a great principle, and many details flow from it.

Today, too, there are women who doubt that they will ever receive their dower. They think that asking for it will please no one, and that refusing to forgive it will make no one happier. So, like it or not, they let it go. Forgiveness of this kind cannot be trusted. Mawlānā Ashraf Thānawī used to say that the true test of gladly forgiving is to put the full amount of the dower into the hands of the wife first, as its owner. She may later give it to the husband of her own will, without pressure.

The same attitude of giving gladly should guide the inheritance of sisters and wives. It is not unusual that after a mother or father dies, the sons take over the whole property and give the girls nothing. If the people who seize it feel any moral or religious unease, the most they do is go and excuse themselves to their sisters. The sisters know they will not get their share anyway, so they go along and forgive against their will. In addition, the share of the surviving wife is not given to her after the husband's death, and a stepmother in particular gets none. All of this is open usurping of rights. The one exception is a person who forgives gladly, and that forgiveness is possible and valid.

Ashraf Thānawī also pointed out that the text speaks of the willingness of the whole self, and not simply the happiness of the heart. The heart's happiness alone is not enough to make another person's property lawful. People who give money in bribes or as interest do so after a good deal of calculation and for many apparent gains, but this is not giving gladly, and it cannot be trusted. If such people questioned their conscience honestly, they would find that their self flatly refuses to agree to such giving. This is why good cheer and free will have been given the deciding role.

The same applies to donations. If money is sought for mosques, religious schools or any other need, the giver must be giving freely and gladly. If someone with tribal, social or legal authority or influence pressures him, and the gift does not come from his own free choice and pleasure, it is not lawful to accept and use it. It should be returned to the giver.

### Food given with a generous heart

The words "enjoy it freely" suggest something about the heart. The food of the chivalrous, the fityān, and of the generous, the askhiyāʾ, is wholesome, because they feed others only out of a good heart. The food of the miserly is spoiled, because they think only of themselves and give others food out of duty and not from the heart. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "The food of the generous is a remedy, and the food of the miser is a disease."

### In short

- The dowry is the woman's own right and an obligation on the husband, to be paid to her and not to her guardians, and to be given with a good heart.
- Before Islam it was wronged in three ways: it was not given to the girl, it was paid grudgingly, and it was extracted from a powerless wife by pressure. The verse corrects all three.
- A husband may enjoy any part of the dowry that his wife gives up freely, but forgiveness won by pressure is worthless.
- The rule behind it is that no one's property is lawful to another unless it is given gladly. This applies to inheritance, bribes, interest and donations too.
- On its inner side, food given from a generous heart is a remedy, and food given grudgingly is a disease.
