<<<VERSE 2:178>>>
After the brief introduction to the nature of righteousness in verse 177, the text turns to its subsidiary injunctions, and the first is the law of qiṣāṣ, equal retaliation. "O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder—a free man for a free man, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female." The verse continues: "But if the offender is pardoned by the victim's guardian, then blood-money should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord. But whoever transgresses after that will suffer a painful punishment". The killer is to be killed whatever the status of the parties. If the aggrieved party relents and forgoes retaliation without forgiving the offence entirely, the killer must pay the fixed blood-money (diyah) fairly and promptly, and the claimant must pursue it in a recognised manner without harassment. This law of pardon and blood-money is a relief granted by Allah's grace, for otherwise nothing but death would face the killer. Whoever crosses Allah's limits after that, by filing a false or doubtful murder charge or by reopening a case after pardon, is severely punished.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder"**. Qiṣāṣ means likeness, and in usage returning like for like. As a term of the Sharīʿah it is the equal retaliation for an aggression against a person's body, allowed only on condition that the principle of like for like is strictly observed, as verse 194 of this sūrah says more plainly, "aggress against him in the like manner as he did against you", and as the end of Sūrat al-Naḥl says: "If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered" (16:126). It applies exclusively to culpable homicide, where someone is killed deliberately with a lethal weapon. The believers are told not to transgress the limits as nations before them did, changing what Allah ordained. In the Jāhiliyyah the Jewish tribe of Banū al-Naḍīr had defeated Qurayẓah and made it a rule that a Naḍīr man who killed a Qurayẓah man was not killed but paid a hundred wasq of dates, while a Qurayẓah man who killed a Naḍīr man was killed, or, if Naḍīr chose ransom, paid two hundred wasq. Allah commanded justice in the penal law and the avoidance of the path of the misguided who alter His commands in disbelief and transgression.

**"a free man for a free man, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female."**. These words refer to the event behind the revelation. Just before Islam, war broke out between two Arab tribes and many men and women, free and slave, were killed on both sides. The case was still unsettled when both tribes entered Islam and began to discuss retaliation. The stronger tribe insisted on nothing less than a free man for their slave and a man for their woman from the other side, even if he was not the killer. The verse refutes this barbaric demand: the one who killed is the one to be killed, and an innocent free man or man may not be killed for a killer who was a slave or a woman. The verse does not mean that no retaliation is taken from a man who kills a woman or a free man who kills a slave. Its opening words prescribe retaliation "in cases of murder" generally, and other verses state it more explicitly, such as "a life for a life" (5:45), which is said to abrogate the earlier wording. The Sunnah makes clear that a man is killed for a woman, and that religion is taken into account, so that a Muslim is not killed for a disbeliever even if the Muslim is a slave and the disbeliever free. Al-Bukhārī reports from ʿAlī that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said a Muslim is not killed for a disbeliever, and no sound opinion or authentic hadith contradicts it, though Abū Ḥanīfah held that a Muslim may be killed for a disbeliever on the general wording of 5:45 in Sūrat al-Māʾidah. The four imams, Abū Ḥanīfah, Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī and Aḥmad, and the majority hold that a group is killed for one person they murder. ʿUmar said of a boy killed by seven men that if all the people of Ṣanʿāʾ had joined in killing him he would have killed them all, and no Companion disagreed, which amounts to a near consensus (ijmāʿ). One opinion attributed to Aḥmad is that only one person is killed for one, and Ibn al-Mundhir attributes this also to Muʿādh, Ibn al-Zubayr, ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān, al-Zuhrī, Ibn Sīrīn and Ḥabīb ibn Abī Thābit.

**"But if the offender is pardoned by the victim's guardian"**. This refers to accepting blood-money in cases of intentional murder, as Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Abū al-Shaʿthāʾ, Mujāhid, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, ʿAṭāʾ, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān held. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported Ibn ʿAbbās as explaining that pardon means the victim's relative accepting the diyah after capital punishment has become due. The pardon may also be the victim's own, before death. The indefinite "anything" of the blood implies that even a partial pardon by the heirs waives retaliation, and the mention of "his brother" is a gentle entreaty to pardon and a reminder that killing does not sever the brotherhood of faith.

**"then blood-money should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously."**. The relative who accepts blood-money should collect his right with kindness and without force, insult or injury, and the killer should pay it in a good way, without delay, fraud, resistance or further harm, so that no one needs to go to court. One reading describes the customary instalments: three years for a full diyah, two for half, one for a third. The diyah in the Sharīʿah is one hundred camels, or one thousand dinars, or ten thousand dirhams, roughly nineteen pounds of silver by current measures. Because the pursuit follows from the pardon, one of al-Shāfiʿī's two opinions is that the blood-money is itself a duty, while the other, which is preferred, is that retaliation is the duty and the blood-money merely compensation for forgoing it, so that if someone pardons without naming blood-money nothing is owed.

**"This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord."**. Allowing blood-money for intentional murder lightens what was required of earlier nations. Saʿīd ibn Manṣūr reports Ibn ʿAbbās as saying that the Children of Israel were bound to apply retaliation and were not permitted to pardon for blood-money, while Allah allowed this Ummah to do so, and Ibn Ḥibbān records this in his Ṣaḥīḥ. Qatādah said that Allah had mercy on this Ummah by giving them the diyah, which no nation before had: the people of the Torah could execute or pardon but could not take blood-money, the people of the Gospel were required to pardon with no diyah legislated, and this Ummah may execute, or pardon and accept blood-money. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān and al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas said the same. Allah gave latitude and did not impose a single course as He made retaliation obligatory for the Jews and pardon for the Christians.

**"But whoever transgresses after that will suffer a painful punishment"**. Whoever kills the killer after taking or agreeing to the blood-money will suffer a painful and severe torment from Allah, as Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿAṭāʾ, ʿIkrimah, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, al-Suddī and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said. One reading adds that such a person is himself killed and is neither forgiven nor allowed to pay blood-money. The punishment is the Fire in the Hereafter or execution in this world.

### Further rulings

If the murderer is fully pardoned, for example when both surviving sons of the victim forgo their right, he is free of any claim. If the pardon is partial, one son pardoning and the other not, the killer is released from retaliation but the son who did not pardon is entitled to half the diyah. Likewise a settlement between the parties on an agreed sum cancels retaliation and makes payment of that sum obligatory, subject to conditions set out in the books of fiqh. The heirs of the victim, however many, inherit the right to retaliation and blood-money according to their shares in the estate. Blood-money taken is divided among them by those shares, and if retaliation is chosen the right is held by all in common. Since retaliation is indivisible, a pardon by any one heir is effective and covers the retaliation rights of the others, who then receive blood-money according to their shares. Although the right of retaliation belongs to the heirs, by the consensus of the Muslim community they may not carry it out themselves. They must obtain it through a Muslim ruler or his deputy, because the details of qiṣāṣ are intricate and beyond the ordinary person, and heirs ignorant of when retaliation does or does not apply might commit excesses in the heat of anger. By unanimous agreement the right must be secured through the agency of an Islamic government, as al-Qurṭubī notes.

Islam's principle of retaliation in like measure, an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth, means that a killer forfeits his own life. This effectively discourages the taking of life, since fear of losing one's own life deters one from taking another's, so that the lives of all members of society are protected. The object of qiṣāṣ is the protection of society, not revenge. It also cools the vengeful feelings of the victim's family and so forestalls further violence. Yet Islam permits a settlement between the killer and the bereaved, who may demand execution, accept blood-money or forgive outright. These alternatives aim to keep brotherly feeling alive in Muslim society and to prevent an atmosphere of rivalry, and blood-money has the further advantage of compensating the family for its loss.

### The law of friendship

In the devotional reading, the verse addresses the body, heart and spirit together: O totality of the servant, if you want to step into the street of friendship, first detach your heart from life and throw away everything you know of states and deeds, for in the law of friendship your life will be taken as retaliation and all your knowledge will be the blood-money, and even more is needed. If you are the man for the work, enter. Otherwise nothing is accomplished with self-love and defilement. In the tracks of manliness the plane tree lives long, and in the tracks of defilement the jasmine fades fast. In the world, whenever someone is killed, retaliation or blood-money is due from the killer. In the law of friendship both are due from the one who is killed. The Pīr of the Path said: how should I have known that there is retaliation for those slain by friendship, or that friendship is sheer resurrection and the slain of friendship must ask for the blood-money? He burns some and kills some, and none who is burned regrets it and none who is killed turns away. How You kill us, and how we love You, how we love the Killer! One is burnt and left unsettled, another slain and bewildered in the field of solitude, one hangs on reports while another is mingled with face-to-face vision, one is in a whirlpool and another longs for water, yet the drowned is not sated and the thirsty cannot sleep.

### In short

- Qiṣāṣ, like-for-like retaliation (cf. 16:126, 5:45), is prescribed for deliberate murder: the killer himself is executed, whether free, slave, man or woman, and the verse's wording refutes a tribe's demand for a free man or a man in place of a slave or woman who had killed.
- A Muslim is not executed for a disbeliever (ʿAlī, al-Bukhārī), though Abū Ḥanīfah differed, and a group is executed for one victim (ʿUmar, near-ijmāʿ).
- The heirs may pardon in exchange for diyah, collected and paid with courtesy, a mercy not granted to the Jews (retaliation only) or the Christians (pardon only). Killing after accepting diyah brings painful punishment.
- Partial pardon or settlement ends retaliation and transfers the claim to blood-money, and the right must be exercised through the ruler, not privately (al-Qurṭubī).
- Inwardly, the law of friendship demands the lover's own life as retaliation, and none so slain regrets it.

<<<VERSE 2:179>>>
Verse 179 states the wisdom of the law just laid down. "There is ˹security of˺ life for you in ˹the law of˺ retaliation, O people of reason, so that you may become mindful ˹of Allah˺". The law of equal retaliation is presented to people of reason as preserving life, not merely taking it. Fear of the punishment deters killing and thereby saves lives.

### Phrase by phrase

**"There is ˹security of˺ life for you in ˹the law of˺ retaliation"**. The explanation finds great benefit in prescribing execution for murder. A would-be killer who expects that penalty holds back. Both his life and his intended victim's are thereby preserved, protecting the sanctity of life. Blood is spared and further attacks between people stopped, and there is a continuation of existence and a lesson in it. The earlier Books contained the saying that killing stops killing, and the Qur'an expresses the idea with far greater clarity and eloquence. Abū al-ʿĀliyah said that Allah made retaliation a life, for how many a man who thought of killing was stopped by this law for fear of being killed in turn. Mujāhid and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr give similar explanations, as do Abū Mālik and al-Ḥasan. The same view comes from Qatādah, al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān.

**"O people of reason"**. You who have sound minds, comprehension and understanding grasp this.

**"so that you may become mindful ˹of Allah˺"**. Perhaps by this you will be compelled to refrain from Allah's prohibitions and from what He counts as sin, warding off the killing of one another for fear of retaliation. Taqwā means doing all acts of obedience and refraining from all that is forbidden, keeping to His sacred law and doing as He instructs.

### The greater life

The devotional reading first recognises the same deterrence. Expecting execution, the would-be killer refrains, and both parties remain alive. By the allusive explanation, however, there is a greater life in abandoning retaliation, for when someone has been annihilated in Allah, He becomes his successor, and life from Him is more perfect than remaining with oneself. In the image, Allah inherits from them and becomes their successor. The Successor's abiding is dearer than the annihilated person's former life.

### In short

- Retaliation preserves life: certainty of execution deters the would-be killer and so protects both him and his intended victim (Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Mujāhid and others).
- The Qur'an states this with more eloquence than the older maxim that killing stops killing.
- The law is addressed to people of reason and aims at taqwā, keeping people from Allah's prohibitions.
- Inwardly, the greatest life belongs to the one annihilated in Allah, whose place He Himself takes.
