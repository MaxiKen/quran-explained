<<<VERSE 2:26>>>
The previous verses dared the doubters to produce a single surah like the Qur'an and then promised Gardens to those who believe and do good. Now the Qur'an turns to a specific complaint that the deniers had been making about its style. Allah had compared the hypocrites to a man who lit a fire and to travellers caught in a storm, and elsewhere He had spoken of a fly and a spider. The objectors said that a Book from God would never stoop to such lowly images. This verse answers them. "Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller." The believers, hearing such a comparison, "know that it is the truth from their Lord," while the disbelievers "argue, 'What does Allah mean by such a parable?'" Then comes the deeper point: the same parable becomes a test through which "He leaves many to stray, and guides many," and the only people it leads astray are "the rebellious," whose marks are listed in the verse that follows.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito"** answers the sneer directly. In human speech, being "ashamed" of something means refusing to do it. When Allah says He is not ashamed of a thing, it means simply that He does not refrain from it. Allah is far above shyness and embarrassment, and the expression is used to meet the objectors on their own terms. How could He disdain to mention a creature which all created beings together could never produce? The mosquito is among the tiniest and least regarded of creatures, so it serves as the extreme case: if a mosquito is fit for a parable, nothing is unfit. Grammatically, "parable" (mathal) is the direct object of the verb "strike" or "set forth," and the little word mā that follows it is read either as "whatever that parable may be" or as an extra word that stresses how slight the thing is. Baʿūḍah, the word for mosquito, is the singular of baʿūḍ, the small flying insects.

**"or what is even smaller"** renders the Arabic fa-mā fawqahā, literally "or what is above it." Most scholars understand "above" here as "bigger than it," so that the verse covers everything from the tiniest insect upward and the size of the creature makes no difference to the point being made. Others read it as "beyond it in smallness," which is the sense the translation adopts. Both readings lead to the same conclusion. A hadith uses the very same phrase: ʿĀʾishah reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that no Muslim is pricked by a thorn, fa-mā fawqahā, or anything beyond that, without a good deed being written for him and a sin erased. Muslim recorded it. Some scholars take "what is above it" to mean a fly or a spider in particular, since those are the creatures the Qur'an had already used and the objectors had mocked. A gnat, a fly, a spider or an ant: whatever the lowly creature, the lesson is the same.

**"As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord"** describes the first response to the parable. Mujāhid explained that the believers accept these comparisons whether they concern great matters or small, because they recognise them as the truth from their Lord, and by them Allah increases their guidance. The parable about a mosquito is as much a sign to them as a parable about the heavens.

**"And as for the disbelievers, they argue, 'What does Allah mean by such a parable?'"** is the second response. The question is not a request for information. Mā here is an interrogative of rejection, and "by such a parable" specifies what is being rejected. In plain terms they are saying, "What use is there in this?" They treat the smallness of the image as proof that the words cannot be from God.

**"Through this test, He leaves many to stray, and guides many"** is Allah's reply. One parable, two opposite results. As-Suddī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions that "many" whom He leaves astray means the hypocrites. Allah guides the believers through the parables, while the hypocrites, who know in their hearts that the parables are true, reject them anyway, and so their straying grows. That is how Allah leads them astray: by their own refusal. As for the believers, their guidance is increased by more guidance and their faith by more faith, because they hold firmly to what they know to be true. This is a gift He grants them.

**"And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious"** names the only people whom the parable harms. The word is al-fāsiqīn, those who have stepped outside obedience to Allah. When a date bursts out of its skin the Arabs say it has "fasaqat," and the mouse earned the name fuwaysiqah from its habit of leaving its hole to do damage. ʿĀʾishah reported in the two Ṣaḥīḥs that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that five animals are fawāsiq, to be killed whether one is in iḥrām or not: the crow, the kite, the scorpion, the mouse and the biting dog. In every case the idea is a creature that breaks out of its proper bounds. Fāsiq can describe both a disbeliever and a disobedient Muslim, but the disbeliever's fisq is the graver, and it is this kind that the verse means, because the next verse describes these people as breakers of Allah's covenant and spreaders of corruption.

### Why the verse came down

Several accounts explain the occasion, and they fit together. As-Suddī reported in his Tafsīr from Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and some of the Companions that when Allah set forth the two parables about the hypocrites, the man who kindled a fire and the people in the rainstorm, the hypocrites said that Allah is far too exalted to make examples like these, and Allah sent down these verses, from "Surely Allah does not shy away" to "the losers" at the end of verse 27. Saʿīd reported from Qatādah that Allah does not shrink from the truth when He uses something as a parable, whether it is great or small, and that when He mentioned the fly and the spider in His Book the people of misguidance asked why He would mention such things, so He revealed this verse. Another account attributes the objection to the Jews. All of these groups shared one assumption: that the Word of God would never employ contemptible creatures like a gnat or an ant, since such a thing would embarrass even a man with ordinary dignity.

The Qur'an's reply is that when a speaker wants to describe something detestable, whether a thing, a person or a situation, using a gnat or something smaller as the image breaks no rule of eloquence or logic and offends neither dignity nor modesty. A parable is judged by whether it fits, not by the size of what it mentions. The Qur'an adds that doubts of this kind arise only in minds that disbelief has drained of the power to see things in proportion, while believers are never troubled by them.

### What the mosquito teaches

Abū Jaʿfar ar-Rāzī reported from ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas that this verse is an example Allah gave of worldly life. A mosquito lives as long as it is hungry, but when it has fed and grown fat, it dies. So it is with the people Allah describes in the Qur'an: when they have gathered the pleasures of this life, Allah takes them away. He then recited, "When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair" (6:44). The same observation is made by others: the gnat is strong and flies while hungry, and once satiated it splits open and dies, just as "one exceeds all bounds once they think they are self-sufficient" (96:6-7).

Those who read "what is above it" as the fly see a lesson there too. The fly is insolent. However often you drive it away, it comes back. Had that quality been placed in the lion, no human being would be safe, but when Allah created strength in the lion He also created in it an aversion to people, and when He created boldness in the fly He paired it with weakness. In this pairing of qualities the perfection of His wisdom and the reach of His power are on display.

There is a further reflection on why nothing is too small for Allah to mention. Set beside the existence of the Real, the whole of creation is less than a speck of dust in the air, the vanishing of one limited thing in another. For that reason the Throne and the gnat stand equal before His power. Creating the Throne is not hard for Him and creating the gnat is not trivial for Him, for He is too holy to be touched by difficulty or ease. Since that is so, He is no more ashamed to draw a parable from a gnat than from the Throne and everything beneath it.

### Other parables in the Qur'an

The Qur'an uses small and ordinary things for its lessons again and again. It says, "O humanity! A lesson is set forth, so listen to it carefully: those idols you invoke besides Allah can never create so much as a fly, even if they all were to come together for that. And if a fly were to snatch anything away from them, they cannot even retrieve it from the fly" (22:73). It says, "The parable of those who take protectors other than Allah is that of a spider spinning a shelter. And the flimsiest of all shelters is certainly that of a spider, if only they knew" (29:41). It compares a good word to a good tree whose root is firm and whose branches reach the sky, always giving fruit by its Lord's permission, and an evil word to an evil tree torn out of the earth with nothing to hold it, and then explains that "this is how Allah sets forth parables for the people, so perhaps they will be mindful" (14:24-27). It sets forth the parable of a slave who owns nothing beside a free man who gives generously from a good provision (16:75), and of a dumb man who is a burden on his master beside a man who commands justice on a straight path (16:76). And it draws an example from the listeners' own lives, asking whether any of them would let their slaves become equal partners in their wealth (30:28). None of these images is grand. All of them are exact.

### A test that divides people

The verse itself gives the purpose of such parables: they are a trial. For people willing to think and understand, they become a means of guidance. For people who refuse to understand, out of indifference or out of stubborn hostility, they become a cause of deeper confusion. There is wisdom in the choice of each image, and the believer trusts that wisdom while the disbeliever makes fun of it and asks why God speaks of gnats, flies and spiders. Many are misled by these examples because they will not consider them, and many are guided because they learn from them. Allah misleads no one who does not deserve it, and the ones who deserve it are those who refuse to obey Him.

The root of the difference is willingness to learn. A person who does not want to learn never thinks seriously about an argument. He glances at it, looks for a flaw, and seizes on anything that can be made to look irrational or trivial. A person who wants to learn gives the argument real thought and soon realises that he is facing the truth. It makes no difference to such a person that the truth has been illustrated with a gnat or some other insignificant creature. The deniers' tactics, then, are the same in every age: belittling God's message, hunting for flaws in it, and portraying it as concerned with trivialities. The call of truth asks for no more than an honest look.

### Who counts as a fāsiq

Because the verse ends by naming al-fāsiqīn, a word that recurs throughout the Qur'an, it is worth fixing its meaning. The root fasaqa means to go out or to pass beyond a limit. In the language of the Sharīʿah, fisq means going outside the circle of obedience to Allah and transgressing His commands. Transgression does not always stop at disobedient actions. It can lead on to outright denial, so the Qur'an frequently applies the word to a kāfir. A Muslim who sins habitually is also called a fāsiq, and this is how the jurists ordinarily use the term, placing the fāsiq on one side and the kāfir on the other. In their usage, someone guilty of a major sin who has not repented, or who keeps up minor sins until they become a habit, is a fāsiq, while a man who commits such sins openly and without any shame is counted a kāfir. In this verse the fāsiq meant is the one whose rebellion has reached denial.

### Lessons drawn from the verse

One practical rule follows from verse 26. If a speaker or writer wants to explain something useful for the spiritual guidance of his audience, there is nothing sinful or improper in referring to a thing commonly thought contemptible or dirty, and it does not lower his dignity. The Qur'an itself, the hadith, and the writings of the Sufis and other great Muslim scholars all contain images of this kind, and their authors set aside the usual notions of delicacy for the sake of the real goal.

A second lesson concerns how Allah's guidance works. The verse does not say that the parable creates the straying. It says that through the parable Allah leaves astray those who are already rebellious. Guidance and misguidance both pass through the same words. What differs is the heart that receives them, and the verse that follows describes that heart.

### A reading for the inner life

Those whose inner eyes have been opened do not look at created things, or at traces and effects, for their own sake. If they look at all, it is to take heed, or to sharpen the faculty of insight. For such a person a gnat is a lesson and a fly is a lesson, and nothing in creation is beneath notice, because everything in it points beyond itself to the One who made it.

### In short

- The verse answers those who mocked the Qur'an for using images like a mosquito, a fly and a spider: Allah does not refrain from any parable, however small its subject, because a parable is judged by its fitness, not its size.
- Believers recognise such parables as the truth from their Lord, while disbelievers ask what God could mean by them.
- One parable produces two outcomes. It increases the guidance of those who think and the straying of those who refuse, and the only people it harms are the fāsiqīn, those who have broken out of obedience to Allah.
- The mosquito itself carries a lesson: it feeds, grows fat and dies, like people who gather this world's pleasures and are then taken away (6:44).

<<<VERSE 2:27>>>
Verse 26 ended by saying that Allah leads none astray through His parables except the rebellious. This verse tells us who they are. They are "those who violate Allah's covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land." Three marks are given: a broken covenant, severed relationships and corruption. Then the verdict: "It is they who are the true losers." The verse is both a description of the deniers and a mirror held up to anyone who wants to know what rebellion against Allah looks like in practice.

### Phrase by phrase

**"those who violate Allah's covenant after it has been affirmed"** opens the description. What covenant is meant? Scholars give several answers that complement each other. The broadest view is that it is Allah's covenant with all His creatures: to obey Him and to avoid what He has forbidden. This covenant has been repeated in His Books and on the tongues of His Messengers, so ignoring it is breaking it. Many connect it with the pledge taken from all humanity before anyone came to the earth, when Allah brought forth the descendants of Ādam (Adam) and asked, "Am I not your Lord?" and they replied, "Yes, You are! We testify" (7:172). Having affirmed Allah as their only Lord, people are bound never to disobey Him, and His Books and prophets come to remind them of this pledge, renew it and teach them how to live by it. Those who break it can hardly be expected to learn from those very prophets and Books.

A second view is that the verse speaks of the disbelievers and hypocrites among the People of the Book. Their covenant was the pledge recorded in the Tawrāh that they would follow Muḥammad ﷺ once he was sent, believing in him and in what he brought. They broke it when they rejected him after they knew the truth about him and concealed that truth from people, even though they had sworn to Allah to do otherwise. Allah tells elsewhere how they tossed that covenant behind their backs and sold it cheaply. On this reading "after it has been affirmed" means after it was confirmed and bound upon them in their own scriptures.

A third view, reported from Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān and also held by az-Zamakhsharī, is that the verse covers every disbeliever, idol worshipper and hypocrite. Allah took from them all a pledge to believe in His Oneness by showing them the signs that testify to His Lordship, and He bound them to obey His commands and avoid His prohibitions, knowing that His Messengers would bring proofs and miracles beyond the power of any creature. They broke the covenant when they denied what had been proven to them and rejected His Prophets and Books while knowing they were true. This explanation has been called very good, and it includes the other two.

**"break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained"** is the second mark. Qatādah said it refers to the bonds of kinship, and this is like Allah's words, "Now if you hypocrites turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ties of kinship" (47:22). Ibn Jarīr aṭ-Ṭabarī preferred this explanation. Others take the phrase more widely: everything Allah has commanded people to keep joined and nurtured, which they then cut, falls under it. Some include faith in the Prophet ﷺ along with kinship and other matters, and some mention the oaths and family ties that bound the objectors to Muḥammad ﷺ himself. On the grammar, "that it be joined" stands in for the pronoun in "what Allah has commanded," so the sense is "what Allah has commanded to be joined."

Taking the wide reading, the ties Allah has ordered to be maintained include every kind of relationship: the servant's bond with Allah, a person's bonds with parents and relatives, with neighbours and friends, the bond of one Muslim with another, and the bond of one human being with another. Islam itself can be described as the fulfilment of one's obligations in all these relationships, and that is also what following the Sharīʿah means. Religion is the divinely ordained law that binds us to discharge the rights of Allah (ḥuqūq Allāh) and the rights of His servants (ḥuqūq al-ʿibād). Neglect of these obligations breeds every sort of disorder among people, so that transgressors end up destroying others and themselves.

**"and spread corruption in the land"** is the third mark. Corruption here is first of all the damage done by transgression and by obstructing faith: keeping people away from the religion of Muḥammad ﷺ and from the Qur'an. The message of truth seeks to bring people into harmony with their own nature, so whoever blocks others from receiving it, by inventing groundless objections against the call raised by one of God's servants, is guilty of spreading corruption on the earth. The verse thus traces disorder in human society to its root: the sundering of the relationships Allah commanded people to keep.

**"It is they who are the true losers"** is the sentence passed. Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said the loss is in the Hereafter, as in the similar verse, "it is they who will be condemned and will have the worst abode" (13:25). Ibn Jarīr explained the word khāsirūn, losers, as the plural of khāsir, one who has reduced his own share of Allah's mercy by disobeying Him, just as a merchant loses in trade by eating into his capital. The hypocrite and the disbeliever forfeit their share of the mercy Allah has stored up for His servants on the Day of Resurrection, and that is the moment when they will need it most desperately. Others say the loss covers both this world and the next, and that these are the duped, who end in a Fire made everlasting for them. The mention of loss carries a hint that real loss is the loss of the next world, since whatever is lost in this one is too small to deserve serious attention. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk related a rule from Ibn ʿAbbās: whenever Allah applies a description such as "losers" to people outside Islam, it refers to disbelief, but when such terms are applied to Muslims, they refer to sin.

### The mirror image in Sūrat ar-Raʿd

The qualities listed here are exactly the opposite of the qualities of the believers, and the Qur'an sets the two portraits side by side in Sūrat ar-Raʿd. There it asks whether the one who knows that the revelation is the truth can be like one who is blind, and then describes the people of reason as "those who honour Allah's covenant, never breaking the pledge, and those who maintain whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment" (13:19-21). A few verses later it describes their opposites in almost the same words as our verse: "those who violate Allah's covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land" (13:25), and gives them the curse and the worst abode.

### Three duties

Read together, the three marks of the rebellious point to three duties. A human being's first duty toward God is faithfulness to the covenant struck between Creator and creatures at the very beginning, namely that he remain God's servant. The second concerns his conduct toward his fellow human beings: it should be such that all the ties God has commanded him to keep are strengthened, not cut. The third is that he must not turn people away from the divine call by manufacturing baseless arguments against it. The rebellious fail in all three.

### Lessons drawn from the verse

- The mention of violating a covenant with Allah signals that breaking a contract or agreement with one's fellow human beings is also a grave sin, one that can rob a person of the ability to do good deeds.
- It is obligatory to maintain the relationships the Sharīʿah commands us to keep intact, and forbidden to break them. According to this verse the basic cause of disorder in human society is the cutting of these bonds.
- Real loss is measured in the Hereafter. Those who act against divine commands are the true losers, however prosperous they appear.

### A reading for the inner life

On the level of allusion, the verse describes someone who once travelled the way of spiritual desire and then went back to the life of ordinary people. He claimed he was ready to abandon his lower self, but when things became serious he proved insincere, trading the allusions of reality for the concessions of the law. The return of one who travels the path with his outward self (nafs) is blameworthy so long as a single dirham remains in his bag, and in the same way the return of one who seeks with his heart (qalb) is displeasing so long as a single breath (nafas) remains in his spirit (rūḥ). Whoever has died to the religion of whims finds in the decree of death a sweet place to drink.

"Cut what Allah has commanded to be joined" is read in the same spirit. The cords that bind a person to the Real are tied by cutting the cords that bind him to creation. Joining what belongs to Him is never complete without cutting what belongs to you, and when the order is reversed, so is the result. Among the things a servant is commanded to join is the sacred bond between the people of this path. One works sincerely for that bond rather than merely handing out favours. The outward efforts of such servants are devoted to connecting the cords of the path and setting its conditions in order, while their hearts wait on the guardianship of Allah Most High over His people. Corruption of the path "in the land" happens when the elders of the path stop attending to the small details of the affairs of those behind them, too busy to guide an aspirant with their words or to sharpen the aim of a seeker with their care. This neglect displeases the Real.

Finally, the verse is turned inward on the seeker himself. Part of breaking the covenant is any moment in which your innermost self turns away from witnessing Him. Part of cutting what you were commanded to join is any breath in which a worldly concern appears and His right goes unmet. And every hour that passes without your seeing Him is your own share of corruption in the land. That is loss made plain, a grave trial and a vast calamity.

### In short

- The rebellious of verse 26 are identified by three marks: they break Allah's covenant after it is confirmed, they cut the ties He commanded to be kept, and they spread corruption.
- The covenant is understood as the pledge of obedience taken from all creation and affirmed at "Am I not your Lord?" (7:172), as the People of the Book's pledge to follow the Prophet ﷺ, and as the pledge of Oneness that Allah's signs impose on everyone.
- The ties include kinship above all, and more broadly every relationship Allah ordered kept, with Him and with people.
- They are the losers because they have thrown away their share of Allah's mercy, like a merchant who has lost his capital, and the real loss is in the Hereafter.

<<<VERSE 2:28>>>
The verses before this one established that the Qur'an is beyond doubt, answered the deniers' objections and set out the fate of those who break Allah's covenant. Now the tone changes from argument to appeal. The Qur'an points to the blessings Allah has poured on human beings and asks how anyone could repay them with denial. "How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will all be returned." In a single sentence the verse sweeps through the whole of a human life: nothing, then life, then death, then life again, then the return to Allah. Each stage is a gift, and together they are an argument that needs no learning to grasp.

### Phrase by phrase

**"How can you deny Allah?"** is a question of astonishment. The Arabic kayfa, "how," can express amazement at people who persist in denial despite clear evidence, or it can be a rebuke, and here it is both. The immediate address is to the people of Makkah. On the face of it, they had never denied Allah's existence. What they denied was the Prophet ﷺ. Yet the Qur'an treats their rejection of the Messenger as a denial of Allah Himself, because to deny the one He sent is to deny Him. Behind the question stands Allah's own testimony to His existence: He is the Creator and the Sustainer, with full authority over His servants. How can anyone deny His existence or worship others alongside Him when the evidence lies in his own being?

**"You were lifeless and He gave you life"** states the first blessing. Ibn Jarīr reported from ʿAṭāʾ that Ibn ʿAbbās explained it this way: you did not exist before, you were nothing until Allah created you, and after He has made you die He will restore you to life at the Resurrection. Ibn ʿAbbās added that this is like Allah's words, "Our Lord! You made us lifeless twice, and gave us life twice" (40:11). "Lifeless" (amwāt) is taken by some in a more concrete sense: you were sperm drops in the loins of your fathers, and He gave you life in the wombs of your mothers and in this world by breathing the spirit into you. Another way to picture it is that before a person existed he was, at most, countless lifeless particles scattered without purpose, and Allah gathered them, formed a human being from them and gave it life. The Qur'an presses the same point elsewhere: "Or were they created by nothing, or are they their own creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? In fact, they have no firm belief" (52:35-36), and, "Is there not a period of time when each human is nothing yet worth mentioning" (76:1). There are many other verses on this theme.

**"then He will cause you to die"** is the second stage, when the lifespan Allah allotted comes to its end. The verse counts death among the blessings, and this deserves a pause. Life is the chief blessing, since without it no other blessing can be enjoyed. But physical death is also a blessing, because it is the door to the everlasting life of the next world, after which there is no death at all.

**"and again bring you to life"** is the Resurrection, when Allah will gather the scattered particles of every human being and give them a new life. So the sequence is this: the first "death" was the state of lifelessness before a person received life from Allah, the second death comes when his term is complete, and the second life is given on the Day of Judgment. This matches the two deaths and two lives confessed in 40:11.

**"and then to Him you will all be returned"** closes the circle. After the Resurrection people will rise from their graves, be assembled to give account of their deeds, and be rewarded or punished according to what they did in the world. Allah will requite each person for his works.

### Why begin with life

The earlier verses had affirmed the existence and Oneness of Allah and the truth of prophethood with self-evident proofs, and had refuted the false notions of the doubters. This verse and the next speak instead of Allah's blessings, and the point is this: if people will not take the trouble to weigh the arguments already given, they should at least do what any person of sound nature does and be grateful to their benefactor, for gratitude alone would show them why they ought to obey Him. Verse 28 deals with the blessings that belong to a person's very being, namely that he had no life until Allah gave him existence. Verse 29 goes on to the general blessings that human beings share with other creatures: the earth and all it contains, on which life immediately depends, and the skies, with which life on earth is bound up.

The same appeal can be put as a chain of favours. God created human beings from nothing, and that favour alone is great enough to demand complete surrender to Him. But He did not create them and leave it at that: He gave them an earth made to suit their needs perfectly. And there is more, since immediately after death a person is brought before the Lord of the universe to be judged. A situation like this demands that a person devote himself entirely to God, remembering and obeying Him throughout life and remaining His humble servant to the end.

### A proof of the Resurrection

The verse also serves as a proof against those who denied that the dead would rise. The disbelievers' position is strange: how can they deny the Oneness of Allah when the signs of His power are visible in their own existence? They were nothing and He brought them to life. The One who gave life the first time can plainly give it a second time, and the One who will give it a second time will hold people to account. The verse states the Resurrection and, in the same breath, supplies its evidence from the listener's own body and history.

### A call to wake up

Read as an allusion, the verse is a summons shouted to someone who has lost the thread of his own life. You who have let the end of your own thread slip from your hand! You who have tumbled into the well of your own mortal nature! Is there a clearer road than this that you are waiting for? Then why not walk it? Is there a wider field than this? Then why not ride out? Is there a brighter candle than this? Then why are you falling off the path? So many years have passed over you and you have still caught no scent. You have sat at so many tables and are still hungry. You have worn a thousand garments and are still naked. O Muslim, the playing field is open, so where are the riders? The court is in session, so where are the plaintiffs? The physician is present, so where are the sick? The beauty is unveiled, so where are the lovers?

"You were lifeless and He gave you life" is then heard as Allah's own address: You were dead and I brought you to life, so why do you not look? You were ignorant and I made you knowers, so why do you not perceive? I showed you the road, so why do you not walk? What is needed is a person able to catch the scent, for otherwise the world is already full of the east wind's fragrance.

The Pīr of the Ṭarīqah turned the verse into a prayer: "O God, what can a servant do against the beginningless decree? When he owns nothing, what is left to him? What is his effort worth? Whatever You want is what gets done. How could a servant rescue himself by striving?" The movement from death to life, in other words, is Allah's doing from first to last.

It is also said that death is of three kinds and life of three kinds. One death comes by the curse, one by regret and one by generous gift: the first belongs to the disbelievers, the second to the disobedient and the third to the God-fearing. Life likewise begins as a life of fear, grows into a life of hope and ripens into a life of love. Fear shows itself in kindness, hope in service, and love in remembrance.

### In short

- The verse expresses amazement that anyone could deny Allah when his own existence proves the Creator: he was nothing, Allah gave him life, He will take it, give it again and bring him back for judgment.
- Ibn ʿAbbās explained the two deaths and two lives, and linked the verse to the confession of the people of Hell in 40:11.
- Life is the first and greatest blessing, and even death is a blessing, because it opens onto a life that never ends.
