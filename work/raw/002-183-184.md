<<<VERSE 2:183>>>
From the will the passage moves to the fast. "O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you—as it was for those before you—so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺". Addressing the believers of this Ummah, Allah orders them to fast, that is, to abstain from food, drink and sexual relations with the sincere intention of doing so for Him alone, because fasting purifies the soul and cleanses it of the evil and bad behaviour that may mix with it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you"**. Literally ṣawm means to abstain. In the terminology of Islamic law it is abstention from eating, drinking and sexual intercourse, continuously from dawn to sunset, with the intention of fasting. If someone eats or drinks even a minute before sunset the fast is invalid, and if someone abstains all day without the intention there is no fast either. Ṣawm is an act of worship (ʿibādah) regarded as a pillar and sign of Islam, and its merits are too many to list here. One early reading recalls that at first the abstention began from the time one slept before dawn, or from the dawn prayer, and that intimacy and food were forbidden after the night prayer or after sleeping, a rule later abrogated by the verses permitting relations on the nights of the fast and eating and drinking until the white thread is distinct from the black.

**"as it was for those before you"**. The command is accompanied by the statement that the obligation is not peculiar to the Muslims, for fasting was enjoined on the earlier communities too, who are an example in this, so the Muslims should perform the duty more vigorously and obediently than the nations before them. The reference shows the importance of fasting and encourages the Muslims: whatever inconvenience fasting involves was faced by earlier communities as well, and a hardship shared by many is easier to bear. "Those before you" is general, covering every religious community from Ādam to the last of the Prophets ﷺ, so that fasting, like ṣalāh, was prescribed to the Ummah of every prophet without exception. Those who take it to mean the Christians or the People of the Book give an example rather than excluding others. Nor does the verse imply that the earlier fasts were identical in every respect, for there were differences in number and timing. The thought resembles "To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life" (5:48).

**"so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺"**. Fasting cleanses the body and narrows the paths of Shayṭān. In the Ṣaḥīḥayn the Prophet ﷺ tells young men who can afford marriage to marry, and those who cannot to fast, for it will be a shield for them. Fasting curbs the desires that prompt disobedience and grows in a person the power to control his desires, which is the foundation of taqwā, the special Qur'anic term tentatively translated as fear of Allah, abstinence and warding off evil. So one guards oneself against sin and against His punishment by good deeds, of which fasting is among the best.

### The spirit of the fast

The main aim of fasting is to reduce dependence on material things and strengthen spiritual resolve so that a person may enter the higher realms of faith and piety. Ramaḍān is thus a unique opportunity to concentrate on doing good and abstaining from evil, gladdening the believer's heart and setting his mind at rest. Though the fast is prescribed for one month a year, its spirit endures, teaching a person to withdraw from the world while remaining part of it and to fulfil his spiritual obligations amid the turmoil of a materialistic world. Ramaḍān is the month of heightened God-consciousness. Instead of gossip, lying, slander and other marks of bad character, the believer concentrates on cultivating good habits, and his inner self moves day by day towards righteousness, devotion and piety. This is the path the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ himself laid down, spending Ramaḍān in prayer and praise, especially at sunset when breaking the fast, the moment when the believer feels closest to his Creator. Perhaps for this reason he counted the fasting person at the time of breaking the fast among the five whose supplications are never turned down, with the just ruler, the oppressed, the parent praying for his children and the traveller in Allah's cause.

### The guests of the Real

In the tongue of allusion, fasting has been written for the faithful because they will be the guests of the Real, and tomorrow in Paradise He wishes to bring hungry guests to a banquet. The generous like their guests to arrive hungry so that the feast is sweeter to them, and the Lord of the Worlds created Paradise and all within it for the faithful, needing none of it Himself. A Sufi master once sent out an invitation and nobody came. He raised his hands and said that if the Lord sent His servants to the Fire tomorrow, Paradise and its bliss would be like his own empty table, for a table's value lies in people sitting and eating at it. Allah created all the treasuries of blessing for the faithful who eat, while He Himself does not eat. That is why He says that fasting belongs to Him and He Himself rewards it. One of the masters explained: self-sufficiency belongs to Me, I neither eat nor drink, and I reward those who fast without reckoning, for by not eating they sought conformity with Me, and the first station of friendship is conformity. If conformity with the angels in saying "Āmīn" at the end of the Fātiḥah earns forgiveness of past and future sins, as the report says, consider what eminence accrues to heart and religion through conformity with Allah in not eating, even though the servant's abstention is temporary and effortful while Allah's is a beginningless attribute. It is also said that He ascribed fasting to Himself so that the hands of claimants would not reach it: at the Resurrection, when those you wronged gather round and carry off your acts of worship in settlement, the Lord will keep your fasting in the treasury of His bounty and tell them that it is His and they have no share in it.

### In short

- Ṣawm is abstention from food, drink and intimacy from dawn to sunset with intention, a pillar of Islam.
- It was prescribed for every earlier community (cf. 5:48), which both dignifies it and makes its hardship easier to bear, though details differed.
- Its purpose is taqwā: it shields against desire (the hadith of the young men) and builds self-control.
- Ramaḍān trains character and brings closeness to Allah, and the faster's prayer at ifṭār is answered.
- Inwardly, the faithful fast as hungry guests invited to the banquet of Paradise, and fasting belongs to Allah alone.

<<<VERSE 2:184>>>
Verse 184 sets the measure of the fast and its concessions. "˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺." It continues: "For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew". The fast falls on a fixed number of days so that it does not weigh on hearts and weaken their resolve and endurance.

### Phrase by phrase

**"˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days."**. The days are few and specified, those of Ramaḍān, thirty days, and Allah fixed a small number to make things easy for those under the obligation.

**"But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺."**. The sick person meant is one who cannot fast without unbearable hardship or strongly fears that his illness will worsen, as the words "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship" in verse 185 indicate, and the jurists agree on this. The text says "on a journey" rather than "a traveller" to show that merely leaving home is not enough: one must have embarked on a journey, not gone five or ten miles. The Qur'an does not fix the distance, but guided by the Prophet's ﷺ words and the Companions' practice, Abū Ḥanīfah and many jurists set it at three days' walking in three daily stages, which later jurists put at forty-eight miles. One is exempt only while the journey continues. A short halt does not end it, but an intended stay of fifteen days at one place, a period fixed by a statement of the Prophet ﷺ, removes one from the status of being on a journey, while someone who intends to spend fifteen days moving between different places remains a traveller and keeps the concession. "An equal number of days" means that the sick person and the traveller must fast other days to match those missed, so that fasts abandoned under compulsion are made up by qaḍāʾ. The phrasing suggests that the obligation arises only once the sick person recovers and the traveller returns home and lives long enough to make them up, so one who dies before that owes no qaḍāʾ and need not leave a will for a fidyah. The make-up fasts may be consecutive or spread out, in any order, since the wording leaves the choice open.

**"For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺."**. In the early days of Islam those who had the strength to fast but did not wish to could pay a ransom in charity instead, with the reminder that fasting was better. The aim was to accustom people to fasting. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from ʿĀʾishah that ʿĀshūrāʾ had been a day of fasting, and that when Ramaḍān was made obligatory whoever wished fasted and whoever wished did not, and al-Bukhārī records the same from Ibn ʿUmar and Ibn Masʿūd. Muʿādh said that at first whoever wished fasted and whoever wished fed a poor person for each day. Al-Bukhārī records from Salamah ibn al-Akwaʿ that when this verse came down those who did not wish to fast paid the fidyah until verse 185 abrogated it, and Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, al-Tirmidhī, al-Ṭabarānī and others report it from him as well. Ibn ʿUmar said it was abrogated, and ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, as al-Suddī reports from Murrah, explained the phrase as those who find fasting difficult. The abrogation by "So whoever is present this month, let them fast" (2:185) applies to the healthy resident who must fast. According to the consensus of the Ummah the ransom remains for the very old and for those with a permanent illness and no hope of recovery, as al-Jaṣṣāṣ and al-Maẓharī state. Al-Bukhārī reports that ʿAṭāʾ heard Ibn ʿAbbās recite the verse and say that it was not abrogated but applies to the old man and woman who can fast only with difficulty and choose to feed a poor person for each day. Such a person does not have to fast other days, since he is unlikely to improve, and pays a fidyah per day. Al-Bukhārī held the same, citing Anas, who for a year or two after growing old fed bread and meat to a poor person for each day, and Abū Yaʿlā al-Mawṣilī gives it with a connected chain from Ayyūb ibn Abī Tamīmah: Anas could no longer fast, so he prepared a dish of tharīd and invited thirty poor people and fed them. The same applies to pregnant and nursing women who fear for themselves or their children. Ibn ʿAbbās said the verse remains valid for these two and is not abrogated, and they pay the fidyah without making up the days.

**"But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them."**. Whoever feeds an extra poor person, or gives more than the minimum ransom, does good that is good for him in reward.

**"And to fast is better for you, if only you knew"**. Fasting is better than breaking the fast and paying the ransom, if you knew the goodness in it, so do it. This was the first of Allah's rulings on fasting, and afterwards He made the fast a duty on every able person who has reached puberty.

### The stages of the fast

A long hadith from Muʿādh ibn Jabal in the Musnad of Aḥmad describes three changes in ṣalāh and three in ṣawm in the early period. When the Prophet ﷺ came to Madīnah he fasted three days each month and the tenth of Muḥarram. Then Ramaḍān was ordained with the option of fasting or paying ransom, fasting being preferred. Then the verse of witnessing the month removed the option for those with strength, the ransom remaining for the very old. The third change concerned the nights: at first eating, drinking and intimacy after ifṭār were allowed only until one slept, sleep marking the start of the next fast, until the verse making relations lawful on the nights of the fast permitted all three until dawn, and suḥūr soon after rising became the sunnah, as hadiths in al-Bukhārī, Muslim and Abū Dāwūd confirm.

### Rulings on the ransom

The ransom for one missed fast is half a ṣāʿ of wheat, about 1.632 kilograms, or its market value, given to a poor person, roughly the amount a person eats in a day, or one mudd of the staple food of the town. It should not be given as part of the wages of those who serve a mosque or madrasah. The ransom for one fast should not be split between two recipients, and as a precaution the ransom for several fasts should not be given to one person on a single day, though some scholars permit it and the payment counts if the precaution is not observed. Whoever cannot even pay the ransom should seek forgiveness through istighfār and intend in his heart to pay when he can.

### Fasting for Allah and through Allah

In the devotional reading, whoever is present in the month fasts for Allah, and whoever is present with the Creator of the month fasts through Allah. The fast for Him leads to recompense and the fast through Him to nearness. The first is the fulfilment of worship and belongs to every worshipper (ʿābid), the second is the healing of spiritual desire (irādah) and belongs to every seeker. The first is performed by the outward self and the second by the innermost selves. The first abstains according to the plain expressions of the Sharīʿah, the second according to the allusions of reality (al-ḥaqīqah). Whoever is present in Ramaḍān abstains from what breaks the fast, while whoever is present with the Real abstains at all times from witnessing created things. Whoever fasts with his lower self drinks of Salsabīl and Zanjabīl, whoever fasts in his heart drinks of what brings love through the blessing of Allah's approval, and those who fast with their innermost selves are those of whom He says "their Lord will give them a purifying drink" (76:21), a drink not turned about in the hand but appearing from His kindness, a drink of intimacy and not from a cup. The sick person and the traveller allude to the one whose spiritual desire loses its vigour so that he turns back to other than Him, whether through a permissive interpretation, a lack of strength and endurance or weakness in bearing the decrees of reality.

### In short

- The fast is a small, fixed number of days, the thirty of Ramaḍān.
- The genuinely sick and those on a real journey (three stages, later reckoned as forty-eight miles, not a stay of fifteen days) may break the fast and make up the days later in any order.
- The option of ransom for the able was the first stage, abrogated by 2:185 (Salamah ibn al-Akwaʿ, ʿĀʾishah, Ibn ʿUmar), but remains for the permanently unable, the aged (Ibn ʿAbbās, Anas) and nursing or pregnant women who fear harm.
- The ransom is half a ṣāʿ of wheat or its value per day, given to the poor, and giving more is better, while fasting is better still.
- Inwardly, the worshipper fasts for Allah and the seeker fasts through Him, and the fast of the innermost self is rewarded with the pure drink of 76:21.
