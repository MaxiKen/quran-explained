<<<VERSE 2:102>>>
Verse 102 explains what the Jews turned to once they had thrown the Book of Allah behind their backs. "They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, “We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.” Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew". It is one of the longest verses in the surah, and it raises three questions at once: where the sorcery of the Jews came from, who Hārūt and Mārūt were, and what Islam says about magic.

### Phrase by phrase

**"They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon"**. The verb "followed" continues the previous verse: having cast the Scripture away, they took up what the devils used to recite in the days of Sulaymān's (Solomon's) kingdom, and acted on what the devils had written against his kingdom. As-Suddī told the background. Before Sulaymān's time the devils used to climb up towards heaven and eavesdrop on the angels discussing what was about to happen on earth, deaths, events and other matters of the unseen. They passed this on to the soothsayers, the soothsayers passed it on to the people, and the people found it came true. Once the soothsayers trusted them, the devils began to lie, mixing falsehood into the true news until there were seventy lies for every truth. People wrote these sayings down in books, and soon the Children of Israel were saying that the jinn knew the unseen. When Sulaymān was sent as a prophet he gathered up these books, locked them in a chest and buried it beneath his throne, and any devil who came near the chest was burned. He declared that he would cut off the head of anyone he heard claiming that the devils knew the unseen. After Sulaymān died, and the scholars who knew the truth about him had passed away, a new generation arose. A devil appeared to some of the Israelites in human form and offered to show them a treasure they could never exhaust. He led them to the throne and told them to dig beneath it, but refused to come closer himself, saying they could kill him if they found nothing. They dug up the buried books, and Satan told them that this was the magic by which Sulaymān had ruled men, devils and birds. The rumour that Sulaymān had been a sorcerer spread, and the Israelites adopted the books. When Muḥammad ﷺ came, these were the books they used to argue against him.

ʿImrān ibn al-Ḥārith heard Ibn ʿAbbās tell the same story more briefly: a devil would bring down one true word from heaven and, once trusted for it, add seventy lies that won people's hearts. Sulaymān seized the material and buried it under his throne. After his death a devil stood in the street and offered to guide people to a guarded treasure of Sulaymān's that had no equal. They dug it up and said, "This is magic", and nation after nation copied it. Al-Kalbī gave a different version. The devils wrote out magic and talismans and attributed them to Āṣaf ibn Barakhiyā, heading the text "This is what Āṣaf ibn Barakhiyā taught King Sulaymān", and buried it under his place of worship without his knowledge, at the time when he was stripped of his kingdom. After his death they unearthed it and told people that Sulaymān had left it for them to learn. The scholars of the Children of Israel said, "Allah forbid that this should be Sulaymān's knowledge", but the common people said it was, sought it out, and abandoned the scriptures of their prophets. Sulaymān was blamed for this until Allah sent Muḥammad ﷺ and cleared him in His own words. One of the readings in this verse's wider commentary says the books were buried when his kingdom was taken from him, and that those who found them said, "Your kingdom was only thanks to what is in here", then took to studying them and rejected the Scriptures of their prophets. Khuṣayf added a tale that Sulaymān used to ask every newly sprouted tree what disease it cured, until the carob tree answered that its purpose was the destruction of his sanctuary, and confirmed that it would indeed destroy it.

One early gloss says the devils had written of the collapse of Sulaymān's kingdom and of forty days of sorcery and white magic. A modern reading places the story in the decline of the Jewish people. When superstition and apathy set in, certain individuals exploited the mood for commercial gain by supplying success-seekers with magical prescriptions. To make their trade flourish they attributed their art to Sulaymān, claiming that the extraordinary powers he exercised over the spirits and the winds really rested on his knowledge of magic, and that the spirits had handed this knowledge on to them. This claim to authority did much to spread sorcery, which became a highly popular art among the Jews.

**"Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved"**. The Jews were impertinent and senseless enough to attribute magic and sorcery to a prophet. They said: "Look at this Muḥammad, he counts Sulaymān among the prophets when he was nothing but a sorcerer." So while condemning them for learning the black arts of the devils, the Qur'an inserts a parenthesis to deny this vile allegation. Sulaymān never practised magic out of disbelief, and never wrote this sorcery and white magic. It was the devils who disbelieved, teaching people sorcery, and it is said that the Jews in turn taught it to others. The devils lied about Sulaymān's kingdom, claiming it was established through black magic. Allah declared his innocence.

**"They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon"**. Babylon (Bābil) is a town in lower Iraq. The names Hārūt and Mārūt stand in place of "the two angels", or explain who they were. Here scholars differ sharply. Al-Qurṭubī related the view that the verse denies anything was sent down to the two angels, the negation covering both halves: Sulaymān did not disbelieve, and nothing was sent down to the two angels. On this reading the Jews had claimed that Jibrīl (Gabriel) and Mīkāʾīl (Michael) brought magic down to the two angels, and Allah refuted the claim. Ibn Jarīr reported through al-ʿAwfī that Ibn ʿAbbās said of this clause, "Allah did not send magic down", and ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas said the same. Ibn Jarīr judged this the correct explanation, taking the whole verse to mean that what the devils recited in Sulaymān's reign was magic, that Sulaymān did not disbelieve, and that Allah did not send magic with the two angels, Jibrīl and Mīkāʾīl, as the Jewish sorcerers alleged when they said that Allah had sent magic to Sulaymān son of Dāwūd by their words. The devils disbelieved and taught magic to the people of Babylon through the agency of two men, Hārūt and Mārūt, who on this view were ordinary human beings. These were the words of aṭ-Ṭabarī, but other scholars found this explanation implausible. Ibn ʿAbbās is also quoted as saying that the two were sorcerers who taught people magic, and a variant reading has al-malikayn, "the two kings", instead of "the two angels".

Many among the early generations held that Hārūt and Mārūt were angels who came down from heaven to earth and did what the verse describes. To reconcile this with the angels' immunity from error, scholars answer that Allah knew from eternity what these two would do, just as He knew what Iblīs would do while still counting him among the angels when He said "Prostrate before Adam" and all did so but Iblīs (20:116). What Hārūt and Mārūt did was in any case less evil than what Iblīs did. Al-Qurṭubī reported the angel view from ʿAlī, Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn ʿUmar, Kaʿb al-Aḥbār, as-Suddī and al-Kalbī. Another gloss has it that the angels themselves were not taught sorcery, and offers the alternative that the devils taught people what the angels had been inspired with. One modern reading draws a comparison: when angels came to the people of Lūṭ (Lot), who practised homosexuality, they came as handsome boys in order to test them, and in the same way angels were sent to the Jews in Babylon in the dress of holy men to teach them magic, while making it perfectly clear that this was only a test and a knowledge not to be practised. The Jews ignored the warning, practised magic with fervour and even turned it to illicit ends.

**"The two angels never taught anyone without saying, “We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.”"**. Ibn ʿAbbās, through Qays ibn ʿAbbād and ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, described how it worked. When someone came to the angels to learn magic they would discourage him, saying, "We are only a test, so do not fall into disbelief." They knew good from evil and belief from disbelief, and they knew that magic was a form of disbelief. If the man insisted, they would direct him to a certain place where Satan would meet him and teach him. Once he had learned it, the light of faith would leave him and he would see it shining as it flew away into the sky, and he would cry out, "O my sorrow! Woe to me! What shall I do?" Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī said the angels were sent with magic so that those whom Allah willed to test would be tested, and Allah made them promise to teach no one before announcing, "We are a test for you, do not fall into disbelief." Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded this, and Qatādah said the same. As-Suddī's account is that when a man ignored their warning they would tell him to go to a certain heap of ashes and urinate on it. When he did, a light, the light of faith, would leave him and shine until it entered heaven, and then something black like smoke would descend and enter his ears and the rest of his body, and this was Allah's anger. When he reported what had happened, they would teach him. One gloss has the angels say "we have been tried by calling people to this in order to lighten the torment inflicted on ourselves", and explains "do not disbelieve" as "do not learn it or act on it".

Ibn Jurayj commented that no one dares practise magic except a disbeliever, and that the "test" (fitnah) involves trial and freedom of choice. Whoever learns it is a disbeliever, and whoever renounces it is a believer. Scholars who hold that learning magic is disbelief rely on this verse, and also on the ḥadīth which Abū Bakr al-Bazzār recorded from ʿAbdullāh: whoever goes to a soothsayer or a sorcerer and believes what he says has disbelieved in what was revealed to Muḥammad ﷺ. This ḥadīth has an authentic chain and other reports support it.

**"Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife"**. From the two angels, though not taught by them directly in one reading, people picked up the means of separating spouses, people who are closer and more intimate than any others. This is the devil's work. Muslim recorded from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that Satan sets his throne on the water and sends out his emissaries among mankind, and the nearest to him in rank is the one who causes the greatest discord. One comes back and reports that he kept at a man until he said such and such, and Iblīs replies, "By Allah, you have done nothing." Another reports that he kept at a man until he had separated him from his wife, and Satan draws him close, embraces him and says, "Yes, you have done well." The separation happens because each spouse begins to see the other as ugly or ill-mannered, so that the husband finds a pretext to leave his wife, or hatred is sown between them.

**"although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will"**. Sufyān ath-Thawrī glossed this, "except by Allah's appointed term". Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī said that Allah lets magicians affect whomever He wills and saves whomever He wills from them, and sorcerers never harm anyone except by His leave. The doctrinal point is important: causes in themselves cannot produce the effects usually associated with them, for it is Allah who creates the effects just as He creates the causes.

**"They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them"**. It harms their religion, and harms them in the Hereafter, and brings no benefit worth the harm in this world or the next. One gloss notes that the devils, the Jews and the sorcerers all learn it from one another.

**"although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter"**. The Jews who preferred magic to following the Messenger of Allah ﷺ knew that those who chose it would have no khalāq in the Hereafter. Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and as-Suddī explained khalāq as "share", a portion in Paradise. Some take "they knew" to refer to the angels, some to the Jews who knew it from their own Book, and some to the devils. The Jews knew very well that dabbling in black magic was not only a sin but involved unbelief, and they could see that it would do them no good even in this world, since their sorcery could harm no one unless Allah willed it. Their practice contradicted their knowledge, and they made no use of their understanding to notice the contradiction.

**"Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew"**. Evil was what they chose in place of faith and following the Messenger, if only they would take the advice. They exchanged the revelation and the sacred law of Allah for black magic. The verse first says that they "knew" and then takes the knowledge away again with "if only they knew". Knowledge that is neither acted upon nor properly thought through is no better than ignorance. One gloss reads the ending as "but they do not know", another as "and they did know it from their own Book". Had they believed in Allah and His Messenger ﷺ and kept away from what is forbidden, Allah's reward would have been far better for them than what they chose, as the next verse goes on to say, and as those with knowledge said in another passage: Allah's reward is far better for those who believe and do good (28:80).

### Why angels, and why not prophets?

Scholars have related in connection with this verse certain Jewish traditions that have raised doubts, particularly among Muslims with a Western outlook. Among them is a long and well-known story about a woman named Zuhrah, which rests on no sound Islamic transmission. Scholars who find that it breaches the rules of the Sharīʿah dismiss it as fiction, while those who think it can be interpreted in line with the Sharīʿah have not thrown it out altogether. Whether it is true or false, a proper understanding of the verse does not depend on it in any way. The devotional literature knows the same story, saying that Hārūt and Mārūt, deceived by the disobedience they had grown used to, blamed their fall on the disobedience of the children of Ādam, that their own appetites and the promptings of temptation plunged them into sin, and that, as the storytellers relate, they hang upside down until the Day of Resurrection, their punishment destined to last even longer were it not for God's gentleness towards them.

A clearer account of the angels' mission has been offered by Thānavī. There was a time when black magic had become very popular in the world, and in Babylon especially. Seeing its astonishing effects, ignorant people confused it with the miracles of prophets and supposed the two were of one kind. Some even regarded magicians as holy men worthy of obedience, and others began learning and practising magic as though it were a pious act with divine sanction. Something similar happened in the West after the mid-1960s, and since 1968 the study of magic has even found its way into universities, above all in America. This exaggerated reverence for magic and magicians had become a potent source of misguidance. To root it out, Allah sent two angels to Babylon to inform people of the true nature of magic and its various forms, so that they could tell it apart from the miracles of prophets, stop obeying magicians and stop practising magic themselves. Just as the prophethood of prophets is confirmed by miracles, signs and arguments, the angelic nature of Hārūt and Mārūt was confirmed by signs and arguments of its own, so that people would listen to them attentively and follow their guidance.

Why was this task not given to prophets? For two reasons. First, the need of the hour was to draw a line between prophets and magicians, and since the prophets were in a sense one party to the dispute, it was fitting that a third party should arbitrate. Second, the distinction could not normally be made clear without quoting and reporting the verbal formulas magic uses. Merely reporting heretical speech is not itself heresy, a rational principle the Sharīʿah accepts, so the prophets could have been permitted to quote them. But because the prophets are the embodiment of divine guidance, Allah did not assign them this work and chose two angels instead. Allah's commands are of two kinds, those belonging to takwīn, the order of creation and the cosmos, and those belonging to tashrīʿ, the order of legislation, and the two may sometimes appear not to agree. The created order is woven of good and evil alike, and it is the angels who carry out divine commands in that sphere. So angels are made to do things which in the cosmic perspective always serve the general good, yet which involve some partial disorder and so appear evil, such as the growth and preservation of a tyrant or a dangerous beast, each right within the order of creation and wrong from the standpoint of the law. Prophets, by contrast, are entrusted only with the legislative order, which is nothing but good in its general and particular applications. Reporting the formulas of magic did ultimately serve the legislative order, but there was a real risk, which in fact materialised, that reporting them would encourage the practice. Allah therefore preferred not to make the prophets even an indirect means of so reprehensible an activity. The prophets served the main purpose in their own way, by announcing the basic rulings of the Sharīʿah on magic without the detailed subsidiary rules, since even those might have led to temptation. An analogy makes it clear. The prophets have told us that taking a bribe is forbidden and have explained what bribery is, but they did not spell out the methods by which bribes are given and taken, since such detail would only have taught people how to do it. Or take a branch of magic: a certain formula, it is claimed, will make a man find money under his pillow or in his pocket when he rises in the morning. The Sharīʿah makes plain that such a practice is in principle illegitimate, but it does not tell us what the formula is.

So the two angels came down to Babylon and set about their task. They would explain the basic principles of magic, its forms and its specific formulas, and then dissuade people from involving themselves in these things or with magicians. Their work resembled that of a scholar who, finding that uneducated people sometimes utter heretical phrases out of ignorance, collects in his lectures or writings all the phrases in circulation and tells them what to avoid. People of every kind started coming to the angels to learn the nature and formulas of magic, so that ignorance would not lead them astray in belief or in practice. To teach correctly and protect people from error, the angels took care to warn each enquirer of the dangers of the information they were giving. They made it clear that in permitting them to pass on this knowledge Allah intended to test His servants, to see who would use it to protect his faith by recognising evil and avoiding it, and who would fall into misguidance by adopting an evil he now knew to be evil, a choice that can easily lead to unbelief in practice or in doctrine. They repeatedly urged people to seek this dangerous knowledge only with a good intention, to remain firm in it, and never to misuse it so as to earn perpetual damnation.

The angels could hardly have been more honest. They explained the principles and even the details of magic to everyone prepared to pledge that he would hold fast to his faith. If anyone broke the pledge and fell into transgression or unbelief, that was his own affair and the angels could not be blamed. Some kept their word, but many did not, and used their knowledge of magic to harm people, which is in itself sin and transgression, while some forms of magic involve unbelief. Through misuse of this knowledge some became sinners and others unbelievers. It bears repeating that the angels taught magic to reform people and keep them on the straight path, and that those who misused the teaching did so out of their own perversity. Compare a man who goes to a master of the Islamic sciences, expert in the traditional disciplines and in philosophy too, and a man who practises what he knows. The pupil asks to be taught philosophy, ancient and modern, so that he can defend himself against the doubts philosophers raise about Islamic beliefs and answer those who raise them. Fearing that the pupil may prove insincere and end up pressing philosophy into the service of anti-Islamic ideas, the master warns him, the pupil promises not to misuse what he learns, and the master then trains him thoroughly. If the pupil later breaks his promise and accepts the false theories of the philosophers as truth, no one would hold the teacher responsible. In just the same way there is no room to doubt the integrity of the two angels. Allah alone knows how matters stood, but one may suppose that once they had finished their assigned work they were recalled to the heavens.

### What magic is

According to the authoritative dictionary al-Qāmūs, siḥr means an effect whose cause is hidden, whether the cause lies in something with a luminous quality, such as the effect of certain phrases, or in beings of an extra-sensory reality, such as the effects produced by jinn and devils, or in the power of imagination, as in hypnotism, or in things that are physical but concealed, such as a hidden magnet drawing iron, drugs secretly administered, or the influence of the stars and planets. Since the causes are many, so are the forms of magic. In everyday speech magic means practices that use the cooperation of jinn and devils, or the power of imagination, or particular words and phrases. Ancient philosophers and some modern ones accept, and observation and experiment confirm, that words and phrases possess a certain efficacy of their own, and that reciting or writing certain letters, words or phrases a set number of times produces specific results. Similar results are sought by using a person's hair, nails or clothing, the practice usually called preparing charms. All of this is commonly called magic.

In the terminology of the Qur'an and the ḥadīth, however, siḥr refers to those unusual happenings brought about with the active help of the devils, who are won over by practices that please them. There are several ways of pleasing them: singing their praises, reciting heretical formulas that deny Allah or associate partners with Him, worshipping the stars and planets, and committing certain evil deeds, such as making a charm with the blood of someone unlawfully killed, refusing the prescribed modes of bodily purification, or deliberately living in a constant state of filth and impurity. Just as the help of the angels is won only by speech and conduct they love, remembrance of Allah, fear of Him, obedience to His commands, cleanliness and purity, avoidance of every kind of dirt, physical and otherwise, and good deeds generally, so the cooperation of the devils is obtained by speech and conduct that pleases them. That is why success in black magic comes only to those who are habitually unclean, who neglect purification and the remembrance of Allah, and who are given to evil deeds, and it is claimed that women become effective in it chiefly during menstruation. This is black magic in the exact sense. Sleight of hand, mesmerism, hypnotism and the tricks of jugglers are called magic only by extension or metaphor.

### Kinds of magic

Imām Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, in his Mufradāt, cites the magic of Pharaoh's sorcerers as an example of magic that works on the imagination. The Qur'an says they "deceived the eyes of the people, stunned them, and made a great display of magic" (7:116), and that their ropes and staffs "appeared to him—by their magic—to be slithering" (20:66). The verb in the second verse shares its root with khayāl, imagination, which shows that the ropes and sticks neither became serpents nor moved at all. It was the imagination of Mūsā (Moses) that was affected so that he saw them running about as serpents. The Qur'an also points to the other way of working on the imagination, with the help of the devils, when it asks on whom the devils descend and answers that they "descend upon every sinful liar" (26:221-222).

A third kind of magic is said to change the very nature of a thing, turning a man into an animal or a stone. Imām Rāghib and Abū Bakr al-Jaṣṣāṣ deny that magic can alter a thing's nature entirely, limiting its power to the imagination and to deceiving the onlookers' eyes, and this was also the Muʿtazilah position. Most scholars, however, hold that neither the Sharīʿah nor any rational argument rules out transformation, a living body becoming a stone for instance. The well-known philosophical principle that essences cannot change concerns only the three categories of the impossible, the possible and the necessary: the impossible cannot become possible, nor the possible impossible. And the fact that the Qur'an describes the Egyptian sorcerers' magic as an effect on the imagination does not mean that all magic is of that kind. Some scholars see support for the possibility of transformation in a saying of Kaʿb al-Aḥbār that Imām Mālik records in the Muwaṭṭaʾ from al-Qaʿqāʿ ibn Ḥakīm: "Were it not for these phrases that I recite regularly, the Jews would have turned me into a donkey." Although "donkey" is a common metaphor for a fool, there is no need to leave the literal sense for a figurative one without reason, so the saying means what it says: without the protection of the phrases, the Jewish sorcerers would have turned him into a donkey. It thus establishes two things, that turning a man into a donkey is possible, and that the phrases he recited had the property of neutralising the sorcerers' magic. Asked what they were, Kaʿb taught this prayer: "I seek refuge in Allah the Great, than whom none is greater, and I seek refuge in the perfect words of Allah which no man, righteous or wicked, can overstep, and I seek refuge in all the Beautiful Names of Allah, those I know and those I do not know, from the evil of everything Allah has created, brought into being and spread abroad." In sum, all three forms of magic are possible and do occur.

### Miracles and magic

Through prophets and saints events occur that never happen in the ordinary course and are therefore called kharq al-ʿādah, a breach of the habitual. The miracle of a prophet is called muʿjizah and that of a saint karāmah. A muʿjizah is an unusual event that occurs at the hands of a prophet, purely by Allah's act, the prophet's own will playing no part in producing it, and those who witness it are obliged to believe in it. A karāmah is likewise Allah's act, appearing at the hands of a man of Allah. The basic difference is that the one who performs a muʿjizah announces himself as a prophet, whereas the man of Allah makes no such claim. In both, the usual link of cause and effect is absent and there is usually a complete transformation in the nature and behaviour of things. A general belief in them is required. Allah chooses whom He wills for them, and no amount of rigorous discipline can give anyone the power to make the unusual happen. Irhāṣ is a kind of karāmah that heralds the coming of a prophet and usually occurs at the hands of someone closely related to him: the unprecedented events in the life of Maryam, mother of ʿĪsā (Jesus), or the unusual things that happened to and around Āminah, the blessed mother of the Prophet ﷺ, who felt no pangs of birth and towards the end of her term heard strange voices greeting her though she saw no one. Such events of irhāṣ as the Qur'an mentions must be believed, together with the general truth that irhāṣ accompanies the coming of a prophet.

The effects of miracles sometimes look like the effects of magic, and the resemblance leads ignorant people to confuse the two and to revere magicians quite wrongly. The difference lies first in their natures and second in the signs that accompany each. As to nature, results produced by magic depend on the law of causality just as ordinary events in the physical world do. The only distinction is that in ordinary events the causes are plain or easily observed, while in magic they are hard for the common man to detect. Where causes are evident, effects are traced straight back to them and nothing seems astonishing. Where causes are hidden, the effects provoke wonder, and those ignorant of the causes readily believe that the habitual laws of the universe have been broken. What they fail to realise is that such happenings have their causes just as habitual events do, the cause in extraordinary cases often being the activity of a jinn or a devil. Suppose a letter suddenly drops in front of a man and he finds it was written that very day ten thousand miles away. Onlookers would call it supernatural, not knowing that Allah has given jinn and devils the power to do such things. Once the means is understood, the event no longer looks supernatural or even contrary to habit. In short, everything achieved by magical practices is in fact the work of physical causes, and it wears the illusory appearance of the supernatural only because those causes are hidden from ordinary people. In the West such events are labelled "supernatural", although the Latin natura, like the Greek phusis, originally covered everything created, whether within the field of empirical science or beyond it.

Miracles, on the other hand, happen directly by divine command without the intervention of physical causes. The Qur'an tells how the tyrant Namrūd (Nimrod) threw Ibrāhīm (Abraham) into a blazing fire and how Allah commanded the fire to be cool for him, though not so cool as to harm him, and the fire obeyed, offering him safety instead of scorching him. That was a direct divine act and therefore a miracle. We sometimes hear of men who coat their bodies with certain chemicals and walk into fire unscorched. That is no miracle, because a physical cause, the chemicals, is at work, and people unfamiliar with it are deluded into thinking the performance supernatural. The Qur'an leaves no doubt that a miracle is Allah's own act. At the Battle of Badr the Prophet ﷺ took up a handful of pebbles and threw them at the army of the unbelievers, blinding their eyes, and the Qur'an says of it: "Nor was it you ˹O Prophet˺ who threw ˹a handful of sand at the disbelievers˺, but it was Allah Who did so" (8:17). The miraculous effect of a handful of pebbles was not the Prophet's ﷺ doing but Allah's.

How then is the common man to tell them apart, when the results look the same? Allah has given each its distinguishing marks. First, miracles appear through people whom everyone knows to be clean in body, pure in conduct and God-fearing, while magic works through those who are unclean in body, foul in conduct, and estranged from the worship and remembrance of Allah, a difference anyone can see with his own eyes. Second, Allah has so arranged matters that if a man claims prophethood and claims to work miracles, and yet tries to practise magic, his magic never works. He can be effective in magic only so long as he makes no claim to prophethood.

Can magic affect prophets? It can. Magic works through physical causes, and prophets are not immune to physical causes. Just as they feel hunger and thirst, fall ill and recover through ordinary causes, they can be affected by the hidden operations of magic, which are no less physical. Being affected by magic no more detracts from the dignity of prophethood than hunger or thirst does. Authentic ḥadīths report that the Prophet ﷺ was affected by a spell cast by the Jews, that he learned of it through revelation, and that he took steps to free himself of its effects. The Qur'an likewise tells how Mūsā felt the effects of magic when the sorcerers cast a spell on his eyes and their ropes and sticks seemed to him to be running serpents, so that he was genuinely frightened (20:66).

### The rulings of the Sharīʿah on magic

In the language of the Qur'an and the ḥadīth, siḥr means only those practices in which one seeks the help of jinn and devils by pleasing them through words or deeds that involve unbelief (kufr) and association (shirk), or sin and transgression, and thereby produces extraordinary events. The magic of Babylon mentioned here was exactly this, and this is the form the Qur'an condemns as kufr, as al-Jaṣṣāṣ explained. According to Abū Manṣūr, the soundest view is that not every form of magic is unconditionally unbelief, but only the form that uses actions or speech contrary to the tenets of faith (īmān). The Qur'an and the ḥadīth repeatedly command Muslims to treat the devils as enemies, to hate and curse them, so seeking to please them and win their friendship is in itself a sin. They are pleased only by kufr or shirk, which forfeits faith altogether, or at least by disobedience and transgression that displeases Allah and His angels, such as wallowing in filth and impurity, which adds a further sin. If magic is then used to harm a human being unlawfully, that is one sin more. What the Qur'an and ḥadīth call siḥr, therefore, is never free of unbelief either in belief or in action. One who adopts speech or acts of kufr or shirk to please the devils commits real unbelief in matters of faith, and one who avoids these but indulges in other sins does not escape unbelief in matters of action. This is what the verse means in calling magic kufr. In sum, magic that uses acts or speech of unbelief, such as seeking the devils' help, believing that the stars and planets act of themselves, or claiming prophethood by passing off magical results as miracles, is unbelief by consensus, while magic that involves no act of unbelief but only sinful acts is a major sin.

Five subsidiary rulings follow. First, since magic is rarely free from unbelief in faith or in action, it is forbidden to learn it, teach it or practise it, though some jurists permit learning it in order to protect Muslims from harm, and then no more than is necessary. Second, charms and amulets prepared with the help of jinn and devils fall under siḥr and are forbidden, and so are charms whose words are obscure if one suspects they are invocations of idols or devils. Third, forms of magic other than the Babylonian kind condemned by the Qur'an and ḥadīth are also forbidden if they involve kufr or shirk in any way. Fourth, a form of magic that uses words, actions or other elements to which the Sharīʿah does not object is permissible, provided it is not used for a purpose the Sharīʿah forbids. Fifth, it is permissible to use verses of the Qur'an, the Divine Names or the words of the ḥadīth in charms and amulets, or as recitations and invocations seeking Allah's help towards a desired end, but using the sacred texts for a reprehensible purpose, such as harming someone without just cause, is forbidden even so.

### The inner allusion

The devotional reading finds in the story a portrait of a type. Whoever is torn apart by his whims (ahwāʾ) falls from one heedlessness (ghaflah) into the next, and every kind of ignorant business comes to greet him. Those whose absence from the Beloved is prolonged become a warning to others and a temptation to anyone who follows their road, and whoever imitates their transgression joins their company and becomes one of them. So it was with Hārūt and Mārūt, who became a temptation and a warning for mankind. Whoever listens to such people without reckoning with their ignorance will share their trial (balāʾuhumā) and suffer their distress (ʿanāʾuhumā) in the Hereafter. The allusion is to the man on the spiritual path (ṭarīqah) who inclines to affectation, deception and pretension. He gathers followers whom he leads to the Fire by his falsehood, and by the evil of his darknesses he blocks them from the way of right direction. Those who take heed cross safely over its bridges, while the one disgraced (tahattaka) by his leaning towards falsehood finds his veils (astāruhu) torn and his faults (ʿawāruhu) laid bare to anyone with eyes.

### In short

- After abandoning the Torah the Jews took up the sorcery the devils had attributed to Sulaymān, books of eavesdropped half-truths and seventy-fold lies dug up from beneath his throne, or forged under the name of Āṣaf ibn Barakhiyā. Allah declares Sulaymān innocent of disbelief.
- Hārūt and Mārūt in Babylon are, in the view of most early authorities, two angels sent as a trial, who warned every enquirer "we are only a test, do not disbelieve", while Ibn Jarīr read them as two men and the clause as a denial that Allah ever sent down magic.
- The angels' mission, on Thānavī's explanation, was to teach people to distinguish magic from prophetic miracles, a task given to angels rather than prophets so that guidance would never become even an indirect cause of sorcery.
- Magic that works through the devils is unbelief, magic that involves only sin is a major sin, learning or teaching it is forbidden except as needed for protection, and nothing it does can harm anyone except by Allah's leave.
- Miracles come directly from Allah through the pure, magic through hidden physical causes and the impure, and those who buy into it have sold their souls for a miserable price with no share in the Hereafter.
