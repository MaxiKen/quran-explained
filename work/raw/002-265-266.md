<<<VERSE 2:265>>>
Sincere giving is compared with a garden that has a sound foundation: "And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah’s pleasure and believing the reward is certain" is a fruitful orchard on high ground. Unlike dust lying on bare rock, this soil can sustain growth. The comparison promises a lasting return for gifts offered with trust in Allah, whether the amount is large or small.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah’s pleasure and believing the reward is certain,"** Their purpose is His acceptance, and their hearts trust His promise. They are not like hypocrites who do not hope for the Hereafter's reward because they do not believe in it. Another explanation emphasises the strengthening of the soul through sincere action.

**"is that of a garden on a fertile hill:"** Rabwah denotes elevated ground. The readings rabwa and rubwa express that height. Ibn 'Abbas and Ad-Dahhak add flowing rivers to the description. The image is of a productive orchard, not an exposed rock with a misleading covering.

**"when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce."** Wabil is heavy rain, and the result is abundant fruit. The explanation also compares its yield with twice that of another garden. Readings of the word for produce, ukulaha and uklaha, retain the reference to its fruit or harvest.

**"If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient."** Tall means light rain, or dew in another gloss. Ad-Dahhak explains it through a gentle shower. The garden's fertility allows even this small supply to sustain it. In the same way sincere expenditure does not become barren merely because it is modest.

**"And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do"** No act or intention is hidden from Him. He sees who gives sincerely and who does not, and He rewards accordingly. The picture does not make appearances an infallible measure of the heart.

### How are belief and expectation joined?

Al-Bukhari and Muslim record a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ describing the person who fasts Ramadan with faith and expectation of reward. Faith recognises that Allah commanded the fast. Expectation looks for its return from Him.

The same distinction clarifies the giver's conviction. A person can perform the outward act while aiming only at a worldly response. Here the act is directed towards Allah, with confidence that obedience will not be forgotten. The heart's expectation matches the deed's declared purpose.

This trust does not purchase a right to boast. It brings the giver into the conditions of sincerity and obedience already described. The hope is in Allah's promise, not in a claim that any transfer of wealth must automatically deserve the same reward.

### How does giving strengthen resolve?

Repeated effort towards a goal makes that goal more firmly established in the heart. If the goal is personal desire, further exertion deepens attachment to it. If the determining purpose is Allah's will, the effort strengthens attachment to Him.

Circumstances can make the choice easier or harder. Giving under ordinary conditions has its reward. Giving when obedience requires a special exercise of resolve can deepen the bond further and bring a correspondingly greater reward.

For example, an expenditure may offer no prospect of worldly benefit. A person may need to assist someone whom they would prefer not to help, or extend friendship despite a grudge. Choosing Allah's pleasure in those circumstances trains the heart away from its own dislikes and towards Him.

The value therefore cannot be measured merely by comparing amounts. The struggle involved, the intention and the trust are all within Allah's sight. That is why the final assurance about His seeing belongs so closely to the garden image.

### Why is a small gift not barren?

The garden has suitable ground before either kind of rain arrives. A downpour produces great abundance, but a drizzle is still useful. The contrast is not between a rich giver who matters and a poor giver who does not.

The explanation applies it to a believer's sincere gifts, small or great. Allah accepts and increases the righteous deed according to its quality. A little given with sound intention and observance of the conditions can secure a gain in the Hereafter.

The earlier rock could not hold its covering when rain fell. Here even gentle rain nourishes the garden. The decisive difference is the foundation, which pictures sincere conviction rather than the amount visible to observers.

### What inward growth is pictured?

The allusive reading describes the sincere person's works thriving, wealth receiving increase and spiritual states rising before Allah. Their effort receives thanks, honour and compensation, called khalaf. The final goal is communion and nearness rather than an audience's admiration.

The imagery adds spreading branches, growing surplus and multiplied benefit. It describes the life of an act rooted in devotion. Its contrast with failed bargaining belongs to the next picture of devastating loss.

### In short

- Sincere giving resembles a fertile garden rather than a thin covering on rock.
- Trust in Allah's reward and seeking His pleasure give the act its sound foundation.
- A small sincere gift can bear fruit, and costly obedience can strengthen devotion.
- Allah sees the deed, the intention and the circumstances together.

<<<VERSE 2:266>>>
The next picture asks the reader to feel a loss at the moment of greatest need: "Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath" only to see it burn when old age and dependent children make it indispensable? No one would wish that outcome. It warns against destroying the reward of good deeds before the day when there is no opportunity to replace them.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath"** The orchard is valuable, varied and well supplied with water. The question expects a refusal once the rest of the scene is heard. It makes the reader consider what it would mean to lose a cultivated resource, not merely an unwanted possession.

**"and as they grow very old with dependent children,"** The owner can no longer work as before, while the children are too weak or young to support themselves or help restore the property. Together the details remove ordinary hopes of recovery. The garden supplies the needs of more than one person.

**"a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up?"** I'sar denotes violent revolving winds. The destructive fire consumes the trees and their produce. A gloss discusses the strong wind as cold or hot, while the verse's scene culminates in burning. No useful harvest remains at the time the family most needs it.

**"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect"** The comparison is instruction through a sign. Its purpose is thoughtful recognition of the consequences of one's choices. Allah explains commands, prohibitions and what benefits people here and in the Hereafter through an image they can grasp.

### How did 'Umar ask about this example?

Al-Bukhari records through Ibn 'Abbas and 'Ubayd bin 'Umayr that 'Umar bin Al-Khattab asked the Prophet's Companions whom the verse concerned. When they answered that Allah knew best, he became angry and asked them to distinguish knowing from not knowing.

Ibn 'Abbas said he had an opinion. 'Umar encouraged him to speak and not belittle himself. He answered that it illustrated a deed. When asked which kind, he described a wealthy person who first acts in Allah's pleasure, then falls under Satan's influence and works in disobedience until the earlier good works are annulled.

The explanation sees the burning garden as the end of that progression. Good existed, then evil destroyed its benefit. The person eventually needs the earlier deeds desperately, only to find that their reward is no longer there.

That account gives the image a wider moral application than money alone. It warns about an ending that consumes what a person had hoped to rely upon. The warning calls for preservation of obedience, not complacency because of an impressive past.

### What further explanation is given by Ibn 'Abbas?

Ibn Abi Hatim records through Al-'Awfi another explanation from Ibn 'Abbas. The garden owner has lost everything in old age, without strength to plant again and without children able to help. A storm destroys what he needs at the end of life.

The explanation compares that helplessness with the disbeliever at resurrection. No good deeds remain as refuge or defence, and there is nothing to turn to for support. Just as the old man cannot recover his youthful strength, the person cannot return to earthly life to prepare another provision.

The interpretations have related but distinct emphases. One follows good deeds spoiled by later evil. The other pictures the disbeliever's lack of a resource when brought before Allah. Both place the loss at the point of urgent need and unavailable repair.

### Why specify old age and weak children?

A young owner might be able to establish another orchard. Someone without dependants might bear a loss with fewer demands upon the remaining means of livelihood. Someone whose adult children were capable and independent might look to their help.

The verse removes all three consolations. Work and capital have already gone into establishing the garden, and it has reached the stage of yielding fruit. Then the owner becomes weak while children still need provision. The fire therefore destroys both present income and the family's means of facing the future.

The account makes the magnitude of spiritual loss intelligible through that ordinary vulnerability. After death, a person cannot resume the earthly period of earning deeds. Apparent investment in the Hereafter made through hypocrisy or self-promotion provides no assistance precisely when it was supposed to be available.

### Why can religious activity still serve worldly ambition?

A person may openly choose worldly means to secure fame and profit. Another may use a religious cause to achieve the same ends. Their outward activities differ, but their purpose remains alike.

Religious appearance alone therefore does not establish sincerity. If worldly advancement is the sole objective, calling the activity pious does not change its destination. Such a person reaches the Hereafter without the return that sincere worship would have prepared.

The explanation urges a capacity for reflection because signs do not always disclose their meaning to superficial observation. The orchard's destruction is understood through considering its circumstances and the end towards which one's actions are directed.

### What conditions preserve charitable expenditure?

The surrounding instruction is gathered into six conditions. The property must be halal, lawful. The method must follow the sunnah. The place or cause must be appropriate. The giver must refrain from reminding recipients of favours and refrain from insulting or hurting them. Finally, the intention must seek Allah's pleasure rather than fame.

These requirements concern the whole action, not only the moment when the money is handed over. Sadaqah and khayrat, charity and beneficence, need a lawful beginning, proper direction and respectful aftermath.

Following the sunnah includes protecting other people's rights. It is not rewarded generosity to reduce necessary family expenses without their consent in order to advertise charity. Nor is depriving needy heirs by bequeathing the entire inheritance or giving everything away in accordance with the method described here.

Choice among causes also needs thought. Importance, need and urgency matter. A good intention alone does not make every use of funds commendable. The shariah must permit and approve the destination. A bequest supporting forbidden games and amusements earns punishment rather than the hoped-for reward. Other unsuitable avenues are judged by the same principle.

The six conditions therefore resist two errors at once: thinking only the intention matters and thinking only the outward expenditure matters. The aim, property, recipients, method and treatment of others all belong together.

### What prayer is reported for the time of need?

Al-Hakim records in his Mustadrak a supplication of the Prophet ﷺ asking Allah to make his greatest provision available in old age and at life's end. No additional grading is supplied here.

The prayer fits the picture's concern. Provision has particular urgency when strength diminishes and opportunities narrow. It asks for help then rather than treating the time of vulnerability as unimportant.

The instruction to understand such comparisons is also connected with (29:43), which says that their deeper meaning is grasped by those possessing knowledge of Allah and His signs. Reflection is therefore part of receiving the guidance, not merely an optional appreciation of its literary form.

### How does the inward contrast complete the two gardens?

The sincere person's growing branches and honoured effort are contrasted with failed bargaining. The hypocrite's commodity is lost, strategies fail in old age and troubles follow in every direction. The allusive language calls this ruin talaf and destruction thubur, ending in flame rather than communion.

The contrast refuses to treat the two lives as equivalent merely because both included expenditure. One act grows from devotion and the other is spent in vain. One receives lasting honour while the other's hopes end in loss and increased misery.

The ruined orchard thus completes the warning begun by the barren rock. Neither outward quantity nor past appearance can substitute for sincerity and preserved obedience. The reader is invited to protect the provision needed at the final return.

### In short

- A destroyed orchard pictures lost reward at the moment it is most needed.
- Old age and weak children explain why the loss cannot readily be repaired.
- Ibn 'Abbas applies the image to good spoiled by evil and to helplessness at resurrection.
- Charity needs lawful means, a proper method and destination, sincerity and respect for recipients.
- Religious activity directed solely towards fame remains a worldly pursuit despite its appearance.
