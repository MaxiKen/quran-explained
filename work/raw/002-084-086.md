<<<VERSE 2:84>>>
Verse 84 adds the next articles of the covenant, the ones that governed how the Israelites were to treat each other. "And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other’s blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness".

### Phrase by phrase

**"And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant"**. This covenant was made in the Book, the Torah. The verse supplements the one before it and sets out further terms of the pledge the Israelites took.

**"that you would neither shed each other’s blood"**. Do not kill one another. They had agreed not to slay each other in internecine war. One early reading applies the clause directly to the Jewish tribes of Madinah, Banū Qurayẓah and Banū an-Naḍīr. Allah speaks of "your own" blood here, just as He told the calf-worshippers to repent and "execute yourselves" (2:54), because the followers of one religion are like a single soul. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ described the believers in their mutual kindness, mercy and sympathy as one body: when one limb falls ill the whole body answers with fever and sleeplessness.

**"nor expel each other from their homes"**. No one of you may drive another from his house. The pledge also covered not forcing their own people into exile, that is, not harassing a man until he is driven to migrate. Nor were they to take part in fighting against their own.

**"you gave your pledge and bore witness"**. You agreed to this covenant and testified against your own souls that you knew of it and had witnessed it, fully aware of what you were doing. The agreement was willing. Sometimes a person does not state his willingness in so many words and only the manner of his speech suggests it, but the Israelites' consent was not of that kind. It was as clear and explicit as a witness's statement is.

### How the pledge was broken

The verse was aimed at the Jews living in Madinah in the time of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. They had long suffered from the armed conflicts between the two Arab tribes of the city, al-Aws and al-Khazraj, who before Islam worshipped idols and fought many battles. Three Jewish tribes lived around Madinah, Banū Qaynuqāʿ, Banū an-Naḍīr and Banū Qurayẓah. Although all three followed the law of Mūsā (Moses), ignorant prejudice split them into two camps enmeshed in the political manoeuvring of the pagans. One account places Qaynuqāʿ and an-Naḍīr with the Khazraj and Qurayẓah with the Aws, while Ibn ʿAbbās places Qaynuqāʿ with the Khazraj and both an-Naḍīr and Qurayẓah with the Aws. When war broke out between the Aws and the Khazraj their Jewish allies fought beside them, so that Jew killed Jew, drove Jew from his home and looted his goods, in defiance of clear texts in their own Books. The next verse takes the story up in detail and shows how this behaviour, practising part of the Torah while trampling the rest, became the occasion of these verses and the proof of their duplicity.

### In short

- The Israelites pledged in the Torah not to kill or expel one another, and testified to the pledge with full knowledge.
- "Your own blood" treats the whole community as one soul, as in 2:54, and as the Prophet's ﷺ image of the one body teaches.
- The Jews of Madinah, split between the Aws and the Khazraj, broke exactly these terms in the tribal wars.

<<<VERSE 2:85>>>
Verse 85 holds the Jews of Madinah to the covenant just described and shows how they kept one clause while breaking the others. "But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression", it says, and yet when the expelled come back as captives "you still ransom them". The verse then asks the question at the heart of the whole passage: "Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?"

### Phrase by phrase

**"But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes"**. After the pledge, after confirming it and bearing witness, they broke it. Allah had laid down three particular injunctions for the Israelites: not to kill one another in civil war, not to force their own people into exile, and, if they found one of their own a prisoner of war, to pay a ransom and secure his release. They disregarded the first two and acted on the third alone, which they supposed the easier to carry out.

**"aiding one another in sin and aggression"**. The Arabic verb for aiding has one letter assimilated into another, and a variant reading keeps the two letters separate. They conspired against their own people in disobedience and injustice, in contravention and offence, assisted by their enemies. The indictment of "sin and aggression" suggests that two kinds of right were infringed: Allah's right, through disobedience to Him, and the right of His creatures, through the pain and loss inflicted on them.

**"and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them"**. Captives of your own religion you free by paying ransom to the enemy, delivering them from captivity with money or the like. Variant readings exist for both "captives" and "ransom". This ransoming was itself one of the things to which they were pledged.

**"though expelling them was unlawful for you"**. Their expulsion and killing were unlawful in the first place. Grammatically this clause attaches to the earlier words about expelling, and what lies between is a parenthesis: expulsion was forbidden to you in the same way that failing to ransom was forbidden to you.

**"Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?"**. You believe in the part that requires you to free your captives from the enemy, and you disbelieve in the part that forbids fighting, expelling and assisting against one another. Another reading of the phrase: do you believe in the parts of the Scripture that suit your whim and disbelieve in the parts that do not? A further gloss has it that they ransomed their own captives but left the captives of their friends without ransom.

**"Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life"**. Degradation, disgrace and ignominy in this world, by being killed or taken captive as slaves.

**"and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment?"**. Returned to the most terrible of chastisements, as punishment for defying the Book of Allah that they possessed.

**"For Allah is never unaware of what you do"**. He is not reducing your punishment, because of the transgressions you commit, or, as some say, because of what you concealed. He will hold you to account. A variant reading has "what they do".

### The wars of the Aws and the Khazraj

Ibn ʿAbbās, through Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq ibn Yasār, explained the background. Allah mentioned what they were doing, and that in the Torah He had forbidden them to shed each other's blood and required them to free their prisoners. The Jews of Madinah were divided into two camps, Banū Qaynuqāʿ allied with the Khazraj, and an-Naḍīr and Qurayẓah allied with the Aws. When fighting erupted between the Aws and the Khazraj, Qaynuqāʿ fought beside the Khazraj and an-Naḍīr and Qurayẓah beside the Aws, each Jewish camp fighting its Jewish brethren in the other. They shed one another's blood although they had the Torah with them and knew their rights and duties, while the Aws and the Khazraj were idol-worshipping polytheists who knew nothing of Paradise, the Fire, the Resurrection, divine Books or the lawful (ḥalāl) and the forbidden (ḥarām). When a war ended the Jews ransomed their prisoners and applied the Torah: Qaynuqāʿ ransomed their men captured by the Aws, and an-Naḍīr and Qurayẓah ransomed theirs captured by the Khazraj. They also demanded blood money. During the wars they killed whomever they could, Jew or Arab, and helped the polytheists against their own brethren. So Allah reminded them: do you ransom them by the ruling of the Torah, and kill them though the Torah forbade you to kill them or expel them from their homes, and though it commanded you not to aid the polytheists against your brethren? All of this you do to gain the life of this world. Ibn ʿAbbās added that he had been informed that this conduct toward the Aws and the Khazraj was the reason these verses were revealed. In the victorious camp the Jews would release the prisoners of the defeated party, according to the Torah's rulings, having first killed, expelled and looted furniture and money in the fighting.

Another account describes the same hypocrisy from the Jews' own mouths. In these battles Jews lost their lives and homes as much as non-Jews, so the Jews of Qurayẓah shared in the slaughter and exile of the Jews of an-Naḍīr, and the reverse. Yet when Jews were taken prisoner, each Jewish group would persuade its own Arab friends to accept a ransom and release them. Asked why they showed such solicitude for the prisoners, they answered that ransoming was obligatory on them. Asked why, then, they fought them at all, they said it would be a real disgrace not to go to the aid of their allies, even non-Jewish ones, for fear their allies be humiliated. The verse exposes this duplicity and perversity, and the whole picture, as one modern writer puts it, shows the three tribes, united in Mosaic law, constantly at war with one another in two camps.

### Why they could not be trusted

These noble verses criticise the Jews for applying the Torah at one time and defying it at another, although they believed in the Torah and knew that what they did was wrong. For this reason they were not to be trusted to preserve or convey the Torah, nor to be believed about the Messenger of Allah ﷺ: his description, his coming, his being driven from his land, his Hijrah, and everything else the earlier Prophets had told them of him, all of which they hid among themselves. Allah's verdict on such people is the disgrace of this world and the gravest torment of the next.

### Was their selective faith unbelief?

Scholars note that the Jews addressed here had already become unbelievers by refusing to accept the prophethood of Muḥammad ﷺ. The verse, however, does not point to that aspect of their unbelief (kufr). It rebukes them for having faith (īmān) in one part of the Torah and not in another. Read literally, the words mean that the Jews became unbelievers by not believing in some parts, that is, some injunctions, of the Torah. A divine Book has to be taken whole. Rejecting one part amounts to rejecting all of it, which is plainly unbelief. If instead the verse is taken as a rebuke for failing to act on certain commands, a question arises: how can breaking certain commandments be called kufr, when a believer is not counted an unbeliever so long as he accepts in principle the distinction between ḥalāl and ḥarām as the Sharīʿah lays it down? The answer is that the idiom of the Sharīʿah sometimes labels a very grave sin kufr to stress its gravity and its moral character. The hadith which says that whoever deliberately abandons the ṣalāh has become an unbeliever works in the same way. On this second reading, nothing softens the kufr of which the Jews were already guilty by denying the Prophet ﷺ.

### A punishment in this world and the next

The verse announces punishment not only in the Hereafter but in this world, in the form of humiliation and disgrace, and it came about as foretold. In the Prophet's ﷺ own lifetime the Jews of Banū Qurayẓah were slain or taken prisoner, and Banū an-Naḍīr were expelled for breaking the pact they had made with the Muslims, driven out to Syria, and the Jews were ordered to pay the jizyah.

A doctrinal question is raised about the "harshest punishment" promised to the Jews. Why should the direst punishment be reserved for them rather than for atheists, when the Jews at least believe in Allah? The famous commentator al-Ālūsī answers in his Rūḥ al-Maʿānī that every punishment given to unbelievers is "the direst" in the sense that it has no end or limit. The verse therefore does not mean that the Jews will be punished more severely than every other unbeliever, but that they will receive the kind of punishment that is endless and limitless, in other words a punishment more severe than that of Muslim sinners. If there turn out to be different degrees of punishment among different kinds of unbelievers, that does not conflict with the verse.

### Ritual without obedience

A modern reflection sees in this a permanent pattern. The basic teachings of Islamic law demand a complete renunciation of pagan ways, the suppression of selfish desire and the control of worldly wants, and that is exactly why its commands are ignored in politics. Obeying them would produce true piety, but people dislike such arduous practice. They prefer a pretence of piety made up of superficial rituals, which amounts to issuing a self-made edition of the divine religion, disregarding the Hereafter and prizing the worldly side of religion in the hope that zeal for acts bringing fame and honour will earn the reward that belongs to true piety. Such brazen distortion of religion earns only God's wrath and merits no reward from Him.

### Picking and choosing

The devotional reading puts the verse's logic simply. If you protect their rights by paying their ransom, you must equally hold your hands back from them and give up driving them out of their homelands. Having upheld part of what was made obligatory on you, what stops you from carrying out the rest of your commands? Whoever picks and chooses among his commands, believing some and disbelieving others, forfeits the reward of his deeds on account of what he left undone. They imagined that their actions would profit them. In the Hereafter they will discover that nothing they did was accepted, because it was mixed with harm and emptied of sincerity and devotion. Captives, moreover, come in different kinds. One captive is drowning in the sea of whim (hawā), and his rescue is to be turned toward guidance. Another is held by whisperings (wasāwis), and his ransom is to be shown the road to certainty by the glimmers of proofs.

### In short

- Of the three Torah commands, no killing, no expulsion, ransom your captives, the Jews of Madinah kept only the easiest, ransoming men whom they themselves had helped to kill and expel in the wars of the Aws and the Khazraj.
- Their own explanations exposed the double standard: ransom because we are commanded, fight lest our allies be shamed.
- Believing in part of the Book and rejecting part is treated as unbelief, either literally, since a divine Book is accepted whole, or as a grave sin labelled kufr for emphasis, like deliberately abandoning the ṣalāh.
- The promised disgrace arrived with the fate of Qurayẓah and an-Naḍīr, and the "harshest punishment" means, as al-Ālūsī explained, the endless punishment of unbelief.
- Religion that keeps the convenient rituals and drops the demanding commands is a self-made religion, and the believer must also ransom the captives of whim and whispering within.

<<<VERSE 2:86>>>
Verse 86 names the bargain behind all this. "These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. So their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped".

### Phrase by phrase

**"These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world"**. They have bought this world at the price of the next, preferring the temporary to the eternal, this lower world to the Hereafter, and disbelief to belief. This explains why the Jews must bear so severe a punishment. They disobeyed Allah to satisfy their desires and enjoy worldly pleasures, and for these things they sacrificed their salvation in the world to come.

**"So their punishment will not be reduced"**. It will not be lightened even for an hour. Some say it will never be lifted. Their sin is so great that Allah will neither reduce nor soften their punishment.

**"nor will they be helped"**. They will find no helper to save them from the eternal torment, and no one to grant them refuge from it or to shield them from Allah's chastisement. Allah will allow no one to intercede on their behalf.

### Losing both worlds

The devotional reading observes that whoever prefers anything to God has lost in this world and in the Hereafter alike. It quotes the saying: people turned from us for no cause or reason, and our need of them is even smaller than their need of us.

### In short

- The Jews of Madinah sold the Hereafter for worldly advantage, and that is why their punishment is unrelieved.
- No reduction, no respite and no helper await those who make this trade.
- Whoever prefers anything to Allah loses both worlds.
