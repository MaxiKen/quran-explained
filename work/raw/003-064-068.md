<<<VERSE 3:64>>>
The Prophet is told to call the People of the Book to a shared principle: “that we will worship none but Allah” (3:64). They are invited to worship Allah alone, associate no partner with Him, and refuse to treat one another as lords by obeying commands that disobey Allah. If they turn away, the Muslims are to affirm their submission.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘O People of the Book!’”** addresses the Jews and Christians and those who follow their religious claims. The message is not framed as a private quarrel. It calls people who possess earlier scripture to return to the central teaching shared by all messengers.

**“Let us come to common terms”** means to agree on a fair and upright principle. The common term is a complete statement of belief, not a compromise that gives up the truth. It provides a starting point both sides can recognize.

**“that we will worship none but Allah”** means that worship belongs to Allah alone. The messengers all called people to this. The Qur’an says that every messenger was told that there is no deity except Allah and that He alone should be worshipped (21:25). It also says that a messenger was sent to every community to call them to worship Allah and avoid false gods (16:36). This is the foundation of tawḥīd, the oneness of Allah.

**“associate none with Him”** rules out worshipping an idol, a cross, fire, a false deity, or any created thing. It also rules out treating a human being as a partner in Allah’s right to be worshipped. The invitation is clear and direct: worship Allah alone.

**“nor take one another as lords instead of Allah”** means that people must not obey religious leaders or rulers when they command disobedience to Allah. Ibn Jurayj explains that the prohibition is against obeying one another in disobedience to Allah. It does not forbid ordinary cooperation or respect. It rejects giving a human being the authority to make disobedience into a religion.

An inward reading applies the same warning to the self. The heart should not obey its commanding desires or treat blameworthy caprice as a lord. A person seeking truth should keep to one concern, rather than letting scattered attachments control the heart. The reading connects this to the promise of being in a seat of truthfulness in the Hereafter (54:55), and to the command not to take two gods (16:51).

**“But if they turn away”** means that the People of the Book may refuse the invitation. Their refusal does not require the Prophet to abandon his own belief or continue disputing without end.

**“then say, ‘Bear witness that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺’”** tells the Prophet to restate the Muslims’ position. If the other side rejects a clear common principle, the Muslims should bear witness to their submission to Allah and conclude the exchange.

### How should a message to another community begin?

One explanation draws a principle for preaching and dialogue from the verse. When speaking to people whose beliefs differ, begin with a truth both sides can recognize, such as the oneness of Allah. The Prophet’s letter to Heraclius, leader of the Romans, called him to Islam and promised safety and a double reward if he accepted. It warned that if he turned away, he would bear responsibility for his subjects. The letter then used the invitation to worship Allah alone. Al-Bukhārī records the letter.

The verse also has a connection to the delegation from Najrān. Muhammad bin Ishaq and Az-Zuhrī differ in how they relate the delegation’s agreement to pay. The people of Najrān came before the conquest of Makkah and before the later ruling on jizyah (9:29). One account says their payment was part of a settlement in place of mubāhalah, not the later jizyah tax. The verse prescribing jizyah came afterward and supported the arrangement already made with them.

An earlier event also shows that a practice may precede the verse that later confirms a ruling. ʿAbd Allāh bin Jaḥsh divided booty during an expedition before Badr, and a later revelation established the rule of one-fifth for the Prophet and four-fifths for the fighters. The Qur’an also came in agreement with things ʿUmar had said, including taking the Station of Ibrāhīm as a place of prayer (2:125), the hijab, the treatment of captives at Badr, and not praying for hypocrites. One other statement concerned the possibility that the Prophet’s wives could be replaced by better wives (66:5). These examples show that revelation may affirm a sound judgment already made.

### In short

- The common term is worshipping Allah alone and associating no partners with Him.
- No person should be obeyed as a lord when that obedience means disobeying Allah.
- If the People of the Book turn away, Muslims should affirm their submission and end the dispute.

<<<VERSE 3:65>>>
The verse challenges the People of the Book: “Why do you argue about Abraham” (3:65), when the Torah and Gospel came long after him? The Jews and Christians cannot claim that Ibrāhīm (Abraham) belonged to a religious community that arose after his time.

### Phrase by phrase

**“O People of the Book!”** again addresses Jews and Christians, here in response to their claims about Ibrāhīm, also called al-Khalīl, the intimate friend of Allah.

**“Why do you argue about Abraham”** refers to their dispute over whether he was a Jew or a Christian. The Jews claimed him as one of their own, and the Christians made the corresponding claim for their religion. Ibn ʿAbbās reports that Jewish rabbis and Christian leaders argued this in front of the Prophet ﷺ. The verse rejects the claim from both sides.

**“while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him?”** means that Ibrāhīm lived before the revelation of the Torah to Mūsā (Moses) and before the Gospel given to ʿĪsā (Jesus). Judaism and Christianity, in the forms being debated, came later. Ibrāhīm could not have followed a scripture or community that had not yet appeared.

**“Do you not understand”** calls them to use reason. They argue from religious identities that arose after Ibrāhīm, even though their own scriptures show the order of events. Their claim is not supported by chronology.

The question does not forbid debate on matters people know. It rebukes disputing about a time and person concerning which they have no knowledge. It would be better to discuss the law they actually received than to invent a connection to Ibrāhīm.

### What is the proper limit of religious argument?

A person should not make confident claims about what they do not know. The People of the Book have knowledge of their own later revelations, but they cannot use them to rewrite the history of Ibrāhīm. Allah knows what they do not. The verse exposes the error of arguing from inherited labels rather than evidence.

An inward reading describes possessiveness as a cloak and jealousy as a veil over the heart. These veils cut people off from the truth about Ibrāhīm and make them cling to claims that do not fit the historical sequence. The word ḥijāb, a veil, here describes how pride can block understanding.

### In short

- Ibrāhīm lived before the Torah and Gospel were revealed.
- Jews and Christians cannot claim he belonged to communities that arose later.
- Religious argument must stay within what people can actually know.

<<<VERSE 3:66>>>
The verse distinguishes between informed discussion and baseless claims: “Allah knows and you do not know” (3:66). The People of the Book have some knowledge of their own scriptures, but they argue about Ibrāhīm’s time and religion without evidence. Allah alone knows the full reality.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Here you are! You disputed about what you have ˹little˺ knowledge of”** refers to matters the Jews and Christians know from their own scriptures, including the accounts of Mūsā and ʿĪsā. Their knowledge is limited, but at least they have a text to discuss.

**“but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of?”** rebukes their claims about Ibrāhīm. Their books do not establish that he was Jewish or Christian. They argue without proof about a period before those communities existed.

**“Allah knows”** means that Allah knows Ibrāhīm’s actual belief, the truth of his history, and what people later claimed about him. He knows the seen and unseen and the reality of every matter.

**“and you do not know”** reminds the disputants that human knowledge is limited. They should refer what they do not know to Allah rather than make unsupported claims. One inward reading says that a person should not judge matters for which there is no decisive proof or way of knowing.

The verse criticizes not only an incorrect conclusion but the habit of speaking beyond one’s knowledge. A person can know something about the Torah or Gospel and still know nothing about a period that preceded them. Knowledge of one subject does not grant authority over every other subject.

### In short

- The People of the Book know something about their scriptures but lack evidence for their claims about Ibrāhīm.
- Allah knows the truth of his religion and history.
- People should not argue confidently about matters they do not know.
<<<END>>>

<<<VERSE 3:67>>>
The verse gives the answer: “Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian” (3:67). He submitted to Allah in uprightness and was not a polytheist. His religion was tawḥīd before the later labels arose.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian”** rejects both claims made about him. Ibrāhīm lived before the Torah and Gospel, so he belonged to neither later religious community.

**“he submitted in all uprightness”** describes him as a Muslim, one who submits to Allah. The word ḥanīf means turning away from false religions toward the upright path of worshipping Allah alone. It does not mean following a crooked path or constantly shifting according to personal advantage.

One inward explanation connects ḥanīf with mustaqīm, standing straight in truth. It pictures Ibrāhīm as unwavering in his submission, ready to surrender his wealth, himself, and even his son to Allah’s decree. His uprightness was not a label but a complete state of trust and obedience.

**“and was not a polytheist”** means he did not associate partners with Allah. He rejected shirk and lived with iman, faith in Allah alone. The Makkan idolaters later claimed the religion of Ibrāhīm for themselves, but his way was the opposite of worshipping idols or assigning divine power to created things.

The verse also warns that people in every age can use the names of prophets and saints to defend later additions to religion. Over time, people may mistake those changes for the original teaching. When someone calls them back to pure monotheism, opponents may accuse that person of rejecting the religion of their ancestors. The claim of connection to Ibrāhīm is not enough. The true measure is following his tawḥīd and submission.

The Qur’an makes the same point in al-Baqarah: the claim that people must become Jews or Christians in order to be guided is rejected (2:135). Ibrāhīm’s faith precedes those identities and points to the worship of Allah alone.

### What does it mean to be a ḥanīf?

A ḥanīf turns away from idolatry and false belief toward worship of Allah alone. Ibrāhīm’s uprightness included steadfast faith, surrender, and refusal to trade truth for personal gain. He was neither Jew nor Christian, but a servant devoted to Allah.

### In short

- Ibrāhīm was not part of religions that arose after him.
- He was a Muslim and ḥanīf, upright in the worship of Allah alone.
- He rejected shirk and submitted fully to Allah.

<<<VERSE 3:68>>>
The verse identifies those with the closest claim to Ibrāhīm: “those who have the best claim to Abraham are his followers, this Prophet, and the believers” (3:68). A true connection is shown by following his religion of tawḥīd, not by merely claiming his name. Allah is the Protector of the believers.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Indeed, those who have the best claim to Abraham”** means those most entitled to say that they follow his way. The Jewish and Christian leaders claimed that Ibrāhīm belonged to them, but their religious forms came later. The stronger claim belongs to the people who follow his actual faith.

**“are his followers”** first includes those who followed Ibrāhīm during his own time and adhered to his worship of Allah alone. A person’s closeness to a prophet is measured by faith and obedience, not by ancestry or a later label.

**“this Prophet”** refers to Muhammad ﷺ. His message agrees with Ibrāhīm’s tawḥīd and restores the call to worship Allah alone. He has a stronger claim to Ibrāhīm than those who reject his message while claiming to follow Ibrāhīm.

**“and the believers”** refers to the Prophet’s community and those who follow its guidance. This includes the Muhājirūn, the Anṣār, and those who follow their example. The believers accept Ibrāhīm, Mūsā, ʿĪsā, and all Allah’s messengers without dividing them into rival groups.

Saʿīd bin Manṣūr records a report from Ibn Masʿūd that every prophet had a walī, a close supporter, from among the prophets. The Prophet ﷺ said that his walī among them was his father Ibrāhīm, the khalīl, intimate friend, of his Lord. This describes a spiritual bond through the shared message of tawḥīd.

Another report from Ibn ʿAbbās says that Jewish leaders told Muhammad ﷺ that they had a better claim to Ibrāhīm’s religion and accused him of envy. The verse answers that claim by defining closeness through following Ibrāhīm’s religion, not through an assertion of superiority.

A historical report then describes the wider struggle around the early Muslim community. Jaʿfar bin Abī Ṭālib and his companions migrated to Abyssinia. Muhammad ﷺ migrated to al-Madīnah, and the Battle of Badr followed. After losing people at Badr, Quraysh gathered at Dār al-Nadwah and planned to seek the return of the Muslim emigrants from the Negus. ʿAmr bin al-ʿĀṣ and ʿUmārah bin Abī Muʿayṭ were sent with gifts and tanned skins to Abyssinia. They tried to persuade the Negus that the emigrants followed a liar and should be returned. This account shows how the community’s claim to Ibrāhīm was tested by migration, persecution, and steadfastness.

**“And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe”** means that Allah is their walī, their Protector, Helper, and Preserver. The believers are entrusted with His religion and hold to the declaration of His oneness. Allah’s friendship brings aid, selection, nearness, and protection. The claim to Ibrāhīm is therefore joined to Allah’s care for those who follow the truth.

### Who has the strongest claim to Ibrāhīm?

The strongest claim belongs to those who follow Ibrāhīm’s religion of submission and tawḥīd. This includes Muhammad ﷺ and the believers who accept all Allah’s messengers. A name or inherited identity cannot replace faith and obedience.

### In short

- Ibrāhīm’s true followers, Muhammad ﷺ, and the believers have the best claim to him.
- The believers’ connection is through tawḥīd and following the messengers.
- Allah is their Protector, Helper, and Guardian.
