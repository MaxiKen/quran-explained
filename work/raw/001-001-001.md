The Qur'an opens with the words "In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful". This line, the basmalah, stands at the head of every surah except the ninth, it is the first thing a Muslim says before eating, before setting out, before beginning anything that matters, and it is the first line of the Opening of the Book that is recited in every unit of every prayer. The Companions began the written Qur'an with it. In one short sentence it does three things: it ties the act that follows to Allah, it names Him by the name that belongs to Him alone, and it describes Him by the two names of mercy before any other attribute is mentioned.

The verse is tied to a sentence that comes just before it in practice, the istiʿādhah or seeking of refuge from Shayṭān, which the Qur'an commands before every recitation. The rulings on both, the question of whether the basmalah is a verse of this surah, and the meaning of each of its words are explained below.

### Phrase by phrase

**"In the Name of"** translates bi-smi, which is two words joined: the preposition bi and the noun ism, "name". The preposition has several senses in Arabic, and three of them fit here at the same time: closeness or accompaniment, seeking help, and seeking blessing. So the phrase means "with the name of Allah", "with the help of the name of Allah" and "with the blessing of the name of Allah" all at once. On its own the sentence is incomplete, because the act being done "in the name of Allah" is left unspoken. Arabic grammar supplies a verb that fits the occasion, such as "I begin" or "I recite", and the fitting place for that unspoken verb is after the name, not before it, so that the beginning is made with Allah's name itself and nothing comes ahead of it. The preposition has to stand before the name, because that is how the language works, but even here the written Qur'an makes a small adjustment. In the copy prepared under ʿUthmān with the agreement of the Companions, the alif that would normally be written between the bi and the sīn of ism is dropped, so that the bi is joined straight on to the word "name" and the writing itself begins, in effect, with the name of Allah. In every other place where the same two words meet, as in "Read with the name of your Lord", the alif is kept. The dropping of it belongs to the basmalah alone.

**"Allah"** is the name of the Lord, the proper name of His Essence, the greatest and most comprehensive of His names. It has no plural and no dual, because He is One and has no partner, and no one else can be given it. Some scholars hold that it is the Greatest Name, al-Ism al-Aʿẓam, the name a hadith describes as carrying such blessing that a prayer made with it is granted, though reports differ as to which name that is. One reason for calling it the greatest is that all the other names are used to describe it. In the verses "He is Allah, besides whom there is no god, the Knower of the unseen and the seen, He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful. He is Allah, besides whom there is no god, the King, the Holy, the One free from defect, the Giver of security, the Watcher, the Almighty, the Compeller, the Supreme. He is Allah, the Creator, the Maker, the Giver of forms, to Him belong the Best Names" (59:22-24), a whole series of names is attached to "Allah" as its attributes. "The Most Beautiful Names belong to Allah, so call on Him by them" (7:180), and "Say: invoke Allah or invoke the Most Compassionate, by whatever name you invoke Him, to Him belong the Best Names" (17:110). Abū Hurayrah reported that the Prophet ﷺ said, "Allah has ninety-nine names, one hundred less one. Whoever counts them will enter Paradise." This is in both Ṣaḥīḥs, and the names themselves are listed in a hadith recorded by at-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah, with several differences between the two lists. In short, Allah is the name of the one Reality that holds in itself every attribute of perfection, the Creator and Sustainer, unique and without equal.

**"the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful"** translates ar-Raḥmān ar-Raḥīm. Both names come from the same root, raḥmah, mercy, and the pairing is a deliberate doubling: of all His names only these two are mentioned here, and both speak of mercy. Ar-Raḥmān carries more of the meaning of mercy than ar-Raḥīm, and Ibn Jarīr indicated that there is agreement on this. Al-Qurṭubī gave as the proof that the two names really are derived from "mercy" a hadith that at-Tirmidhī recorded and graded ṣaḥīḥ, in which ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf heard the Prophet ﷺ relate that Allah said, "I am ar-Raḥmān. I created the womb (raḥim) and gave it a name taken from My name. Whoever keeps it joined, I will keep him joined to Me, and whoever cuts it, I will cut him off." Al-Qurṭubī added that the Arabs rejected the name ar-Raḥmān only out of ignorance of Allah and His attributes.

What is the difference between the two? Several explanations were given. Abū ʿUbayd held that they mean the same thing, like the two Arabic words nadmān and nadīm for a companion. Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī said that ar-Raḥmān, which is Allah's alone, is the name that gathers up every kind of mercy He has, while ar-Raḥīm is the mercy that reaches the believers, as in the verse "He is ever Merciful (Raḥīm) to the believers" (33:43). Ibn ʿAbbās said they are two tender names, one of them more tender than the other. Al-ʿAzramī put it in a sentence that Ibn Jarīr recorded: "He is ar-Raḥmān with all creation and ar-Raḥīm with the believers." Those who explain it this way point out that when the Qur'an speaks of Allah rising over the Throne, the name it uses is ar-Raḥmān, "then He rose over the Throne, the Most Compassionate" (25:59) and "the Most Compassionate rose over the Throne" (20:5), to show that His mercy covers the whole of His creation, while it uses ar-Raḥīm for the believers in particular. So ar-Raḥmān is the broader name, the mercy that reaches all creatures in both worlds, and ar-Raḥīm is reserved for the believers. There is, all the same, a supplication that calls on Allah as "the Raḥmān and the Raḥīm of this world and the Hereafter". A simpler statement of the same idea is that ar-Raḥmān is the One whose mercy is shared by all, reaching the whole universe and whatever is still to be created, while ar-Raḥīm is the One whose mercy is perfect in every way a mercy can be. His mercy surrounds everything and embraces every living being, every blessing anyone has comes from it, and the believers receive the greatest share of it.

The two names also differ in who may carry them. Ar-Raḥmān is Allah's alone. "Say: invoke Allah or invoke ar-Raḥmān, by whatever name you invoke Him, to Him belong the Best Names" (17:110) puts the two names side by side as His, and "Ask those of Our Messengers whom We sent before you: did We ever appoint gods to be worshipped besides ar-Raḥmān?" (43:45) does the same. When Musaylimah the Liar styled himself the Raḥmān of Yamāmah, Allah exposed him and fixed the name "the Liar" to him so firmly that he is never mentioned without it, and he became a byword for lying among the settled people and the bedouin alike. Only he and those who followed his error ever called him by that name. It is not permissible to call any created being Raḥmān, since there cannot be anyone besides Allah whose mercy is all-embracing, and like the name "Allah" it has no dual and no plural. From this comes a practical ruling: those who shorten names like ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān or Faḍl ar-Raḥmān to plain "Raḥmān" are doing something that is not allowed and are committing a sin. Ar-Raḥīm, on the other hand, holds nothing that a creature cannot have, since one person can be perfectly merciful in dealing with another, and Allah Himself describes His Messenger ﷺ with it: "There has come to you a Messenger from among yourselves. It grieves him that you should suffer. He is anxious over you, and to the believers he is kind and merciful (raḥīm)" (9:128). He has done the same with other names of His, calling man "hearing and seeing" (76:2). So some of His names are shared with His creatures, and some, such as Allah, ar-Raḥmān, al-Khāliq the Creator and ar-Rāziq the Provider, belong to Him alone.

This explains the order of the three names. Allah began with the name that is exclusively His, then described it by ar-Raḥmān, which no one else may use and which is the softer and more general of the two names of mercy, and then by ar-Raḥīm. The most honoured names come first.

The fact that the only two attributes placed at the very opening of the Qur'an are both attributes of mercy carries a message of its own. It tells us that the heavens and the earth were created, and that the whole universe is kept in being, for no other reason than to display Allah's mercy. He had no need of any of it, and no one could compel Him to make it. It is His own mercy that called the whole order of things into existence and sustains it. The poet Rūmī put it in two lines of Persian whose sense is that there was nothing, neither our being nor any claim of ours to be, and it was Allah's mercy that heard what we had not yet said.

### Why does the Qur'an begin with a name?

Before Islam, in the Age of Ignorance, people opened whatever they did with the names of their idols and gods. The very first verse that Jibrīl (Gabriel) brought down to the Prophet ﷺ struck at that habit by commanding him to begin with the name of Allah: "Read with the name of your Lord." As-Suyūṭī said that the other revealed books also begin with "in the name of Allah". Other scholars held that the full formula, "in the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful", belongs to the Qur'an and to the community of Muhammad ﷺ alone. The two views agree with each other once it is said that all the revealed books share the trait of opening with Allah's name, while these exact words are peculiar to the Qur'an. Reports support this: the Prophet ﷺ used to open what he undertook with the words "bismika Allāhumma", "in Your name, O Allah", and when this verse was revealed he adopted its words instead, and from then on the practice was fixed by his command, his action or his approval. Al-Qurṭubī and Rūḥ al-Maʿānī both note this.

To begin a task in the name of Allah is to acknowledge that He is the Lord, the Being from whom every blessing comes and whose mercy pours continually over His creation, and to pray that He, in His infinite mercy, will come to one's help and bring the work to a good end. It is a person's admission that he is Allah's servant, and it carries with it the assurance of success.

Seen this way the basmalah gives the whole of a person's life a direction. The Qur'an and the hadiths repeat again and again that things are to be begun with Allah's name, and every time a Muslim does so he renews his side of the covenant with Allah: that nothing he does, not even his own existence, comes into being without Allah's will and help. That turns the ordinary business of living into worship. A disbeliever eats and drinks exactly as a Muslim does, but when the Muslim says bismillāh before he eats he is affirming that it was not in his power to obtain this one mouthful of food, which passed through countless stages between the sowing of a seed and the harvesting of the grain, and which needed the labour of the wind, the rain, the sun, the heavens and the earth and a thousand people, and that Allah alone brought it to him by carrying it through all of those stages. A disbeliever sleeps, wakes and goes about his day as a Muslim does, but the Muslim mentions Allah's name as he lies down and as he rises, and so renews his relationship with Him, and his worldly needs and errands take on the character of remembrance and are counted as acts of worship. When he says bismillāh as he climbs into a vehicle he is testifying that man had no power to produce it or to bring it to him, that only the order Allah created brought together from the corners of the world the wood, the steel and the other metals that went into it, the mechanics who shaped them and the driver, and placed this whole army of Allah's creatures at his service for the price of a few coins, coins which he did not create either, since Allah provided the elaborate means of earning them. An act that costs no time and no energy brings an immense return. The basmalah is the real philosopher's stone, one that turns not copper but mere dust into the purest gold, the profane into the sacred. Praise be to Allah for the religion of Islam and its teachings.

### Where is the basmalah said?

Saying bismillāh is recommended before starting any action. The Prophet ﷺ said that no important work receives Allah's blessing unless it is begun with His name. Another hadith extends it even to closing the door of one's house at night, putting out the lamp and covering a vessel. The Qur'an and the hadiths instruct that it be said when eating, when drinking, when performing wuḍūʾ, when mounting an animal or vehicle and when getting down from it, and al-Qurṭubī gathers these instructions together. Some of the particular occasions:

- Before a sermon.
- Before entering the place where one relieves oneself, about which there is a hadith.
- At the beginning of wuḍūʾ. Abū Hurayrah, Saʿīd ibn Zayd and Abū Saʿīd all related from the Prophet ﷺ, "There is no wuḍūʾ for the one who does not mention Allah's name over it," recorded by Aḥmad and the compilers of the Sunan, and it is a ḥasan hadith.
- Before eating. Muslim recorded that the Prophet ﷺ said to ʿUmar ibn Abī Salamah, then a boy in his care, "Say bismillāh, eat with your right hand, and eat from what is nearest to you." Some scholars held that the basmalah before eating is obligatory.
- Before marital relations. Ibn ʿAbbās reported, in a hadith in both Ṣaḥīḥs, that the Prophet ﷺ said that if a man says when he comes to his wife, "In the name of Allah. O Allah, keep Shayṭān away from us and keep Shayṭān away from what You grant us," and a child is decreed for them, Shayṭān will never be able to harm that child.

A hadith in the Musnad of Aḥmad shows what the name does. A man riding behind the Prophet ﷺ said that the animal stumbled and he said, "May Shayṭān perish." The Prophet ﷺ told him, "Do not say 'may Shayṭān perish', for when you say that he swells up and says, 'By my strength I threw him.' When you say bismillāh he shrinks until he is as small as a fly." An-Nasāʾī, in his book on the day and the night, and Ibn Mardawayh in his tafsīr record it from Usāmah ibn ʿUmayr, who said he was the one riding behind the Prophet ﷺ, and in that wording the Prophet ﷺ said that cursing Shayṭān makes him grow as big as a house, while saying bismillāh makes him as small as a fly. That is the blessing in the words.

Because the basmalah is a verse of the Qur'an, a part of a verse in Sūrat an-Naml and a full verse wherever it stands between two surahs, it is given the same respect as the rest of the Qur'an. It is not permissible to touch it without wuḍūʾ, and a person in a state of major ritual impurity, after intercourse, during menstruation or after childbirth, may not recite it as Qur'an until he or she has bathed. Such a person may, however, still say it as a prayer before beginning something, such as eating or drinking, in any condition.

### Is the basmalah a verse of this surah?

All Muslims agree that "In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful" is part of the Qur'an, since it occurs as part of a verse in Sūrat an-Naml, the twenty-seventh surah, in Sulaymān's (Solomon's) letter, and all agree that it is written at the head of every surah except Sūrat at-Tawbah, also called Barāʾah. What the scholars disagree about is whether it is a verse of al-Fātiḥah itself, a separate verse in front of every surah, or a verse of every surah it heads.

Three positions were held:

1. It is a verse of every surah except Barāʾah. This is attributed among the Companions to Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn az-Zubayr, Abū Hurayrah and ʿAlī, among their Successors to ʿAṭāʾ, Ṭāwūs, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Makḥūl and az-Zuhrī, and among the Imams it is the position of ash-Shāfiʿī, of Isḥāq ibn Rāhawayh, of Abū ʿUbayd al-Qāsim ibn Sallām, of ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Mubārak and, in one of the two reports from him, of Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal. Most of the reciters of Kūfah and a group of Companions and Successors counted it as the first of the seven verses of al-Fātiḥah. A hadith recorded by Ibn Khuzaymah supports this, and ad-Dāraquṭnī recorded a hadith from Abū Hurayrah that backs it up, and similar statements are attributed to ʿAlī, Ibn ʿAbbās and others.
2. It is not a verse of al-Fātiḥah or of any other surah. This is the view of Mālik, Abū Ḥanīfah and their followers. In Abū Ḥanīfah's formulation it is a complete and independent verse of the Qur'an in its own right, revealed to be placed at the beginning of every surah to separate and mark off one surah from the next, and it is part of a surah only in an-Naml.
3. Dāwūd said that it is a separate verse at the beginning of every surah without being part of the surah itself, and this position too is attributed to Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal.

Umm Salamah's description of the Prophet's ﷺ recitation bears on the question. She said that he used to mark off each verse as he recited, slowly and distinctly, letter by letter: "In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful. All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds. The Most Compassionate, Most Merciful. Master of the Day of Judgment." Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, Ibn Ḥibbān and al-Ḥākim recorded it, and ad-Dāraquṭnī graded its chain ṣaḥīḥ. A group of scholars recite the surah this way, pausing after the basmalah as a verse of its own, while others run the basmalah straight on into "All praise is for Allah".

### How is it used when reciting and praying?

One begins the recitation of the Qur'an with both sentences, "I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān, the accursed" and then "In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful", and during the recitation the basmalah is repeated, without the seeking of refuge, at the start of each new surah. The one exception is Barāʾah. When a reciter reaches it in the course of his reading he does not say the basmalah before it, but if he begins his recitation with that surah he says both the istiʿādhah and the basmalah. This is set out in the ʿĀlamgīrī from al-Muḥīṭ.

In the prayer:

- It is Sunnah to recite the basmalah after the istiʿādhah at the very beginning of the first rakʿah, and there is consensus that it is recited at the start of each following rakʿah as well. This is unanimously regarded as Sunnah, and some narrations describe reciting it at the start of every rakʿah as wājib, necessary.
- Whether it is said aloud or quietly is disputed. Abū Ḥanīfah and some other Imams prefer that it be said quietly. The whole question is set out in the next section.
- As for the surah that is read after al-Fātiḥah, the basmalah is not recited before it, whether the prayer is one recited aloud or one recited silently, because the practice is not reported from the Prophet ﷺ or from any of the first four caliphs. Sharḥ al-Munyah gives this as the view of Abū Ḥanīfah and Abū Yūsuf, and it is the view preferred in Sharḥ al-Munyah, ad-Durr al-Mukhtār, al-Burhān and other works. Imam Muḥammad, however, considered it preferable to recite the basmalah in that place when the prayer is a silent one, some reports attribute the same view to Abū Ḥanīfah himself, ash-Shāmī quoted a number of jurists in its favour, and it is the view adopted in Bahishti Zewar by Maulana Thanavi. Everyone agrees that reciting it in this position is not makrūh.

### Should the basmalah be recited aloud in prayer?

Those who do not count the basmalah as part of al-Fātiḥah naturally hold that it is not recited aloud. Those who do count it as a verse of every surah divided into two camps.

Ash-Shāfiʿī held that it is recited aloud together with al-Fātiḥah, and this was the opinion of many Companions, Successors and later Imams. Among the Companions it is the view of Abū Hurayrah, Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn ʿAbbās and Muʿāwiyah, and Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr and al-Bayhaqī add ʿUmar and ʿAlī. Al-Khaṭīb reported it from all four caliphs, though the report from them is contradicted. A long list of Successors held it too. From the family of the Prophet ﷺ and the Companions: ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn and his son Muḥammad, ʿAlī ibn ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās and his son Muḥammad, Sālim, and Nāfiʿ the freedman of Ibn ʿUmar. From the great teachers of tafsīr and law: Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, ʿAṭāʾ, Ṭāwūs, az-Zuhrī, Makḥūl, Ibn Sīrīn and ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz. And besides them Abū Wāʾil, Abū Qilābah, Abū ash-Shaʿthāʾ, Zayd ibn Aslam, Ḥabīb ibn Abī Thābit, al-Azraq ibn Qays, Muḥammad ibn al-Munkadir, Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī, ʿAbdullāh ibn Maʿqil ibn Muqarrin and Abū Bakr ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAmr ibn Ḥazm. Al-Bayhaqī adds ʿAbdullāh ibn Ṣafwān and Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyyah to the list, and Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr adds ʿAmr ibn Dīnār.

Their reasoning is simple: if the basmalah is part of al-Fātiḥah, it is recited aloud like the rest of al-Fātiḥah. They also point to reports:

- Abū Hurayrah once led a prayer and recited the basmalah aloud, and when he had finished he said, "Of all of you, my prayer is the one most like the prayer of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ." It is recorded in the Sunan of an-Nasāʾī, in the Ṣaḥīḥs of Ibn Khuzaymah and of Ibn Ḥibbān, and in al-Ḥākim's Mustadrak, and ad-Dāraquṭnī, al-Khaṭīb and al-Bayhaqī graded it ṣaḥīḥ.
- Anas ibn Mālik, asked about the Prophet's ﷺ recitation, said it was unhurried, and demonstrated by reciting "bismillāhi ar-Raḥmāni ar-Raḥīm" while drawing out the words. Al-Bukhārī recorded it.
- Umm Salamah's hadith above, in which the Prophet ﷺ recited the basmalah as a distinct verse at the head of the surah.
- Muʿāwiyah once led the prayer in Madinah and did not recite the basmalah, and the Muhājirūn who were present criticised him for it. When he led the next prayer he recited it aloud. Ash-Shāfiʿī and al-Ḥākim recorded this.

These reports are held to be sufficient proof for reciting it aloud. The counter-evidence, and the technical weighing of the narrations and their weaknesses, is a long discussion that need not be gone into here.

Others held that the basmalah is not recited aloud in the prayer. This was the established practice of the four caliphs and of ʿAbdullāh ibn Mughaffal, and the view of a number of the Successors and later scholars, and it is the position of Abū Ḥanīfah, ath-Thawrī and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal. Mālik went further and held that it is not recited at all in the obligatory prayer, neither aloud nor quietly. This group relies on what Muslim recorded from ʿĀʾishah, that the Prophet ﷺ used to begin the prayer with the takbīr and then with "All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds", and on Anas ibn Mālik's statement in both Ṣaḥīḥs, "I prayed behind the Prophet ﷺ, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar and ʿUthmān, and they used to begin with 'All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds'," to which Muslim adds, "and they did not mention 'In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful' either at the beginning of the recitation or at its end." The Sunan collections record something similar from ʿAbdullāh ibn Mughaffal.

These are the views of the respected Imams, and for all their difference they agree on one thing: the prayer of the one who recites the basmalah aloud and the prayer of the one who recites it quietly are both valid. All favour is from Allah.

### Seeking refuge before the basmalah

Allah says, "So when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān, the accursed. Truly he has no power over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord. His power is only over those who take him as a friend and those who associate others with Allah" (16:98-100). Because of this verse the Muslims agree that it is Sunnah to say the taʿawwudh, "I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān, the accursed", before reciting the Qur'an, whether in the prayer or outside it, as Sharḥ al-Munyah states. The taʿawwudh belongs to recitation in particular. Other tasks are begun with the basmalah alone, and saying the taʿawwudh before them is not Sunnah, as the ʿĀlamgīrī notes in its chapter on disliked things.

"When you recite" means before you recite, in the same way that "when you intend to offer the prayer, wash your faces and your hands" (5:6) means before you stand to pray, as the hadiths show. The Prophet's ﷺ practice confirms it. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī said that when the Prophet ﷺ stood for the night prayer he would say the takbīr, then "All praise is Yours, O Allah, and all thanks. Blessed is Your name, exalted is Your majesty, and there is no god but You," then "There is no god but Allah" three times, and then, "I seek refuge with Allah, the Hearing, the Knowing, from Shayṭān the accursed, from his hamz, his nafkh and his nafth." Aḥmad recorded it, and so did the four compilers of the Sunan, and at-Tirmidhī called it the best known hadith on the subject. Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim related from his father that when the Prophet ﷺ began the prayer he would say three times "Allah is the Greatest, truly the Greatest," three times "All praise is for Allah, abundantly," three times "All praise is for Allah morning and evening," and then "O Allah, I seek refuge with You from Shayṭān the accursed, from his hamz, his nafkh and his nafth." Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah recorded it, and ʿAmr, one of its narrators, explained the three words: hamz is choking, nafkh is pride, and nafth is poetry. Ibn Mājah also recorded the same supplication from Ibn Masʿūd, with the explanation that hamz is death, nafkh is pride and nafth is poetry.

The same words are a remedy for anger. Ubayy ibn Kaʿb said that two men were arguing in the presence of the Prophet ﷺ and one of them grew so angry that his nose swelled. The Prophet ﷺ said, "I know some words which, if he said them, would take away what he feels: 'I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān the accursed.'" Abū Yaʿlā recorded it in his Musnad, and an-Nasāʾī in his book on the day and the night. Sulaymān ibn Ṣurad told of a similar scene in al-Bukhārī's Ṣaḥīḥ: two men quarrelled in front of the Prophet ﷺ while the Companions sat with him, one began cursing the other with his face red with rage, and the Prophet ﷺ said, "I know a sentence which, if he said it, what he feels would leave him: 'I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān the accursed.'" The people told the man what the Prophet ﷺ had said, and he answered, "Am I mad?" Muslim, Abū Dāwūd and an-Nasāʾī also recorded it. There are many other hadiths on seeking refuge, which are gathered in the books of supplications and of the virtues of good deeds.

Why is this said before reading Allah's words and not before anything else? The istiʿādhah means: "I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān the accursed, so that he is kept from harming my religion or my worldly affairs, from holding me back from what I have been commanded, and from luring me into what I have been forbidden." Only Allah can keep Shayṭān's evil away from a human being. That is why Allah told us to be gentle and lenient with the human enemy, whose nature may soften under kindness until he turns from his wrong and even becomes a friend, but told us to seek refuge with Him from the devil, who accepts no bribe, is not touched by kindness, and wants nothing but the ruin of the children of Adam out of the hatred he has borne their father from the start. Only the One who created him can stop him. Three passages of the Qur'an, and only three, join these two commands together: "Show forgiveness, enjoin what is good and turn away from the ignorant. And if an evil whisper comes to you from Shayṭān, seek refuge with Allah. He is Hearing, Knowing" (7:199-200), where the first sentence is about dealing with people and the second about the devil. "Repel evil with what is better. We know best what they say. And say: My Lord, I seek refuge with You from the whisperings of the devils, and I seek refuge with You, my Lord, lest they come near me" (23:96-98). And "The good deed and the evil deed are not equal. Repel evil with what is better, and the one between whom and you there was enmity will become as though he were a close friend. None is granted this except those who are patient, and none is granted it except the owner of a great portion. And if an evil whisper from Shayṭān tries to turn you away, seek refuge with Allah. He is the Hearing, the Knowing" (41:34-36). The Qur'an is full of warnings about this enemy: "O children of Adam, do not let Shayṭān deceive you as he brought your parents out of Paradise" (7:27), "Shayṭān is an enemy to you, so treat him as an enemy. He only invites his followers so that they may be among the people of the Blaze" (35:6), and "Will you take him and his offspring as protectors instead of Me, when they are enemies to you? What an evil exchange for the wrongdoers" (18:50). He assured Adam that he was giving him sincere advice and was lying, so how will he deal with us after swearing, "By Your might, I will surely mislead them all, except Your chosen servants among them" (38:82-83)?

The istiʿādhah has its own virtues. It cleanses the mouth of the idle and foul talk it has been busy with and prepares it to recite the speech of Allah. It is a seeking of Allah's help and an acknowledgment that He is able to do all things. It is the servant's admission of his own weakness and helplessness before the enemy within, whom only Allah, who created him, can drive back and defeat. "Truly over My servants you have no authority, and your Lord is sufficient as a Guardian" (17:65). The contrast between the two enemies is sharp: a believer killed by the human enemy dies a martyr, while the one who falls to the inner enemy becomes a brigand, and a believer defeated by the outward enemy earns a reward, while one defeated by Shayṭān earns a sin and goes astray. Since Shayṭān sees a person from where the person cannot see him, it is fitting that the believer should take refuge from him with the One whom Shayṭān cannot see. Seeking refuge is a way of drawing near to Allah and a shelter with Him from the evil of every evil creature.

Is it required? The majority of scholars hold that the istiʿādhah, in the prayer and before reciting, is recommended rather than obligatory, so that leaving it out is not a sin. Ar-Rāzī recorded from ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ that it is obligatory in the prayer and whenever one reads the Qur'an, and supported him with the apparent sense of the command "seek refuge", with the Prophet's ﷺ constant practice of it, and with the rule that whatever is needed to carry out a religious requirement is itself required, since warding off Shayṭān's evil is necessary. Saying "I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān the accursed" is enough to fulfil it.

### What do "Shayṭān" and "the accursed" mean?

The word Shayṭān comes from shaṭana, to be far away. The devil's nature is remote from that of mankind, and his wicked ways are far from every kind of goodness. Some said it comes instead from shāṭa, to burn, because he was created from fire, and some accepted both while preferring the first. Sībawayh settled it from usage: the Arabs say of a man that he has "tashayṭana" when he behaves like a devil, and if the word came from "burning" they would have said "tashayyaṭa". So the name means the far one, and it is given to every rebel among the jinn and among people. "So We appointed for every Prophet enemies, devils from among mankind and the jinn, inspiring one another with adorned speech as a delusion" (6:112). In Aḥmad's Musnad, Abū Dharr relates that the Prophet ﷺ said, "O Abū Dharr, seek refuge with Allah from the devils of mankind and of the jinn." Abū Dharr asked, "Are there human devils?" and he said, "Yes." In Muslim's Ṣaḥīḥ, Abū Dharr relates that the Prophet ﷺ said that a woman, a donkey or a black dog passing in front of a person who prays without a barrier interrupts the prayer, and when he asked what made the black dog different from a red or yellow one, the Prophet ﷺ said, "The black dog is a devil." Ibn Jarīr recorded with an authentic chain that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb once rode an animal that began to step proudly, and however much he struck it, it kept up its swagger, so he got down and said, "By Allah, you have carried me on a Shayṭān. I did not get off it until I felt something strange in my heart."

Ar-rajīm, "the accursed", means the one driven out, expelled from every kind of goodness, and the Qur'an uses the word of the devils pelted and driven from the heavens: "We have adorned the nearest heaven with lamps and made them missiles to drive away the devils" (67:5), "We have adorned the nearest heaven with the stars, and as a guard against every rebellious devil. They cannot listen to the highest assembly, for they are pelted from every side, driven out, and theirs is a lasting torment, except the one who snatches a fragment, and then a piercing flame pursues him" (37:6-10), and "We have set the great stars in the heaven and beautified it for the beholders, and We have guarded it from every accursed devil, except the one who steals a hearing, and a clear flame pursues him" (15:16-18). There are several verses like these. It was also said that rajīm means the one who throws, because the devil throws doubts and evil thoughts into people's hearts, but the first meaning is the better known and the more accurate.

### What the letters say to the heart

Those who read the Qur'an for its allusions, in the taste of the people of recognition, found a message in the very letters of bi-smi. The bāʾ points to the splendour (bahāʾ) of Allah's Oneness, the sīn to the brilliance (sanāʾ) of the Self-Sufficient, and the mīm to the kingship (mulk) of His divinity: a splendour that stands by itself and is eternal and full of majesty, a brilliance that sustains itself and is generous and full of beauty, a kingship that lasts forever and knows no decline or annihilation. His splendour steals the heart, His brilliance increases love, and His majesty runs ahead of everything beautiful, so that in the language of the poets Venus rejoices to hear His music and the sun is jealous when it sees His beauty. Read another way, the bāʾ is His kindness (birr) to His servants, the sīn His secret (sirr) shared with His friends, and the mīm His favour (minnah) to those who yearn for Him. Without His kindness, how could the servant make himself ready for His secret? Without His favour, how could the servant reach union with Him or find a place at the threshold of His majesty? Without a love that had no beginning, how could the servant be at home with Him without end? How would water and clay ever have dared to love Him, had He not chosen them with His gentleness before time began? Love, the friends of Allah say, is His very Essence, and remembering His description drives away the sorrow of the sorrowful. This world is good only through His name, the next world only through His pardon, and the Garden only through the sight of Him. If it were not for His message and His name here, how could this world be a home for the servant? If it were not for His pardon and generosity there, the servant's case would be hard. And without the sight of Him in Paradise, the sight that lights up the heart, what would make a poor man happy? One of the masters of the Path prayed, "O Allah, we see by Your signs, we live in knowing You, we flourish by Your name, we are happy in remembering You, and we rejoice in finding You." He went on that it is they who are drunk with love from His cup and they who are caught in His snare, and the lovers' verses say that since the sermon of His love was read in the servant's name, the whole world seems to follow the servant's heart.

It has been said that ism, name, comes from simah, a brand or mark. Whoever says "in the name of Allah" receives that stamp and is marked with that brand, and the one who wears the king's brand is safe from the police by day and the patrols by night. Whoever finds a name finds it at His threshold, so be one of His and stop worrying about anyone else. ʿAlī ibn Mūsā ar-Riḍā said, "When the servant says 'in the name of Allah', its meaning is: I have branded myself with the brand of my Lord." On which the lover adds: O Lord, I wear Your brand and am happy in it, but I grieve over my own existence. O Generous One, take my being out of my way, so that Your being may set all my affairs right. The master of the Path said, "O Allah, when Your light lit the lamp of recognition, my heart grew. When Your testimony became my spokesman, my voice grew. When Your nearness lit the lamp of ecstasy, my aspiration grew. When Your desire put my work in order, my effort grew. When Your being set my affairs right, my being grew. O Allah, what have I ever seen from my own being but trial and trouble, while from Your being everything is gift and loyalty? O You who are manifest in kindness and plain in generosity, count what I have done as not done, and do what is fitting for You."

Someone may ask: the Book and the Sunnah contain many names of Allah, all of them great, eternal, pure and beautiful, so what is the wisdom in opening the mighty Qur'an with these three and no others? One answer given is that He chose these three and stopped at them so that His servants would find it easy to call on Him by His names without any loss of reward, since He knew they could never hold so many names in their memory.

### In short

- The basmalah means "with the name of Allah, with His help and with His blessing, I begin." Its three names are placed in order of honour: Allah, the name of His Essence that belongs to Him alone, then ar-Raḥmān, the all-embracing mercy that no creature may be called by, then ar-Raḥīm, the mercy that reaches the believers and that a merciful person can share.
- Islam put Allah's name where the pagans had put the names of their gods. Said before eating, drinking, travelling, wuḍūʾ, intimacy and every important act, it turns daily life into worship and shrinks Shayṭān to the size of a fly.
- It is agreed to be part of the Qur'an and written before every surah except at-Tawbah. Whether it is a verse of al-Fātiḥah was disputed: ash-Shāfiʿī, Aḥmad in one report and many Companions and Successors said yes, Mālik and Abū Ḥanīfah said it is a separate verse of division. Whether to say it aloud in prayer was disputed in the same way, and the prayer is valid either way.
- Before reciting the Qur'an one first says "I seek refuge with Allah from Shayṭān, the accursed," a Sunnah by consensus that most scholars hold is recommended rather than obligatory. It clears the mouth for Allah's words, confesses one's weakness before an enemy who accepts no kindness, and is also the cure for anger.
- Shayṭān means "the far one", far from every good, and rajīm means the one driven out. The two names of mercy at the head of the Qur'an announce that mercy, not need, is the reason the universe exists.
