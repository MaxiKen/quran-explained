Āl ʿImrān, the third surah of the Qur’an, was revealed in Madinah and has 200 verses. Its name means “the family of Imran,” and one of its major passages tells of Maryam (Mary), her family, and the birth and mission of ʿĪsā (Jesus). The surah brings questions of belief into conversation with history and public life. It calls people to worship Allah alone, recognize the continuity of revelation, and remain faithful when belief is tested by argument, prosperity, conflict, or loss.

### How it follows Al-Baqarah

Al-Fatihah ends with a prayer for guidance. Al-Baqarah answers by presenting the Qur’an as guidance, then sets out teachings and duties and describes opposition to the believers. Its closing prayer asks Allah for help against those who reject faith. Āl ʿImrān continues that concern. It addresses how believers should engage with those who reject the message and describes striving for Allah’s cause in word and action. The two surahs thus connect the request for guidance with the work of holding to it and calling others toward it.

### When it was revealed

The surah is Madinan. The opening eighty-three verses are linked in the material to a delegation from Najran that came to Madinah in the ninth year after the Hijrah, identified there as 632 CE. The later passage about the call to settle a dispute concerning ʿĪsā is connected with that setting (3:61). The surah’s discussion of earlier prophets and revelation also places its message within a long history of calls to worship Allah alone.

### What the surah covers

The opening establishes Allah’s oneness, knowledge, power, and authority, then distinguishes between verses whose meaning is clear and those that call for humility rather than speculation. It warns against rejecting revelation and against mistaking worldly wealth or status for lasting success. It recalls past communities and the consequences of denying messengers, while presenting divine support and accountability as central lessons of history.

A substantial part of the surah tells the story of Maryam and her family, including her mother’s dedication, the care of Zakariyya, and the announcement and birth of Yahya (John) and ʿĪsā. It describes ʿĪsā’s mission, signs, disciples, and deliverance from a plot against him. These passages challenge claims about his status by comparing him with Adam and inviting a fair resolution of disagreement. The surah also calls the People of the Book and the wider community to common ground: worship Allah alone and do not give partners to Him. It affirms the shared message of the prophets, including Ibrāhīm (Abraham), and the duty to believe in all of them.

The surah then turns to the believers’ own conduct. It teaches unity, warns against harmful alliances and influences, calls for generosity and justice, and sets out the duty of pilgrimage. Its extended account of the battle of Uhud faces the pain of setback directly. It considers the choices and failures that led to loss, the Prophet’s leadership, forgiveness, steadfastness, and the honor given to those killed in Allah’s cause. Defeat is not treated as proof that faith has failed. Rather, hardship tests and distinguishes people, while final judgment belongs to Allah.

In its closing movement, the surah points to signs in creation and portrays people who remember Allah and reflect on the heavens and earth. Their reflection leads to prayer for forgiveness, protection, and a good end. Allah’s answer promises that no sincere work is lost, whether done by a man or a woman. The surah ends by urging believers to endure, persevere, remain steadfast, and be mindful of Allah.

### The surah’s central message

The material presents the oneness of Allah as the central point shared by the prophets. It describes a line of messengers, from Adam, Nūḥ (Noah), Ibrāhīm, Ismāʿīl (Ishmael), Isḥāq (Isaac), Yaʿqūb (Jacob), Mūsā (Moses), Hārūn (Aaron), and ʿĪsā to Muhammad ﷺ, all calling people to the same truth. The material reports that 124,000 prophets came in different times and places. Their agreement is presented as evidence for the message they carried, while the Qur’an is described as the criterion that distinguishes truth from falsehood. The surah’s practical call follows from this belief: hold firmly to Allah, act with integrity, and meet trial with patience and trust.

### In short

- Āl ʿImrān follows Al-Baqarah by carrying the request for guidance into the challenge of living and defending faith.
- Its central belief is the oneness of Allah and the shared call of the prophets.
- It addresses revelation, the family of Imran, ʿĪsā, relations with other communities, and the trials at Uhud.
- It closes by joining reflection, prayer, perseverance, and mindfulness of Allah.
