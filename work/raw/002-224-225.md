<<<VERSE 2:224>>>
An oath must not turn Allah's name into a barrier against obedience: "Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse for not doing good". Someone who has sworn to withhold kindness or refuse reconciliation must not persist merely to preserve the oath. The right course is to do the good and make kaffarah, the compensation required for breaking a binding oath.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse"** An oath invokes Allah as witness. That sacred name must not become a justification for stubbornness or harm. Frequent swearing can create these obstacles, as people put themselves under promises that conflict with better conduct.

**"for not doing good,"** Kindness and righteous action must not be abandoned because someone has sworn against them. An oath cannot transform refusing a good deed into a virtue. The deed should be done and the oath compensated for.

**"not guarding against evil,"** Respect for Allah includes avoiding the breaking of family ties. A person must not claim that honouring an angry promise requires continuing what Allah disapproves of.

**"or not making peace between people."** An earlier oath is no excuse for declining to settle a dispute. Restoring relationships is itself an act of obedience. Swearing not to help cannot give the refusal a sacred character.

**"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing"** Allah hears the oath and knows the intention behind it. He also knows the person's circumstances, subsequent actions and the compensation that releases the person from the oath. Invoking His name cannot hide an unwillingness to do good.

### Whose family dispute is connected with the verse?

One account concerns 'Abdullah Ibn Rawahah and his sister's husband, Bashir ibn al-Nu'man. Al-Kalbi relates that Ibn Rawahah had sworn not to visit him, speak to him or bring about reconciliation between the couple. He argued that because he had sworn by Allah, failing to carry out the oath would be forbidden. The verse warned against that reasoning.

Another description includes his withholding kindness from both his sister and her husband. The answer is the same: return to the better action and make amends for the oath. The obligation to respect Allah's name does not mean using that name to sustain estrangement.

Anger and obstinacy can lead people to make such promises. They then decline an invitation to help by saying that an oath prevents them. Such oaths were common among the Arabs. Refusing kindness is already wrong. Attaching the name of the merciful Lord to that refusal makes it worse. Religious language must not become protection for a grudge.

### What if keeping an oath means continuing harm?

The majority view is that an oath against good should be broken, compensation made and the good performed. Ibn 'Abbas explains the verse in this way. The explanation is also given from numerous early authorities. These include Masruq and Ash-Sha'bi, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i and Mujahid, Tawus and Sa'id bin Jubayr, 'Ata' and 'Ikrimah, Makhul and Az-Zuhri. Al-Hasan, Qatadah, Muqatil bin Hayyan, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Ad-Dahhak, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani and As-Suddi are also named with that view.

The principle has support in prophetic teaching. Ahmad and Muslim record a warning that persisting in an oath that harms relatives is more sinful than breaking it and paying the required compensation. The outward appearance of keeping one's word must not conceal the greater offence of continuing a wrongful family rupture.

Abu Musa Al-Ash'ari relates in the Two Sahihs that the Prophet ﷺ would choose a better action when it became clear after he had made an oath, with Allah's willing, rather than allow the earlier oath to prevent it. Muslim also records from Abu Hurayrah an instruction to make compensation and perform the better deed when an oath stands in its way.

The same moral direction appears in (24:22), where people with means are told not to swear off giving help and are urged towards pardon. The distinction between casual and deliberate oaths is further addressed in (5:89). Compensation respects the seriousness of a binding oath without requiring continued wrongdoing.

### What does this require of religious speech?

The mention of Allah should be kept above trivial worldly bargaining. An inward warning pictures misuse of His name as a net cast to catch worldly debris. Sacred speech is not a tool for securing petty advantage, defending resentment or escaping responsibility.

The command therefore concerns both conduct and the way conduct is defended. Do not refuse righteousness, and do not make Allah's name the reason for refusing it. His hearing and knowledge reach beyond the claim of loyalty to an oath to the purpose that claim actually serves.

### In short

- An oath must not prevent kindness, mindfulness of Allah or reconciliation.
- If a binding oath stands against a better deed, do the better deed and make compensation.
- Keeping an oath that harms family ties can be worse than breaking it in the prescribed way.
- Allah's name is not a shield for anger or a tool for worldly advantage.

<<<VERSE 2:225>>>
The verse distinguishes an unintended expression from a chosen commitment: "Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths, but for what you intended in your hearts." A habitual phrase that slips from the tongue is not treated like a deliberate oath or a knowingly false statement. Allah judges what the heart has actually undertaken. He forgives slips and does not rush to punish.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths,"** Laghw means an ineffectual or unintended oath. A person may say "No, by Allah" or "Yes, by Allah" in ordinary conversation without intending to bind himself. Such speech carries neither sin nor the duty to make compensation when it is genuinely unintentional.

**"but for what you intended in your hearts."** The contrast is with a real intention, deliberate falsehood or consciously assumed obligation. Ibn 'Abbas and Mujahid explain it as swearing while knowing that the claim is false. Mujahid also connects it to the deliberate oaths discussed in (5:89).

**"And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing"** Allah forgives unintended slips. His forbearance means that even where punishment is deserved, He does not hasten it. The closing words do not erase intention. They explain why accidental words are not handled as though the heart had knowingly chosen them.

### What kinds of speech count as unintended?

'A'ishah describes the ordinary phrases people use at home, such as answering a question with a casual "by Allah". Abu Dawud records through 'Ata' her report of the Prophet's ﷺ explanation. Buying, selling and everyday conversation can contain such expressions without any intention to establish an oath.

Another explanation includes a statement about the past that turns out false even though the speaker honestly thought it true. Someone might swear that a particular person has arrived because that is what the available information suggests, while in fact the person has not arrived. The error does not become deliberate lying simply because the words included an oath.

A further case is a verbal slip about the future. A person intends to say something else, but an oath comes out mistakenly. These cases share the absence of a knowingly false or binding intention. They are not sins for which the person is condemned in the Hereafter, and compensation is not required for them.

Ibn Abu Hatim also transmits explanations from Ibn 'Abbas that include an oath made in anger and an oath attempting to forbid something Allah has permitted. The latter is said not to require compensation, and Sa'id bin Jubayr gives a similar view. These are reported explanations of laghw. They must be kept distinct from the practical case of a deliberately binding oath that is broken.

### Can an old habit of speech survive conversion?

Abu Hurayrah reports in the Two Sahihs that the Prophet ﷺ instructed someone who inadvertently swore by Al-Lat and Al-'Uzza to affirm that worship belongs to Allah alone. These were idols by which some new Muslims had previously been accustomed to swear. The old words could slip out even after belief had changed.

The instructed response deliberately affirms the oneness of Allah. The statement of sincere devotion counters the expression associated with shirk, assigning partners to Him. The habitual slip is not treated as though it expresses a renewed, intended allegiance to the idols. Yet the tongue is trained by consciously saying what agrees with faith.

### What is the difference between sin and compensation?

Not every sinful oath is described as carrying the same legal consequence. Ghamus means a knowingly false oath, or perjury. It is a sin because the speaker knows the claim is untrue. In the Hanafi view given here, it does not require kaffarah, the specified compensation for breaking an oath. That absence of compensation does not mean the perjury is innocent.

Laghw can also be used in a wider legal sense for an oath that carries no compensation. Under that usage even ghamus may fall within the category, despite being sinful. It is therefore essential to distinguish two questions: was there a sin, and is this the kind of oath for which a specified compensation is required?

A mun'aqidah oath is an established, deliberately binding oath about doing or not doing something. Acting against it requires compensation. The innocent slip and deliberate perjury discussed above are not treated as this same category. The verse's contrast between the tongue and heart must not be reduced to the mistaken rule that every oath without compensation is free of wrongdoing.

Another explanation of idle oaths concerns swearing to commit a transgression. If the person abandons the sinful act and makes amends for the oath, Allah does not take him to task for carrying out the transgression. Persisting in it is a different matter. This keeps the warning of the preceding verse in view: the oath cannot make a forbidden course obligatory.

### What did 'Umar say about an angry family oath?

Sa'id bin Musayyib relates a case of two Ansari brothers who inherited property. One asked for the inheritance to be divided. The other angrily said that if the request were repeated, all his property would be spent on the door of the Ka'bah.

'Umar told him that the Ka'bah did not need his money. He should break the oath, make compensation and reach an agreement with his brother. 'Umar recalled the Prophet's ﷺ prohibition of vows directed against oneself, involving disobedience to the Lord, breaking family ties or disposing of something one does not own. Abu Dawud records this under the subject of swearing in anger.

The case shows why a sweeping statement made in a quarrel must not be allowed to control the future at the expense of reconciliation. Its practical instruction preserves both duties: repair the relationship and address the oath in the prescribed way.

### What is hidden in the heart?

The inward emphasis is on genuine purposes and settled resolutions. Accidental sounds do not have the same weight, for good or evil, as what is cherished and decided within. Good intention carries a beautiful recompense. Evil intention exposes a person to prolonged distress.

Allah's knowledge reaches those inner secrets, not merely the form of words heard by other people. His forgiveness of slips is therefore not indifference to moral responsibility. It locates responsibility where the willing and knowing heart is involved.

### In short

- An unintended, habitual oath is not treated as a deliberately assumed obligation.
- An honest mistake differs from knowingly swearing to a falsehood.
- Lack of a required compensation does not always mean lack of sin, as the discussion of perjury shows.
- Allah knows the heart's intentions, forgives slips and delays punishment with forbearance.
