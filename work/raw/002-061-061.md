<<<VERSE 2:61>>>
Verse 61 closes the list of favours with the Israelites' complaint about the food, and then widens out into a verdict on the whole people. They said, "O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal", and asked for herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils and onions instead. Mūsā (Moses) answered, "Do you exchange what is better for what is worse?", and told them that any village would have what they wanted. Then the verse states the outcome: "They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah", because they rejected His signs and killed His prophets, and all of this was "for their disobedience and violations". Allah is saying, in effect: remember My favour in sending you manna and quails, a good, pure, wholesome food that cost you no effort, and remember your ingratitude in asking Mūsā to swap it for something inferior.

### Phrase by phrase

**"when you said, 'O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal'"**. The "same meal" was the manna and quails, which they ate day after day. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī said that they grew bored and impatient with the food they were given and began to remember the life they had lived before, when their diet was lentils, onions, garlic and herbs. This happened in the wilderness of Tīh, and one view links it to the city of verse 58. The divine provision had been given so that, freed from the struggle for a living, they could devote themselves wholly to carrying out God's commands, but they began demanding spicy cooked dishes instead. Discontented with the plain necessities, they hankered after worldly luxury.

**"So call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces"** means ask your Lord for us. The Arabic "of" here is explanatory: the things named are what the earth grows.

**"of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions"**. Herbs, cucumbers, lentils and onions are well known. The word rendered "garlic", fūm, was discussed at length. Ibn Masʿūd read it as thūm, which is plainly garlic, and Ibn Abī Ḥātim narrated from al-Ḥasan that Ibn ʿAbbās explained fūm as garlic. Al-Ḥasan also mentioned that in the old languages the expression fūmū lanā meant "bake for us", and Ibn Jarīr commented that if this is right, fūm is one of those words whose pronunciation shifted, the letter fāʾ replacing thāʾ because the two sound alike. Others said fūm is wheat, the grain used for bread, and al-Bukhārī noted the view that it covers every grain or seed that is eaten, which is why some translations render it as "corn" or "grain". Allah knows best.

**"Moses scolded them, 'Do you exchange what is better for what is worse?'"**. The question in Arabic opens with a particle of rebuke. Mūsā was criticising them for asking for inferior food while they lived in ease, eating what was tasty, wholesome and pure, and what came to them with no labour or effort. They were not content with what Allah had chosen for them, and had no patience with the way He managed their food and clothing. In their confusion they slid back into their old habits of coarser eating and settled for a lower state.

**"You can go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for"**. The word miṣr means any city, as Ibn ʿAbbās said, though Ibn Jarīr reported from Abū al-ʿĀliyah and ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas that it refers to Miṣr, the Egypt of Firʿawn (Pharaoh), and one early commentary glosses it as "the place you came from". The correct view is that it means any town at all, as Ibn ʿAbbās and other scholars held. Mūsā's point was that what they were asking for is cheap and plentiful in any city they might enter, so he would not trouble Allah with a request for it, least of all when it was the inferior choice. One account adds that they refused to change their minds, that Mūsā then prayed, and that Allah's reply was: go down to a city, whichever it may be, and there in its fields and markets you will find what you demanded. Because the request sprang from boredom and arrogance and there was no need to grant it, it was turned down. On the view that connects this episode with verse 58, Allah directed them through Mūsā to a certain town in the wilderness, where they could till the land and grow what they liked.

**"They were stricken with disgrace and misery"**. The two words are dhillah and maskanah, and the verb pictures them as stamped or cast upon the people. The verse indicates that the Children of Israel were struck with humiliation and that it would not cease: they would go on suffering humiliation at the hands of everyone who dealt with them, along with the disgrace they felt within. Al-Ḥasan said that Allah humiliated them and left them with no protector, placing them under the feet of the Muslims, who arose at a time when the Magians were already collecting the jizyah from the Jews. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas and as-Suddī said that "misery" means poverty, and ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī said it means paying the tax. Other commentators take "disgrace" as the poll tax and "misery" as the garb of poverty, and one adds that the marks of poverty and lowliness accompany them even when they are rich, the way a coin never loses the stamp of its mint. All of this came from following their whims and repeatedly turning away from what Allah had chosen for them. Scholars describe the disgrace in two directions: they lost respect in the eyes of others and lost largeness of spirit in themselves.

**"and they invited the displeasure of Allah"**. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk said that they deserved Allah's anger. Ibn Jarīr explained the verb, bāʾū, as "they went back carrying" the wrath, comparing the verse of Sūrat al-Māʾidah in which Hābīl tells his brother that he wants him to bear his sin along with his other sins (5:29), where the same root is used of carrying a burden away with oneself. So the sense is that they returned laden with Allah's anger, that His wrath settled on them and that they had earned it. One commentary paraphrases: they deserved to be forsaken by Allah.

**"for rejecting Allah's signs and unjustly killing the prophets"**. This is what the Children of Israel were repaid with, humiliation and misery, and the wrath was part of the humiliation they had earned by defying the truth, disbelieving in Allah's signs and belittling those who carried His law, the prophets and their followers. They rejected the Messengers and even killed them, among them, as one commentary notes, Zakariyyā and Yaḥyā, slain without any right and for no crime. There is no worse disbelief than disbelieving in Allah's signs and murdering His prophets. One early commentary applies the first charge to the Jews of the Prophet's ﷺ own time, reading the signs rejected as Muḥammad ﷺ and the Qur'an. Two hadiths illuminate the charge. In a hadith of the Two Ṣaḥīḥs the Messenger of Allah ﷺ defined kibr, arrogance, as refusing the truth and looking down on people, and both halves of that definition fit the conduct described here. And Imām Aḥmad recorded from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said the people in the worst torment on the Day of Resurrection will be a man killed by a prophet or who killed a prophet, an unjust ruler, and one who mutilates the dead.

**"This is a fair reward for their disobedience and violations"** gives a further reason for the punishment. Disobedience is doing what is forbidden. Violation, or transgression, is overstepping the limits between what is allowed and what is forbidden. One early commentary points to their disobedience over the Sabbath and to their killing of prophets and declaring forbidden things lawful. The repetition of "this was because" is for emphasis. Allah knows best.

### A disgrace that lasts

Scholars treat the disgrace of this verse as permanent. One form it takes is that temporal power has been taken from the Jews for good. The only exception scholars allow is the forty days, close to the Day of Judgement, in which the Dajjāl (Antichrist), who will be of Jewish stock, will hold an irregular sway like a robber's, which cannot be called sovereign power in any proper sense. Allah had made clear to them through Mūsā that if they persisted in disobedience they would always live under the domination of other nations, as the Qur'an says: your Lord declared that He would send against them, until the Day of Judgement, people who would make them suffer terribly (7:167). The classical summary of how the Companions, their successors and the great commentators understood the verse runs: however rich they become, they will be despised by other people, and whoever gets power over them will humiliate them and fasten on them the marks of servitude.

The fullest parallel is the verse of Sūrat Āl ʿImrān: they are stricken with disgrace wherever they are found, except by a rope from Allah or a rope from men, and they have drawn Allah's anger and been branded with misery for the same reasons given here (3:112), an explanation of which aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Muzāḥim transmitted. The rope from Allah refers to those whom Allah Himself, by His own command, has exempted from this disgrace, such as children, women and those wholly devoted to prayer and worship who never take up arms against Muslims. The rope from men is a peace treaty made with the Muslims, or leave to reside in a Muslim land as jizyah-paying subjects, the jizyah being the tax on non-Muslims under Muslim rule that frees them from military service and similar duties. And since the Qur'an says "from men" rather than "from Muslims", a third case is possible: the Jews may make political arrangements with non-Muslims and live in peace under their backing and protection.

Scholars also examine the kind of exception the verse makes. An exception can be of two sorts. In the first, the thing excepted is or was part of the whole from which it is excepted, as in "the tribe came except Zayd", where Zayd is a member of the tribe but excluded from the act of coming. In the second, the thing excepted never belonged to the whole, as in "the tribe came except the donkey". If the exception in the verse is of the first kind, the meaning is that all Jews live in disgrace always and everywhere, apart from the two situations named, protection of women, children and the like by Allah's own command, or protection under a treaty with Muslims or with some non-Muslim nation. If it is of the second kind, the meaning is that the Jews as a group remain essentially and permanently in disgrace, with the exception of some individuals who find protection under Allah's command, and of others who receive the support of other nations and so disguise their own disgrace.

### The modern doubt

Reading 3:112 alongside this verse, a twentieth-century commentator addresses a doubt that arises in some Muslim minds at the sight of the state of Israel established in Palestine. The Qur'an seems to say that the Jews will never have a sovereign state, yet they have taken Palestine and set one up. His answer is that, behind the appearances, this is not an independent sovereign state but a stronghold established by the Western powers in the midst of Muslim countries to protect their own interests. In his judgement it could not survive a month without their backing, and those powers regard it as their instrument. It therefore lives, in the Qur'an's words, "through a rope from men". He adds that the part of Palestine in question amounts to a mere dot on the world map when set against the vast territories ruled by Christian states, by Muslim states, and even by states whose people do not believe in Allah at all, and he asks whether so small a holding, kept under an American and British umbrella, can cancel the disgrace that Allah has decreed. On this reading there is no real ground for misgiving about what the Qur'an says.

### Masters of separation

The devotional commentary gathers the verse into a single moral. The Israelites were neither satisfied with the good that God chose for them nor patient under His management of their basic food and clothing. In their confusion they fell back on their habitual eating of impure things and found contentment at a lower level, so He returned them to the suffering of ignominy and bound them to perpetual disappointment. They went so far as to shed the blood of prophets, shamelessly violating the sanctity of God's command. Since they would not repent, He punished them for their ugly deeds and left them to the contemptible states their own souls had chosen. Sincere counsel (naṣīḥah) had failed, and so retribution and public disgrace (faḍīḥah) overtook them. The Israelites, it is said, were a people divided in their aspirations and scattered in their aims. They would not be content with one food, and they would not be satisfied with one object of worship either, so that when they saw a people devoted to idols they said to Mūsā, "Make for us a god like their gods" (7:138). This is the mark of the masters of separation (arbāb at-tafriqah). Patience with the One is hard, as God says of those who, when their Lord alone is mentioned in the Qur'an, turn their backs in aversion (17:46). A people who cannot bear one dish will struggle to bear one Lord.

### In short

- Bored with manna and quails, the Israelites asked for the herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils and onions of their old life, and Mūsā rebuked them for trading the better for the worse. Fūm was read as garlic, as "bake for us", as wheat, or as grain in general.
- "Any village" means any town, and the request, born of boredom and arrogance, was refused.
- Disgrace and misery were stamped on them: humiliation under others, poverty, and the tax, marks that follow them even in wealth.
- The cause was rejecting Allah's signs and killing His prophets, the worst of disbelief, together with disobedience and transgression. A hadith defines arrogance as rejecting truth and despising people.
- Sūrat Āl ʿImrān (3:112) adds the two exceptions, a rope from Allah and a rope from men, which scholars use to explain any apparent respite, including the modern state.
- The deeper fault was a divided heart, unable to be content with one food or one God.
