<<<VERSE 5:87>>>
A warning is given against a piety that oversteps: "Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress." The verse was revealed about Companions who wanted to abandon marriage, food and sleep, and even to mutilate themselves, in order to draw nearer to Allah.

Their intention was good. They said so themselves. The answer given to them defines the shape of Islamic devotion, and it closes with the statement that Allah does not like transgressors.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O believers! Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you"** prohibits declaring unlawful what Allah has permitted. One explanation specifies good food, drink and women, and forbids treating them as unlawful in an attempt to practise asceticism and devotion.

**"and do not transgress."** is explained in two directions. Do not exaggerate and make things hard for yourselves by prohibiting what is permitted. Do not exceed the limits by indulging excessively in permitted things either, but take what satisfies your need and avoid extravagance.

One gloss identifies the transgression meant here with cutting off the male organs, and describes the transgressors as those who move from the lawful to the unlawful by mutilating themselves.

**"Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors"** closes the warning. The explanation adds that Allah does not love those who overstep His limits, and rather hates that conduct.

Two other verses give the measure. People are told to eat and drink without wasting through extravagance (7:31), and the servants of the Merciful are described as neither extravagant nor miserly when they spend, holding a middle way between those extremes (25:67).

Allah legislated a middle way between those who go to extremes and those who fall short. It permits neither excessive application nor neglect.

### What did the Companions propose?

'Ali bin Abi Talhah reports Ibn 'Abbas saying the verse was revealed about some Companions of the Prophet ﷺ who said they should cut off their male organs, abandon the desires of this life and travel through the land as monks do. Ibn Abi Hatim also collected the report, and Ibn Marduwyah recorded a similar narration through Al-'Awfi from Ibn 'Abbas.

When the Prophet ﷺ heard of it he summoned them and asked whether they had said so. They answered that they had. He then said that he fasts and breaks his fast, prays and sleeps, and marries women, and that whoever follows his Sunnah belongs to him while whoever abandons his Sunnah does not.

The Two Sahihs record 'A'ishah describing some Companions asking the wives of the Prophet ﷺ about his worship in private. One said he would not eat meat, another that he would not marry women, and a third that he would not sleep on a bed.

Hearing this, the Prophet ﷺ asked what was the matter with people saying such things. He said that he fasts and breaks the fast, sleeps and rises to pray, eats meat and marries women, and that whoever is not pleased with his Sunnah is not of him.

Another account describes a number of Companions resolving to keep fasting and night vigil without interruption and to abstain from women, perfume, meat and sleeping on beds.

### Who are named in the longer reports?

One account says the verse concerns ten Companions. It names Abu Bakr, 'Umar, 'Ali, 'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud, 'Uthman ibn Maz'un al-Jumahi, al-Miqdad ibn al-Aswad al-Kindi, Salim the client of Abu Hudhayfah ibn 'Utbah, Salman al-Farisi, Abu Dharr and 'Ammar ibn Yasir. They made their pledge in the house of 'Uthman ibn Maz'un.

The pledge was to eat and drink only enough to stay alive, to have no relations with their wives, never to eat meat or fat, and to cut off their male organs. Allah forbade such acts and revealed the verse about them.

A longer narrative sets the scene. The Messenger ﷺ sat one day and admonished the people, describing the Day of Judgment and mentioning only what would instil fear. Their hearts softened and they wept.

Ten Companions then gathered in the house of 'Uthman ibn Maz'un al-Jumahi. The names given are Ma'qil ibn Muqarrin, Salman al-Farisi, al-Miqdad ibn al-Aswad, Salim the client of Abu Hudhayfah, Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, 'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr, 'Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud, 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and Abu Bakr al-Siddiq.

They agreed to fast by day and stand in prayer by night, never to sleep in beds or eat meat or fat, never to sleep with their wives or use perfume, to wear coarse wool, to give up the worldly life, to wander the land as monks and to cut off their male organs.

When the Messenger ﷺ heard of it he gathered them and asked whether he had been informed correctly that they had agreed on such things. They said that it was so, but that they had intended nothing but good.

He answered that he had not been commanded to do those things, and that their own selves have a right over them. Fast and break the fast. Stand in prayer at night and sleep, for he stands at night and also sleeps. He sometimes fasts and sometimes does not, and he eats meat and fat.

The two lists differ in some names, and both are preserved as reported rather than merged into one roster.

### Is there another reported occasion?

Yes. A report through 'Ikrimah from Ibn 'Abbas describes a man who came to the Prophet ﷺ and said that he had forbidden meat to himself, because whenever he ate it he felt the urge for women. This verse and the one following are given as revealed on that account.

The reports therefore differ between a group pledge and an individual's vow. Both illustrate the same prohibition, and neither is cancelled by the other.

### How can a lawful thing be made unlawful?

The legal discussion identifies three forms. The first is in belief, that is, holding something to be unlawful when it is not. Where its lawfulness is established by decisive proof, a person who considers it unlawful becomes a disbeliever through that open contravention of the divinely ordained law.

The second is verbal. A person declares something unlawful for himself by an oath, for example swearing that he will never drink cold water, or will not eat a particular lawful food, or will not do something permitted. The detailed forms of oath are set out in books of fiqh.

In that case the oath takes effect. The rule given is that taking such oaths unnecessarily is a sin, and if one has been taken, the person must break it and make amends through the kaffarah, the expiation of an oath, whose details come later in this chapter.

The third form is practical. A person neither believes nor says anything, but decides in practice to abandon a lawful thing permanently.

If he treats that abandonment as an act of thawab, merit and reward, it is bid'ah, innovation in the established religion, and rahbaniyah, monasticism. That is a grave sin stated categorically in the Qur'an and Sunnah, acting against it is wajib, a binding obligation, and adhering to such a restriction is itself sinful.

If the restriction is not undertaken for merit but for another reason, such as a physical or spiritual illness that leads a person to give something up permanently, there is no sin in it. Reports of some Sufis and other pious elders abandoning certain lawful things are placed in this third category. They found those things harmful to their desiring selves, or a pious elder advised them to abstain as harmful in their case, so they gave them up as treatment and remedy.

### What is the right attitude toward lawful things?

Transgression here means abandoning something lawful as an act of merit without a valid excuse. An ignorant person takes that to be taqwa, the fear of Allah, while in Allah's sight it is transgression and is impermissible.

The following verse therefore commands eating of the good and pure provision Allah has given and fearing Him in whom they believe. Leaving good and pure things aside as a means of earning reward is not taqwa. Taqwa lies in using them as Allah's blessings and showing gratitude for them.

Abandoning something as a cure for a physical or spiritual illness does not fall under this rule.

### Why does the passage come here?

The subject returns to subsidiary rulings after the long discussion of the People of the Book. A particular link is also noted. Monasticism had been mentioned in a complimentary sense shortly before, in respect of one feature of it, which is the abandonment of worldly desires.

Since someone might infer that monasticism as a whole is praiseworthy, it was fitting at this point to forbid making unlawful what Allah has made lawful. Renunciation of worldly life and restraint of appetite are favourable within a certain limit, but transgressing the divinely ordained limits in this matter is blameworthy and unlawful.

### What is the deeper problem with renunciation?

The connection between a person and Allah is a living relationship established at the level of the heart and is inward in nature. When religion declines and that inward relationship weakens, a mentality develops that the relationship must be built up from outward means.

One such means is giving up worldly pleasures, in effect renunciation or monasticism. It is assumed that staying away from material things will bring a person nearer to Allah. Some Companions were influenced by such thoughts and intended to stop eating meat and sleeping at night, to castrate themselves and to leave their homes to live as hermits, with some taking a vow or oath to that effect.

They were prohibited from this and told that nearness to Allah is not attained by treating permitted things as forbidden. Whatever a person achieves comes from remaining within natural limits, not from inventing stricter ones.

### In short

- Declaring lawful things unlawful, whether by belief, by oath or by settled practice for merit, is forbidden.
- The reports name a group pledge of ten Companions and an individual's vow about meat, and both are preserved.
- The Prophet ﷺ answered with his own practice of fasting and breaking fast, praying and sleeping, eating meat and marrying.
- Transgression runs in two directions, prohibiting the permitted and exceeding in its use, with a middle way legislated between them.
- Restraint for a genuine medical or spiritual reason is not the monasticism condemned here, and taqwa lies in gratitude for blessings rather than in refusing them.

<<<VERSE 5:88>>>
The prohibition is followed by a positive command: "Eat of the good, lawful things provided to you by Allah." The verse does not leave the believer between two refusals. It commands enjoyment of what is pure and lawful, and joins that enjoyment to mindfulness of Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Eat of the good, lawful things provided to you by Allah."** is a command to take what Allah has bestowed as food that is lawful and good. One explanation describes eating items that are pure and lawful.

The command carries its own limit. Another explanation says to consume the lawful and pure things from Allah's provision, and not to consume the unlawful, such as what is taken wrongfully or what is impure.

**"And be mindful of Allah in Whom you believe"** joins the eating to faith. Mindfulness is explained as guarding against Allah in all affairs, obeying Him and seeking His pleasure while staying away from defiance and disobedience.

One gloss specifies fearing Allah concerning mutilation and forbidding what He has made lawful. Another describes mindfulness as obeying His instructions and avoiding His prohibitions, because He is the One in whom they have faith and that faith requires it.

### Why does the command to eat follow the prohibition?

Because the two verses form one instruction. The first removes a false piety, and the second establishes the right relation to provision. Eating what is lawful and good is not a concession to weakness. It is part of obedience.

Faith in Allah is named as the ground of mindfulness. The One who made these things lawful is the One to be feared, so gratitude and restraint belong together rather than competing.

### What does mindfulness look like in this matter?

It looks like using blessings as blessings. Taqwa here is described as taking what Allah has provided and showing gratitude for it, not as refusing it in order to earn merit.

It also looks like staying within limits. The prohibition of transgression in the preceding verse covers both making the lawful unlawful and exceeding in its use, so mindfulness governs the quantity as well as the intention.

### In short

- The command is to eat what Allah has provided that is lawful and good, and to avoid what is unlawful or impure.
- Mindfulness of Allah is tied to faith in Him, and covers obedience, seeking His pleasure and avoiding defiance.
- The two verses together remove a false piety and establish gratitude as the right relation to provision.
- Restraint is required in quantity as well as in intention, since transgression runs in both directions.
