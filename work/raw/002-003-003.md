The verse before this one said that the Book guides the mindful. Now the surah says who they are, and it begins with three marks: they are the people "who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them". The first is a matter of the heart, the second of the body, the third of possessions. Together they are a short definition of faith and of the practice that goes with it, and the scholars treated this verse as the place where the Qur'an says, in its own words, what a believer is.

### Phrase by phrase

**"who believe"** translates yuʾminūn, from īmān. ʿAbdullāh, in the report of Abū al-Aḥwaṣ, said that īmān is to trust, and Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, said the same: they trust. Az-Zuhrī said that īmān is the deeds. Ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas said that "they believe" means they fear Allah. Ibn Jarīr aṭ-Ṭabarī weighed these and preferred to describe the believers as having faith in the unseen with the tongue, with deeds and with conviction. Fear of Allah is then part of the general meaning of īmān, and so is following up what the tongue affirms with action. Īmān, on this account, is a broad word: affirming Allah, His Books and His Messengers, and making that affirmation real by living up to what it implies.

In the language, īmān on its own simply means trust, and the Qur'an sometimes uses it that way. The Prophet ﷺ is described as one who trusts in Allah and trusts the believers (9:61), and the brothers of Yūsuf (Joseph) told their father that he would never believe them even when they spoke the truth (12:17). Sometimes the word is placed beside deeds, as in the exception made for "those who believe and do righteous deeds" (95:6). When īmān is used without restriction, it takes in beliefs, deeds and the statements of the tongue, and it increases and decreases. Some scholars explained īmān as khashyah, fear of Allah, pointing to "those who fear their Lord unseen" (67:12) and to the one "who feared the Most Gracious unseen" (50:33). Fear is the heart of both faith and knowledge, for "it is only those who have knowledge among His servants that fear Allah" (35:28).

A more precise definition looks at how the word is used. In Arabic, īmān means accepting someone's statement with complete certainty because of one's total confidence in him. Confirming something you can see for yourself is not īmān. If one man says a cloth is black and another agrees, that is taṣdīq, confirmation, not faith, because the second man relies on his own eyes and not on the first man's word. In the vocabulary of the Sharīʿah, īmān is accepting with complete certainty what a prophet has said, out of trust in him and without needing to see for oneself. This is worth stressing because "faith" in everyday modern speech has come to mean little more than a strong feeling. Īmān in this sense is a matter of the mind in the old meaning of the word, a settled certainty, not an emotional state.

**"in the unseen"** translates bil-ghayb. The early Muslims explained the ghayb in several ways, all of them correct and all pointing to the same thing. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, in the report of ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, said it means believing in Allah, His angels, His Books, His Messengers, the Last Day, His Paradise and His Fire, the meeting with Him, life after death and the Resurrection. Qatādah ibn Diʿāmah said much the same. Another gloss lists what is hidden from people now: Paradise, Hell, the Bridge over Hell, the Scale, the Resurrection and the Reckoning. It has also been said that the unseen is what the Qur'an has revealed of hidden things together with what it has not revealed, and it has even been said that the unseen is Allah Himself.

In the language, ghayb is whatever is not evident to people and cannot be reached by the five senses. The Qur'an uses the word for everything that cannot be known by the senses or by reason alone but has been reported by the Prophet ﷺ: the essence and attributes of Allah, destiny, Paradise and Hell and what is in them, the Day of Judgment and what happens on it, the revealed books and all the prophets before Muhammad ﷺ. In short, everything named in the last two verses of this surah. This verse states the creed in essence, and the last two verses of the surah give its details. So belief in the unseen comes to mean firm faith in everything the Prophet ﷺ taught, provided it has reached us by sound and undeniable transmission, and that is how the great majority of Muslim scholars define īmān in their statements of creed, such as al-ʿAqīdah aṭ-Ṭaḥāwiyyah and the creed of an-Nasafī. On this definition īmān is faith and certainty, not mere knowledge. Satan has knowledge of the truth, and so did many disbelievers, who knew perfectly well that the Prophet ﷺ was truthful and his teaching true, yet had no faith in him and did not accept it with their hearts, and so were not Muslims.

Believing in what one has not seen is the believer's special honour. ʿAbdur-Raḥmān ibn Yazīd said that they were sitting with ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd and spoke of the Companions and how their deeds were greater than their own. ʿAbdullāh said that the matter of Muhammad ﷺ had been plain to those who saw him, and swore by the One besides whom there is no god that no one would ever attain a better kind of faith than faith in the unseen. Then he recited the opening of this surah, from the letters and "This is the Book" down to the words about those who are successful. Saʿīd ibn Manṣūr recorded this, as did Ibn Abī Ḥātim, Ibn Mardawayh and al-Ḥākim in his Mustadrak, who said it is authentic by the standard of al-Bukhārī and Muslim although the two of them did not record it.

A hadith says the same. Ibn Muḥayrīz asked Abū Jumuʿah to narrate something he had heard from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and he told of a meal with the Prophet ﷺ at which Abū ʿUbaydah asked whether anyone was better than they were, who had accepted Islam with him and fought beside him. The Prophet ﷺ said yes: those who would come after them and believe in him without having seen him. Aḥmad recorded it. Abū Bakr ibn Mardawayh recorded another route in his tafsīr, from Ṣāliḥ ibn Jubayr: Abū Jumuʿah al-Anṣārī came to Bayt al-Maqdis, Jerusalem, to pray, and Rajāʾ ibn Ḥaywah was among those who went out to greet him. As he was leaving he said they had a right over him and a gift coming, and told them that ten of them, Muʿādh ibn Jabal among them, had once asked the Prophet ﷺ whether anyone would earn a greater reward than they had, who believed in Allah and followed him. He answered: what prevents you, when the Messenger of Allah is among you bringing revelation down from heaven? People will come after you who are given a book between two covers, and they will believe in it and act on it. Their reward is greater than yours, even twice as much.

On the inward reading, these are the people who love Allah without having seen Him, who affirm His uniqueness and His oneness in essence and attributes, who hold fast to His Messenger without having seen him and walk straight along his Sunnah. Five hundred years after him, with nothing before them but black ink on white paper, they accept him with spirit and heart. Whatever he reported of the world of the Kingdom, the Lote Tree of the furthest limit, the Gardens of Refuge, the Throne and the end of this world, they testify to and believe in. These are the ones the Prophet ﷺ called his brothers when he said how he longed to meet them.

**"establish prayer"** translates yuqīmūna ṣ-ṣalāh. Ibn ʿAbbās said it means performing the prayer with everything that is obligatory in it, and in the report of aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk he explained it as completing the bowing, the prostration, the recitation, the humility and the attendance at the prayer. Qatādah said it means keeping to its times and to its ablution, bowing and prostration. Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said it means keeping its times, completing one's purification for it, and completing its bowing, prostration, recitation, tashahhud and blessings on the Prophet ﷺ. The verb comes from iqāmah, to set something straight. So the phrase does not mean merely saying one's prayers but performing them correctly in every respect, keeping every condition, whether obligatory (farḍ), necessary (wājib) or recommended (mustaḥabb), and it includes regularity, constancy, inward attention, humility and awe. It is not about one particular prayer. It covers all the farḍ, wājib and nafl prayers, and in the first place the five prescribed prayers with their ablution, their bowing and prostration and their appointed times. In short, the mindful are those who pray regularly and steadfastly according to the rules of the Sharīʿah and keep the inward and outward manners of prayer. In the language, ṣalāh means supplication. In the terminology of the religion it is the act of bowing and prostration with the other specified actions, performed at set times with known conditions and features.

The inward reading describes how they pray: as though they were gazing upon Allah and whispering with Him in private, living out the Prophet's ﷺ words, "Worship Allah as if you see Him, for if you do not see Him, surely He sees you." He also said that when the servant stands for prayer he is before the eyes of the All-Merciful, and when he looks around, Allah says: child of Ādam, at whom are you looking? Are you looking at someone better for you than I? Look at Me, for I am better for you than the one you are looking at. So when you come to prayer, strive to keep your thoughts in it and turn your heart away from the marketplace. Have courtesy, and know the worth of private conversation with the Giver of blessings, for only a person of low aspiration is granted that conversation and then busies his heart with the blessings.

**"and donate from what We have provided for them"** translates wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, said it means the zakāh due on their wealth. In the reports of Abū Mālik and Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn ʿAbbās, and of Murrah from Ibn Masʿūd and other Companions, it means a man's spending on his family, and this was before zakāh was made obligatory. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk said that at first spending in charity was a way of drawing near to Allah at one's own discretion and according to one's means, until the obligation of charity was revealed in the seven verses of Sūrat Barāʾah, which replaced the earlier state of affairs. The position adopted by most commentators is that the phrase includes every form of spending in Allah's way: the obligatory zakāh, the necessary alms and voluntary charity, since the Qur'an generally uses the word infāq for voluntary giving or giving in general and keeps the word zakāh for the obligatory kind. Other glosses say simply that they give in charity from the wealth they have been given, or that they give the zakāh, and it was said that the verse refers to Abū Bakr and his companions. The phrase also teaches by its wording. Everything a person owns is Allah's gift and a trust in his hands, so that giving it all away would be no more than just, and no favour to Him. In His mercy He asks only for spending "from" what He has provided, a part and not the whole.

Allah often names prayer and spending together. Prayer is His right and His worship: it singles Him out for devotion, praises and glorifies Him, calls on Him and shows the servant's dependence on Him. Spending is kindness to His creatures by giving them what benefits them, and those with the first claim on it are relatives, then the wife, then servants, then everyone else. Every kind of required charity and required spending falls under this phrase. Ibn ʿUmar reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that Islam is built on five things: testifying that there is no god but Allah and that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah, establishing prayer, giving zakāh, fasting Ramaḍān and performing Ḥajj to the House. The two Ṣaḥīḥs record it, and there are many other hadiths to the same effect.

The inward reading adds that the mindful recognise the blessings and favours placed upon them, undertake to be grateful for them, and at the command of the Sharīʿah treat the poor gently, comfort them and regard them as the Real's deputies in receiving charity. That is the road of Muslims in general, who pay what is obligatory or add something voluntarily. The road of the people of inner reality is different: they give away whatever they have and still consider that they have fallen short. Someone asked ash-Shiblī how much zakāh was due on two hundred dirhams. He asked whether the man meant his own or ash-Shiblī's, and when the man said he had not known they were different, he explained: if you give, five dirhams are due, and if I give, the whole two hundred, with five more as a token of thanks. The common people of the community discharge the obligatory zakāh, and the fruit of their work is that they ask, "Lord, are You pleased with what we have given?" The fruit of the deed of the elect, who give everything, is that Allah asks them, "My servant, are you pleased with Me for what you have done?" The two are far apart, and the state of Abū Bakr bears witness to it. After he had given away all he owned, he came one day into the Prophet's ﷺ presence wrapped in a white blanket fastened at the front with a thorn of the date palm. Jibrīl came down with Allah's greeting and the question why Abū Bakr's cloak was pinned with a thorn. The Prophet ﷺ said that he had spent his wealth before the conquest. Jibrīl said that Allah sent him His greetings and asked whether he was content with Him in this poverty of his, or angry. Abū Bakr said, "Should I be angry with my Lord? I am content with my Lord."

It has been said that the servant stands upright through three things, heart, body and possessions. Without faith in the unseen his heart will not go straight in the religion, and neither clarity nor intimacy will appear in him. Without the obligatory prayers his body will not be kept sound and straight with any constancy. And until he separates the zakāh from his possessions, those possessions will not settle down with him.

### Why these three

The two verses that describe the mindful tell the reader who the guided people are and whose path is the Straight Path, so that whoever asks for that path knows whose company to join and whose beliefs and way of life to adopt. That is why, as soon as their qualities are listed, the surah says that they are the ones on guidance from their Lord and the ones who succeed. Of the three qualities named here, faith is the most important, since it is the principle behind every other principle and no good deed is accepted or valid without it. The other two are deeds, and deeds are many. Why only these two? Because every obligatory or necessary good deed concerns either a person's own body or his possessions. Among the acts of worship done with the body the most important is the prayer, so the Qur'an names it. Among those done with possessions, the word infāq covers them all. By naming two deeds the verse has included every form of worship and every good deed, and it describes the mindful as people who are complete in faith and complete in action. It gives a full definition of īmān and, at the same time, the meaning of Islam, since Islam is the sum of faith and practice.

### Īmān and Islam

In the language, īmān means accepting and confirming something in the heart, and Islam means obedience and submission. Īmān belongs to the heart. Islam belongs to the heart too, but also to every other part of the body. In the Sharīʿah, however, neither is valid without the other. Faith in Allah and His Prophet ﷺ in the heart is not enough unless the tongue declares it and submission follows, and a declaration of faith and allegiance on the tongue is not valid without faith in the heart. So the two words differ in their meaning, and the Qur'an and the hadith sometimes speak of the difference, but in the law they are bound together and the Qur'an itself shows that one does not stand without the other.

Where outward submission is not accompanied by inward faith, the Qur'an calls it nifāq, hypocrisy, and treats it as worse than open rejection: "Surely the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of Hell" (4:145). In this world people can judge only what is outward and can never be certain of what is inside a man, so the Sharīʿah requires that those who declare themselves Muslims be treated as Muslims in worldly affairs, while in the next world their fate is worse than that of ordinary disbelievers. Where inward recognition is not accompanied by outward affirmation and allegiance, the Qur'an calls that kufr, denial of the truth. Of the disbelievers it says, "They know him as they know their own sons" (2:146), and of Firʿawn's people, "Their souls knew them to be true, yet they denied them in their wickedness and their pride" (27:14). Sayyid Muhammad Anwar Shāh explained it like this: īmān and Islam cover the same ground in the spiritual journey and differ only in where they begin and end. Īmān starts in the heart and is perfected in outward deeds. Islam starts in outward deeds and is perfected when it reaches the heart. So īmān is not valid if the heart's acknowledgment never reaches outward affirmation, and Islam is not valid if outward affirmation never reaches confirmation by the heart. Al-Ghazzālī and as-Subkī reached the same conclusion, and in his Musāmarah Ibn al-Humām reports that all the reliable scholars agree on it.

A modern note on the same point warns against the confusion that has grown up around these two words under Western influence. Some claim that Islam is only a matter of the "heart" or of "religious experience", and so see no need for fixed ritual or a fixed moral code, treating religion as a purely personal relationship between a person and "his" God. Others insist on nothing but outward performance of rituals or conformity to rules of their own choosing, indifferent to the inward dimension, and some have gone on to replace the rituals Allah ordained with acts of social service dressed up as worship. Others again reduce Islam to a system of social organisation or even to statecraft, so that Muslims who fail to set up a state of a certain shape would cease to be Muslims, which applied to history would mean that no Muslim ever existed. Against all of these, Islam means establishing a relationship of obedience and servitude to Allah. It does not arise from vague experiences or from social regimentation. It requires accepting every doctrine and acting on every command laid down in the Qur'an, the hadith and the Sharīʿah, which cover every sphere of life, from worship to social, political and economic dealings, manners and morals, and whose essential purpose is to produce in a person genuine obedience to Allah. Acting on the Sharīʿah does bring worldly benefits, individual and collective, and those benefits may be called the reason the commands exist, but they are not their essential object, a servant should not obey for their sake, and a Muslim's success as a Muslim does not depend on obtaining them. When a person has fully submitted to Allah's commands in all he does, he has already succeeded as a Muslim, whether or not the worldly benefits follow.

The same three qualities can be seen as the growth of a seeker. The urge to make out the world of meanings behind the world of forms, when it leads to discovery, becomes faith in the unseen. The urge to submit to a higher power becomes bowing to the Almighty. The wish to give oneself for something greater becomes spending one's wealth in Allah's cause.

### In short

- Īmān is accepting with certainty what the Prophet ﷺ reported, out of trust in him and without seeing for oneself. It lives in the heart, is spoken by the tongue, is proved by deeds, and it increases and decreases.
- The unseen is everything beyond the senses that revelation reports: Allah and His attributes, the angels, the Books, the Messengers, destiny, death and Resurrection, the Reckoning, Paradise and Hell. Believing without seeing is a higher faith than the Companions' own, by Ibn Masʿūd's oath and by the Prophet's ﷺ words about those who would come after.
- Establishing prayer means praying on time, in purity, with complete bowing, prostration, recitation and humility, and keeping it up for life.
- Spending from what Allah provides covers zakāh, required support and voluntary charity, and it asks for a part, not the whole, of what is in any case His gift and His trust.
- Faith, prayer and spending stand for heart, body and possessions, so the verse defines the whole of Islam, which is faith and practice together, neither valid without the other.
