<<<VERSE 4:92>>>
The law of the unintended death: "It is not lawful for a believer to kill another except by mistake. And whoever kills a believer unintentionally must free a believing slave and pay blood-money to the victim's family" (4:92). The verse opens the section of the surah that deals with killing, and it holds two obligations, an expiation owed to Allah and a compensation owed to people.

### Phrase by phrase

**"It is not lawful for a believer to kill another except by mistake"** states that a believer may not kill his believing brother under any circumstances, the mistake excepted. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from Ibn Masʿūd the boundary set by the Messenger of Allah ﷺ:

> The blood of a Muslim who testifies that there is no deity worthy of worship except Allah and that I am the Messenger of Allah is sacred, except in three instances: life for life, the married adulterer, and whoever reverts from the religion and abandons the community of the faithful.

Even for these three, the killing is not the business of ordinary citizens, for it belongs to the Muslim leader or his deputy.

The occasion of the exception is told in several reports. ʿAyyāsh ibn Abī Rabīʿah al-Makhzūmī, the half brother of Abū Jahl through his mother Asmāʾ bint Makhrabah, had embraced Islam and fled to Madinah, taking refuge in one of its fortresses, for he feared coming out in the open about his faith. His mother, greatly distressed, swore by Allah to stay out in the sun, neither eating nor drinking, until her sons brought him back. Abū Jahl and al-Ḥārith ibn Hishām, his half brothers on the mother's side, went looking for him in the company of al-Ḥārith ibn Zayd ibn Abī Anīsah. They found ʿAyyāsh in his fortress and told him that his mother had not entered a house since he left and had sworn to touch no food until he returned, giving him the assurance that they would coerce him into nothing and come between him and nothing of his religion. When they spoke of his mother's distress and gave their pledge, he came down. They took him out of Madinah, tied him up with strings and gave him a hundred lashes each, then brought him to his mother, who refused to untie his fetters until he disbelieved in what he had believed, and he was left bound in the sun until he yielded them part of what they wanted. Al-Ḥārith ibn Yazīd went to him and said that if what he had followed was guidance, he had now left it, and if it was error, he had been in error already. This is al-Kalbī's account, carried by Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq on the authority of ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān ibn al-Qāsim's father. The wound between the two men stayed open. ʿAyyāsh killed al-Ḥārith, whom other reports call al-Ḥārith ibn Yazīd al-ʿĀmirī or al-Ḥārith ibn Shadīd, out of revenge for the torture he and his brother had suffered for their Islam. The man had later embraced Islam and performed the hijrah, but ʿAyyāsh did not know it. On the day of the Makkan Conquest he saw him, took him for a disbeliever still, attacked him and killed him, and Allah sent down this verse. ʿAbdur-Raḥmān ibn Zayd ibn Aslam told a version in which the verse came down about Abū ad-Dardāʾ, who killed a man just as he embraced the faith, holding the sword above him. Told of it, Abū ad-Dardāʾ said the man had only pronounced the faith to avert death, and the Prophet ﷺ asked him whether he had opened his heart. The basis of that story stands in the Ṣaḥīḥ, though not about Abū ad-Dardāʾ.

**"must free a believing slave"** is the first obligation, the expiation for the great sin committed, even though it was a mistake, and the slave freed must be a believing one, not a non-Muslim. Imām Aḥmad recorded the story of a man of the Anṣār who brought a slave-girl and said that he had to free a believing slave, so if the Prophet ﷺ saw that she was a believer, he would free her. He asked her whether she testified that there is no deity worthy of worship except Allah, and she said yes. He asked whether she testified that he was the Messenger of Allah, and she said yes. He asked whether she believed in resurrection after death, and she said yes. He said: then free her. The chain is authentic, and not knowing the name of the Anṣārī Companion does not lessen its authenticity.

**"and pay blood-money to the victim's family"** is the second obligation, involving the killer and the family of the deceased, who receive the diyah as compensation for their loss. Imām Aḥmad and the compilers of the Sunan recorded from Ibn Masʿūd that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ determined the diyah for unintentional killing as camels of different year-classes, twenty of each class, and that is the wording of an-Nasāʾī. The jurists then count the kinds. The quasi-intentional killing carries one hundred camels of four kinds, 25 of each, and the accidental killing one hundred camels of five kinds, 20 of each, while payment in cash runs to ten thousand dirhams or one thousand dīnārs for both, as in the Hidāyah. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from Abū Hurayrah the case of the two women of Hudhayl who quarreled, whereupon one threw a stone at the other and killed her together with her unborn child. The Prophet ﷺ decided that the diyah of the child was the freeing of a male or female slave, and that the diyah of the woman was owed by the elders of the killer's tribe. The report shows that in what appears intentional, the diyah follows the pattern of the accidental.

**"unless they waive it charitably"** means the diyah must be delivered to the family unless they forfeit their right, remitting it as a charity to the killer. The diyah is distributed as inheritance under the Sharīʿah, an heir who forgives his share forgives that much of it, and if all the heirs forgive, the whole diyah stands forgiven. A victim with no legal heir has his diyah retired to the Bayt al-Māl, the state treasury, for diyah is inheritance and inheritance follows this rule.

**"But if the victim is a believer from a hostile people, then a believing slave must be freed"** narrows the case. If the murdered person was a believer whose family were combatant disbelievers, they receive no diyah, and the killer owes only the freeing of a believing slave as a redemption.

**"And if the victim is from a people bound with you in a treaty, then blood-money must be paid to the family along with freeing a believing slave"** covers the people of covenant status, the ahl adh-dhimmah, or those with whom a peace treaty runs. They deserve the diyah, the full diyah if the deceased was a believer, together with the freeing. The Sunna fixes the shares of this diyah, a third of the diyah of a believer for a Jew or a Christian, and two thirds of a tenth of it for a Magian. The diyah of a Muslim and of a Dhimmi is equal, in the report of the Marāsil of Abū Dāwūd as the Hidāyah quotes it, and the covenant is general, covering the Dhimmi, the permanent protected resident, and the Musta'min, the holder of a temporary guarantee of peace. The diyah of such people is valid only when people of their kind exist to receive it. If they have none, or those people are Muslims, the diyah is deemed non-existent, for a Muslim does not inherit from a disbeliever, and the diyah of an heirless Dhimmi is retired into the Bayt al-Māl, as al-Durr al-Mukhtār rules.

**"Those who are unable, let them fast two consecutive months"** gives the substitute for one who cannot find or afford a slave to free: two months of fasting kept continuous. If the fast is broken without a cause such as illness, the count starts over, though for a woman her menstruation does not break the continuity. The verse names no substitute of feeding the needy for this fasting, as it does in the case of ẓihār, though al-Shāfiʿī, in the more correct of two opinions of his, allows that transition here. For one who lacks the strength to fast even so, the course is to keep repenting until strength returns.

**"as a means of repentance to Allah"** names the purpose of it all, the repentance of the one who killed by mistake, and Allah is Ever All-Knowing of him and All-Wise in what he must do.

### The eight cases and the three kinds

Linked with the earlier verses on fighting and killing, the ruling takes in every case, for the person killed falls under one of four conditions: a Muslim, a Dhimmi, a beneficiary of a peace pact assured of life, property, honour and religion, or a belligerent disbeliever. Killing is of two types, intentional and accidental, so eight forms stand in all. The intentional killing of a Muslim carries the duty of even retaliation, qiṣāṣ, set out in sūrat al-Baqarah, with the ruling of the Hereafter following in verse 93. The accidental killing of a Muslim is the case of verse 92 itself. A Muslim was once subjected to qiṣāṣ for the intentional killing of a Dhimmi, in the report of Dāraquṭnī. The treaty case appears in this verse, and the fifth case appeared earlier at verse 90, where Allah declared no permission against the peace-seeker. The sixth goes with the fourth, since the covenant covers both the Dhimmi and the Musta'min, as al-Durr al-Mukhtār sets out. The seventh and eighth, the killing of belligerent disbelievers, stand justified by the legalisation of jihād itself, as the Bayān al-Qur'ān concludes.

The jurists divide the act itself into three kinds. Intentional killing is done with a weapon of iron or the like of it in cutting power, a sharp-edged bamboo or a sharp rock. Quasi-intentional killing is intended, but done with what does not cut off parts. Accidental killing, qatl al-khaṭaʾ, happens under misapprehension, when someone aims at a man taking him for game or a belligerent, or aims at game and strikes a man down. The second and third kinds both oblige the diyah and both carry sin, differing in weight, for the second is the more sinful through the presence of intention, and the third carries the lesser sin of heedlessness, as in the Hidāyah. The reality of these kinds belongs to the law of this world, while their being sinful turns on the volition of the heart and the intention behind the act, which Allah alone knows, so that by His knowledge the first may count non-intentional and the second intentional. Further rulings follow. The stated diyah applies to a male victim, and a woman's is half of it. The expiation, the freeing or the fasting, must come from the killer himself, while the diyah is the responsibility of his caretakers, named al-ʿāqilah in the terminology of the Sharīʿah. The transfer of the burden to them has its justice, for the guardians are not wholly faultless, having failed to stop his gross heedlessness against human life, and the fear of paying will make them careful in the future. In the freeing, a bondman and a bondwoman are equal, the Qur'anic word for a slave being general, though the physique freed must be sound, without deformity. And in intentional killing there is no expiation of this kind at all. There the duty is repentance alone.

### The weight the many carry

The inward teaching sees the same mercy at work. Allah mitigated the matter of the one who kills by mistake by giving the burden of restitution to the larger group, the ʿāqila. The elite are responsible for the blood-money of the weak and oppressed in the community, the people of deeper knowledge for the aspirants, and the shaykhs for the poor. Their way is to bear the burdens that afflict the weak.

A leader's mistake runs on the same principle. Al-Bukhārī recorded from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ sent Khālid ibn al-Walīd to Banū Jaḥshimah, who called them to Islam. Not knowing how to say we have become Muslims, they kept saying that they had become Sabians, and Khālid began killing them. When the news reached the Prophet ﷺ he raised his hands and said:

> O Allah! I declare my innocence before You of what Khālid did.

He sent ʿAlī to pay the diyah of those killed and to compensate the property destroyed, down to replacing the dog's bowl, and the report shows that the mistake of the leader or his deputy is paid from the treasury of the Muslims. Killing is a heinous and irreversible crime in every case, and the accidental form is dealt with more leniently. One who kills unintentionally, then fully realises the gravity of it, sincerely seeks forgiveness and pays the due compensation may hope for clemency, and the desire for self-reform that grows from facing the enormity is itself a gain. To that end the directives are given, and the self-punishment of a continuous fast is the starting point.

### In short

- A believer's blood is sacred, and only three causes lift the protection, each judged by the authority, not the crowd.
- The verse came down over ʿAyyāsh's revenge-killing of a man who had secretly entered Islam, a mistake in the eyes of the law and a wound of Makkah underneath it.
- Two obligations follow the accidental killing: the freeing of a believing slave, tested for faith as the Anṣārī's slave-girl was, and the diyah, camels by year-classes or its cash value, borne by the ʿāqila.
- Hostile families receive nothing, treaty people their full due, and inability turns the penance into two continuous months of fasting.
- Eight cases and three kinds of killing cover the field, the leader's error is paid from the treasury, and the strong carry the burdens of the weak.

<<<VERSE 4:93>>>
The warning: "And whoever kills a believer intentionally, their reward will be Hell" (4:93). After the ruling on the accidental killing comes the ruling on the deliberate, a sin so grave that the Qur'ān names it alongside shirk.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And whoever kills a believer intentionally"** means killing deliberately, with something lethal, aware that the slain is a believer. The verse came down over a calculated crime. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of al-Kalbī from Abū Ṣāliḥ, tells that Miqyās ibn Ṣubābah, a Muslim, found his brother Hishām murdered among Banū an-Najjār and went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, who sent with him an emissary of Banū Fihr, instructing him to greet the clan with the greeting of peace and to demand that they hand over the killer, if they knew him, for his brother's retaliation, or else pay the blood-money. They answered that they heard and obeyed, that they did not know the killer, and paid one hundred camels. Close to Madinah, the devil whispered to Miqyās that accepting his brother's blood-money would be a slur on him, and that killing his companion would avenge his brother in surplus of it. He fractured the skull of the man of Fihr with a rock, rode off with the camels to Makkah as a disbeliever, and boasted in verse:

> Through him I avenged Fihr and charged his blood-wit to the chiefs of Banū an-Najjār, the lords of Fari castle. By killing him I gave vent to my vengeance and eased my tension, and I was the first to return to the idols.

The verse was revealed, and the Prophet ﷺ sentenced him to death upon the Conquest of Makkah. He was chased and captured in the marketplace and executed.

**"their reward will be Hell"** is the promise, abiding therein for the one who earns it. The gravity of the sin stands in the company it keeps, for the Qur'ān joins it to shirk: "And those who invoke not any other god along with Allah, nor kill such person as Allah has forbidden, except for just cause" (25:68), and again, "Say: Come, I will recite what your Lord has prohibited you from: Join not anything in worship with Him" (6:151), with many verses and hadiths forbidding murder. In the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, Ibn Masʿūd narrates that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that blood offences are the first disputes to be judged between the people on the Day of Resurrection. In a hadith Abū Dāwūd recorded, ʿUbādah ibn aṣ-Ṣāmit states that the believer remains unburdened in righteousness as long as he does not shed prohibited blood, and that when he sheds forbidden blood he becomes burdened. Another hadith states that the destruction of this earthly life is less significant before Allah than killing a Muslim man or woman. For this life, the ruling of even retaliation stands in sūrat al-Baqarah, which requires the deliberate killer to be killed in return, or pardoned against the blood-money. The Sunna then marks out a middle kind between the intentional and the unintentional, the quasi-deliberate, struck with what is not usually lethal, which carries no retaliation but the diyah instead, described as intentional yet treated as unintentional in the fixing of the payment period and the sharing of the burden by the killer's clan, with redemption more urgent in this case and in the intentional one.

**"Allah will be displeased with them, condemn them, and will prepare for them a tremendous punishment"** adds the wrath and the curse, which is removal from mercy, to the prepared chastisement. The warning may be read of the person who deems such killing lawful, or as the requital if he were requited, and it would be nothing new for the threat to be forgone, since He forgives whatever He wills other than the setting of partners with Him.

### Will his repentance be accepted

Ibn ʿAbbās held that the repentance of one who deliberately murders a believer is not accepted. Al-Bukhārī recorded that Ibn Jubayr, finding the people of knowledge of al-Kūfah divided on the question, travelled to Ibn ʿAbbās to ask him, and was told that this verse was the last to be revealed on the subject and nothing abrogated it. Muslim and an-Nasāʾī also recorded it. The majority of the scholars of the earlier and later generations, however, said the killer's repentance can be accepted. If he repents and returns to Allah humbly and submissively, performing righteous deeds, Allah will change his evil deeds into good deeds and compensate the deceased for his loss by rewarding him for his suffering: "Except those who repent and believe, and do righteous deeds" (25:70). That verse is not to be considered abrogated, nor restricted to disbelievers who become Muslims, for that contradicts its general, encompassing indications and would need evidence to support it. And the word goes wider still: "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves! Despair not of the mercy of Allah" (39:53). This verse is general, covering all types of sins, including kufr, shirk, doubt, hypocrisy and murder, so everyone who repents sincerely from any of these errors will be forgiven, for Allah forgives not that partners be set up with Him in worship, but He forgives anything else to whom He wills, a word placed in this surah both before and after this verse to encourage hope. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs carry the story of a man of the Children of Israel who killed one hundred people, then asked a scholar whether repentance was possible for him. The scholar asked what could prevent his repentance and told him to go to another land where Allah was worshipped. The man set out to emigrate there and died on the way, and the angel of mercy was the one to take him. Though the report concerns an Israeli, it fits the Muslim community even more, for Allah relieved the Muslims of the burdens and restrictions placed on the Jews and sent the Prophet ﷺ with the easy way of ḥanīfiyyah, the pure monotheism.

Abū Hurayrah and several among the Salaf read the verse as his punishment if Allah decides to punish him, which is the case with every threat issued for every sin, since good deeds a person has done may hold the punishment back. Even if the murderer enters the Fire, as Ibn ʿAbbās stated for the one whose repentance was not accepted or who lacked saving good deeds, he does not remain there for eternity, but for a long time. The mutawātir hadiths state it plainly:

> Whoever has the least speck of faith in his heart shall ultimately depart the Fire.

The belief of the Ahl as-Sunnah wa'l-Jamāʿah is settled on this: the killer who is a believing Muslim will not remain in Hell eternally, but will be punished in it for a long period and then removed from it through Allah's grace.

### The inner reading

As the slaying of another is forbidden, so is the slaying of yourself, for whoever follows his whims sets out to shed his own blood. Whoever gives no good counsel and admonition to an aspirant, and does not assist him in his resolve, is endeavouring to shed that aspirant's blood. Such a person is held captive by his own state, and it is fitting that his consequence be an unpleasant one, for he should not enjoy the states from which he held others back. The command given of old still runs: O David, when you see someone seeking Me, become a servant to him.

### In short

- The verse answered the crime of Miqyās ibn Ṣubābah, who took the blood-money, killed his escort and fled to the idols, and was executed after the Conquest of Makkah.
- Deliberate murder keeps company with shirk in the Book, heads the judgement list of the Day of Resurrection, and outweighs the ruin of the whole world.
- Ibn ʿAbbās held its repentance barred, the majority held it open, and the verses of hope, with the man of the one hundred killings, weigh for mercy.
- The threat is conditional like every threat, and the believing killer, punished long, leaves the Fire at last with the least speck of faith, as the Ahl as-Sunnah wa'l-Jamāʿah hold.
