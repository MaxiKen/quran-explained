The second of the eight verses on the rules of Ḥajj fixes its time and its etiquette. "˹Commitment to˺ pilgrimage is made in appointed months. Whoever commits to ˹performing˺ pilgrimage, let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage." It continues: "Whatever good you do, Allah ˹fully˺ knows of it. Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness. And be mindful of Me, O people of reason". The previous verse required whoever enters iḥrām for Ḥajj or ʿumrah to complete it. ʿUmrah has no fixed date and may be done at any time of year, but for Ḥajj the months, dates and timings of each act are fixed. So this verse opens by stating that Ḥajj has known months.

### Phrase by phrase

**"˹Commitment to˺ pilgrimage is made in appointed months."**. The word ashhur is the plural of shahr, month. Al-Bukhārī relates that Ibn ʿUmar identified the months as Shawwāl, Dhū al-Qaʿdah and the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, a report whose chain Ibn Jarīr completed and declared authentic and which al-Ḥākim records as meeting the criteria of the Two Shaykhs. Abū Umāmah narrates the same. They have been the months of Ḥajj since the Jāhiliyyah. The same is reported from ʿUmar, as well as ʿAlī and Ibn Masʿūd. Further reports come from ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Zubayr and Ibn ʿAbbās. Further reports come from ʿAṭāʾ, as well as Ṭāwūs and Mujāhid. Further reports come from Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, as well as al-Shaʿbī, as well as al-Ḥasan and Ibn Sīrīn, and from Makḥūl, as well as Qatādah, as well as aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Muzāḥim, as well as al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān. Some say the whole of Dhū al-Ḥijjah is included. Ibn Jarīr preferred the first view, noting that it is common usage to call two months and part of a third "months", just as an Arab says he visited someone this year or this day though he was there for only part of it, and just as the Qur'an speaks of hastening away from Minā in two days when it is really a day and a half. The verse shows that iḥrām for Ḥajj is assumed only in these months, as reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, as well as Jābir, as well as ʿAṭāʾ, as well as Ṭāwūs and Mujāhid. This is because a Ḥajj in specified months cannot be entered before them, just as prayer has a fixed time before which it is not valid. Al-Shāfiʿī records Ibn ʿAbbās saying that no one should assume iḥrām for Ḥajj before its months because of this verse. Ibn Khuzaymah records him saying that it is of the Sunnah of Ḥajj to assume iḥrām in its months, an authentic report. A Companion's statement that something is "of the Sunnah" counts as a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ for the majority, all the more when it comes from Ibn ʿAbbās, the interpreter of the Qur'an. Ibn Marduwayh relates from Jābir that the Prophet ﷺ said no one should assume iḥrām for Ḥajj except in its months, with a reasonable chain, and al-Shāfiʿī and al-Bayhaqī record through Ibn Jurayj that Abū al-Zubayr heard Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh asked whether one enters iḥrām for Ḥajj before its months and answer no, which is more reliable than the marfūʿ version. Since the months begin with Shawwāl, iḥrām for Ḥajj before Shawwāl is not permitted. Some imams hold such a Ḥajj simply invalid, while Abū Ḥanīfah rules it valid but makrūh.

**"Whoever commits to ˹performing˺ pilgrimage"**. Whoever assumes iḥrām in these months, taking the duty upon himself, is bound to complete the rites. Ibn Jarīr relates from al-ʿAwfī that the scholars agree that "commits" here means making it a binding obligation. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that its meaning concerns entering iḥrām for Ḥajj and ʿumrah, as well as ʿAṭāʾ said it means assuming iḥrām, and Ibrāhīm, as well as aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and others said the like.

**"let them stay away from intimate relations"**. Those in iḥrām for Ḥajj or ʿumrah must avoid rafath, meaning intercourse, as in "It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast" (2:187), whatever leads to it, such as embracing, kissing and speaking to women of such things. Ibn Jarīr relates from Nāfiʿ that ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar said rafath is intercourse or mentioning it with the tongue, by men or women. ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ said it is intercourse and foul speech, which is also the view of ʿAmr ibn Dīnār, and ʿAṭāʾ added that they used to forbid even hinting at the subject. Ṭāwūs and Abū al-ʿĀliyah said it includes telling one's wife that one will be with her once iḥrām ends. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that it covers intercourse, kissing, fondling and foul words to one's wife. That rafath means relations with women is the view of Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn ʿUmar. It is also the view of Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, as well as ʿIkrimah and Mujāhid. It is also the view of Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī and Abū al-ʿĀliyah, who related it from ʿAṭāʾ and Makḥūl. It is also the view of ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, as well as ʿAṭāʾ ibn Yasār and ʿAṭiyyah. It is also the view of al-Rabīʿ, as well as al-Zuhrī and al-Suddī. It is also the view of Mālik ibn Anas, as well as Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān and ʿAbd al-Karīm ibn Mālik. It is also the view of al-Ḥasan, as well as Qatādah, as well as aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and others. Rafath is a comprehensive word covering intercourse, its preliminaries and open talk of love-making, all forbidden in iḥrām, though an indirect or implied reference is not prohibited.

**"foul language"**. Literally fusūq means transgression, and in the Qur'an's usage disobedience, which in its general sense includes every sin. Miqsam and others relate from Ibn ʿAbbās that it is disobedience. This is the view of ʿAṭāʾ, as well as Mujāhid, as well as Ṭāwūs and ʿIkrimah. It is also the view of Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, as well as Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb and al-Ḥasan. It is also the view of Qatādah, as well as Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, as well as al-Zuhrī, as well as al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, as well as ʿAṭāʾ ibn Yasār, as well as ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān. Ibn Wahb relates from Nāfiʿ that ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar took it to mean the acts Allah has forbidden in the Sacred Area, that is, what is prohibited specifically in iḥrām, an interpretation well suited to the occasion, since sins in general are forbidden in every state. Others say it means cursing, citing the authentic hadith that cursing a Muslim is fusūq and fighting him is kufr. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Zayd ibn Aslam said it means slaughtering for idols, as in "a sinful offering in the name of any other than Allah" (6:145). Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk said it is insulting one another with bad nicknames. Those who take it as all kinds of disobedience are correct. This is because Allah forbids wrongdoing in the Ḥajj months in particular though it is forbidden all year, saying of the sacred months, "So do not wrong one another during these months" (9:36), of the Sacred Area, "whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it, We will cause them to taste a painful punishment" (22:25). The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that whoever performs Ḥajj to this House without rafath or fusūq returns as sinless as on the day his mother bore him. Things that are not sins in themselves but become impermissible through iḥrām are six: intercourse with its preliminaries and love talk, hunting land game or guiding a hunter, cutting hair or nails, and using perfume, which apply equally to men and women, and for men wearing stitched clothing and covering the head and face, while Abū Ḥanīfah and Mālik also forbid women to cover the face in iḥrām. Intercourse, though included in fusūq, is named separately as rafath to stress its gravity. This is because the other prohibitions can be amended by expiation. By contrast, intercourse before the standing at ʿArafāt nullifies the Ḥajj itself, requires the sacrifice of a cow or camel and obliges the pilgrim to perform the Ḥajj again.

**"and arguments during pilgrimage."**. Jidāl is the effort to overturn an adversary, hence a rough altercation. Ibn Jarīr relates from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd that it means arguing with one's companion until one angers him, which Miqsam and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk relate from Ibn ʿAbbās and which is also reported from Abū al-ʿĀliyah, as well as ʿAṭāʾ, as well as Mujāhid and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr. Further reports come from ʿIkrimah, as well as Jābir ibn Zayd, as well as ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī and Makḥūl. Further reports come from al-Suddī, as well as Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, ʿAmr ibn Dīnār and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk. Reports also come from al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, as well as Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, as well as ʿAṭāʾ ibn Yasār, as well as al-Ḥasan, as well as Qatādah and al-Zuhrī. Some take the word in its general sense, while others, mindful of the place and the state of iḥrām, relate it to a particular quarrel. In the age of ignorance people differed over the place of the standing: some held ʿArafāt necessary, others insisted on Muzdalifah and claimed that Ibrāhīm had stood there. They differed over the time too, some performing Ḥajj in Dhū al-Ḥijjah and others earlier in Dhū al-Qaʿdah, each side accusing the other of error. The Qur'an said "no arguing" and ended all such quarrels, proclaiming the truth that the obligatory standing is at ʿArafāt, the necessary stay at Muzdalifah, and the Ḥajj in no days but those of Dhū al-Ḥijjah. Once the divine command is given quarrelling is forbidden. One reading mentions heated argument about the obligatoriness of the pilgrimage. In one of the recited variants the three nouns are read in the accusative, fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa, an absolute negation, while in the other the first two take the nominative. The sense is the same. On this interpretation the prohibitions are confined to acts otherwise permissible but forbidden by iḥrām, just as eating and drinking are forbidden only in fasting and prayer. Those who take fusūq and jidāl generally reply that although sin and quarrelling are deplorable everywhere, they are graver in iḥrām: in those blessed days and in the sacred land, where everyone comes to worship at his best, chanting labbayk and clothed in a garment that constantly reminds him that he is devoted to worship in Allah's sight, any sin or dispute becomes sinfulness at its worst. The wisdom of forbidding these three in particular is that the time and place of Ḥajj expose people to them: long absence from family in iḥrām and the mingling of men and women in their thousands at the maṭāf, the masʿā, ʿArafāt, as well as Muzdalifah and Minā make desire hard to control. Therefore, obscenity is forbidden first, the crowds devoted to their rites give occasion for sins such as theft, hence "no sin", and the crush of space breeds quarrels, hence "no arguing". The Qur'an's eloquence appears in the phrasing: instead of commanding "do not be obscene, do not sin, do not quarrel", it negates them outright, hinting that such things have no place in Ḥajj and cannot even be imagined there.

**"Whatever good you do, Allah ˹fully˺ knows of it."**. Having forbidden evil in deed and speech, Allah encourages righteous deeds, including voluntary charity, stating that He knows the good they do and will reward them with the best rewards on the Day of Resurrection. Abstaining from sin and from the violations of iḥrām is not enough. The pilgrim should treat the occasion as one that comes once in a lifetime and devote himself to worship, remembrance (dhikr) of Allah and good deeds, assured that every act of virtue is known to Allah and generously rewarded. Whatever one abstains from in the Sacred Precinct, Allah accepts.

**"Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness."**. Al-Bukhārī and Abū Dāwūd record from Ibn ʿAbbās, and ʿIkrimah relates from him, that the people of Yemen used to go to Ḥajj without enough supplies, saying they were the people of tawakkul, reliance on Allah, and then begged in Makkah. Therefore, Allah revealed this verse. ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ said people would travel and lay their burden on others. One account says they took provisions wrongfully from the settlements on their way and were forbidden to do so. Ibn Jarīr and Ibn Marduwayh relate from Ibn ʿUmar that when people assumed iḥrām they would throw away their provisions and seek others. Allah forbade this and required them to carry flour and sawīq. The verse corrects those who set out for Ḥajj and ʿumrah unprepared in the name of trust in Allah and then have to ask for help, or suffer privation and trouble others. Taking provisions is not against tawakkul but is its essence, which is to gather the means Allah has provided to the best of one's ability and then place one's trust in Him, as the Prophet ﷺ explained it. Abandoning all effort to acquire the means of subsistence and calling it tawakkul is sheer ignorance. Having required people to provide for the journeys of this life, Allah directs them to the provision needed for the Hereafter, taqwā, as He says, "the best clothing is righteousness" (7:26), mentioning the material covering first and then the spiritual covering of humility, obedience and taqwā, and declaring the latter better and more beneficial. Taqwā literally means guarding, and with it one guards against asking others. One reading adds that the best provision in this world is trust in Allah. Another that taqwā here is restraint from begging, so that the pilgrim travels at his own expense and burdens no one.

**"And be mindful of Me, O people of reason"**. Fear My torment, punishment and affliction, which fall on those who defy Me and disregard My commands, O people of intellect and understanding, fearing Me above all in the Sacred Precinct.

### The pilgrimage of the heart

In the devotional reading, the pilgrimage of the bodily selves is in known months. Iḥrām binds only in them. Whoever lets that time pass has missed the pilgrimage. Likewise the pilgrimage of hearts has known times and is fitting only in them: the appropriate season is youth. A person lacking spiritual desire (irādah) in his youth has no communion in old age. The one whose season of quest and desire has passed is suited only to the worship whose outcome is the Garden, not the desire whose outcome is communion. The prohibition of lewdness, wickedness and disputing alludes to the traveller on the path of desire, who stops at nothing on the way and mixes nothing with his desire. Whoever argues with him, opposes him or is rude to him, he yields to them all, disputing with no one for this world and competing with no one for worldly fortune, self or honour, as Allah says of His servants that "when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace" (25:63). Content with Allah's knowledge and judgment, he has no need to be seen, judged or known by creatures. Ordinary piety avoids errors. The elect’s piety excludes all but Allah from their hearts’ innermost secrets.

### In short

- Iḥrām for Ḥajj is valid only in its months, Shawwāl, Dhū al-Qaʿdah and the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah (Ibn ʿUmar, as well as Ibn ʿAbbās, as well as Jābir). Abū Ḥanīfah holds an earlier iḥrām valid but makrūh.
- Once committed, the pilgrim must shun rafath (intercourse, its preliminaries and talk of it), fusūq (sin, especially the violations of iḥrām) and jidāl (angry quarrelling, including the old disputes over the place and time of the standing), and a Ḥajj free of these returns him sinless (Abū Hurayrah).
- Intercourse before ʿArafāt nullifies the Ḥajj, which is why it is named separately from the other five iḥrām prohibitions.
- Good deeds are known and rewarded. Provisions must be taken, for tawakkul means using the means and then trusting Allah (the Yemenis, as well as Ibn ʿAbbās). The best provision is taqwā (7:26).
- Inwardly, the heart's pilgrimage has its season in youth. The elect's piety is to keep the heart's secret free of all but Allah (25:63).

<<<VERSE 2:198>>>
Verse 198 removes a scruple about earning during the pilgrimage and then turns to the central rites. "There is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord ˹during this journey˺. When you return from 'Arafât, praise Allah near the sacred place and praise Him for having guided you, for surely before this ˹guidance˺ you were astray".

### Phrase by phrase

**"There is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord ˹during this journey˺."**. Al-Bukhārī records from Ibn ʿAbbās that ʿUkāẓ, Mijannah and Dhū al-Majāz were trading posts in the Jāhiliyyah. The account also states that after Islam people disliked trading during the Ḥajj season until this verse came down. Abū Dāwūd and others record from him that they avoided business during the Ḥajj, saying these were days of dhikr, and Mujāhid relates the same from him, the verse meaning "do engage in trade". This is also the explanation of Mujāhid, together with Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and ʿIkrimah. It is also the view of Manṣūr ibn al-Muʿtamir and Qatādah. It is also the view of Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, together with al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas and others. Ibn Jarīr relates with a strong chain that when Ibn ʿUmar was asked about trade during Ḥajj he recited this verse, and Aḥmad records from Abū Umāmah al-Taymī that he told Ibn ʿUmar that they hired out their camels to pilgrims and walked with them, that they had long done so, and that some claimed their Ḥajj was invalid. Ibn ʿUmar asked whether they recited talbiyah, circled the House and travelled between Ṣafā and Marwah. Did they stand at ʿArafāt, cast pebbles and shave their heads? The man said yes. Ibn ʿUmar recalled a man asking the Prophet ﷺ the same question. No answer came until Jibrīl descended with this verse. The Prophet ﷺ then summoned the man and affirmed that they were pilgrims indeed. Ibn Jarīr relates that Abū Ṣāliḥ asked ʿUmar, as Leader of the Faithful, whether they used to trade during Ḥajj. He replied that their livelihood came from nothing else. In the Jāhiliyyah the Arabs had distorted worship and dealings with absurd customs, turning devotion into amusement and not sparing even the Ḥajj, setting up bazaars, exhibitions and trade promotions at the great gathering of Minā. When Islam swept these away the Companions, ready to stake anything for Allah's pleasure, began to suspect even trading and working for wages during the Ḥajj days as a relic of ignorance that Islam might forbid outright. The verse makes clear that one who earns a profit by trade or wages by work incurs no sin. Yet the two words "the bounty of your Lord" reform the old trade fair: earn if you must, but treat the earning as Allah's favour and be grateful, never making the pilgrimage an exercise in minting money, and "no blame" hints that avoiding such occupation where possible is better still. This is because it touches the perfect sincerity the Ḥajj deserves. Everything turns on intention. If the basic aim is to earn, with the Ḥajj as an adjunct, or if both aims weigh equally, this is contrary to sincerity and reduces the reward and blessings of the Ḥajj. Unforeseen difficulties may leave someone who intended Ḥajj short of funds. Trading or employment to meet those needs does not harm sincerity. Still, keeping the five special ritual days free from work and trade is far better. They should be devoted to worship and dhikr. Some scholars accordingly forbid trade on those days. In the devotional reading, whatever one seeks of Allah's bounty within what He has decreed is praiseworthy if it carries a portion for the Muslims or strengthens the religion, while whatever is sought solely for one's own worldly fortune is flawed.

**"When you return from 'Arafât"**. ʿArafāt, grammatically a plural, is the name of a well-known plain outside the limits of the Ḥaram. It is the place of the standing and a pillar of the rites. Imam Aḥmad and the Sunan compilers record from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Yaʿmar al-Diylī that he heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say three times that Ḥajj is ʿArafāt, that whoever stands at ʿArafāt before dawn has performed the Ḥajj, and that the days of Minā are three, with no sin on whoever leaves after two or stays. The time of standing runs from noon on the day of ʿArafah until dawn on the Day of Sacrifice, the tenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah. At the Farewell Ḥajj the Prophet ﷺ stood after praying ẓuhr until sunset and told the people to learn their rites from him. ʿUrwah ibn Muḍarris ibn Ḥārithah ibn Lām al-Ṭāʾī came to him at Muzdalifah at the time of prayer and said that he had come from the two mountains of Ṭayyiʾ, exhausting himself and his mount, and had stood on every hill he passed. Asked whether his Ḥajj was valid. The Prophet ﷺ replied that whoever prayed this prayer with them, stood with them until they moved on, and had stood at ʿArafāt before that by day or night, had completed his Ḥajj and its rites, a hadith of Aḥmad and the Sunan compilers that al-Tirmidhī graded ṣaḥīḥ. All pilgrims must reach the plain and stay there between noon and sunset. It is the most important obligation of Ḥajj, for which, if missed, there is no substitute by expiation or ransom. As for the name, ʿAbd al-Razzāq relates from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib that Allah sent Jibrīl to Ibrāhīm to teach him the rites, and when Ibrāhīm reached ʿArafāt he said "ʿaraftu", I know this place, having been there before. So it was named. Ibn al-Mubārak relates from ʿAṭāʾ that Jibrīl taught Ibrāhīm the rites and he kept saying "ʿaraftu, ʿaraftu". The like is attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, together with Ibn ʿUmar and Abū Mijlaz. A clearer explanation is that ʿArafāt is the plain where man comes to know his Lord and learns through worship and dhikr how to draw near to Him, and where Muslims from East and West meet and know one another. ʿArafāt is also called al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām, together with al-Mashʿar al-Aqṣā and Ilāl. The hill in its middle is Jabal al-Raḥmah, the Mount of Mercy. Ibn Abī Ḥātim relates from Ibn ʿAbbās that in the Jāhiliyyah people left ʿArafāt when the sun sat on the mountain tops like a turban on a man's head. By contrast, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ delayed until sunset. Ibn Marduwayh adds, with a ḥasan chain, that he then stood at Muzdalifah, prayed fajr early and moved on when dawn had broken. In the long hadith of Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh in Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ remained standing until the sun had set, its yellow glow had faded and its disc had vanished. He seated Usāmah behind him and tightened al-Qaṣwāʾ's nose-rope until her head touched the saddle. With his right hand he gestured for calm progress. At each sandy rise, he slackened the rope until she climbed it. They reached Muzdalifah in this way. There he led maghrib and ʿishāʾ with one adhān and two iqāmahs, without voluntary prayer between them. He lay down until dawn. When the light was clear, he prayed fajr with adhān and iqāmah, then mounted al-Qaṣwāʾ. At al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām he faced the qiblah in supplication and glorification, saying lā ilāha illā Allāh. He remained until daylight was very bright, then moved quickly before sunrise. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record that Usāmah ibn Zayd was asked about the Prophet's ﷺ pace and said it was slow, quickening a little when he found room. Pilgrims should press on together from where the multitude streams forth, with no feeling of superiority over one another.

**"praise Allah near the sacred place"**. On the way back from ʿArafāt it is necessary (wājib) to spend the night at Muzdalifah and remember Allah there, particularly as He has taught. Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām is the name of a mountain in Muzdalifah, also called Quzaḥ, at its far end. Al-mashʿar means a sign or waymark and ḥarām sacred. Therefore, the name marks it as a sacred monument of Islam. The adjoining plain is Muzdalifah. ʿAbd al-Razzāq relates from Ibn ʿUmar that all of Muzdalifah is al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām. He also described it as the mount and its surroundings, while Ibn ʿAbbās, together with Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, together with ʿIkrimah, together with Mujāhid, together with al-Suddī, together with al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, together with al-Ḥasan and Qatādah said it is the area between the two mountains. Imam Aḥmad records from Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim that the Prophet ﷺ said that all of ʿArafāt is a standing place except ʿUranah, all of Muzdalifah is a standing place except the bottom of Muḥassir, all of Makkah is a place of sacrifice. All the days of tashrīq are days of sacrifice. In a hadith of Muslim he stood there remembering Allah and supplicating until his face began to glow. The remembrance includes every kind of dhikr, with heart and tongue, the talbiyah "labbayka Allāhumma labbayk", the tahlīl and supplication. But it refers particularly to offering maghrib and ʿishāʾ together at Muzdalifah, the act of worship peculiar to that plain, which the next words seem to indicate.

**"and praise Him for having guided you"**. Remember Him in the manner He has taught, without adulterating it with personal opinion, for personal reasoning would demand that maghrib be prayed at its own time and ʿishāʾ at its own. By contrast, on that night Allah prefers maghrib delayed and joined with ʿishāʾ. From this emerges the basic ruling that man is not independent in worship and remembrance: he may not worship as he wishes or remember Allah as he chooses, but every act of worship and dhikr has its etiquette and must be performed as stipulated. Allah does not like addition, subtraction or change of time or place, even if the change seems to increase the worship. People tend to add features to voluntary prayers and charity without any reason approved by the Sharīʿah, then treat these as necessary though Allah and His Messenger ﷺ did not, and even regard those who omit them as in error. This verse exposes their position, for such invented forms of worship reflect the Jāhiliyyah, when the disbelievers concocted rites from their own opinions and reduced a serious act to a few customs. The verse reminds the Muslims of Allah's favour in teaching them the rites of Ḥajj according to the guidance of Ibrāhīm al-Khalīl, His religion’s chief rites and the observances of His pilgrimage, the particle "as" giving the reason for the remembrance.

**"for surely before this ˹guidance˺ you were astray"**. Before the guidance, the Qur'an, the Messenger ﷺ and Islam, you were among the misguided and the disbelievers, unaware of the sacred laws. Each of these meanings is correct. In the devotional reading, having gone so far as to perform what He asked, remember His favour, for had He not desired you, you would not have desired Him, and had He not chosen you, you would not have preferred His good pleasure.

### The spirit of the rites

The sequence of the verse follows the sequence of the days. The standing at ʿArafāt falls on the ninth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, the night that follows is spent at Muzdalifah, and the morning of the tenth, the Day of Sacrifice, begins with the remembrance at al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām before the pilgrims move on to Minā. Each station has its own act: the standing and supplication at ʿArafāt, the combined prayers and the night at Muzdalifah, and the dhikr at the sacred waymark until the light is bright. Fear of Allah is the basic element of worship, and as long as the pilgrim keeps that frame of mind it does not matter if he mistakes the order of a rite or attends to some private business in the holy land. What matters is entering into the spirit of the pilgrimage, fear of Allah, remembrance, prayerfulness, thanksgiving and wholehearted submission, and doing nothing that runs counter to it. This is because the lesson of the Ḥajj is the equality of all before the one supreme God. Whoever does not absorb it there is unlikely to apply it in the rest of his life.

### In short

- Trading and working for wages during Ḥajj are lawful (Ibn ʿAbbās on the fairs of ʿUkāẓ and Dhū al-Majāz, together with Ibn ʿUmar and the camel-hirers), but earnings should be received as Allah's bounty. The days of the rites are best kept free for worship.
- Ḥajj is ʿArafāt (ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Yaʿmar, together with ʿUrwah ibn Muḍarris): the standing from noon to sunset on the ninth, with no substitute if missed, named from Ibrāhīm's "ʿaraftu".
- The Prophet ﷺ left ʿArafāt only after sunset, unlike the Jāhiliyyah, and at Muzdalifah joined maghrib and ʿishāʾ, stayed the night and stood at al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām in remembrance until just before sunrise (Jābir, Usāmah).
- Dhikr there must follow the form Allah taught, which shows that worship may not be altered by personal opinion, the mark of Jāhilī religion.
- This guidance, after former misguidance, is Allah's favour, and inwardly the servant desired Him only because He first desired the servant.
