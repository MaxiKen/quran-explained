After a long account of the Israelites' defiance and its punishment, verse 62 states the rule that governs everyone. "Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians", it says, naming four communities, and then lays down one condition for all of them: whoever "believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve". Having described the fate of those who defy His commands, fall into what He forbade and overstep His limits, Allah now makes clear that the righteous and obedient among the earlier nations received the reward of their good deeds, and that this remains true until the Day of Judgement for everyone who meets the condition.

### Why the verse comes here

The previous verse told how the Israelites drew Allah's wrath on themselves through habitual insolence and disobedience. A listener, or a Jew hearing it, might conclude that after such transgression no repentance of theirs could be accepted. This verse removes that misgiving by stating a general principle. Whatever a person's past conduct, once he submits himself fully to Allah's commands in both belief and deed he is acceptable to Allah and will receive his reward. Since the Qur'an is Allah's final message, full obedience after its revelation can only mean accepting Islam and following the Last Prophet, Muḥammad ﷺ. The verse therefore assures everyone that on accepting Islam all his former transgressions, whether of belief or of action, are forgiven and he becomes worthy of the rewards of the next world. Whoever follows the unlettered Prophet and Messenger ﷺ gains lasting happiness, with no fear of what is to come and no sorrow over what is gone.

### The occasion of revelation

The reports connect the verse with Salmān al-Fārisī and the monks he had lived among before reaching the Prophet ﷺ. Mujāhid related that when Salmān told the Prophet ﷺ the story of those monks, the Prophet ﷺ said that they would be in the Fire. Salmān said that the whole earth turned dark for him, until this verse was revealed, from "Indeed, the believers" to "nor will they grieve", and it was as though a mountain had been lifted off him. As-Suddī gave the fuller form. The verse came down about the monks whose company Salmān al-Fārisī had kept. When he came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ he described their worship and their striving: they prayed and fasted, he said, they believed in you, and they testified that you would be sent as a prophet. When he had finished praising them the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, "Salmān, they are among the people of the Fire", and then Allah revealed this verse. A report traced through Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd, and from other Companions, says that the verse was revealed about Salmān al-Fārisī, a notable of Jundishapur, and that what follows it was revealed about the Jews.

### Phrase by phrase

**"the believers"**. On one reading these are those who believed in the prophets before the coming of Muḥammad ﷺ, that is, those among the earlier peoples who believed in Mūsā (Moses) and all the other prophets. On another, they are those who believe in what was revealed to Muḥammad ﷺ. When Allah sent him as the last Prophet and Messenger to all the children of Ādam (Adam), mankind became obliged to believe in him, obey him and refrain from what he forbade, and those who do so are the true believers. His Ummah was given the name muʾminūn, believers, because of the depth of its faith and certainty and because it believes in all the previous prophets and in the matters of the unseen.

**"Jews"**. The Jews are the followers of Mūsā who took the Torah as their reference for judgement. Scholars offer several accounts of the name Yahūd. It can be derived from a word meaning to turn back in repentance, as in Mūsā's prayer, "we have turned (hudnā) to You", so that they were called Yahūd because they repented, or because they were kind to one another. It was also said that the name comes from Yahūdhā (Judah), the eldest son of Yaʿqūb (Jacob). Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ said they were so called because they swayed when they read the Torah. One early commentary describes those meant here as the people who deviated from the religion of Mūsā.

**"Christians"**. When ʿĪsā (Jesus) was sent, the Children of Israel were required to follow and obey him, and his followers and companions are called an-Naṣārā because they gave help and support to one another. They are also called Anṣār, helpers, from the words of the disciples, al-Ḥawāriyyūn, when ʿĪsā, son of Maryam, asked who would stand up with him for Allah and they answered that they would (61:14). It was also said that they took the name from a land called an-Nāṣirah (Nazareth), a view reported from Qatādah, Ibn Jurayj and Ibn ʿAbbās. Allah knows best. Naṣārā is the plural of Naṣrān.

**"and Sabians"**. Who the Ṣābiʾūn were is disputed. Sufyān ath-Thawrī related from Layth ibn Abī Sulaym that Mujāhid said the Sabians stand between the Magians, the Jews and the Christians and have no specific religion, and the like is reported from Ibn Abī Najīḥ, ʿAṭāʾ and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr. Others said they were a sect of the People of the Book who read the Zabūr (the Psalms), and others that they were a people who worshipped the angels or the stars. One early commentary calls them a Christian sect whose members shaved the middle of their heads, read the Gospel, venerated the angels and said, "our hearts have turned (ṣabaʾat) to Allah". Another calls them a Christian or Jewish sect, and another a group said to have followed prophets on the faith of Ibrāhīm (Abraham). A modern writer identifies them with followers of Yaḥyā (John the Baptist) who lived in ancient Iraq, counted as People of the Book, prayed facing the Kaʿbah in Makkah, and are now extinct. Other scholars admit that nothing is definitely known about their beliefs and practices, and think it most probable that they worshipped the stars. The view that seems nearest the truth, and Allah knows best, is Mujāhid's, shared by Wahb ibn Munabbih: the Sabians were neither Jews, Christians, Magians nor polytheists. They had no particular religion that they followed and enforced, but went on living according to their natural disposition (fiṭrah). This is why the idolaters of Makkah used to call anyone who embraced Islam a Ṣābiʾ, meaning one who had left every religion on earth. Some scholars added that the Sabians are those whom no prophet's message ever reached.

**"whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good"** means whoever from among these groups believes, in the time of our Prophet ﷺ, and performs righteous deeds according to the Law given to him, or, as another gloss puts it, does right in what lies between him and his Lord. The grammar is noted: the verbs "believes" and "does" are singular because "whoever" is singular, while the pronouns that follow are plural because the meaning is plural. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah related that Ibn ʿAbbās, commenting on this clause, said that Allah afterwards revealed, "Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them" (3:85). His statement means that Allah accepts no deed or work from anyone unless it conforms to the law of Muḥammad ﷺ, now that He has sent him. Before his coming, everyone who followed the guidance of his own prophet was on the right path, rightly guided and saved. So the verse describes the position before Muḥammad ﷺ was sent, and since then Islam, devotion and dedication to Allah alone, is the only way of life Allah accepts.

**"will have their reward with their Lord"** means the reward of their deeds awaits them with Him, in Paradise.

**"And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve"**. They will have no fear of the torment to come and no grief over anything they left behind. The same promise is made to the close servants of Allah, who will have no fear nor grieve (10:62), and the angels announce it to the dying believer who said "Our Lord is Allah" and stood firm: do not fear, do not grieve, rejoice in the Paradise you were promised (41:30). One commentary adds a further sense: they will have no fear on the day when death itself is slain and Hell is closed, and their security is everlasting.

### Only two articles of faith?

The verse names only faith in Allah and faith in the Last Day, and some have taken this to mean that these two suffice for salvation. Scholars reject that reading. The Qur'an repeatedly declares that whoever does not believe in the prophets, the angels and the Books of Allah is not a Muslim. Faith in Allah is the first article of the Islamic creed and faith in the Day of Judgement the last, and by naming the first and the last the verse says compactly that the whole creed, from beginning to end, is required. There is a further reason. A person can know anything about the essence and attributes of Allah, or about what will happen on the Day of Judgement, only through the prophets and the Books of Allah, and those Books reach the prophets through an angel. Faith in Allah and the Last Day is therefore impossible without faith in the angels, the Books and the prophets.

### Why mention the Muslims in an invitation to Islam?

If the verse invites people to Islam, why does it name those who have already accepted it? Scholars answer with an analogy. A king in a similar position might declare that his laws apply impartially to all his subjects, and that whoever obeys them will be rewarded regardless of whether he was formerly a friend or a foe. The friend has always been loyal, so the warning and the promise are really aimed at the foe, but the form of words carries a message of its own: the king's favour does not flow from personal attachment to his friends but depends on the quality of obedience and loyalty, so the foe too can earn it by acquiring that quality. That is why the Muslims are named alongside the non-Muslims, and it should never be taken to imply that salvation can be reached without accepting Islam.

A modern commentator draws out the same point from the other side. The Muslims are not set apart here but grouped with the other communities associated with prophets, which shows that no community is inherently superior to another in God's sight and that only true belief and righteous action are rewarded with salvation. Belonging to a particular community confers no status with God. The people who gather round a prophet in his lifetime live by true belief and righteous action: they hear his call, their spirits are stirred, an intellectual revolution takes place inside them, and their whole pattern of life changes from the rule of personal desire to the rule of God's teaching. These are the people to whom the prophets gave glad tidings of eternal blessing. As generations pass, religion turns into a kind of national inheritance. Tidings that were given on the basis of faith and deeds come to be treated as the fruit of ethnic affiliation, and people begin to assume a special relationship with God that others do not share, so that anyone born into the community is assured of salvation whatever his faith and deeds amount to: paradise is for us, hell is for them. God has no such relationship with any community. He looks only at a person's thoughts and deeds, and in the Hereafter people will be judged by their deeds and character, not by the group they belong to.

### The misreading to avoid

The verse has been pressed into service by some modern writers, pleased with their own liberalism and tolerance, to claim that it opens salvation to every "person of good will" whatever creed he follows. Read in its context and alongside the other relevant verses of the Qur'an, it does no such thing. It promises salvation in the next world to those who accept Islam, and it is an invitation extended to the Jews, the Christians, the Sabians, and in fact to the followers of every religion and to unbelievers too. The specific names serve only as examples.

### The devotional reading

The mystical commentary reads the verse as a statement about the breadth of divine acceptance. The diversity of paths, given the unity of their source, does not stand in the way of a gracious acceptance. For anyone who affirms the Real through His signs and believes what He has said about His truth and His attributes, the differences between laws and the variety of names under which religion appears do not decide who merits His good pleasure. That is why the verse joins "the believers" with "the Jews" and the rest, and then says "whoever believes", meaning that if they fear God in their different ways of knowing Him, all of them have a beautiful homecoming and an ample reward. The believer, muʾmin, is anyone who is under the protection (amān) of the Real, and for someone under His protection it is only fitting that no fear should touch him and no grief. Scholars read this, like the rest of the verse, in the light of Ibn ʿAbbās's statement and the verse of Āl ʿImrān (3:85): the acceptance is real, and the way into it, since the coming of Muḥammad ﷺ, is his religion.

### In short

- The verse states the rule for every community: faith in Allah and the Last Day together with good deeds earns reward and security, whatever a person's past.
- It was revealed when Salmān al-Fārisī grieved over the fate of the monks he had known.
- Ibn ʿAbbās explained that it was followed by 3:85, so that since the sending of Muḥammad ﷺ only Islam is accepted, while before him each people was saved by following its own prophet.
- The Sabians are variously described, most plausibly as a people with no fixed religion living by their fiṭrah, which is why the Makkans called converts to Islam "Sabians".
- Naming the first and last articles of faith stands for the whole creed, and naming the Muslims shows that reward depends on obedience, not on belonging.
