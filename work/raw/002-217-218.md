<<<VERSE 2:217>>>
This verse opens with a question raised by disbelievers of Quraysh after a raiding party of Companions killed a man on a day that turned out to belong to a sacred month. "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months." The answer begins: "Fighting during these months is a great sin. But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing." It continues: "And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can. And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."

### Phrase by phrase

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months."**. Ibn Abī Ḥātim relates from Jundub ibn ʿAbdullāh that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ assembled a party under Abū ʿUbaydah ibn al-Jarrāḥ, who wept at the thought of parting from him, so the Prophet ﷺ relieved him and appointed ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh instead, giving him a sealed letter not to be opened until he reached a certain place and telling him to compel none of his men to go on. When ʿAbdullāh read it he said the words of istirjāʿ, "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return" (2:156), declared that he heard and obeyed Allah and His Messenger, and read it to his companions, two of whom turned back while the rest went on. They came upon Ibn al-Ḥaḍramī of Quraysh and killed him, not knowing whether the day was in Rajab or in Jumādā, and the polytheists accused the Muslims of murder in the sacred month, whereupon this verse was revealed. Ibn Isḥāq al-Madanī, as transmitted by Ziyād al-Bakkāʾī to ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Hishām, gives the fuller account. In Rajab, after returning from the first expedition of Badr, the Prophet ﷺ sent ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh ibn Riʾāb al-Asadī with eight men, all Emigrants and none of the Anṣār, ordering him to march two days before reading his instructions and to force no one to accompany him. From Banū ʿAbd Shams ibn ʿAbd Manāf there was Abū Ḥudhayfah ibn ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, and of their allies ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh himself, the commander, and ʿUkkāshah ibn Miḥṣan of Banū Asad ibn Khuzaymah. ʿUtbah ibn Ghazwān ibn Jābir was an ally of Banū Nawfal ibn ʿAbd Manāf, Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ was of Banū Zuhrah ibn Kilāb, and the allies of Banū Kaʿb included ʿĀdī ibn ʿAmr ibn al-Rabīʿah, who was not of the line of Ibn Wāʾil, Wāqid ibn ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbd Manāf ibn ʿArīn ibn Thaʿlabah ibn Yarbūʿ of Banū Tamīm, and Khālid ibn Bukayr of Banū Saʿd ibn Layth, while Suhayl ibn Bayḍāʾ of Banū al-Ḥārith ibn Fihr was also with them. After two days' march ʿAbdullāh opened the letter and found the order to go on to Nakhlah, between Makkah and al-Ṭāʾif, and there to watch the caravan of Quraysh and gather news of them. He said that he heard and obeyed, told his companions that he had been forbidden to force anyone, that those who sought martyrdom should come with him and those who disliked it should turn back, and that he himself would carry out the command, and not one of them turned back. In the Ḥijāz, at Buḥrān near al-Furuʿ, Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ and ʿUtbah ibn Ghazwān lost the camel they rode by turns and went back to look for it, while the rest reached Nakhlah. There a caravan of Quraysh passed carrying raisins, provisions and merchandise, with ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī, whose name was ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbād, ʿUthmān ibn ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Mughīrah and his brother Nawfal of Makhzūm, and al-Ḥakam ibn Kaysān, a freedman of Hishām ibn al-Mughīrah. The travellers were alarmed at the sight of the Companions until they noticed ʿUkkāshah's shaven head and concluded that these were pilgrims for ʿumrah with nothing to fear. The Companions conferred. It was, they believed, the last day of Rajab, and they said that if they let the caravan pass it would reach the Sacred Area and be safe from them, while if they attacked they would be killing in the sacred month. They hesitated, then encouraged one another and resolved to kill whom they could and seize what the caravan carried. Wāqid ibn ʿAbdullāh al-Tamīmī shot ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī dead with an arrow, ʿUthmān and al-Ḥakam surrendered, and Nawfal escaped, and the party returned to al-Madīnah with the caravan and the two prisoners. Ibn Isḥāq adds, from the family of ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh, that ʿAbdullāh set aside a fifth of the booty for the Messenger of Allah ﷺ before the fifth had been prescribed and divided the rest among his men. When they arrived the Prophet ﷺ told them he had not commanded them to fight in the sacred month, and he left the caravan and the prisoners untouched and took no share. The raiders were stricken and thought themselves ruined, their Muslim brethren reproached them, and the Quraysh declared that Muhammad ﷺ and his Companions had violated the sacred month, shedding blood, seizing property and taking captives in it, while the Muslims still in Makkah answered that it had happened in Shaʿbān. The Jews rejoiced and read omens in the names: ʿAmr meant the war had begun, al-Ḥaḍramī that war had come, and Wāqid that it had flared up, but Allah turned all of this against them. Al-Zuhrī relates from ʿUrwah ibn al-Zubayr that the day was the last of the sacred month, that some of the party said they knew for certain the day belonged to the sacred month and should not be violated out of greed, but that the view of those who wanted worldly gain prevailed, that Ibn al-Ḥaḍramī was the first man killed in fighting between Muslims and disbelievers, and that the Quraysh sent a delegation to ask the Prophet ﷺ whether he permitted fighting in the sacred month. Al-Zuhrī also names the killer as ʿAbdullāh ibn Wāqid al-Laythī and dates the killing to the last day of Rajab. Another account says the Muslims thought it the thirtieth of Jumādā al-Thānī, but that month had only twenty-nine days that year, so the new moon had risen the evening before and it was in fact the first of Rajab, in the year 2 AH. One early gloss confines the question to warfare in Rajab. Arab feeling ran high over the age-old respect for the sacred months, and this unintentional violation let the opponents taunt the Prophet ﷺ and his followers for not respecting even them.

**"Fighting during these months is a great sin."**. Fighting in Rajab, Dhū al-Qaʿdah, Dhū al-Ḥijjah and Muḥarram is a grave matter, heinous in sin and deserving great punishment. In the grammar, "fighting in it" is an inclusive substitution for "the sacred month", "fighting" is the subject and "great" its predicate. The Muslims had not acted deliberately, but through a misunderstanding about the date, and the verse concedes the principle while refuting the accusers. This verse proves that fighting is forbidden in the four sacred months, as do other verses, such as "of which four are sacred. That is the Right Way" (9:36), and the Prophet's ﷺ words in the sermon of the Farewell Ḥajj that four of the months are sacred, three consecutive and the fourth the Rajab of Muḍar. ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ swore that this injunction stands for ever, and several other Successors regard it as unabrogated, but according to al-Jaṣṣāṣ the majority of jurists hold it abrogated by consensus, so that fighting is no longer forbidden in any month. They differ over what abrogates it: some name "fight the polytheists together" in 9:36, many the verse "kill the polytheists ˹who violated their treaties˺ wherever you find them" (9:5), taking "wherever" to include whatever month or time, and others the Prophet's ﷺ own conduct, since he besieged al-Ṭāʾif in the sacred months and sent Abū ʿĀmir al-Ashʿarī on the expedition of Awṭās during them. Al-Bayḍāwī, on the opening of Sūrat al-Barāʾah, and Rūḥ al-Maʿānī on this verse report consensus on the abrogation. Al-Maẓharī answers all these arguments: the sanctity of the sacred months is plainly affirmed in the very Verse of the Sword, which states that the months with Allah are twelve, of which four are sacred, and the Farewell Sermon, delivered only eighty days before the Prophet ﷺ died, affirmed it clearly, so these verses cannot be its abrogators, and the siege of al-Ṭāʾif took place in Shawwāl, not Dhū al-Qaʿdah. What can be conceded is that the absolute prohibition admits an exception allowing Muslims to defend themselves and counter-attack if the disbelievers start fighting in these months, and this much is abrogated and clarified by "a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month" (2:194). The gist is that initiating fighting in these months is forbidden for ever, but if disbelievers attack, the Muslims may fight back in defence. Al-Jaṣṣāṣ reports from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh that the Prophet ﷺ never fought in a sacred month unless the disbelievers began the fighting.

**"But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah."**. If the Muslims had killed in the sacred month, the disbelievers of Quraysh had hindered people from the path of Allah, His religion and obedience, disbelieved in Him, barred the believers from the Sacred Mosque and expelled its own people, the Prophet ﷺ and the believers, from it, which is a greater sin with Allah than killing the man they killed, deserving a greater punishment than the killing of ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī. Disbelief, setting up idols in the Holy Mosque and driving the Muslims out of it are a greater evil than killing a disbeliever in a state of war. Nothing is more abhorrent to Allah than a man steeped in great crimes trying to discredit another by magnifying his minor mistake. When the call to truth was raised among the Quraysh they not only denied it but sought to stop others following it, closing the doors of Allah's House to His servants, turning the Prophet's ﷺ followers out of their homes and tormenting them with extreme cruelty to force them to forsake their faith.

**"For persecution is far worse than killing."**. Al-fitnah, trying to force the Muslims back into disbelief after they had believed, is worse with Allah than killing, and the idolatry of the Quraysh is worse than the slaying of ʿAmr. Persecuting a person for his faith is a crime worse than any other in the eyes of Allah. In the devotional reading, some acts of disobedience are worse and more intractable than others, and bad manners at the door do not carry the consequences they do on the intimate carpet. When the lower self errs its punishment is deferred, the burning of the Hereafter, but when the heart errs the punishment is immediate, separation, and heedlessness in hearts is graver than the harm of error to the lower self, for the lower self is kept from its good fortune while the heart is kept from the Real.

**"And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can."**. The people of Makkah will go on fighting with unrelenting viciousness in order to turn the believers from Islam to disbelief, from the path of truth to their false path, if they are able. In the devotional reading, this alludes to the people of heedlessness who seek to lure you towards their heedlessness and are content only when you break the pact of your spiritual desire by returning to your former state, and whoever breaks his pledge to Allah deforms his heart.

**"And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter."**. Whoever renounces Islam and dies in disbelief has his works thwarted and his good deeds rejected, so that they count for nothing and yield no reward. Death in disbelief is specified as the condition because, according to al-Shāfiʿī, one who returns to Islam does not have his earlier deeds invalidated, is rewarded for them and need not repeat such deeds as the Ḥajj. Several rulings concern apostates. Deeds going to waste in this world means that the apostate's wife passes out of the bond of marriage, that he inherits nothing from a relative who dies a Muslim, that all the prayers and fasts of his Muslim years are reduced to nothing, and that no funeral prayer is offered for him and he is not buried in the Muslims' graveyard, while in the Hereafter it means no reward for his worship and eternal Hell. If an apostate becomes a Muslim again, he can at least hope to be saved from Hell and the injunctions of Islam apply to him for the rest of his life, but the jurists differ over whether a man who has already performed Ḥajj must repeat it if able, and whether the reward of his earlier prayers and fasts returns to him in the Hereafter: Abū Ḥanīfah holds that he must repeat the Ḥajj and does not accept that he is rewarded for his earlier worship, while al-Shāfiʿī differs on both points. For an original disbeliever, the reward of good deeds done in disbelief is held in abeyance: if he embraces Islam he receives a matching reward for them, as the hadith says that one embraces Islam with all the good one did before, but if he dies a disbeliever everything is lost. The fate of the apostate is thus worse than that of the original disbeliever, which is why jizyah may be accepted from the latter while a male apostate who does not return to Islam is killed and a female apostate imprisoned for life, since their conduct insults Islam and an insult to so binding an authority deserves no lesser punishment.

**"It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."**. They are the people of Hell, abiding in it for ever, neither dying nor leaving it.

### In short

- The raid of ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh at Nakhlah, in which Wāqid ibn ʿAbdullāh killed ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī and two prisoners and a caravan were taken, fell on a day the raiders took for the end of Jumādā but which was in Rajab, and the Quraysh accused the Muslims of violating the sacred month (Jundub, Ibn Isḥāq, ʿUrwah via al-Zuhrī).
- Fighting in the four sacred months is a grave sin, and whether the ban is abrogated (al-Jaṣṣāṣ on the majority, 9:5, 9:36, al-Ṭāʾif and Awṭās) or stands with an exception for self-defence (ʿAṭāʾ, al-Maẓharī, 2:194, Jābir) is disputed, the latter view holding that only initiating fighting remains forbidden.
- The Quraysh's own crimes, barring the path of Allah, disbelief, expelling the people of the Sacred Mosque and persecuting believers for their faith, are far graver than the killing.
- The disbelievers will never stop trying to turn the believers from their religion, and an apostate who dies in disbelief loses everything in both worlds, with the rulings on marriage, inheritance, burial, repeated Ḥajj (Abū Ḥanīfah against al-Shāfiʿī) and penalty that follow.

<<<VERSE 2:218>>>
The raiders of Nakhlah were relieved of their fear of sin but still asked whether their action earned reward. This verse gave them hope: "Surely those who have believed, emigrated, and struggled in the Way of Allah" may expect His mercy. Forgiveness and reward remain in Allah's hands.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Surely those who have believed, emigrated, and struggled in the Way of Allah"** The description joins belief in Allah and His Messenger with leaving home and striving for His religion. The believers emigrated from Makkah to al-Madīnah. One early report connects their striving with the killing of ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī.

**"they can hope for Allah's mercy."** Their faith and effort give grounds for expecting His compassion, reward, forgiveness and Garden. The words answer the raiders' fear that release from blame might still leave them without reward.

**"And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** Allah forgives His believing servants and treats them with mercy. The raiders were not punished for the incident. Their pardon illustrates the closing names without making reward independent of Him.

### What happened to the prisoners?

Ibn Isḥāq relates that the Qur'anic answer lifted the Muslims' sorrow. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ then accepted the captured caravan and its two prisoners. Quraysh sought to ransom ʿUthmān ibn ʿAbdullāh and al-Ḥakam ibn Kaysān.

He first required the safe return of Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ and ʿUtbah ibn Ghazwān. He feared for them and warned that killing them would lead to the killing of the prisoners. Once they returned, he accepted the ransom.

Al-Ḥakam ibn Kaysān became a committed Muslim and remained with the Prophet ﷺ. He later died as a martyr at Biʾr Maʿūnah. The account describes seventy Companions sent to Najd to teach Islam, whom Banū Sulaym killed except for two. ʿUthmān ibn ʿAbdullāh instead returned to Makkah and died a disbeliever.

ʿAbdullāh ibn Jaḥsh and his companions then asked whether the raid would count as a battle earning the fighters' reward. They had thought that pardon for killing in the sacred month might bring no further recompense. Revelation raised their hopes.

### What does persecution disclose?

The account connects striving with repeated attacks and obstruction of worship. Persecuted believers may be driven from home and compelled to defend themselves with arms. Their emigration and peaceful missionary effort also show devotion under pressure.

This process separates people by their responses. Persecutors expose selfish aims and lack of fear of Allah. Among the persecuted, some retain faith and trust through torment, while others abandon them. The trial reveals persistence in trust and devotion.

### What is outward and inward emigration?

The devotional reading places emigration after faith and gives it outward and inward forms. Outwardly, one leaves home and means to seek knowledge. Another journey concerns seeking the Known. Travelling outside these purposes is given no weight in this reading. A saying of the Prophet ﷺ distinguishes knowers and learners from the remaining rabble.

Seeking knowledge and seeking the Known are not treated as equal states. The seeker of knowledge travels through personal effort and experiences hunger, suffering and distress. Moses' request for food during his exhausting journey illustrates this in (18:62).

The seeker of the Known is drawn by the Real. In the corresponding image, Moses waits thirty days for Allah's speech, sustained by divine protection without weariness or hunger.

Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq describes the honour of seekers of knowledge through the angels' feathers. He pictures seekers rising from the dust tomorrow and riding on those feathers. He recalls the Prophet's ﷺ saying that angels lower their wings in approval of the seeker. He then asks what greater conveyance might be imagined for seekers of the Known. The contrast expresses divine favour.

Inward emigration begins by leaving the soul for the heart. The next stages lead to the secret core and then to the spirit. The final passage is from the spirit to the Real. The stages portray release from inward confinement while the outward acts of belief and striving retain their meaning.

### In short

- Ibn Isḥāq's account describes the prisoners' ransom and their different later lives.
- The raiders received grounds to hope for reward as well as forgiveness.
- Persecution reveals whether trust and devotion endure under pressure.
- The inward reading distinguishes journeys seeking knowledge and the Known, and a passage through soul, heart, secret and spirit.
