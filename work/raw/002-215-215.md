<<<VERSE 2:215>>>
The previous verses gave emphatic commands: leave disbelief and hypocrisy and enter Islam completely, heed no one against the command of Allah, spend wealth and give life for His pleasure, and show fortitude in every hardship. From here the surah turns to details of that obedience concerning wealth, life, marriage and divorce, all within the continuing theme of righteousness. "They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say, "Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah."".

### Phrase by phrase

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate."**. These details are special in that they answer questions the Companions put to the Prophet ﷺ, with the answer coming directly from Allah through him, so that in effect the ruling (fatwā) was given by Allah Himself, which is unremarkable, since in the verse "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding women. Say, "It is Allah Who instructs you regarding them"" (4:127) Allah attributes the giving of rulings to Himself. One may also say the rulings are the Prophet's ﷺ, conveyed to him by revelation. Such question-and-answer injunctions occur at nearly seventeen places in the Qur'an. Seven are here in al-Baqarah, one in al-Māʾidah and one in al-Anfāl, these nine being questions of the Companions, while two in al-Aʿrāf and one each in Banī Isrāʾīl, al-Kahf, Ṭā Hā and al-Nāziʿāt, six in all, were asked by disbelievers, and the Qur'an answers them all. Ibn ʿAbbās said that he had never seen a people better than the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ, who for all their attachment to the faith and their closeness to him asked very few questions, only about thirteen matters answered in the Qur'an, because they never asked unless it was absolutely necessary. The question here, "what they should spend", is repeated in the same words in verse 219, yet the two answers differ, and the wisdom of this appears from the backgrounds. The present verse was revealed when ʿAmr ibn al-Jamūḥ, a wealthy elderly man, came to ask the Prophet ﷺ what he should spend of his wealth and on whom, as Ibn al-Mundhir relates, though according to a report of Ibn Jarīr the question came from the Muslims in general. It therefore has two parts, what and how much to spend, and where it should go. The question in verse 219, as Ibn Abī Ḥātim relates, arose when the command to spend in the way of Allah was revealed and some Companions asked for an elaboration of what money or thing they should spend, a single point of inquiry. The answer here gives more weight to the second part, the recipients, which is answered frontally and clearly, while the first part is answered as a corollary, and the later verse, restricted to what should be spent, is answered with "the surplus". Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said this verse was revealed about voluntary charity, and Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid explained the question as how they should spend. One early report notes that this was before the verse of inheritance was revealed.

**"Say, "Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers."**. "Of good" explains "whatever", covering small and large amounts, and this concerns the half of the question represented by the giver, after which Allah addresses the other half, the receiver. Any permissible and pure wealth you spend should go to parents, close relatives in need, needy orphans, the poor who have nothing, and the traveller cut off from family and home, the guest who happens by, for these are the most deserving of it. A hadith lists those with the greatest claim on one's generosity as one's mother, father, sister and brother, then the nearest and then the next nearest relatives. Maymūn ibn Mihrān once recited this verse and remarked that these are the proper channels of spending, and that Allah did not mention among them drums, pipes, wooden images or the hangings that cover walls. One early report says that giving charity to parents was later abrogated by the verse of inheritance. Several rulings follow. First, these two verses do not concern the obligatory zakāh, whose threshold of holdings and obligatory rate were fully fixed through the Prophet ﷺ, neither of which is specified here, so the verses refer to voluntary charities (al-ṣadaqāt al-nāfilah). This also removes the doubt raised by the inclusion of parents, to whom zakāh may not be given under the Prophet's ﷺ teaching, since the verses have nothing to do with zakāh. Second, even a gift given or food served to parents and other near relatives counts as spending in the way of Allah and earns reward with Him if the intention is to obey Him. Third, voluntary charity should come only from what is surplus to personal needs. Spending in a way that hurts one's own family, overrides their rights and reduces them to straitened circumstances is no act of merit, and one who leaves his debts unpaid while squandering money on voluntary charities is not liked in the sight of Allah. Abū Dharr al-Ghifārī and others took the statement about spending the surplus (al-ʿafw) as an obligation, holding that it is not permissible to keep any money or goods beyond one's needs even after paying zakāh and discharging all dues, and that giving everything extra as charity (ṣadaqah) is necessary (wājib). But the majority of the Companions, their successors and the great imams understood the verses to mean that whatever is spent in the way of Allah should be surplus to needs, not that all surplus must be given away as an obligation, and the consistent practice of the Companions bears this out.

**"Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.""**. Whatever good works you perform, by spending on these or otherwise, Allah knows them, as He knows your intention, and will reward you for them in the best manner, no one being wronged even by the weight of an atom. Nothing is hidden from Him, however small. The words also answer the first part of the question as a corollary: Allah has placed no restriction on the amount, and whatever you spend according to your capacity will earn a matching reward with Him.

### Spending for others

Man thinks the best use of his life and wealth is for the benefit of his family, and is happy to spend on his own desires and ambitions. Islam urges him to spend for the cause of Allah, which is a quite different form of expenditure, spending on others rather than on oneself. Another tendency is to pour all one's energy into attaining a high worldly position, something visible, whereas the everlasting reward, which cannot yet be seen, ought to be the chief goal. What man dislikes may please Allah, and only that will benefit him in the next, infinitely vaster world. In Allah's sight evil lies in preferring one's own pleasure to the pleasure of one's Creator, and what profits a man here may only harm him there. In the devotional reading, throwing away money on the path of the Sharīʿah is beautiful, but not like throwing away the spirit in the field of the Ḥaqīqah and becoming separate from all others at the moment of contemplation. Keeping the stipulation of loyalty is beautiful, but not as much as becoming separate from self and stepping onto the carpet of limpidness. One person asks how he should spend his property, and the Sharīʿah answers: from 200 dirhams, 5 dirhams, and from 20 dinars, half a dinar. Another asks, and the Ḥaqīqah answers that he will not be with Him along with body and spirit. The story of the wage-earners is one thing and the story of the recognisers another: the wage-earner's recognition reaches as far as recognising the spirit, while the recogniser's reaches throwing the spirit away. Those fortunate Companions asked how to spend not because they had failed to find their way to poverty, but in the hope of receiving this caress from the Exalted Presence, that whatever they had given and would give, the Lord knew. It is like Moses, called by name on that pitch-black night in the desert of the Mount. Burning with the pleasure of the address, he asked who was speaking to him, not from ignorance but drowned in yearning for the vision of the Real, hoping to be called again and perhaps to light up, and when asked whether he did not know who was calling, he said that he knew but was waiting for the caller to say, "It is truly I. I am Allah—the Lord of all worlds" (28:30). So here: whatever you have said, I know, ask of Me without shame or dread. The verse at the beginning of this section alludes to the worshippers' spending of their wealth in order to reach recognition, and the verse at its end to the recognisers' spending of their lives in struggle in order to reach the Recognised.

### In short

- The verse answers ʿAmr ibn al-Jamūḥ's question (Ibn al-Mundhir), or the Muslims' generally (Ibn Jarīr), about what to spend and on whom, one of the few questions the Companions asked (Ibn ʿAbbās), and its twin in verse 219 asks only what.
- Voluntary charity (Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān) goes first to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor and travellers, the nearest having the first claim, not to drums, pipes and wall hangings (Maymūn ibn Mihrān).
- It is not zakāh, which has fixed thresholds and may not be given to parents, and gifts to kin made for Allah's sake are rewarded.
- Give from surplus without harming one's family or neglecting debts, and the majority, against Abū Dharr, hold that giving away all surplus is not wājib.
- Allah knows every good deed and intention and rewards without limit, and inwardly the lover spends not wealth but his very spirit (28:30).
