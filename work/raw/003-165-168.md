<<<VERSE 3:165>>>
> “Why is it when you suffered casualties ˹at Uḥud˺—although you had made your enemy suffer twice as much ˹at Badr˺—you protested, “How could this be?”? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “It is because of your disobedience.” Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything” (3:165)

The verse answers the believers’ question about their losses at Uhud by reminding them of Badr and identifying disobedience as a cause of the setback. It asks them to examine their conduct rather than assume that faith guarantees an easy victory. The losses came during jihad, the struggle to defend the Muslim community.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Why is it when you suffered casualties at Uhud”** refers to the seventy companions killed and many others wounded in the battle. The loss was painful, especially after the believers had seen the Quraysh retreat at the beginning of the fighting. The verse does not minimize their grief. It addresses their bewilderment about how the reversal occurred.

**“although you had made your enemy suffer twice as much at Badr”** recalls the earlier victory. Reports say that at Badr the Quraysh, who had come from Makkah, lost about seventy fighters and seventy were captured, while the Muslims lost about seventy at Uhud. In this comparison, the Quraysh’s killed and captured are counted together. The point is not to reduce human lives to arithmetic, but to remind the believers that they had already seen a greater loss inflicted on the enemy.

**“you protested, ‘How could this be?’”** captures the question of people who wondered why the Muslims had suffered despite following the Prophet ﷺ. The question arose from distress, but it could become a misplaced accusation if it assumed that Allah’s support guaranteed victory regardless of human conduct.

Some reports from Ibn Abbas, transmitted through Umar ibn al-Khattab, connect the setback to accepting ransom for captives at Badr. Other early explanations, including those of Muhammad ibn Ishaq, Ibn Jurayj, Ar-Rabiʿ bin Anas, and As-Suddi, connect it to the archers’ disobedience at Uhud. These readings emphasize different lessons: one points to a prior decision, the other to the command abandoned during the battle. Both ask the believers to reflect on their choices rather than blame Allah for the outcome.

**“Say, ‘It is because of your disobedience’”** refers directly to the archers who left the position the Prophet had assigned them. Their action allowed the Quraysh cavalry to attack from behind and turned the course of the battle. The Arabic term ʿiṣyān means disobedience. The verse names a real cause without saying that every casualty was personally responsible for the error.

The blame is not assigned to the whole community in the same way. Some archers left, while Abdullah ibn Jubayr and a small group remained at their post. Some companions defended the Prophet, and others fled in the confusion. The verse identifies the disobedience that led to the reversal while leaving room for differences in each person’s conduct.

**“Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”** means Allah could grant victory or allow a setback. His power is not limited by the size of either force. The believers should not assume that a previous victory guarantees the next one, nor that one loss proves Allah has abandoned them.

### Learning without despair

The verse does not tell the believers to deny the enemy’s strength or to reject military planning. It asks them to see that their own actions mattered. The archers’ assignment was clear, and leaving it had consequences. A community can trust Allah while reviewing its errors and changing its practice.

The difference between Badr and Uhud is not that Allah helped at one and ignored the other. The believers at Uhud were tested after an early success, and the Qur’an later states that Allah forgave them. Their loss became a lesson in discipline, patience, and humility.

### In short

- Badr and Uhud had different outcomes, despite the believers’ earlier success.
- The archers’ disobedience contributed to the reversal at Uhud.
- The verse calls for honest correction, not despair or blaming every casualty.

<<<VERSE 3:166>>>
> “So what you suffered on the day the two armies met was by Allah’s Will, so that He might distinguish the ˹true˺ believers” (3:166)

The verse says that the events at Uhud took place by Allah’s permission and decree, and that the trial made the believers’ sincerity evident. Divine decree and human responsibility are both present: Allah governs the outcome, and people answer for their choices.

### Phrase by phrase

**“what you suffered on the day the two armies met”** refers to the battle between the Muslims and Quraysh at Uhud. The community experienced death, injury, confusion, and the rumor that the Prophet ﷺ had been killed.

**“was by Allah’s Will”** means that nothing in the battle was outside Allah’s authority. This does not mean the companions’ decisions were irrelevant or that the archers were not responsible for disobeying the Prophet. Allah’s decree encompasses human choices without making them meaningless.

**“so that He might distinguish the true believers”** means the trial made sincerity visible through conduct. Some companions remained firm, some made mistakes and later repented, and the hypocrites withdrew before the battle. Allah already knew what was in every heart. The event showed the community what was hidden and clarified who was prepared to remain faithful under pressure.

The Qur’an elsewhere describes God’s plan overcoming the plots of those who oppose the messengers (3:54). At Uhud, the setback did not defeat the message. It revealed the need for discipline and tested the believers’ patience.

### In short

- The encounter took place within Allah’s decree.
- Divine decree does not remove responsibility for disobedience.
- The trial made faith and hypocrisy visible in people’s actions.

<<<VERSE 3:167>>>
> “and expose the hypocrites. When it was said to them, “Come fight in the cause of Allah or ˹at least˺ defend yourselves,” they replied, “If we had known there was fighting, we would have definitely gone with you.” They were closer to disbelief than to belief on that day—for saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts. Allah is All-Knowing of what they hide” (3:167)

This verse describes the hypocrites who withdrew before Uhud under the leadership of Abd Allāh ibn Ubayy, also called Ibn Ubayy. They then claimed they would have joined if they had known there would be fighting. The battle exposed a difference between their public words and their actual intention.

### Phrase by phrase

**“and expose the hypocrites”** means the trial made their conduct visible. The believers’ loss did not create hypocrisy, but it revealed who had already been unwilling to stand with the community. Allah knew the state of their hearts, and the event made their choices apparent to other people.

**“When it was said to them, ‘Come fight in the cause of Allah or at least defend yourselves’”** refers to the appeal made to the people who had withdrawn. The invitation offered more than one reason to return: they could fight alongside the Muslims, or help protect Madinah and their families. Ibn Jubayr and others urged them to come back.

Several early authorities explain “defend yourselves” in distinct ways. Ibn Abbas and ʿIkrimah are among those reported to understand it as joining the fight. Saʿid ibn Jubayr and Ad-Dahhak are also associated with explanations of the call. Abu Salih and Al-Hasan appear among the transmitters; Al-Hasan bin Salih explains the alternative as helping through supplication, while As-Suddi is reported to understand it as taking up defensive posts. The common point is that they could still have supported the community even if they did not want to fight directly.

**“they replied, ‘If we had known there was fighting, we would have definitely gone with you’”** was an excuse. Quraysh had come in a large force from Makkah after Badr, and the possibility of battle was clear. The claim that they had not expected fighting did not match the circumstances.

**“They were closer to disbelief than to belief on that day”** describes the direction of their conduct at that moment. Their outward claim of faith was undermined by their withdrawal and their dishonest explanation. The verse does not say that every believer who felt fear or retreated during the battle was a hypocrite. It refers to those whose words and intentions were exposed as false.

**“for saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts”** identifies their contradiction. They spoke as though they would have joined the Muslims, while their behavior showed they had already chosen to stay away. This warns against using words of loyalty as a substitute for sincere action.

**“Allah is All-Knowing of what they hide”** means that no private intention is concealed from Allah. People may not know the motives of others, so they should avoid making confident claims about an individual’s inner state. The verse identifies a group whose behavior was exposed through the revelation.

### Distinguishing fear from hypocrisy

The companions who fled after the Muslim lines broke were not automatically the same as those who withdrew before the battle and then lied about why. Fear can affect a believer in a moment of danger. Hypocrisy in this account involved a settled refusal to support the community and a false claim about the reason.

The verse condemns the deception, not every person who is unable to fight. The invitation included defending the community, showing that people could contribute according to their ability. The hypocrites refused both and then blamed their absence on not knowing what would happen.

### In short

- The hypocrites withdrew before Uhud and gave an excuse that contradicted the situation.
- The call offered them a chance to fight or help defend Madinah.
- The verse distinguishes deliberate hypocrisy from fear or weakness in a believer.

<<<VERSE 3:168>>>
> “Those who sat at home, saying about their brothers, “Had they listened to us, they would not have been killed.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Try not to die if what you say is true!”” (3:168)

The verse answers the people who stayed behind and blamed the dead for going to Uhud. They claimed that following their advice would have prevented the deaths. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ is told to challenge them to prove that staying at home can prevent death. The opening word alladhīna means “those who” and points back to the people making this accusation.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Those who sat at home”** refers to people who did not join the fight. In the immediate context, the verse addresses hypocrites who had urged the Muslims to stay in Madinah and later used the casualties to justify their advice.

**“saying about their brothers”** refers to the men killed at Uhud. The hypocrites called them brothers while criticizing their decision to go out. Their words did not comfort the bereaved. They used the dead to promote doubt and to claim that they had been right all along.

**“Had they listened to us, they would not have been killed”** is a claim about an alternative that no one can verify. The speaker assumes that staying home guarantees safety. The preceding verses have already rejected the idea that death can be avoided by choosing a different place or refusing a journey.

Jabir bin Abdullah reports that this verse was revealed about Abdullah ibn Ubayy bin Salul, the leader of the hypocrites. His statement turned the loss at Uhud into an argument against the believers’ decision to follow the Prophet.

**“Say, ‘Try not to die if what you say is true!’”** challenges the claim directly. If staying home guarantees that a person will not die, then those making the claim should be able to keep death away from themselves. They cannot. Life and death belong to Allah, and no one controls the appointed time.

The challenge is not a request to seek danger or to test Allah. It exposes the false certainty of those who blame the dead. Believers should take precautions and act wisely, including in jihad on the path of Allah, but they cannot promise that a particular decision would have prevented death.

A spiritual reflection reads the challenge as a call to extend the life of the soul and defend it from death’s assaults. In that interpretation, ʿanhā means “from it.” This is a figurative lesson about the soul, not a replacement for the verse’s direct challenge about physical death.

### Answering blame after a loss

Families who have lost someone should not be confronted with accusations that the deceased would have lived if only they had listened to one person. The verse rejects that kind of hindsight. It protects the bereaved from claims that pretend to know the unseen and redirects attention to Allah’s knowledge and decree.

The hypocrites’ words also reveal the difference between advice offered for a community’s good and advice used to undermine faith after a tragedy. Counsel should be weighed before a decision. Afterward, people should learn from what happened without using the dead as proof of their own superiority.

### In short

- Those who stayed behind blamed the dead for not following their advice.
- The verse rejects the claim that staying home can guarantee survival.
- Believers should not burden bereaved families with speculation about what might have happened.
