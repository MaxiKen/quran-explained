<<<VERSE 7:31>>>
The surah turns to the body, and then to the table. "O Children of Adam! Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat and drink, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful". Two commands and one prohibition. The first answers the practice of going round the House with nothing on. The second answers the belief that good food was forbidden during the days of pilgrimage. And both of them come back to the same principle, that the way of religion is a middle way between two extremes.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O Children of Adam!"** is the address, and it speaks to the whole of humankind once more.

**"Dress properly"** translates the instruction to take your adornment. In this place the adornment is clothing, and what is meant is clean and proper clothes that cover what must be covered. One reading of the verse has the answer in it: there were people who used to walk round the House naked, so Allah ordered them to take their adornment, and the people were commanded to wear their best clothes for every prayer.

**"whenever you are at worship"** translates the phrase about every place of prostration, and it means at the time of every ṣalāh and at the ḥajj. The place of prostration covers the masjid, and the ṣalāh, and the rukūʿ and the sujūd within it, and each of them takes the same instruction.

**"Eat and drink"** is the permission, and it is wider than it looks. Some readings fill in what is meant: eat of the meat and the fat, drink of the milk. Because the object is left unnamed, the permission reaches everything ḥalāl and stops at everything ḥarām. The instruction comes to human beings because in the days of ignorance they forbade themselves meat and fat while setting out on the ḥajj, and went into the sacred precinct with nothing on, the men by day and the women by night. Allah made both practices unlawful.

**"but do not waste"** is the limit. The word means to cross the line. It takes several forms, and the next section takes them one at a time.

**"Surely He does not like the wasteful"** gives the ground. One explanation runs: Allah does not like the people who go past the limit in something permitted or in something prohibited, who go to an extreme over what He allowed, who permit what He forbade, or forbid what He allowed. What He likes is that what He allowed be treated as allowed and what He forbade be treated as forbidden. That is the justice He commanded.

### The practice this verse ended

Muslim, an-Nasāʾī and Ibn Jarīr recorded, in the wording of Ibn Jarīr, a report going back to Ibn ʿAbbās. The idolaters used to walk round the House naked, men and women alike. The men did it during the day, and the women at night. A woman would say as she went round: today some of it or all of it will be shown, and whatever is shown of it is not permitted. The answer to that is this verse, with its instruction to take the adornment at every place of prostration.

Ibn ʿAbbās gave the sense of the command in another way: wear clean and proper clothing that covers the private parts. And the report adds that people were commanded to wear their best clothes for every prayer. The same reading came from ʿAṭāʾ, and from as-Suddī, and from aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Qatādah held it, and so did Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī. Mālik held it, as it is reported from az-Zuhrī, and so did a number of the early generations. Their reading of the verse is that it came down about the idolaters who went round the House with nothing on.

### What the command gave rise to afterwards

This verse and the Sunnah together encourage the wearing of the best clothes for prayer, and especially for the Friday prayer and for the two feasts. It is also recommended for a man to wear perfume for the prayer, because perfume is part of adornment, and to use the tooth stick, since that completes the adornment.

The best colour for clothes is white. Aḥmad narrated from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

> Wear white clothes, for they are among your best clothes, and wrap your dead in them. And among the best of what you use on the eyes is antimony, for it clears the sight and helps the hair grow.

The report has a sound chain of narration, made up of transmitters who meet the conditions that Muslim set, and Abū Dāwūd, at-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah recorded it too. At-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ.

The instruction also carries a ruling about the covering of the body. Covering what must be covered is a condition of the prayer, so that without it the prayer is as though it had not been made at all. And what the verse asks for is not the bare minimum. It asks for clothing that looks well on the person, which is what the word for adornment carries, and what it asks for is a farḍ, an obligation, and not a preference. Because of that, the prayer is makrūh, disliked, for a man with his head bare, or with his shoulders or his elbows uncovered, whether the shirt itself is short-sleeved or has simply been rolled up. The prayer is disliked either way. In the same way it is disliked to pray in clothing a person would not want to be seen in in front of his friends or in public as something unbecoming, such as a vest on its own without a shirt even if the vest has long sleeves, or going without a cap and making do with a piece of cloth or a small handkerchief knotted or tucked around the head. When nobody would wish to appear in front of his friends or anyone else in that bare-headed state, how could it be a desirable way to appear before Allah, the Master of all the worlds? So the prayer is makrūh with the head, the shoulders and the elbows uncovered, and that ruling is taken from the word for adornment in this verse and from the explanations of the Holy Prophet ﷺ. The same command belongs to the ṭawāf of the Kaʿbah, since the House is the place of worship above all places.

So the instruction in this verse was first revealed to wipe out the custom of nakedness in the age of ignorance, and the generality of its words has yielded many other rulings as well. The same is true of the next sentence. Eat and drink and do not be extravagant came down to erase the custom of treating good food as a sin during the days of the ḥajj, and here too the generality of the words proves a great many rulings. The whole passage is a demonstration of the Sharīʿah, since it shows how a command given for one occasion extends to the Muslim ummah in every place and time.

### Eating and drinking as a duty

Eating and drinking is not only permitted. This duty is farḍ, an obligation, on everyone from the point of view of the law. If a person gives it up although he is able to eat, to the point where he dies, or becomes too weak to carry out what is required of him, then he is sinning and committing an offence in the sight of Allah.

### Everything is permitted until it is forbidden

One ruling drawn from this verse, as al-Jaṣṣāṣ set it out in his book on the rulings of the Qur'an, is this. In the basic state of things every kind of food and drink is permitted and lawful unless the unlawfulness of a particular thing is proved by evidence from the law. Without that evidence, everything is taken as permitted and lawful.

What points to this is that the object of the command to eat and drink was not named in the verse. It did not say what to eat or what to drink. The masters of Arabic expression have established that leaving the object unnamed in such a place is an indication of its generality. A person may eat and drink everything, with the exception of the things that have been declared forbidden.

### The many faces of extravagance

The last sentence of the verse proves that eating and drinking is not only permitted but commanded, and that going to excess in it is forbidden at the same time.

One form of excess is crossing the line out of the permitted and into the forbidden. A person begins eating and drinking things that are not allowed, and that this is forbidden is perfectly obvious.

Another form is the opposite one. A person takes what Allah has made lawful and holds back from it as though it were forbidden, with no valid legal excuse that the law would accept. The point is worth stating plainly. It is a crime and a sin to use what is forbidden, and it is equally rebellion against the divine law and a very grave sin to treat what is permitted as forbidden.

By the same measure, eating and drinking past the limits of hunger and need is also excess. This is why the jurists wrote that eating more than what is needed to remove hunger is not permitted. And it also falls under the same ruling if a person eats much less than he needs, while having the ability and the choice, until he grows weak and cannot carry out what is required of him. Both kinds of excess are being forbidden when the Qur'an says that the extravagant are the brothers of the satans (17:27). And in Sūrat al-Furqān it says of the true servants of Allah that when they spend they neither overspend nor underspend, and that the moderate way lies between the two (25:67).

### What the early generations said about moderation

ʿUmar, the greatest of the two ʿUmars, gave this advice. Avoid eating and drinking too much, because it spoils the body, brings on disease and slackens activity. Take the middle way in eating and drinking instead, for it is good for the health of the body and it is far removed from excess in it. He also said that Allah does not like a heavy scholar, that is, a scholar of religion who has grown fat and slow from eating more than he needs by choice. And he said that a person does not go to ruin until he begins to prefer his own desires over his faith.

The righteous elders of the early period said that to be always busy with the business of eating and drinking, or to put it ahead of matters of more importance so that it looks as though a person has no other worthy purpose in his life, falls under excess. Their saying is well known, that a person should eat to live and not live to eat.

In a hadith, the Holy Prophet ﷺ counted the habit of compulsively satisfying every desire as it comes under the same heading. His words are: it is also excess for a person to eat everything he desires. Ibn Mājah recorded this from Anas.

Al-Bayhaqī reports that the Holy Prophet ﷺ once saw ʿĀʾishah eating twice in one day, and he asked her whether she would like eating to become her only pastime.

The command to be moderate is not confined to eating and drinking. The middle course is desirable in what a person wears, in where he lives, and in nearly everything else. The advice of Ibn ʿAbbās on the same point was that a person may eat and drink as he likes and wear what he likes, while watching for two things. Let there be no excess past the measure of need, and let there be no pride and no arrogance attached to it.

### Eight rulings from one sentence

Eight rulings come out of the command to eat and drink and not to be extravagant.

1. Eating and drinking as much as is needed is obligatory.
2. Unless the unlawfulness of a thing is proved by evidence the law admits, everything is permitted.
3. To use what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden is excess, and it is not allowed.
4. To treat what Allah has permitted as forbidden is also excess, and it is a grave sin.
5. Once a person has eaten his fill, eating more is not permitted.
6. Eating so little that a person grows weak and cannot carry out his obligations is also excess.
7. To be always thinking about eating and drinking is also excess.
8. It is not necessary that a person get whatever he happens to want at a given moment.

These rulings have their religious benefits. And if a person looks at them from the medical side, a better prescription for health and wellbeing would be hard to find. The key is moderation in eating and drinking, and that is the safe ground away from every disease. It is also the way a person keeps the dhikr, the remembrance of his Lord, from being crowded out by the concerns of the belly.

### The whole of medicine in half a verse

There is a story told about this. Hārūn al-Rashīd, the caliph, had a personal physician who was a Christian. The physician said to ʿAlī ibn Ḥusayn ibn al-Wāqidī that the Muslim Book had nothing in it about medicine, though there were only two fields of knowledge in their time, the knowledge of religion and the knowledge of bodies, which is medicine. ʿAlī ibn Ḥusayn answered that Allah had put the whole science of medicine into half a verse of the Qur'an, in the words: eat and drink and do not be extravagant. Ibn Jarīr reports the same saying with reference to some other righteous elders of the early period.

The rulings named above are gathered in Rūḥ al-Maʿānī on this passage. The court physician then asked whether there was anything in the sayings of the Prophet about medicine. ʿAlī ibn Ḥusayn answered that the Holy Prophet ﷺ had reduced the whole science of medicine into a few sayings: that the stomach is the nursery of diseases, that abstinence from harmful things is the root of all medicine, and that a person should give his body what it is used to taking. On hearing that, the Christian physician said that the Book and the Prophet had left nothing of medicine to Jālīnūs.

Al-Bayhaqī reports from Abū Hurayrah that the Holy Prophet ﷺ said:

> The stomach is the reservoir of the body. Every artery and nerve of the body takes its fill from this reservoir. If the stomach is in proper order, the veins return with healthy food from it. If it is not in proper order, the veins spread out through the body carrying disease.

Those who work in the discipline of hadith have raised doubts about the wording of some of these reports. All of them agree, however, on the emphasis that is placed on eating moderately and on taking precautions, and that emphasis is found in countless reports.

### The inner reading

In the language of learning, taking the adornment at every place of prostration means covering what must be covered during the prayer. In the language of unveiling, the adornment of every servant is to stand in the station where the heart is present, to hold on to the Presence, and to keep the witnessing of the Real unbroken. What must be covered is covered before strangers, and the limit of what may be seen belongs to the maḥram relations, since the command of dress is a command about sight as much as about cloth. It has also been said that the adornment of the worshipper's soul is the marks left by prostration, and that the adornment of the heart of the one who recognises Allah is the light of finding Him. The worshipper has the attribute of servanthood in his prostration, and the one who recognises Him sits on the carpet of nearness in the calm of witnessing.

### In short

- The command to dress properly answers the practice of walking round the House naked, and it obliges clean clothing that covers the body, with the best clothes for the prayer.
- The prayer is disliked with the head, shoulders and elbows bare, because what the verse asks for is clothing that looks well on the person.
- To eat and drink is a duty as well as a permission. Everything is lawful unless evidence makes it unlawful, and the verse does not name what may be eaten because the permission covers everything except what is forbidden.
- Excess has many faces: eating what is forbidden, refusing what is allowed, overeating, undereating, and thinking about food all the time. Eight rulings come out of the one sentence.
- Moderation is the middle way in food, dress and living, and it is the ground on which a person is safe from both greed and show.

<<<VERSE 7:32>>>
The question in this verse is put to the people who had made things unlawful on their own authority. "Ask, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?” Say, “They are for the enjoyment of the believers in this worldly life, but they will be exclusively theirs on the Day of Judgment. This is how We make Our revelations clear for people of knowledge.”" The verse refutes anyone who forbids a kind of food, drink or clothing according to his own understanding, without resting on what Allah has legislated.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Ask, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who has forbidden the adornments ... Allah has brought forth for His servants?”"** is a rebuke in the form of a question, and the answer is meant to be obvious. The adornments meant are the clothes they had declared off limits for the days of the ḥajj, whether in the sacred precinct or during the ṭawāf of the Kaʿbah. The clothing named is the adornment that Allah created for His servants.

**"and lawful provisions"** translates aṭ-Ṭayyibāt, the good and pure things of sustenance. It names meat and fat, because in the days of ignorance people used to deprive themselves of meat and fat during the days of pilgrimage. The whole question is about ḥalāl, what Allah has made lawful, set against the ḥarām they were treating as though it were lawful.

**"Say, “They are for the enjoyment of the believers in this worldly life"** is the first half of the answer. In this world the good things are shared out among everyone. The believers have the right to them, and other people come to enjoy them alongside them.

**"but they will be exclusively theirs on the Day of Judgment"** is the second half, and it is the part that matters. On the Day of Resurrection these things will be for the believers and for nobody else, since the disbelievers will have no share in them and have been kept out of Paradise. What the phrase says is that the good things of this world belong by right to the obedient servants of Allah, and that in the life to come they will be theirs alone.

**"This is how We make Our revelations clear for people of knowledge."** is the close. The verses are set out in detail for people who reflect, because they are the ones who profit from them.

### Three ways of reading the promise

The sentence about the believers has been explained in more than one way, and the explanations sit together.

On the first, all the blessings and comforts of this world are the rightful share of the obedient believers, with the safeguard that they must not become a cause of trouble for them in the Hereafter. That was the explanation given by ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAbbās. The disbelievers and the sinners receive these blessings too, and often they receive more of them, but what they receive becomes their ruin in the Hereafter and brings on a punishment that lasts for ever. Once the outcome is put beside the enjoyment, this is not the kind of comfort and honour anyone would welcome.

On the second, every blessing and comfort of this world is laced with unending effort, with the fear that it may decline, and with anxiety that never stops. Pure blessing and pure comfort simply do not exist here. Whoever receives these blessings on the Day of Judgment will receive them in a state of absolute purity, with no effort to reach them, no fear that they will decline or be lost, and no worry after them.

On the third, the arrangement of this world is what it is because this world is the place of deeds and not the place of reward. No line can be drawn here between the genuine and the counterfeit, or between the good and the bad, in the matter of what is available. The table is open to everyone, and the blessings come from ar-Raḥmān, the All-Merciful, who is the Most Compassionate to the whole world. But the arrangements in this world follow the will of Allah, and it is His established practice that when the obedient believers fall short in their duties, other people overcome them and take the treasures of worldly blessing. The believing servants are then left with hunger and poverty. This law works only here, in the place of deeds. Once the Hereafter comes, all blessings and all comfort are kept back for the obedient.

### Why forbidding good things is not religion

The people who have been reprimanded in this verse are the ones who go to excess in worship and bring self-invented restrictions into it. They hold back from what Allah has made lawful and make it unlawful for themselves, and then they call that act obedience to Allah and worship of Him. That is what the disbelievers of Makkah did. They thought it was not permissible to wear clothes during the circuit in the days of the ḥajj, and they thought that holding back from good food that Allah had made lawful was an act of worship.

Setting something as lawful or unlawful is the sole right of the One who created it. Nobody else is permitted to interfere in the matter. So the people who treat good clothing and good food made lawful by Allah as forbidden to themselves deserve His anger and His punishment. Living in tattered rags when a person has the means is not a teaching of Islam, and it is not something worth imitating, as some ignorant people suppose.

Many of the righteous elders of the early period and the jurists among the Imāms of Islam, whom Allah had given good means, used to wear elegant and even expensive clothes. The Prophet ﷺ himself, when his means allowed it, adorned his body with the best of clothing. One narration says that when he came out one day he was wearing a cloak worth a thousand dirhams. Abū Ḥanīfah is reported to have used a cloak worth four hundred guineas. Mālik always used decent and elegant clothing, and someone had taken it on himself to supply him with three hundred and sixty pairs of clothes each year. A pair that he wore for a day he would not wear again, because after a single day he gave it away to a poor student.

The reason for all of it is a saying of the Holy Prophet ﷺ. When the Exalted Lord blesses a servant of His with ample means, He likes to see the effect of that blessing on the things around him, and his clothing is one of them. Letting such a blessing be seen is therefore a form of gratitude. The opposite attitude, wearing worn-out or untidy clothes when a person has the means, is ingratitude, and the Sunnah asks for neither extreme.

Two things must be guarded against at the same time: hypocrisy and showing off, and pride and arrogance. A person should not dress well in order to put himself above others or to establish that he is superior or special. And the righteous elders of the early period were free of any such thing.

### Why the same people wore plain clothes

The Prophet ﷺ, ʿUmar and some of the Companions are reported to have worn ordinary or patched clothing, and there were two reasons for it.

The first is that whatever came into their hands they spent on the poor and the needy and on their religious aims. For their own persons there was too little left to afford fine clothes. The second is that they were the leaders of the people, and they kept themselves in that plain and inexpensive bearing so that others with ample means would take the lesson, and so that the ordinary people and the poor would not be overawed by their wealth.

The same holds for the noble Sufis. When they keep those who are new to the path from fine clothing and good food, they do not mean that giving these things up for good is a work of merit. Far from it. What they are doing is putting a brake on the desires of the self during the first stage of the search for truth, and the brake is a treatment and a medicine. When the seeker reaches the stage where he has learned to control his desires, and the self can no longer be caught by what is unlawful, then all the masters of the spiritual orders use good clothing and good food and recommend them, exactly as the early righteous elders did. At that point these fine blessings become a way of recognising the Creator and drawing near to Him.

### The way of the Prophet ﷺ in food and clothing

The essential practice of the Holy Prophet ﷺ, the Companions and those who followed them is that a person should not be unnaturally concerned about food and clothing. What is within easy reach is good enough, and it should be used with gratitude. Suppose the clothing is coarse and the food plain. There is no need then to bother about finding something finer by borrowing, or to fret so much over what is missing that a person falls into some other difficulty.

And the opposite is equally true. When fine clothing and good food are available without effort, there is no need to go to the other extreme and ruin them or refuse to use them at all. Going out of one's way after fine clothes and fine food is one constraint a person puts on himself. Doing the reverse, wasting what is good and choosing what is worse, is another self-imposed constraint, and a blameworthy one.

### The inner reading

The tongue has an ornament, and it is dhikr, the remembrance of Allah. The heart has an ornament, and it is reflection. There is nothing that has no ornament. For the soul, the ornament is beautiful practice joined to the quality of struggle. The ornament of the heart is unbroken union at the moment of contemplation. The ornament of the secret core is the realities of nearness in the field of face to face vision.

The question in the verse, about who has forbidden the adornment of God, points to the fact that these adornments are not held back from the seekers, and are not refused to the hearts that are present. The storehouse is full, and all it waits for is the seeker. The table of gentleness and mercy is spread, and all it waits for is the eater. One of the masters of the path said in whispered prayer:

> O seekers, hurry, for the hard cash is near. O night travellers, do not sleep, for the morning is near. O hurriers, be glad, for the abode is near. O thirsty ones, be patient, for the spring is near. O strangers, rejoice, for the master of hospitality is near. O seekers of the Friend, be glad, for the answer is near. O Opener of my heart, what harm would it do You to open my heart and lay Your balm on my spirit? How can I look for gain when my hands hold no capital? What if You threw me into good fortune out of Your bounty?

### In short

- The question is a rebuke to those who made things unlawful on their own authority, without resting on what Allah legislated.
- The adornments and the good provisions named were the clothing forbidden during the pilgrimage and the meat and fat forbidden during it.
- The good things of this world are open to everyone, and on the Day of Judgment they are for the believers exclusively. Reading the promise three ways, they are the believers' rightful share now, they will be pure of every effort and fear then, and this world is the place of deeds rather than of reward.
- Wearing good clothing when a person has the means is a form of gratitude, since Allah likes to see the effect of His blessing on His servant. Worn-out clothing when a person has the means is ingratitude.
- The plain clothing of the Prophet ﷺ and the early generations came from generosity with what they had and from a wish not to overawe the people, and the Sufis only withhold good things from a beginner as a treatment.

<<<VERSE 7:33>>>
The verse turns from what was wrongly forbidden to what is truly forbidden. "Say, “My Lord has only forbidden open and secret indecencies, sinfulness, unjust aggression, associating ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺—a practice He has never authorized—and attributing to Allah what you do not know.”" The list is short and each item is a heading. Together they cover nearly every kind of prohibition there is, whether it belongs to belief or to conduct, and whether it is done by a person alone or takes away the rights of somebody else.

### Phrase by phrase

**"open and secret indecencies"** is the first item. The Arabic word is fāḥishah, and it covers grave sins, of which the plainest is fornication. The open kind is done in the sight of people. The secret kind is hidden and done privately, and it takes in the taking of secret lovers.

**"sinfulness"** is the second. The Arabic word is al-ithm, and it covers every sin that falls on a person himself, the disobedience that concerns him and his own Lord and takes nothing from anybody else. One poet pictured it by saying that he sipped at sin until his mind wandered off, which is what sin does to a mind, and then drank jars of it out in the open.

**"unjust aggression"** is the third, and it covers the sins that concern other people, the wrongs done to their persons, their wealth or their honour. It is the overbearing that takes what is not one's own and puts a weight on those who cannot resist.

**"associating ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺—a practice He has never authorized"** is the fourth. What is being named is shirk, the greatest of the prohibitions, and the phrase adds what makes it indefensible. No scripture and no proof has ever come down sanctioning it. It is the one ḥarām that no prophet and no book ever left room for, and the verse names it among the ordinary wrongs to show that the prohibition stands on its own evidence. The verse does not argue about which partner is being associated. It points out that nothing at all has been sent to warrant the association.

**"and attributing to Allah what you do not know"** is the fifth, and it is the one that ties this verse back to the two before it. It covers saying things about Allah without knowledge, in His names, in His attributes, in His actions and in His sacred law. It covers forbidding crops and cattle, and it covers forbidding good things and clothing. It is the same charge that was laid against the people who defended the naked circuit of the House with the claim that Allah had commanded it.

### Why this list and these people

This detail was given here because it covers nearly every kind of prohibition and sin, whether the matter belongs to belief or to conduct, whether it was done by a person on his own or took the rights of others. Aḥmad recorded from ʿAbdullah, and the two ṣaḥīḥ collections carry the report, that the Prophet ﷺ named the worst of the major sins, and the list he gave includes the wrongs done to parents and the false word.

It was also given because the people of the age of ignorance were caught up in all of these crimes at once. They had taken partners alongside Allah, they had invented laws about their cattle and their crops, they had wronged one another, and they had done indecent things in the open. So the verse is a further demonstration of their ignorance, and the shape of the ignorance is worth pausing on. They held back from what had been made lawful for them, and at the same time they did not hesitate for a moment to do what had been declared unlawful.

### Two kinds of loss

The hint in the passage is that these people were suffering from a twofold ignorance.

On one side they deprived themselves of the good things of life that Allah had made lawful for them, denying themselves their use as though the things were unlawful, and doing so for no reason at all. On the other side, there were things that were genuinely and really unlawful, things whose use would bring the anger of Allah and punishment in the Hereafter, and those they embraced with both arms. They discovered only afterwards that what they had embraced was their own undoing in the life to come. So they were robbed twice over: robbed of the blessings they had in this world, and robbed of the blessings they had a chance of having in the next.

### The danger of excess in religion

There is a lesson here that goes well beyond the people it was addressed to. It is a necessary outcome of going to excess in religion, and of introducing invented practices into it, that the people who get caught up in it become habitually heedless about the root of the religion and its essential requirements.

So the harm from excess and invention is twofold. Getting involved with them is a sin in itself. And then, set against that, there is something far graver: the loss of the religion of Allah as it truly is, and of the genuine way of the Holy Prophet ﷺ. A person may be so busy with the addition he has made that he no longer notices what he has dropped.

### How the same fault appears in every age

The mistake is not confined to one period. Some of the Arab tribes used to perform their circuit of the Kaʿbah with nothing on at all, thinking that it brought them nearer to God. And in the same period they would hold back from certain things, such as goat's milk or meat, when setting out on the ḥajj, imagining that they were doing something great and righteous. The cattle they had declared off limits in this way are named in Sūrat al-Anʿām, and the answer to the whole practice is the command to turn away from the impurity of idolatry and from the words of falsehood (22:30). As-Suddī and Mujāhid are among those who read the verse in this setting.

Actions of that kind have been taken up by people in every age. Such people do not build the requirements of religion into their ordinary daily life. Then, on certain occasions, they make a display of living by the religion of God while attending to irrelevant and pointless observances, and they claim that these trivialities are what carry out God's will to the fullest extent.

What God wants instead is set out in the verse. A person should hold back from being a spendthrift, which is to say that he should not cross the limits God laid down. He should not treat permitted things as forbidden, or forbidden things as permitted. He should turn away from shameful acts, and from whatever ordinary sense can see to be plainly evil. He should give up all excesses. When the truth appears to him, he should accept it and put everything else aside. He should clean himself thoroughly of ascribing partners to God, and he should not give to anyone the sublime relationship that belongs by right to the one God alone.

And one thing above all should not happen. A person should not choose a path for himself and then lay it on God with no reason for doing so. He should remain a servant of God throughout, and should not take up ways that are unfitting for a servant of God.

### The blessings of the two worlds

The two kinds of blessing are not the same kind. In the Hereafter the good things of God come as a reward, and a reward is given only to those God decides to send to Paradise. The blessings received in this world are different. They come for a limited period, and they come for the purpose of a test, so a person receives a share in them according to the paper he has been set.

The way to meet the requirements of that test is not to keep away from the provisions God has given for it. It is to use them inside the limits set for them. A person should answer the blessing of God with gratitude, and not with renunciation or with a display of bravado.

### In short

- The verse lists five things that are truly forbidden, and the list covers nearly every kind of prohibition there is.
- Open and secret indecencies are the first, sinfulness the second, unjust aggression the third, associating others with Allah the fourth, and saying about Allah what is not known the fifth.
- Sin covers what a person does against his own soul, and unjust aggression covers the wrongs done to others, so the two together take in conduct on both sides.
- The people addressed were caught up in every one of the five at once, and the shape of their ignorance was that they refused what was lawful for them and embraced what was unlawful.
- Excess in religion and invented practices carry a double harm, since the practice itself is a sin and the loss of the religion as it truly is is graver still.
