<<<VERSE 7:31>>>
This verse answers two customs of the pagan Arabs at once: "O Children of Adam! Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat and drink, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful". They counted circling the Kaʿbah naked as true worship and as reverence for Allah's House. They also used to go without food and drink during the days of Hajj. Ibn Jarīr reported that they ate only enough to stay alive and kept away in particular from ghee, milk and other pure foods. The verse was revealed against both practices.

### Phrase by phrase

**"O Children of Adam! Dress properly"** is literally "take your adornment" (zīnah). Ibn ʿAbbās explained that people used to circle the House naked, so Allah ordered them to take adornment. That means clean, proper clothes that cover the private parts. People were also commanded to wear their best clothes for every prayer. Other glosses are "put on what covers your nakedness" and "dress in clean, pure and beautiful clothes".

**"whenever you are at worship"** is literally "at every masjid". It means at every prayer, and when circling the Kaʿbah. Most explanations take masjid here in the sense of prostration (sajdah, sujūd).

**"Eat and drink"**: eat meat and fat, and drink milk, choosing whatever you like from the good things Allah has allowed. Eat and drink as you wish during the days of Hajj.

**"but do not waste."** Do not be excessive, and do not declare wholesome foods such as meat and fat unlawful. Do not go beyond what is permitted. ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī reported Ibn ʿAbbās as saying this concerns food and drink. Al-Bukhārī reported Ibn ʿAbbās as saying, in effect: eat what you like and wear what you like, so long as you avoid two things, extravagance and arrogance. Ibn Jarīr recorded through a ṣaḥīḥ chain that Ibn ʿAbbās said Allah has permitted eating and drinking, provided there is no waste or pride in it.

**"Surely He does not like the wasteful"**: those who abandon the lawful and run after the unlawful, and those who overstep the limits. Ibn Jarīr explained that Allah does not like people who cross the limits in what is allowed or in what is forbidden. They go to extremes in what He allowed, permit what He forbade, or forbid what He permitted. He likes the allowed to be treated as allowed, without excess, and the forbidden as forbidden. That is the justice He has commanded.

### Who was the verse revealed about?

Muslim, an-Nasāʾī and Ibn Jarīr recorded, in Ibn Jarīr's wording, that Ibn ʿAbbās described the idolaters going round the House naked, men by day and women by night. Women would say the words quoted under 7:28, and Allah replied with this verse. Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Mujāhid, Qatādah, ʿAṭāʾ, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and as-Suddī said the same, as did Mālik from az-Zuhrī and several of the Salaf. All of them said the verse came down about the idolaters who circled the House naked.

The verse also struck at the second custom. Naked ṭawāf was immodest and ill-mannered. Its opposite, going without the good food Allah gave, with no valid excuse, had nothing to do with religion. Forbidding oneself what Allah has made lawful is effrontery and excess in worship, and Allah dislikes it. So eat and drink as you wish during Hajj, but without excess. Abstaining completely from lawful food is itself a kind of excess. So is forgetting the real purposes of Hajj and the remembrance (dhikr) of Allah, and doing nothing but eat and drink.

### Is the verse limited to its occasion?

The verse was revealed to end one custom of the Jāhiliyyah. Yet the scholars of tafsīr and the jurists of the Muslim Ummah agree unanimously that a command revealed about one event is not confined to that event. What counts is the generality of the words, and the command applies to everything those words cover. That is why most of the Companions and their Successors, and the mujtahid imams, drew many rulings from this verse.

### Can one pray without proper covering?

The most important ruling concerns ṣalāh. Naked ṭawāf is forbidden here, and the same ruling applies to prayer, which becomes ḥarām and void without covering. The Prophet ﷺ said that circling the House is a form of ṣalāh. Besides, since most explanations take masjid in this verse to mean sajdah, the verse plainly forbids nakedness in prostration. If it is forbidden in prostration, it is forbidden in every other position of the prayer: rukūʿ (bowing), qiyām (standing) and quʿūd (sitting). The Prophet's ﷺ statement makes this clearer still. A hadith in at-Tirmidhī adds that an adult woman's prayer is not valid without a proper covering for head and body, such as a khimār, ridāʾ, chādar, dupatta or large scarf.

Covering the body is obligatory outside prayer too. Other verses and hadiths prove it, among them the verse a little earlier: "clothing to cover your nakedness" (7:26). In sum, proper covering is the first human and Islamic duty on everyone, binding in every condition. In ṣalāh and ṭawāf it is obligatory in the highest degree.

### Should one dress well for prayer?

By calling clothing zīnah, adornment, the verse hints at more than bare covering. The preferred practice in prayer is to wear what adorns and looks fitting, within one's means. Ḥasan used to put on his best clothes for prayer. He would say, "Allah loves beauty, so I make myself beautiful for my Lord, for He has said, take your adornment at every masjid." So the verse proves two things. Covering is obligatory in prayer, and it is recommended and praiseworthy to wear neat, clean, good clothes there, within one's means.

This verse and the Sunnah encourage wearing one's best clothes for prayer, above all on Fridays and for the two ʿĪds. Men are also encouraged to put on perfume for prayer, as a part of adornment, and to use the siwāk, the tooth-stick, which rounds it off. The best colour for clothes is white. Imam Aḥmad recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ told people to wear white, as among their best clothes, and to shroud their dead in it. He added that ithmid (antimony) is among the best kinds of kohl, since it clears the sight and helps the lashes grow. The chain is sound and its narrators meet Imam Muslim's conditions. Abū Dāwūd, at-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah also recorded it, and at-Tirmidhī called it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ.

### How much must be covered?

Satr is the part of the body whose covering is farḍ at all times, and above all in prayer and ṭawāf. The Qur'an gave the command in brief and left the details to the Prophet ﷺ, who explained them. A man's satr runs from the navel to the knees. A woman's satr is her whole body except the face, the palms and the feet.

These details are all in the hadith narrations. If a man's body below the navel, or his knees, are bare, that way of dressing is a sin in itself, and his prayer is not properly performed in it. If a woman's head, neck, arms, shins or calves are bare, that way of dressing is not allowed in itself, and her prayer is not properly performed either. A hadith says that angels of good do not come to a home where a woman's head is uncovered.

The exemption of a woman's face, palms and feet means that their being uncovered causes no defect in her prayer. It does not mean she may go about with her face uncovered before non-maḥram men, those she may lawfully marry, without an excuse the Sharīʿah accepts.

Covering is a precondition of prayer, without which the prayer counts as not performed at all. And prayer asks for more than bare covering, since the verse speaks of zīnah. So it is makrūh, disliked, for a man to pray bareheaded or with his shoulders or elbows bare. That holds whether the shirt has half sleeves or is rolled up. Praying in clothes one would not wear in front of friends or in public is also makrūh. Examples are an undershirt alone, even with full sleeves, or a small cloth or handkerchief knotted round the head in place of a cap. No ordinary person would like to appear bareheaded before friends. How, then, could that be a fitting way to appear before Allah, the Master of all the worlds? This dislike is drawn from the word zīnah in the verse and from the Prophet's ﷺ explanations.

### What rulings come from "eat and drink, but do not waste"?

This sentence too was revealed against a single custom, the pagan belief that eating good food on Hajj was a sin. Its general words, though, yield many rulings.

Eating and drinking as needed is itself an obligation in the Sharīʿah. Someone who gives them up while able, until he dies or grows too weak to carry out his duties, is sinning and committing a crime in Allah's sight.

Al-Jaṣṣāṣ, in his Aḥkām al-Qurʾān, drew another ruling from the verse. In principle every food is permissible and ḥalāl unless a proof from the Sharīʿah shows that a particular thing is forbidden. The verse hints at this by not naming what is to be eaten and drunk. The masters of Arabic style have shown that leaving out the object in such a sentence signals generality. So one may eat and drink everything, except what has been declared ḥarām.

The last words, "do not waste", show that eating and drinking are not merely allowed but commanded, while excess in them is forbidden. Isrāf means crossing a limit, and it takes several forms.

- Crossing from the ḥalāl into the ḥarām, by eating and drinking forbidden things. That this is ḥarām is obvious.
- Treating what Allah made ḥalāl as ḥarām, without a reason the Sharīʿah accepts. Using the ḥarām is a sin, and in the same way, making the ḥalāl ḥarām is a revolt against Allah's law and a very grave sin.
- Eating and drinking beyond hunger and need. For this reason Muslim jurists (fuqahāʾ) have written that eating more than is needed to remove hunger is not permissible.
- Eating far less than needed, when one could eat, so that one grows weak and cannot carry out one's duties.

The Qur'an forbids both kinds of excess: "Surely the wasteful are ˹like˺ brothers to the devils" (17:27). Sūrat al-Furqān praises "those who spend neither wastefully nor stingily, but moderately in between" (25:67).

In all, eight rulings come out of this one sentence.

1. Eating and drinking as needed is obligatory.
2. Everything is ḥalāl unless a proof the Sharīʿah accepts shows it is forbidden.
3. Using what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden is isrāf and not allowed.
4. Treating as ḥarām what Allah made ḥalāl is isrāf and a grave sin.
5. Eating more once one is full is not allowed.
6. Eating so little that one becomes too weak for one's duties is isrāf.
7. Thinking about food and drink all the time is isrāf.
8. One need not have whatever one fancies the moment one fancies it.

### Why does moderation matter so much?

Imam Aḥmad recorded that al-Miqdām ibn Maʿdīkarib al-Kindī heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say that a human being fills no vessel worse than his own stomach. A few mouthfuls that keep his back upright are enough for him. If he must have more, let a third be for food, a third for drink, and a third left for breathing. An-Nasāʾī and at-Tirmidhī also collected this hadith. At-Tirmidhī called it ḥasan, or ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ according to another manuscript.

Al-Fārūq al-Aʿẓam told people to avoid eating and drinking too much, because it ruins the body, breeds disease and makes one sluggish. He urged moderation instead, as good for the body's health and far from waste. He also said that Allah does not like a fat scholar, meaning one who has grown heavy by overeating out of choice. And he said that a person is not ruined until he puts his own desires before his faith.

The righteous elders of the early period said that being busy with eating and drinking all the time also counts as isrāf. So does putting food before weightier matters, as if life had no worthier aim. Their saying is well known: eat to live, do not live to eat.

The Prophet ﷺ counted it as isrāf to satisfy every craving as soon as it arises. Ibn Mājah reported from Anas that he said it is waste to eat everything you desire. Al-Baihaqī reported that the Prophet ﷺ once saw ʿĀʾishah eating twice in one day. He asked her whether she would like eating to become her only pastime.

The call to moderation is not limited to food and drink. Moderation is very desirable in what one wears, where one lives and almost everything else. Ibn ʿAbbās's saying, given above, joins them: eat and drink as you like and wear what you like, avoiding only excess beyond need, and pride.

These rules carry religious benefit. Seen medically, a better prescription for health would be hard to find. Moderation in eating and drinking is a shelter from every disease.

### Half a verse of medicine

The caliph Hārūn ar-Rashīd had a Christian physician. He once said to ʿAlī ibn Ḥusayn ibn al-Wāqidī that the Qur'an contained nothing about medicine. Yet, he said, there are only two kinds of knowledge in our time: knowledge of religion, and knowledge of bodies, which is medicine. ʿAlī replied that Allah had put the whole of medicine into half a verse: "Eat and drink, but do not waste." The same saying is reported from some other early elders.

The physician then asked whether their Prophet had said anything about medicine. ʿAlī answered that the Prophet ﷺ had summed up medicine in a few sayings: that the stomach is the house of disease, that keeping away from what harms is the root of all medicine, and that each body should be given what it is used to. The physician said, "Your Book and your Prophet have left no medicine for Galen (Jālīnūs)."

Al-Baihaqī reported in Shuʿab al-Īmān, from Abū Hurairah, that the Prophet ﷺ called the stomach the body's reservoir. Every vein and nerve of the body draws from it. If the stomach is sound, the veins carry healthy nourishment back from it. If it is not, they spread disease through the body. Hadith experts (muḥaddithūn) have doubts about some of the wording in these narrations. All of them agree, though, on the stress on moderate eating and care found in countless hadiths.

### The inner reading: two kinds of adornment

In the language of learning, the adornment here is covering the private parts in prayer. In the language of unveiling, every servant's adornment lies in the station of contemplation. It is the heart being present, clinging to the Presence and witnessing the Ḥaqīqah without break. It has been said that the adornment of the worshipper's soul is the mark left by prostration, and the adornment of the knower's heart is the lights of finding. The worshipper wears servanthood in his prostration. The knower rests on the carpet of nearness in the calm of witnessing.

### In short

- The verse ended naked ṭawāf and the pagan habit of fasting from good food on Hajj, and its general words bind well beyond that occasion.
- Covering the body is always obligatory and is a precondition of prayer and ṭawāf. A man covers navel to knee, a woman all but her face, palms and feet.
- Good, clean clothes for prayer, within one's means, are recommended. Praying in clothes one would not wear in public is disliked.
- Everything is lawful until proven otherwise. Isrāf includes eating the forbidden, forbidding the lawful, overeating and starving oneself.
- Moderation in food is both part of religion and the root of good health.

<<<VERSE 7:32>>>
The rebuke continues against those who forbade what Allah allowed: "Ask, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?” Say, “They are for the enjoyment of the believers in this worldly life, but they will be exclusively theirs on the Day of Judgment. This is how We make Our revelations clear for people of knowledge.”" Some people forbid certain food, drink or clothing on their own judgement, without relying on what Allah has laid down. They add self-made restrictions to worship and call it obedience. So did the disbelievers of Makkah, who would not allow clothes in ṭawāf on Hajj and counted going without good, lawful food as worship.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Ask, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who has forbidden"**: say this, O Muhammad, to the idolaters of Makkah who forbid things out of false opinion and invention. The question disowns them. It is a stern rebuke.

**"the adornments"**: the adornment Allah created, which includes garments. It covers wearing clothes during the days of pilgrimage, in the Sacred Precinct and while circling the Kaʿbah. In the pre-Islamic days they entered the Sacred Precinct completely naked, the men by day and the women by night.

**"and lawful provisions"**: aṭ-Ṭayyibāt, the good things of Allah's provision, delicious foods and drinks. They include meat and fat, which they used to forbid themselves during the days of pilgrimage. Allah made those practices unlawful.

**"Allah has brought forth for His servants?”"** Allah made them for the people who believe in Him and serve Him in this life. The disbelievers may share them for now, but that does not change whom they were made for.

**"Say, “They are for the enjoyment of the believers in this worldly life"**: for those who believe in Muhammad ﷺ and the Qur'an, though believer and sinner alike enjoy them in this world.

**"but they will be exclusively theirs on the Day of Judgment."** The word may be read khāliṣatun, "exclusively theirs", or khāliṣatan, describing their state. In the Hereafter the believers will have all of it to themselves, and no disbeliever will share in it, for Paradise is forbidden to the disbelievers. The good things are the believers' by right, even if others share them now.

**"This is how We make Our revelations clear for people of knowledge.”"** In this way Allah explains the Qur'an, setting out the lawful and the unlawful. He does it for people who reflect and believe it is from Him, since they are the ones who benefit. The verse refutes those whose excess and ignorance led them to think Allah is pleased when good clothes and good food are given up.

### Who decides what is lawful?

Declaring anything ḥalāl or ḥarām is the right of the Most Holy Being who created it, and no one else may interfere. Those who treat as ḥarām the good clothes and food Allah has allowed deserve His anger and punishment. Living in rags while having the means is no teaching of Islam. Nor, as some ignorant people think, is it something Islam holds up as a model.

### Did the early Muslims wear fine clothes?

Many of the early righteous elders and the imams of law, when Allah had given them means, often wore elegant and even costly clothes. The Prophet ﷺ himself, when his means allowed, wore the best of clothes. One narration says that he once came out wearing a cloak (ridāʾ) worth a thousand dirhams. Imam Abū Ḥanīfah is reported to have used a cloak worth four hundred guineas. Imam Mālik always wore decent and elegant clothes. Someone had taken it on himself to supply him with three hundred and sixty sets of clothes a year. Each set he wore for a single day and then gave to some poor student.

The reason is a saying of the Prophet ﷺ. When Allah gives a servant ample means, He likes to see the mark of His blessing on him, his clothing included. Letting that blessing show is a form of gratitude. Wearing worn-out or untidy clothes while having the means is ingratitude.

Two things must be guarded against, though: hypocrisy and showing off, and pride and arrogance. One should not dress well to impress others or to set oneself above them. The early elders were plainly free of such motives.

### Why did some of the best people dress simply?

The Prophet ﷺ, al-Fārūq al-Aʿẓam and some other Companions are reported to have worn plain or patched clothes. There were two reasons for this. First, they spent whatever income came to them on the poor and needy and on their religious aims, which left too little for fine clothes for themselves. Second, they were leaders of the people. They kept a simple and inexpensive appearance so that the well-off would take the hint, and so that ordinary people and the poor would not feel crushed by their standing.

The same holds for the noble Sufis, the rightly guided mystics who stress purifying the soul. When they make new seekers give up fine clothes and tasty food, they do not mean that giving these up for good earns merit (thawāb). It is a remedy, prescribed to rein in the self's unruly desires at the start of the search for truth. Once the seeker has learnt to master his desires and can no longer be lured into what is ḥarām, all the masters of the spiritual orders use and recommend good clothes and good food, like the early elders in general. At that stage these blessings become a means of knowing the Creator and drawing near Him. At the start they could have become obstacles, through ingratitude or the triumph of desire.

### What is the Sunnah in food and dress?

The basic way of the Prophet ﷺ, the Companions and the Tābiʿūn was not to fuss unnaturally over food and clothing. Whatever is easily to hand is good enough and should be used with gratitude. If the clothing is coarse and the food is dry, there is no need to strain after something better. One should not borrow for it, or brood over what is missing until it lands one in some other trouble.

In the same way, when good food and clothing come naturally, one should not go out of one's way to spoil them or avoid them. Chasing after fine food and clothes is a burden one lays on oneself. Its opposite, spoiling what is good and setting it aside for what is poor, is a self-imposed burden too, and a blameworthy one.

### Why do the good things belong to the believers?

The verse goes on to give the reason. All blessings, fine clothes and good food among them, were really created for obedient believers. Others enjoy them because of the believers, since this world is the place of deeds, not of rewards. Here no line is drawn between the genuine and the counterfeit, the good and the bad, where blessings are concerned. The feast is open to everyone, for the blessings come from al-Raḥmān, the All-Merciful to the whole world.

Yet the order of this world follows Allah's will, and His usual way here is this. When His obedient servants fall short in their duties to Him, others overpower them and seize the treasures of the world. The believers are then left in hunger and poverty. That law works only in this passing world of deeds. In the Hereafter every blessing and comfort will be kept for Allah's obedient servants. So the sentence means: tell them that all the blessings of this life are by right the believers' own, and on the Day of Qiyāmah they will be theirs alone.

Ibn ʿAbbās explained the verse differently. All the world's blessings and comforts belong by right to obedient believers alone, on condition that they do not become a source of trouble in the Hereafter. Disbelievers and sinners also receive these blessings in this life, often in greater measure. But for them the blessings will turn into ruin in the Hereafter, bringing punishment that lasts forever. Judged by the outcome, that is no comfort or honour anyone would want.

Others explain it in yet another way. Every blessing and comfort of this world comes mixed with endless striving, fear of decline and anxiety that never ends. Pure blessing and pure comfort do not exist here. Whoever receives blessings on the Day of Judgement will have them in perfect purity. There will be no toil for them, no fear of losing them and no worry after them. All three explanations fit the sentence, and that is why the Companions and the Tābiʿūn held to them.

### How should a believer use these gifts?

In the Hereafter Allah's bounties will come as a reward, and only those He sends to Paradise will receive them. The bounties of this world are different. They are available for a limited time, for the purpose of testing people. Each person receives his share as part of his own "test paper". The way to pass this test is not to keep away from the provisions Allah has given for it. It is to use them within the limits set for them. A person should answer Allah's blessings with gratitude, not with renunciation or a show of bravado.

### The inner reading: the adornments of Allah

Remembrance adorns the tongue, and reflection adorns the heart. Each thing has its own ornament. The ornament of the soul is good practice joined to struggle. The ornament of the heart is unbroken union in the moment of contemplation. The ornament of the secret core is the realities of nearness in the field of face-to-face vision.

When the Exalted Lord asks, "Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah?", He hints that these adornments are not held back from those who seek them, or from people whose hearts are present. The storehouse of blessings is full, and all it lacks is seekers. The table of kindness and mercy is laid and waits only for guests.

The master of the spiritual path (ṭarīqah) said in his whispered prayer: "Seekers, hurry, for the treasure is near! Night travellers, do not sleep, for the morning is near! You who hasten, rejoice, for the dwelling is near! Thirsty ones, be patient, for the spring is near! Strangers, be glad, for the generous host is near! Seekers of the Friend, take heart, for the answer is near!" And: "O Opener of my heart! What harm would it do if You opened my heart and laid Your balm on my spirit? How can I look for profit when my hands hold no capital? What if, out of Your bounty, You cast me into good fortune?"

### In short

- Only Allah, the Creator, decides what is lawful. Forbidding the good clothes and food He allowed is a sin, not piety.
- Wearing good clothes when one has the means is gratitude, so long as there is no showing off or pride. The simple dress of early leaders came from generosity and from their role as leaders.
- The Sunnah is to use gratefully what is at hand, neither chasing luxury nor spoiling what is good.
- The world's good things belong by right to the believers though others share them now. In the Hereafter they will be the believers' alone, pure and lasting.

<<<VERSE 7:33>>>
Having rebuked those who forbade what Allah allowed, the surah now lists what He has truly forbidden: "Say, “My Lord has only forbidden open and secret indecencies, sinfulness, unjust aggression, associating ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺—a practice He has never authorized—and attributing to Allah what you do not know.”" It is by leaving these things that His pleasure is won. The idolaters were lost in a twofold ignorance. They denied themselves, for no reason, the good things Allah had made lawful. At the same time they eagerly embraced what was truly forbidden, which would bring Allah's anger and the punishment of the Hereafter. So they were doubly deprived: of the blessings of this world, and of the blessings they might have had in the next.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Say, “My Lord has only forbidden open and secret indecencies"**: the fawāḥish are grave sins such as fornication, both the open kind and the hidden kind. One explanation gives open adultery and the taking of secret lovers as examples. Imam Aḥmad recorded from ʿAbdullāh that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that no one is more protective of honour than Allah, and that is why He forbade indecencies, open and secret. He added that no one loves praise more than Allah. The hadith is also in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs. The open and hidden fāḥishah was explained under Sūrat al-Anʿām.

**"sinfulness"**: the word is ithm. As-Suddī said al-ithm means disobedience, and Mujāhid said it covers every kind of disobedience. One explanation takes it as intoxicants and quotes a poet: "I sipped sin until my mind went astray. Thus indeed does sin take away minds."

**"unjust aggression"**: baghy. As-Suddī said it is transgressing against people without any justification. Others call it unlawful high-handedness, or oppressing people in their persons, their wealth or their honour. Mujāhid said that the oppressor commits oppression against himself. So ithm is the sin a person commits against himself, while baghy is transgression against others, and Allah has forbidden both. Put another way, ithm covers the sins whose harm falls on oneself, and baghy covers the sins that touch other people's rights and dealings.

**"associating ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺"**: this forbids shirk, calling on partners besides Allah and worshipping others alongside Him.

**"a practice He has never authorized"**: no warrant was ever sent down for it, no scripture, no proof and no decisive evidence.

**"and attributing to Allah what you do not know.”"** This means lies and inventions, such as the claim that Allah has a son, and the other false creeds of which the idolaters had no knowledge. It includes forbidding crops and cattle, good things and clothing, which He never forbade. It also covers speaking about Allah without knowledge concerning His names, attributes, actions and sacred law. A similar verse says, "So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood" (22:30).

### Why does the list name these sins in particular?

The list covers almost every kind of prohibition and sin. It takes in matters of belief and of conduct, sins against oneself, and the seizing of other people's rights. Shirk and lying about Allah are, plainly, grave sins. The list was also chosen because the people of the Jāhiliyyah were caught up in every one of these crimes. It exposed one more side of their ignorance: they kept away from what was lawful for them and did not hesitate to take what was forbidden.

### Where does excess in religion lead?

Some Arab tribes circled the Kaʿbah completely bare, thinking it brought them closer to Allah. In the pre-Islamic period they also gave up certain things, such as goat's milk or meat, when setting out on Hajj, imagining they were doing great acts of piety. People of every age have fallen into this kind of misguided action. They do not bring the demands of religion into their everyday lives. Only on certain occasions do they make a great show of religion, by paying special attention to some pointless act. Then they claim that by attending to trifles they are doing Allah's will to the fullest.

A sad but certain result of excess (ghuluww) in religion and of invented practices (bidʿah) is that those caught up in them grow used to neglecting the root of religion and its real demands. So the ḥarām that comes from excess and innovation is twofold. Getting involved in them is a sin in itself. Far graver, by comparison, is being cut off from Allah's true religion and from the genuine way of the Prophet ﷺ. We seek refuge with Allah from such a fate.

### What does Allah want instead?

Allah wants a person to keep from being wasteful, which means not overstepping the limits He has set.

- He should not treat the permitted as forbidden, or the forbidden as permitted.
- He should avoid shameful acts, and whatever common sense shows to be plainly evil, and shun every kind of excess.
- Whenever the truth comes before him, he should accept it and let everything else go.
- He should cleanse himself completely of shirk. He should give no one the exalted bond that is the right of the one God alone.
- He should not choose a path for himself and then ascribe it to Allah without reason. He should remain always Allah's servant and adopt no way unfit for a servant.

### In short

- Allah has forbidden only real evils: indecencies open and hidden, sin against oneself, aggression against others, shirk, and speaking about Him without knowledge.
- The idolaters were doubly deprived. They forbade themselves lawful blessings while embracing the true prohibitions.
- Excess and invented practices are sins in themselves, and they lead people away from the essentials of religion.
- What Allah wants is a servant who keeps His limits, accepts the truth and attributes nothing to Him without knowledge.
