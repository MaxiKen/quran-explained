<<<VERSE 4:71>>>
From obedience to arms: "O believers! Take your precautions and go forth either in groups or together". After the command to obey Allah and His Messenger comes the prescription of jihād for the obedient and faithful, to vitalize their faith and raise the word of Allah high.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Take your precautions"** commands the faithful to guard against their enemies, preparing the necessary weapons and supplies, increasing the troops fighting in His cause, wary and vigilant. The command teaches two things at once. Gathering the means that work toward a good purpose does not contradict tawakkul, trust in God. And the text limits itself to the command of means. No guaranteed security is promised by their support, for means bring nothing but a certain peace of mind. The Qur'an says: "Nothing will befall us except what Allah has decreed for us" (9:51).

**"go forth either in groups"** means group after group, party after party, expedition after expedition. ʿAlī ibn Ṭalḥah reported that Ibn ʿAbbās read it as groups, expedition after expedition, with the like from Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, as-Suddī, Qatādah, Ad-Daḥḥāk, ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurrāsānī, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān and Khusayf al-Jazarī. The thubat is the small group, the military company called a sariyyah in the campaigns of the Prophet ﷺ, for going alone in fighting is very likely to hurt.

**"or together"** means all of you, marching as one large army.

### The rule beyond the battlefield

Even in normal circumstances this is what the sharīʿah teaches: do not travel alone. In one hadith a lone traveler is called one satan, two of them two satans, and three a group. Another says: the best companions are four, the best military company four hundred, and the best army four thousand, from the Mishkāt as reported by al-Ṭabarānī. The instruction to be vigilant, organizing defence through peaceful policies and strategic preparations, guarding against enemies individually and collectively, runs through the believer's life, for this world is a place of trial where even miscreants may oppress without justification. The two Ṣaḥīḥs hold the guarantee that stands over all the marching: whoever goes forth in Allah's cause, He will either admit him into Paradise by death, or help him return safely home with whatever reward and booty he gained, as Ibn Jarīr's records of the verse's occasion confirm.

Read inwardly, fleeing toward God, firār ilā God, is among the attributes of those who seek, and fleeing with God among the attributes of those who arrive. Rest with God cannot be found except by those sincere in fleeing to Him, and fleeing from everything other than Him is the concern of anyone who declares His unity.

### In short

- Precautions and marching orders are commanded together, and means do not cancel tawakkul, for nothing befalls but what Allah has decreed.
- The march is in companies or as one army, and the lone traveler is called a satan.
- The mujāhid's guarantee covers both ends of the road, martyrdom and safe return with reward.
- Inwardly, the true march is the flight to God, and rest is found only by the sincere in flight.

<<<VERSE 4:72>>>
The lingerer described: "There will be some among you who will lag behind so that if you face a disaster, they will say, 'Allah has blessed us for not being there among them.'". The verse that follows the marching orders shows who stays home, and why.

### Phrase by phrase

**"There will be some among you who will lag behind"** was revealed about the hypocrites, as Mujāhid and others said, and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān read the lingering as staying behind and not joining jihād. It is also possible that the person lingers while luring others away from joining, as ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, may Allah curse him, used to linger behind and lure others to refrain, as Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Jarīr stated. The one meant is Ibn Ubayy, but the description runs wider than one man. Al-Qurṭubī says the characteristics cannot be those of the believers, and signify the hypocrites, who claimed Islam outwardly and were therefore identified as a group from among the believers.

**"if you face a disaster"** means death, martyrdom, or, by Allah's wisdom, defeat by the enemy.

**"'Allah has blessed us for not being there among them'"** shows the reasoning: since he never joined them in battle, he counts his safety a favour, unaware of the reward he might have gained from enduring war or martyrdom.

### The two faces of the lingerer

Seen at the battle of Uḥud and after, these are people who want success in the Hereafter at no cost in this world. They show up enthusiastically where worldly gain is possible, and find excuses when a religious struggle brings worldly loss. They occupy the same materialistic ground as before Islam, lacking the conviction that the Hereafter is what truly matters. Read inwardly, their beliefs are not firmly established in one state. Tied to worldly concerns, they are grateful when something unpleasant nears the Muslims, saying praise be to God for protection from what afflicted them, and they incline toward the Muslims when a blessing comes. They lose in both worlds, neither complete disbelievers nor loyal believers, having dropped the commitment to protect the rest in lives, property and honour.

### In short

- The lingerer is the hypocrite, Ibn Ubayy chief among them, who stays back and holds others back.
- Disaster draws from him the sigh of relief, booty will draw the other sigh, and both reveal the same unbelief.
- The Uḥud pattern of wanting the Hereafter without risk is his signature, and his loss runs through both worlds.

<<<VERSE 4:73>>>
The other face of the same man: "But if you return with Allah's bounties, they will say—as if there had been no bond between you—'We wish we had been there with them to share the great gain!'". Verse 72 caught him at the disaster. Verse 73 catches him at the triumph.

### Phrase by phrase

**"if you return with Allah's bounties"** means victory, triumph and booty.

**"as if there had been no bond between you"** means as if he were not a follower of the religion, no acquaintance, friendship or tie of affection left, for he had already dropped the commitment to protect their lives, property and honour.

**"'We wish we had been there with them to share the great gain!'"** reveals the arithmetic: a share of the booty assigned and taken possession of. That is his ultimate aim, the great success being abundance and a good portion.

### The cry that convicts

The regret is real but its object convicts him. Had the lingering been principle, it would have kept him home at the triumph too. Instead he cries out as if no bond had ever existed, wishing to have marched only where marching paid. The reports of the passage, from Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān's reading to Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Jarīr's record of Ibn Ubayy's practice, and al-Qurṭubī's verdict on the verse, all describe the type. The jihād he avoided, and the path he would not walk, remain what they were, and the uḥud pattern of his hesitation stands recorded in the two Ṣaḥīḥs' picture of the type, as Ibn ʿAbbās's reading of the passage confirms.

### In short

- Booty turns the sigh of relief into the cry of regret, and both are confessions.
- The bond of religion he denies by his conduct was already dropped in his commitments.
- His great success is a share of the spoils, and the believer's great success is the path itself.
<<<END>>>

<<<VERSE 4:74>>>
The command that answers them both: "Let those who would sacrifice this life for the Hereafter fight in the cause of Allah. And whoever fights in Allah's cause—whether they achieve martyrdom or victory—We will honour them with a great reward". The lingerer wanted the Hereafter without risk. The fighter is defined by the opposite trade.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Let those who would sacrifice this life for the Hereafter fight"** carries two readings. One addresses the hypocrites: let those who sell this world for the next fight instead of lingering, they who trade their religion for the world's meager goods out of disbelief. The sounder reading addresses the sincere: those who rate the Hereafter above this world are the ones to fight in the way of Allah, elevating His religion, aspiring to the Hereafter alone, and paying this world's benefits and conveniences for progress along the path of God.

**"in the cause of Allah"** means purely for His cause, for the real struggle is the one waged purely for the sake of God, not for fame or the safety of lip service.

**"whether they achieve martyrdom or victory"** covers both ends: slain a martyr or triumphant over the enemy, the two outcomes are one.

**"We will honour them with a great reward"** means an immense compensation with Allah, the plentiful reward of Paradise and the pleasure of Allah.

### The two ends of one road

The guarantee stands over both: whoever fights in the cause of Allah, killed or victorious, earns the great wage, as the two Ṣaḥīḥs record, and the treatment of the fighting verses of 2:191 runs under the whole passage. Read inwardly, the jihād of someone who has not slain the lower self in himself is not sound. First the fear of death is removed from the heart, and then the lower self is surrendered to it. The great wage means that His remaining after the fighter is better for him than his life through his lower self. Their speaker said: are You not my compensation for myself? That is honour enough. Besides You I have no goal, nor anything to request.

### In short

- The believer sells this life for the Hereafter, and the fight is the market of that sale.
- Martyrdom and victory are one wage, and the two Ṣaḥīḥs guarantee both ends of the road.
- Inwardly, the sound struggle begins with slaying the fear of death, and its wage is God Himself.
