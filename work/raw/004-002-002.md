<<<VERSE 4:2>>>
"Give orphans their wealth when they reach maturity, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin." This verse commands the guardian of an orphan to hand over the orphan's property in full once the orphan has grown up and can manage it, and it forbids two ways of cheating: swapping the orphan's good property for the guardian's bad property, and mixing the orphan's wealth with the guardian's so that it gets eaten up. It ends by calling such conduct a great sin. The verse is the first of several in this surah that protect orphans, and it connects to the surah's opening command of taqwa, because the rights of orphans are rights of kinship that cannot be enforced by courts alone.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Give orphans their wealth"** means hand over what belongs to them when they reach maturity and sound judgement. An orphan (yatim) is a child whose father has died. In the terminology of Islamic law, a yatim is a child whose father has died. The word literally means "the lone one," and a lone pearl in its shell is called an orphan pearl (Ad-Durr al-Yatim). Orphanhood ends at puberty. The property may have come by gift or inheritance, and its protection and upkeep fall on the guardian, whether appointed by the father or by the government. The guardian must spend on the orphan's maintenance from the orphan's own funds, but should not hand the property over before maturity, since an immature child may lose it, which is haram. So "give" here first means protect and conserve, so that the property can be handed over intact at the right time. A detailed explanation of mixing property appears in connection with 2:220, Section 27 of Surah al-Baqarah.

**"When they reach maturity"** marks the time when the orphan is no longer a minor and can distinguish what is good for him from what is not, as clarified later in this surah.

**"And do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables"** forbids substituting bad for good: a lean goat for a healthy one, a bad coin for a good one. Some guardians let the number of items stay the same while swapping a good item of the orphan's for a bad item of their own, excusing themselves by saying "a sheep for a sheep" or "a dirham for a dirham." Such substitution is a breach of trust even when the count is unchanged, and it also covers any deal that ends in loss for the orphan.

**"Nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own"** forbids pooling the orphan's property with the guardian's and consuming from the common fund, whether the holdings are kept together or separately. Even a religiously careful person may fall into this, so the verse warns against it. The word "eat" is used because eating is a major use of property, but in usage "eating up" covers every kind of unlawful consumption.

**"For this would indeed be a great sin"** is the warning. Sayyidna Ibn Abbas said "hub" (great sin) is an Ethiopian word used in Arabic for a major sin. Any unlawful appropriation of an orphan's property, whether by lack of supervision, substitution, or mixing, is a great sin. A stern warning to those who eat up an orphan's property appears toward the end of this section at 4:10.

### Protecting orphans as a test of taqwa

Orphan boys and girls are the weakest members of a family and society, so dealing with them justly is a severe test of being imbued with the fear of Allah. Orphans deserve just and compassionate treatment with maximum protection of their rights, as they say. It is a major sin to divide joint property so that some receive valuable shares and others worthless shares, merely to make the lists tally. One reading adds that whoever is given charge over others and acts unjustly toward them has the Lord as his adversary, who avenges His servants what He will not avenge for His own sake, while the protector who acts with justice and beneficence has his right protected by God. Muqatil and al-Kalbi said the verse was revealed about a man from Ghatafan who had a great fortune belonging to his orphaned nephew. When the verse was revealed he separated the nephew's wealth from his own for fear of sin. A report tells that the nephew claimed the fortune at puberty, the uncle refused, and they took the dispute to the Prophet, who then received this verse. The uncle surrendered the wealth and the Prophet said that whoever is saved from the stinginess of his ego in this way will abide in Allah's Garden. When the youth spent the wealth in Allah's way, the Prophet said the reward is confirmed but the sin still persists, and when asked how, he said the reward is for the youth while the sin persists for his father.

### The prohibition of marrying female orphans without a dowry

The next part of the verse (and the following verses) addresses marriage. If a guardian fears he cannot deal justly with an orphan girl in his care, he should marry other women of his choice, two or three or four. If he fears he cannot do justice even then, one only, or what his right hand possesses. This was revealed about a man who was caring for a female orphan, married her without desiring to, mixing her money with his and keeping her portion from her. Aishah explained that the verse concerns the orphan girl who lives with her guardian and shares his property: her wealth and beauty may tempt him to marry her without an adequate dowry that another suitor might have given, so guardians were forbidden to marry such orphan girls unless they treated them justly and gave a suitable dowry. Otherwise they were to marry other women. When people asked the Prophet again, Allah revealed 4:127, where "those whom you desire to marry" refers to the guardian who does not desire to marry an orphan girl under his supervision because she is neither wealthy nor beautiful. Guardians were forbidden to marry orphan girls who had property and beauty unless they were just to them, since they usually avoid marrying girls who are neither beautiful nor wealthy.

### The permission to marry four women

**"Two or three or four"** means a man may marry as many women as he likes other than orphan girls, up to four. Allah's statement in 35:1 about angels with wings, two or three or four, does not mean other angels do not have more than four wings, since some angels have more. But men are prohibited from marrying more than four wives, because the verse specifies what men are allowed. If more were allowed, the verse would have mentioned it, as Ibn Abbas and the majority of scholars stated. Ahmad recorded that Salim reported from his father that Ghilan bin Salamah Ath-Thaqafi had ten wives when he became Muslim, and the Prophet told him, "Choose any four of them and divorce the rest." During the reign of Umar, Ghilan divorced his remaining wives and divided his money among his children. Umar rebuked him, saying the devil had conveyed news of his imminent death, and ordered him to take back his wives and money or else his grave would be stoned like the grave of Abu Righal of Thamud, who was saved while in the Sacred Area but tormented when he left it. Ash-Shafii, At-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah, Ad-Daraqutni and Al-Bayhaqi collected the hadith up to the Prophet's statement "Choose any four of them". Only Ahmad collected the full version. The Prophet's command to keep only four, even for a man who already had more upon embracing Islam, shows that no one may keep more than four wives at any time, and this applies even more to marrying more than four.

### Marrying only one wife

If a man fears he cannot do justice between his wives by marrying more than one, he should marry only one, or satisfy himself with female captives, for treating captives equally is not obligatory but recommended. In another verse Allah says, "You will never be able to do perfect justice between wives even if it is your ardent desire" (4:129). "That is nearer to prevent you from ta'ulu" means from doing injustice. Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Marduwyah and Abu Hatim Ibn Hibban recorded in his Sahih that Aishah said the Prophet said this verse means from doing injustice. But Ibn Abi Hatim said his father held this hadith to be mistakenly attributed to the Prophet and that it should be attributed to Aishah. Ibn Abi Hatim lists Ibn Abbas, Aishah, Mujahid, Ikrimah, Al-Hasan, Abu Malik, Abu Razin, An-Nakhai, Ash-Shabi, Ad-Dahhak, Ata Al-Khurasani, Qatadah, As-Suddi and Muqatil bin Hayyan as saying ta'ulu means to deviate from justice.

### The dowry is obligatory

**"And give to the women whom you marry their saduqat (dowry) as a nihlah (gift)."** Ali bin Abi Talhah reported Ibn Abbas saying nihlah refers to the dowry. Muhammad bin Ishaq narrated from Az-Zuhri that Urwah said Aishah said nihlah means obligatory. Muqatil, Qatadah and Ibn Jurayj said it means obligatory, and Ibn Jurayj added "specified". Ibn Zayd said in Arabic nihlah refers to what is necessary. So Allah commands that a man not marry unless he gives his wife her right, and no one after the Prophet may marry a woman except with the required dowry, nor by false promises about it. The man must pay the dowry with a good heart, as he gives a gift with a good heart. If the wife, of her own good pleasure, remits part or all of it, her husband may take it and enjoy it without fear of harm.

### In short

- The verse commands guardians to protect orphans' property and hand it over at maturity, and forbids swapping bad for good or mixing it with their own.
- Such conduct is a great sin, and protecting orphans is a severe test of taqwa.
- The verse also regulates marriage: a guardian who fears injustice to an orphan girl should marry other women, up to four, or only one.
- The dowry is obligatory, and a wife may freely remit part or all of it.
- The material adds the stories of the Ghatafan orphan and Ghilan bin Salamah, and the rulings drawn from them.
