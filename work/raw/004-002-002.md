<<<VERSE 4:2>>>
The first ruling of the surah follows straight from its opening address. Humanity was told to fear the Lord who made one family of all people, and the first members of that family to be protected are the ones with no father to protect them: "Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own". The verse assumes what was common: a guardian, usually a relative of the child, holding property that belongs to an orphan. It commands him to guard it honestly, to hand it over whole when the child grows up, and to leave it alone in the meantime. The closing words leave no room for bargaining: "For this would indeed be a great sin".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Give orphans their wealth"** takes up the duty of the guardian. Whether he was appointed by the orphan's father before his death, or by the government, the property and the child are in his charge. He may meet the costs of the orphan's maintenance and upkeep, but he must not place the property itself in the hands of an immature minor, who could lose it somewhere. The handover is to happen at the right time, and the surah returns to it in verse 5, where the property is to be given once the orphan is mature and can tell what is good from what is not. Read in that light, the command to give is, before anything else, a command to protect: keep the property sound and whole so that it can be handed over intact when the time comes.

**"˹when they reach maturity˺"** gives the timing. The property is released when the orphan comes of age and shows sound judgement, not at a fixed number of years alone.

**"and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables"** forbids the neat trick that kept the count right while robbing the child. People would leave the number of items owned by the orphan untouched, but take a good one out and put a bad one of their own in its place. A weak animal of the guardian's must not be swapped for a fat animal of the orphan's, as Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib and az-Zuhrī explained it. Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī and ad-Daḥḥāk put it generally: nothing of bad quality may be given for something of good quality. As-Suddī painted the scene: a caretaker of orphans takes a fat sheep from their property and leaves one of his own weak sheep in its place, saying, "a sheep for a sheep". He takes a sound dirham and hands over a counterfeit one, saying, "a dirham for a dirham". The excuse would be that nothing was taken, only exchanged. The answer of the Qur'an is that this exchange is taking, and it is forbidden. The prohibition reaches further than direct swaps: any deal the guardian makes with anyone else that ends in a loss for the orphan falls under it.

**"nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own"** strikes at the commonest form of the abuse. The guardian holds the orphan's property together with his own, spends from the pooled stock, and lets the orphan do the same. No separate accounts are kept. Even a religiously careful Muslim could fall into thinking there was no sin in such an arrangement. Hence the explicit prohibition. Either the orphan's property is kept absolutely separate and spent from separately, or, if a joint household expense is unavoidable, the accounts must be so clear that the guardian can be sure he has spent nothing of the orphan's on himself or on his own interests. The question is treated at length where the Qur'an discusses it in Surah al-Baqarah (2:220).

**"For this would indeed be a great sin"** closes the warning. The word translated "great sin" is ḥūb. Ibn ʿAbbās said it comes from the Ethiopian language and means a major sin, and Arabic uses it with that meaning. The same judgement is reported from Mujāhid and ʿIkrimah, from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, al-Ḥasan and Ibn Sīrīn, from Qatādah and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, from ad-Daḥḥāk and Abū Mālik, and from Zayd ibn Aslam and Abū Sinān. Adding the orphan's property to one's own is a grave sin and a major mistake. The sternest warning of all comes a few verses later, where those who consume the property of orphans are told what awaits them (4:10).

### Who counts as an orphan

The word for orphans, al-yatāmā, is the plural of al-yatīm, which literally means the lone one or the unique one. A pearl that sits in its shell all alone is called ad-durr al-yatīm, the orphan pearl. In the terminology of Islamic law, a yatīm is a child whose father has died. Among animals, the word is used for an offspring whose mother has died. The status does not last forever. A hadith states that orphanhood ends with puberty, so once a child reaches maturity the legal protections aimed at orphanhood give way to ordinary adulthood.

### The occasion: a fortune withheld in Ghatafan

The verse came down over a real case, and the case shows exactly the abuse it targets. A man of Ghatafan held in his hands a great fortune that belonged to his orphaned nephew. When the nephew reached puberty he claimed what was his, and the uncle refused to give it up. So they took their dispute to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and this verse was revealed. The uncle at once said: "We obey Allah and we obey His Messenger. We seek refuge in Allah from the great peril." And he handed the wealth over to his nephew. The Prophet ﷺ then said that whoever is saved from the stinginess of his own soul and hands over what he was holding back in this way will abide in Allah's Garden.

The story has a coda. The young man, once he had his fortune, spent it in the way of Allah. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The reward is confirmed, but the sin still persists." Those present asked how a sin could persist when the wealth was being spent in Allah's way. He answered: "The reward is confirmed for the youth, but the sin persists for his father."

### Every use of the orphan's property is "eating"

The verse speaks of not eating the orphan's property along with one's own, because eating is the most common end-use of what a person possesses. In the usage of the Qur'an, however, "do not eat up" covers every kind of impermissible use: using, consuming, exhausting. Spending anything from an orphan's property unlawfully, by whatever means, is absolutely ḥarām (forbidden).

The manner of the prohibition carries a quiet rebuke. Those who misappropriate the property of orphans are usually people who own property of their own. They have lawful means at hand and still stoop to consuming what belongs to a fatherless child. The verse names that for what it is, a shameful act.

Orphan boys and girls are the weakest members of a family or a society. Dealing with them as they deserve is therefore the hardest test of whether a person truly fears Allah. They are owed justice and compassion together, and their rights deserve the strongest protection. It counts as a major sin, for instance, to divide a jointly held property so that some receive valuable shares and others receive worthless ones, with the only aim being to make the listed items tally.

### The guardian's true opponent

There is a sharper way to read the verse, and it turns the guardian's eyes upward. Whoever is given charge over others and then acts unjustly toward those in his charge makes an adversary of his Lord. Allah avenges His servants in a way He does not avenge for His own sake. The protector of an orphan who acts with justice and kindness finds his own right protected by Allah. The one who behaves badly and aggressively finds Allah himself standing opposite him, taking the orphan's side.

### In short

- Orphans, children whose fathers have died, are to receive their property in full once they reach maturity and sound judgement. Until then the guardian protects it so it can be handed over whole.
- Swapping bad possessions of one's own for good ones of the orphan's, even with the counts matching, is forbidden taking, and so is any deal that loses the orphan money.
- The orphan's property is to be kept separate, or under accounts so clear that nothing of it is spent on the guardian.
- Every unlawful use of an orphan's property is "eating" it, and every form of it is forbidden. It is a great sin, in the Ethiopian-derived word ḥūb that Ibn ʿAbbās explained.
- The verse came down when an uncle of Ghatafan refused his nephew's fortune, and he surrendered it the moment he heard it. Whoever is saved from his own stinginess in that way abides in Allah's Garden.
- An unjust guardian makes Allah his adversary, for Allah avenges those under charge as He does not avenge His own cause.
