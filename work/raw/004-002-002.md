<<<VERSE 4:2>>>
After the call to honour family ties, the surah turns to the weakest member of the family, the orphan: "Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin." The guardian of an orphaned child is usually one of the child's relatives, so this command is one more part of keeping the rights of kinship.

Orphans are the weakest people in a family or in a society. Dealing with them fairly and kindly is therefore the hardest test of whether a person truly fears Allah. They are owed justice and compassion, and their rights must be given the greatest protection possible. The verse names three ways a guardian can wrong them: by holding back their property, by swapping good for bad, and by mixing their wealth with his own.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺"**. "Orphans" translates al-yatāmā, the plural of al-yatīm. In the language, yatīm means the lone or unique one. A pearl that lies alone in its shell is called ad-durr al-yatīm, the orphan pearl. Among animals, a yatīm is a young one whose mother has died. In the terminology of Islamic law, a yatīm is a child whose father has died. When the child reaches puberty he is no longer called a yatīm. A hadith recorded in Mishkāt states it plainly:

> "Orphanhood ends with puberty."

Other explanations agree on what the command means. The orphan's property is to be handed over in full once he reaches adolescence, which is the legal age, and shows sound judgement. The guardian is forbidden to use any part of it or to take it for himself.

**"and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables"** means do not swap bad for good. Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib and az-Zuhrī said that it means not putting a weak animal of yours in place of a fat animal of the orphan's. Ibrāhīm an-Nakhaʿī and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk said it means not giving something of poor quality for something of good quality. As-Suddī described what guardians used to do. One of them would take a fat sheep from the orphan's flock and put a lean sheep of his own in its place, saying, "A sheep for a sheep." Or he would take a good dirham and give back a fake one, saying, "A dirham for a dirham."

Other explanations put it as taking the unlawful in place of the lawful, which means keeping what is good from the orphan's property and leaving him your own faulty goods. They also say: do not consume the orphan's wealth, which is forbidden to you, while leaving your own wealth, which is lawful for you.

**"nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own"**. The Arabic says: do not devour their property by adding it to yours. Mujāhid, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, as-Suddī and Sufyān ibn Ḥassīn said it means: do not mix the two together so that you eat up both. Other explanations say the same, do not absorb it into your own property by combining them.

**"For this would indeed be a great sin"**. The Arabic word is ḥūb. Ibn ʿAbbās said that it comes from the Ethiopian language and means a major sin, and that Arabic uses it in the same sense. He called it a major and weighty sin. The same meaning was given by Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, al-Ḥasan, Ibn Sīrīn, Qatādah, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, Abū Mālik, Zayd ibn Aslam and Abū Sinān. Adding their property to yours is a grave sin and a major mistake, so avoid it. Other explanations call it a great crime before Allah, and say that it will be met by Allah's punishment.

The words cover every unlawful taking or use of an orphan's property. It makes no difference whether it comes from neglecting to look after it, from swapping bad for good, or from pooling it with one's own. The stern warning for those who devour orphans' property comes later in the same passage (4:10).

### What a guardian must do

If an orphan owns property, either given to him or received through inheritance, the duty of protecting both the property and the child falls on the guardian. It makes no difference whether the father appointed the guardian before he died or the government did. The guardian must see that the child's necessary upkeep is covered. But he must not put the property into the child's hands before maturity, because an immature child may lose it somewhere.

So "give orphans their wealth" does not mean handing it over at once. A little later the surah says when: once it is certain that they have become mature and are able to tell what is good for them from what is not. The command here therefore means to protect the property, so that it can be given to them at the right time.

The verse also hints at how far a guardian's duty goes. It is not enough that he does not eat up or squander the orphan's property himself. He must do everything he can to guard it and keep it in good condition, so that it can be handed over to the orphan when he is grown.

### Swapping good for bad

Some people left the number of the orphan's possessions unchanged, but took something good out of them and put something bad of their own in its place. They would swap a lean goat for a healthy one, or a bad coin for a good one. This is also a breach of trust. A person driven by his lower self might excuse it by saying that he did not take what belonged to the orphan, he only exchanged it. The Qur'an forbids it in plain words.

The ban is wider than the swap itself. It also covers any deal the guardian makes with someone else that ends in a loss for the orphan.

A related wrong is the unfair division of shared property. It is a major sin to divide property that is held jointly so that some people receive valuable shares and others receive worthless ones, only so that the list of items adds up.

### Mixing the accounts

The words "mixing their wealth with your own" forbid eating up the orphan's property by any impermissible means. It makes no difference whether it is taken from property held jointly or from property held apart. The common practice was for the guardian to keep the orphan's property together with his own, eat from it himself and let the orphan eat from it too. There was no separate accounting, so even a practising Muslim might think there was no sin in it. That is why the prohibition on pooling is stated here.

The warning gives two choices. One is to keep the orphan's property completely separate and to spend from it separately, so that there is no danger of taking too much. The other is to run a shared household budget, but then the accounts must be so clear that one can be sure nothing belonging to the orphan was spent on oneself or for one's own benefit. A longer discussion of this appears in the passage on 2:220, in section 27 of Al-Baqarah.

The wording also hints that the people who misuse orphans' property are generally people who have property of their own. By implication they are reproached for stooping to eat up an orphan's property unlawfully when their own lawful belongings are at hand, and that is a shameful act.

### Why the verse says "eat"

The verse speaks of "eating" the orphan's property because eating is one of the main ways property is used up. In everyday speech, though, every kind of using, consuming and exhausting is called "eating up", whether it is done by eating or by any other use. The Qur'an's words "do not eat up", lā taʾkulū, carry this broad sense, and it takes in every impermissible use. Spending anything from an orphan's property unlawfully, by any means at all, is ḥarām, forbidden.

### The uncle from Ghaṭafān

Muqātil and al-Kalbī said that the verse was revealed about a man from Ghaṭafān who held a large fortune that belonged to his orphaned nephew. When the nephew reached puberty he asked for the fortune, and his uncle refused. They took their dispute to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and this verse was revealed. When the uncle heard it, he said, "We obey Allah and we obey His Messenger. We seek refuge in Allah from the great peril." Then he handed the wealth over to his nephew. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

> "Whoever is saved from the stinginess of his own self and does what he did will abide in Allah's Garden."

When the young man received his fortune, he spent it in the way of Allah. The Prophet ﷺ said, "The reward is confirmed, but the sin still persists." Some people asked, "O Messenger of Allah, we know the reward is confirmed. How can the sin still persist when he is spending his wealth in the way of Allah?" He answered, "The reward is confirmed for the young man, while the sin persists for his father."

Another account says that when the man from Ghaṭafān heard the verse, he decided to separate his nephew's wealth from his own, for fear of committing a sin. A third says that the verse came down about an orphan who asked his guardian for his property and was refused.

After it was revealed, some guardians found it hard to keep up their guardianship of orphans. Some of them had eight or ten wives in their care and did not treat them all equally. The next verse was revealed in response.

### The inner reading

Whoever is given charge over others and then deals unjustly with those under his care will find that his opponent is his Lord. Allah takes revenge for His servants in a way He does not take revenge for His own sake. If the protector of an orphan acts with justice and kindness, Allah guards his right. If he behaves badly and with aggression, Allah is his adversary.

### In short

- Orphans, children whose fathers have died, must have their property protected and handed over in full when they come of age and show sound judgement. The guardian must not use any of it.
- Three things are forbidden: swapping one's bad possessions for the orphan's good ones, mixing the orphan's wealth with one's own so as to eat up both, and any deal that causes the orphan a loss. "Eating" stands for every unlawful use.
- A guardian should either keep the accounts completely separate or keep them so clear that no doubt remains. Doing otherwise is a great sin, and a stern warning follows later (4:10).
- The verse is said to have come down about a man from Ghaṭafān and his orphaned nephew. The uncle obeyed and handed over the fortune.
- On its inner side, Allah Himself is the opponent of a guardian who wrongs those in his charge.
