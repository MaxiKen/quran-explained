<<<VERSE 3:121>>>
> “˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you left your home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing” (3:121)

The verse recalls the Prophet’s departure from his home in Madinah to arrange the believers’ positions before the Battle of Uhud. It reminds them that Allah heard their words and knew the intentions behind their decisions.

### Phrase by phrase

**“when you left your home in the early morning”** refers to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ setting out to meet the Quraysh forces near Uhud. The battle took place in Shawwal, in the third year after the Hijrah. The Quraysh had gathered an army after their defeat at Badr. Abu Sufyan’s trade caravan from Syria had returned safely, and its wealth was used to prepare for another confrontation. The force that came toward Madinah included people seeking revenge for relatives lost at Badr.

The Prophet ﷺ consulted the believers about whether to remain in Madinah or go out to meet the advancing force. Some favored defending the city from within, while others who had not taken part at Badr wished to face the enemy in the open. The Prophet returned home, put on his armor, and came back out. When the companions realized they may have pressed their preference upon him, they offered to stay in Madinah if he wished. He replied that a prophet who has put on armor does not set it aside before Allah has decided the matter. The account shows the seriousness of consultation and the commitment to proceed once a decision had been made.

Reports differ slightly over the number who initially marched. Some say about a thousand, while others give a figure near 950. During the journey, Abdullah ibn Ubayy withdrew with about a third of the force, leaving roughly 700 believers to continue. The departure tested morale, but it also made clear that the community’s strength did not come simply from its size.

**“to position the believers in the battlefield”** describes the Prophet ﷺ arranging the army according to the terrain. He placed Uhud behind the Muslim force and assigned the companions to their positions. Musʿab ibn ʿUmayr carried the standard. A group of fifty archers was placed on a rise under Abdullah ibn Jubayr, with orders to protect the rear and remain at their post whether the battle appeared to be going for or against the Muslims. Their task was essential to the plan.

The Quraysh force numbered around three thousand, with cavalry commanded by Khalid ibn al-Walid and ʿIkrimah on the two flanks. They came from Makkah after the losses at Badr and were determined to reverse the result. The Muslim force had begun with about a thousand men, but Abdullah ibn Ubayy left with roughly three hundred. The remaining believers continued toward Uhud and took their assigned stations.

The Prophet ﷺ instructed the archers that they must not leave their place without his order. Abdullah ibn Jubayr led the archers on the rise behind the Muslim force. Musʿab ibn ʿUmayr carried the Muslim standard, while the tribe of Banu ʿAbd al-Dar carried the Quraysh standard. A report says that, before leaving Madinah, the Prophet prayed over Malik ibn ʿAmr of Banu an-Najjar. These details show how the community prepared and how specific responsibilities were entrusted to individuals.

At first the Muslims gained the upper hand. Some archers then saw the opposing force retreat and assumed the battle was over. Abdullah ibn Jubayr reminded them of the Prophet’s command to stay. Most nevertheless left their post, and Khalid ibn al-Walid led cavalry around the hill and attacked from the rear. The Muslims were thrown into confusion. A rumor spread that the Prophet ﷺ had been killed, and some companions scattered while others remained near him. The following verses address the grief and trial of that day (3:154). The lesson of this sequence is not that every setback is a punishment. It is that discipline, patience, and listening to sound instruction matter even when success seems assured.

Reports describe the courage of the companions during the fighting. Abu Dujanah shielded the Prophet ﷺ from arrows, and Talhah ibn ʿUbayd Allah sustained injuries while protecting him. Anas ibn al-Nadr advanced when others had scattered, seeking the reward of shahadah, martyrdom. His death was a shahadah. Such reports honor their courage without calling Muslims to seek conflict. The Prophet had set the conditions for battle and the companions defended their community from an attacking army.

This account also places Uhud beside other conflicts in the Prophet’s life. The Quraysh had earlier been defeated at Badr, and the Muslims later faced the siege at al-Khandaq. The strength of a community was not measured by whether it won every battle. At Badr, a small force overcame a larger one. At Uhud, a change in the archers’ position led to a severe reversal. The Qur’an warns elsewhere that numbers alone did not help the Muslims at Hunayn (9:25), then describes Allah’s mercy and forgiveness (9:27). Together, these events distinguish reliance on Allah from confidence in weapons or headcounts.

This verse itself focuses on the departure and deployment. It shows the Prophet taking practical responsibility while knowing that Allah heard every discussion and knew each person’s intention. The later setback at Uhud would reveal the cost of abandoning a clear instruction, while also showing that Allah’s mercy and guidance remained with the community (3:154).

Reports describe the courage of the companions during the fighting. Abu Dujanah shielded the Prophet ﷺ from arrows, and Talhah ibn ʿUbayd Allah sustained injuries while protecting him. Anas ibn al-Nadr advanced when others had scattered, seeking the reward of shahadah, martyrdom. These accounts are not a call to seek violence. They show the companions’ loyalty when the Prophet was in danger and the battle seemed lost.

**“And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”** means that Allah heard the consultation, the doubts, the withdrawal, and the reassurances. He knew what each person concealed in the heart. His knowledge includes the Prophet’s careful planning and the believers’ fear, patience, and intention. The verse therefore asks the community to act sincerely and remember that the outcome belongs to Allah.

### Why recall the preparation for Uhud?

The opening scene explains that the battle was not entered carelessly. The Prophet sought counsel, prepared the force, and assigned positions. Reliance upon Allah does not mean ignoring material preparation. The believers gathered what means they could, then trusted Allah with the outcome. The later setback at Uhud would reveal the costs of abandoning a clear instruction, while also showing that Allah’s mercy and guidance remained with the community.

### In short

- The Prophet ﷺ consulted the believers and led them from Madinah toward Uhud.
- He assigned the army positions and placed archers to guard the rear.
- Allah heard every word and knew the intentions of all involved.

<<<VERSE 3:122>>>
> “˹Remember˺ when two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower, then Allah reassured them. So in Allah let the believers put their trust” (3:122)

The verse recalls a moment of fear during the preparations for Uhud. Two groups of believers nearly lost heart when Abdullah ibn Ubayy withdrew with his followers, but Allah strengthened them and they did not abandon the Prophet.

### Phrase by phrase

**“when two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower”** refers to Banu Harithah of the Aws and Banu Salamah of the Khazraj. The withdrawal of a large part of the force made the danger feel greater. The two groups briefly considered turning back, but they did not carry out that impulse. Their moment of fear did not cancel their faith.

Jabir ibn Abdullah reports that the verse was revealed about these two groups. Al-Bukhari records his account, and Muslim also transmits it. The verse itself protects their standing by calling them believers and stating that Allah was their protector. It does not condemn them for feeling apprehension in a dangerous moment.

### Steadiness after the withdrawal

Other reports describe Abu Jabir al-Salami urging Abdullah ibn Ubayy not to withdraw, but Ubayy refused to return. Ibn Hisham’s account emphasizes that the two groups’ hesitation was caused by the sudden weakening of the army, not by abandoning belief. Ibn Abbas, Al-Hasan, and As-Suddi likewise explain that the two tribes remained among the believers. Their fear was temporary and their faith remained intact.

The larger force had marched from Madinah toward Uhud after the Quraysh, led by Abu Sufyan, came from Makkah. The companions were defending their community after the Hijrah, not seeking a fight with peaceful neighbors. Banu an-Najjar and Banu ʿAbd al-Dar are among the clans named in the account of the expedition. Later, the Muslims would face another siege at al-Khandaq. The passage treats these battles as tests of discipline and reliance, not as occasions for pride in numbers or calls to shirk, associating partners with Allah. The lesson of jihad in this setting is to remain faithful and protect the community within the Prophet’s commands. The distinction matters: a person can feel fear and still act with faith.

**“then Allah reassured them”** means that Allah preserved them from turning back and strengthened their resolve. The verse names Allah as their wali, their protector and helper. Their steadiness was a gift from Him. It was not proof that they possessed no fear or that their own strength was sufficient.

**“So in Allah let the believers put their trust”** draws a lesson from the incident. Tawakkul means relying on Allah while using the means available. The Prophet ﷺ had consulted the companions, gathered the force, planned the positions, and assigned the archers. Trust did not replace preparation. It kept the believers from believing that numbers, equipment, or the withdrawal of others had the final say.

The Qur’an later describes the difficulty at Hunayn, when the believers’ large numbers did not guarantee success (9:25-27). The contrast teaches that a smaller force may be supported by Allah and a larger one may still face a trial. The believer prepares, follows sound leadership, and entrusts the result to Allah.

### In short

- The two groups were Banu Harithah and Banu Salamah.
- They briefly feared the danger but remained with the believers.
- Allah was their protector, and the event teaches reliance joined to preparation.

<<<VERSE 3:123>>>
> “Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered. So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful” (3:123)

The verse reminds the believers of their victory at Badr when their force was small and poorly equipped. It directs them to taqwa and gratitude, because the victory came from Allah rather than from their numbers or resources.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Allah made you victorious at Badr”** recalls the first major battle between the Muslims and the Quraysh. Badr lies between Makkah and Madinah and was known for its well. The battle took place on a Friday in Ramadan, in the second year after the Hijrah. It is also called Yawm al-Furqan, the Day of Distinction, because Allah made clear the difference between faith and opposition to the message.

Reports place the Muslim force at about 313 people, with only a small number of horses and camels, while the Quraysh had close to a thousand fighters and much more equipment. The exact details in historical reports vary, but all emphasize the disparity. The Muslims were not victorious because they were materially stronger. Allah supported the Prophet ﷺ and those who followed him.

The battle also followed the migration from Makkah to Madinah, when the believers had left homes and property behind. They had faced opposition from Quraysh and sought safety in Madinah. Their victory at Badr was therefore a major turning point, but it was not a promise that every later battle would be easy or that the believers could stop obeying Allah.

**“when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered”** means the believers were few and had limited supplies. The verse asks them to remember their vulnerable position so they do not become proud when circumstances improve. Victory belongs to Allah, and material preparation does not give anyone control over the outcome.

### What does Badr teach about reliance?

Reports describe the Muslim force as 313 people with two horses and about seventy camels, against a much larger Quraysh army. Some accounts say about seventy Quraysh fighters were killed and about seventy captured. These details explain why the verse calls the Muslims a small force, but the numbers are not the basis of victory. Ibn Abbas, Al-Hasan, and As-Suddi emphasize that Allah’s help, rather than equipment or size, made the difference.

The well at Badr gave the place its name. Some historical reports trace it to a man called Badr ibn an-Narayn who dug the well. The battlefield became known as al-Furqan because it marked a clear distinction between the believers and those who fought to extinguish their message. The victory did not make the believers independent of Allah. It increased their responsibility to follow His commands.

A report from Jabir about the two tribes who nearly lost heart at Uhud is recorded by Al-Bukhari. Read alongside Badr, it shows that believers can be brave at one moment and fearful at another. The Prophet’s followers included people from Madinah, the Anṣār, and those who had migrated from Makkah. Allah’s help was not a reward for never feeling fear. It came to a community that continued to return to Him.

An inward reflection describes the spiritual path, the tariqah, as learning to rely on Allah in both ease and danger. A devotional story about Hārūn al-Rashid and a man placed in a room with a lion illustrates this trust: the man believed that Allah could bring him out as He had brought him in. The story is an illustration, not a promise that believers will never be harmed. Its point is that a person’s ultimate security rests with Allah, the true wali and protector.

The events at Uhud and Hunayn make this lesson vivid. At Uhud, a larger opposing force brought the believers into a difficult trial. At Hunayn, the Muslims took pride in their numbers, but those numbers did not protect them from an initial setback (9:25-27). Remembering Badr helps the community understand that neither a small nor a large army guarantees victory.

**“So be mindful of Allah”** means obey Him, avoid what He has prohibited, and remain faithful when success tempts a person toward pride. Taqwa keeps gratitude connected to conduct. The community should not treat divine help as a reward it can demand while neglecting the commands that guide it.

**“perhaps you will be grateful”** calls the believers to recognize the victory as a favor from Allah. Gratitude includes praise, humility, and using success in obedience rather than revenge or arrogance. The memory of Badr should strengthen the believers’ trust and responsibility, not lead them to assume that every future outcome will resemble it.

### In short

- Badr was a victory granted to a small Muslim force against a larger Quraysh army.
- The event is remembered as the Day of Distinction, al-Furqan.
- Taqwa and gratitude keep the community from trusting in numbers instead of Allah.
