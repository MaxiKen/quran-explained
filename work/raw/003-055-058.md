<<<VERSE 3:55>>>
Allah tells ʿĪsā (Jesus), “O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself” (3:55). He will be rescued from those who disbelieve, and those who follow him will be set above their opponents until the Day of Judgment. Allah will judge the disputes people have about him and his message.

### Phrase by phrase

**“˹Remember˺ when Allah said”** introduces Allah’s promise to ʿĪsā at the time his enemies sought to seize him. The verse answers their plot with Allah’s protection and sets out what will happen to ʿĪsā, his followers, and those who reject him.

**“O Jesus!”** addresses ʿĪsā directly. The name refers to the same messenger whom the Children of Israel accused and whom the Christians later differed about. Allah’s words establish his true status and future, rather than leaving it to his opponents’ claims.

**“I will take you”** uses the word tawaffī, whose root meaning is to take something in full. In ordinary speech it is also used for death, since at death a person’s soul is taken. It can also describe sleep. The Qur’an uses the same word for Allah taking people’s souls at night during sleep and for those who die during sleep (6:60, 39:42).

The explanations differ over how this word applies to ʿĪsā. Ibn Taimiyyah, in al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ, describes three uses: taking during sleep, taking at death, and taking the soul and body together. Abū al-Baqā distinguishes common usage, where it can mean death, from the older lexical sense of taking in full. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have understood this promise as Allah taking ʿĪsā while he slept. Al-Ḥasan also understood it as sleep, in a report with a ḥasan chain.

Many interpreters take the phrase here to mean that Allah would take ʿĪsā away completely from his enemies, without allowing them to kill him. On this reading, the next phrase explains the manner of the taking: Allah raises him to Himself. Some who interpret tawaffī as death still do not mean that his enemies killed him. They understand that his death would occur naturally at a later time, after he returns to the world. The sequence in the verse mentions the taking first to point ahead to that eventual death, while his rescue and raising happen then.

**“and raise you up to Myself”** means that Allah raised ʿĪsā alive, body and soul, away from those who sought to kill him. The verses in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ say that the People of the Book did not kill or crucify him, but it appeared so to them (4:157). Another verse states that Allah raised him to Himself (4:158). The raising is not merely an elevation in spiritual rank. The phrase “to Myself,” together with the denial that they killed him, is understood as his physical ascent. Elsewhere, when the Qur’an speaks of raising people in rank, it makes that figurative meaning clear from context (6:165, 58:11).

The rescue fulfills the promise of Allah Almighty that the enemies would not decide ʿĪsā’s fate. One explanation describes this as a distinctive form of emigration, with the heavens as his destination. Other prophets left hostile places and later gained safety or victory: Ibrāhīm (Abraham) went from Iraq toward Ash-Shām, Mūsā (Moses) left Egypt for Madyan, and Muhammad ﷺ migrated from Makkah to Madinah before returning to conquer Makkah. Prophets such as Nūḥ (Noah), Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and Lūṭ (Lot) also faced rejecting peoples. ʿĪsā’s raising is unique, but the wider pattern is that Allah does not abandon His messengers to their enemies.

The verse also counters two claims about ʿĪsā. The Jews claimed that they killed him. The Christians claimed that he died on the cross and was then raised. The Islamic reading is that he was neither killed nor crucified. Allah raised him alive, and he will return near the end of the world and later die a natural death. The Qur’an rejects the claim of crucifixion (4:157) and confirms the raising (4:158). The subject is also discussed in the passage about the People of the Book (4:156-159).

**“I will deliver you from those who disbelieve”** means Allah will remove ʿĪsā from their reach and clear him of their accusations. His enemies alleged that his birth without a father proved indecency or that he claimed divinity. The Qur’an answers that a fatherless birth is a sign of Allah’s power, just as Adam was created without either a father or a mother. It also shows ʿĪsā confessing that he is Allah’s servant. Allah’s protection includes rescuing him from the plot and vindicating his truth.

**“and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment”** promises that those who follow ʿĪsā will have the upper hand over those who reject him. This includes Christians who believe in his prophethood and Muslims who believe in him as Allah’s messenger. Yet not every claim made in his name is correct. The followers who are faithful to his teaching are those who accept the later messenger, Muhammad ﷺ, and the full guidance Allah sent. His earlier followers were instructed to believe in the messenger who would come after him.

The promise of being above the disbelievers is understood both as decisive proof and as worldly victory. The followers of ʿĪsā and Muhammad ﷺ have at different times prevailed over those who rejected them. One historical explanation argues that the modern state of Israel does not overturn this promise, since it interprets that state’s power as dependent on external Christian and communist support rather than as independent Jewish dominance. It also allows that a temporary ascendancy of the Jews near the end of time may occur. These are that explanation’s political claims, while the verse itself states the lasting divine promise.

The followers of ʿĪsā later divided. Some held to the belief that he was Allah’s servant and messenger. Others exaggerated his status, calling him divine or making him part of a trinity. One account says that Constantine’s adoption of Christianity led to changes in its beliefs and practices. Muslims, by contrast, affirm ʿĪsā as prophet and servant, believe in all Allah’s messengers, and follow the final law given to Muhammad ﷺ. This is why they are described as the true followers of every prophet, including ʿĪsā.

The promise of dominance is linked to the return of ʿĪsā. He will return as a follower of Muhammad’s law and lead the Muslim community, while retaining his status as a prophet. His return will occur near the Day of Judgment. He will defeat the Dajjāl, the false Messiah, and help the believers. The community must recognize him by the signs Allah has revealed, rather than accepting any later claimant who falsely says that he is the Messiah.

**“until the Day of Judgment”** marks the duration of the promise. The opponents’ power does not end the truth of ʿĪsā’s prophethood, and the faithful remain supported by Allah through history. The final judgment will settle every dispute that worldly victory cannot resolve.

**“Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return”** reminds ʿĪsā and those who differed about him that all will return to Allah. Power in this world is temporary. Allah alone gives the final decision.

**“and I will settle all your disputes”** means that Allah will judge the disagreements about ʿĪsā and his message. He will distinguish truth from falsehood, including the claims that he was killed, crucified, or divine. No group will be able to hide behind a mistaken belief or accusation.

One explanation lists five promises in the verse: ʿĪsā will not be killed by his enemies, Allah will raise him, his innocence will be established, his faithful followers will prevail over those who reject him, and Allah will judge their disputes on the Day of Judgment. It says the first two promises were fulfilled in the rescue and raising, the third through later revelation that cleared him of slander, and the fourth through the historical strength of those who affirm his prophethood. The final judgment remains to come.

### What does Allah’s “taking” of ʿĪsā mean?

The explanations preserve more than one interpretation of tawaffī. Some understand it as Allah taking ʿĪsā during sleep and raising him alive. Others emphasize its basic sense of taking in full and understand it as removal from his enemies. Some connect it with a natural death after his return. They agree that the Jews did not kill him and that Allah raised him. Reports about his return say that he will descend near the Hour, rule according to the law of Muhammad ﷺ, kill the Dajjāl, break the cross, kill the swine, and abolish the jizyah. The Two Sahihs report his descent. A report from Muslim says he will remain for seven years. Abū Dāwūd al-Tayālisī reports forty years, which one explanation understands as his total time on earth before and after his raising.

Reports differ in their details about ʿĪsā’s age and return. One account says a cloud raised him from Jerusalem on Laylat al-Qadr when he was thirty-three, and that Maryam lived six years after him. Another account speaks of his eventual descent and natural death. These details are reported alongside the central belief that Allah rescued him alive and that he will return.

A hadith also says that ʿĪsā will return to judge by the law of Muhammad ﷺ. In that report he breaks the cross, kills the swine, and abolishes the jizyah. He will accept Islam, and his return will be a sign near the Hour. Another report gives a different period for the time he will remain on earth. These reports are treated as sound in the material, though the durations differ.

The account of ʿĪsā’s raising is connected with the Qur’an’s broader statements about Allah’s power. Adam’s creation without parents is even more extraordinary in one sense, and it shows that Allah is not bound by ordinary causes. The creation of ʿĪsā without a father, his raising, and his later return all demonstrate servitude to Allah rather than divinity. He is al-Masīḥ, the Messiah, but the title does not change his ʿaqīdah, his creed of tawḥīd and servitude to Allah.

### How has the promise to his followers been understood?

The promise that believers will prevail is connected with Allah’s promise to grant succession in the land to those who believe and do righteous deeds, provided they worship Him alone (24:55). Their strength is not an excuse for injustice. The opponents who reject Allah have no protector against Him (13:34). The verse is read as a promise of clear proof, support, and eventual worldly ascendancy, with Allah’s judgment settling the matter at the end.

One historical reading says that a state bearing the name Israel does not disprove the promise, because it views that state as dependent on the military support of foreign powers rather than as an independent Jewish ascendancy. It also allows for a temporary Jewish dominance near the end of time. These are the political conclusions of that reading, not wording stated directly in the verse. The verse itself promises that the faithful will be above those who disbelieve until the Day of Judgment.

The account points to the different paths taken by followers of ʿĪsā. Some preserved the belief that he was a servant and messenger of Allah. Others, including The Christians who went to extremes, called him divine or one person of a trinity. One historical account attributes later changes in Christian practice to Constantine’s adoption of the religion. Muslims affirm ʿĪsā’s prophethood and follow the tawḥīd that he taught, while accepting the final messenger and law. This, rather than merely claiming a connection to al-Masīḥ, is what makes one a true follower of his message.

The victory promised to believers is also compared with earlier moments of success, including Badr and later conquests. When God sent Muhammad ﷺ, the believers were granted power after persecution. The migration from Makkah to Madinah was followed by a return to Makkah, just as Ibrāhīm left Iraq for Ash-Shām and Mūsā left Egypt for Madyan. The peoples of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Lūṭ were destroyed after rejecting their messengers, while the followers of the prophets were rescued. The raising of ʿĪsā is a different form of escape from hostility, but it follows the same pattern of Allah protecting His messenger.

The promise also has an eschatological dimension. A report from the Prophet ﷺ says that ʿĪsā will descend near the Hour. The report is found in al-Ṣaḥīḥayn, the two sound collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim. He will judge according to the law of Muhammad, kill the Dajjāl, and restore justice. A report in the collection of Muslim says he will remain on earth for seven years. Abū Dāwūd al-Tayālisī reports forty years, which has been understood as counting the time before and after his raising together.

Al-Suyūṭī cites a report from Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Durr al-Manthūr about the sequence of ʿĪsā’s raising and eventual death. Al-Rāzī, in al-Tafsīr al-Kabīr, notes that revelation can mention an event due later before one that occurs earlier to signal what will follow. Ibn Ḥajar’s al-Ḥabīr is cited for the community’s consensus about ʿĪsā’s return. Al-Kashmīrī gathered reports about his descent, and ʿAbd al-Fattāḥ Abū Ghuddah annotated that collection, Al-Tasrīḥ. The argument is that the many reports and the Qur’anic references to the return make the event central to recognizing al-Masīḥ when he comes again. A discussion of his return also cites the sign described in al-Zukhruf (43:61).

Some later scholars, including Badr ʿĀlam al-Muhājir al-Madanī, wrote about recognizing the promised Messiah. These writings stress that the Muslim community must know the distinguishing signs of ʿĪsā. Otherwise, a false claimant could mislead people before his return. The same material mentions Idrīs and other prophets as part of the broader history of Allah’s messengers, while affirming that ʿĪsā retains his prophethood when he returns.

The verse’s promise is therefore not only an account of one rescue. It supports belief in tawḥīd, clears ʿĪsā from false accusations, and assures the believers that Allah will protect them. As for the disbelievers, fa-fī al-jaḥīm, they are in the Fire, while the believers are granted bliss. What We reveal about the Messiah is a reminder that human schemes cannot overcome Allah’s decree.

### In short

- Tawaffī can refer to taking in full, sleep, or death, and the explanations differ on its precise force here.
- Allah rescued ʿĪsā from his enemies and raised him alive, body and soul.
- His faithful followers will remain supported, he will return near the end of time, and Allah will finally judge disputes about him.

<<<VERSE 3:56>>>
Allah warns those who reject His messengers: “As for those who disbelieve, I will subject them to a severe punishment” (3:56). Their punishment includes hardship in this world and further punishment in the Hereafter. They will have no helper to protect them from Allah’s judgment.

### Phrase by phrase

**“As for those who disbelieve”** refers to those who reject Allah and His messengers, including those who rejected ʿĪsā and those who reject Muhammad ﷺ. The verse follows the promise that Allah will judge the disputes between people. It now describes the outcome for those who persist in disbelief.

**“I will subject them to a severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter”** includes worldly defeat, being killed or taken captive, loss of wealth and authority, and the jizyah imposed on some communities. The Hereafter brings a more severe punishment in the Fire. The worldly punishment is not an expiation that cancels the punishment to come for those who remain in disbelief.

One explanation compares this to a judge sentencing a person to a year in prison and adding another year if the person misbehaves. The first punishment has already begun, and the additional punishment remains. In the same way, a person’s suffering in this life does not automatically erase the punishment of the Hereafter when that person persists in rejection.

This is contrasted with believers. Suffering in this life may forgive their sins and lighten or remove the punishment due in the Hereafter. The verse’s next statement that Allah does not love wrongdoers signals the difference between the believer who is tested and the disbeliever who persists in injustice.

**“and they will have no helpers”** means that no one can shield them from Allah’s punishment. A protector cannot undo His judgment, as another verse says that wrongdoers will have no protector against Allah (13:34). The worldly influence of a ruler, religious leader, or community will not avail them in the Hereafter. This warning to those who rejected al-Masīḥ stands beside the promise that believers will be granted succession and security when they worship Allah alone (24:55). What We make clear is that no community can rely on power to escape the final judgment. The verse is a remembrance of the consequences of disbelief.

### Does worldly suffering remove the punishment of the Hereafter?

Not necessarily. For a believer, a hardship may expiate sin and lessen the next life’s punishment. For those who persist in disbelief, the punishment of this world does not serve as payment that cancels the final judgment. The verse warns that rejecting the truth has consequences in both lives.

### In short

- Disbelief brings punishment in this world and the Hereafter.
- Worldly hardship does not automatically cancel the punishment of the Hereafter.
- No helper can protect a person from Allah’s final judgment.

<<<VERSE 3:57>>>
The verse contrasts that warning with the promise that “those who believe and do good, they will be rewarded in full” (3:57). Allah gives the believers the complete reward for their faith and righteous actions. Those who believe in al-Masīḥ as Allah’s messenger and follow His oneness are included among the believers. Their promise of security and succession is also described elsewhere (24:55), while wrongdoers have no protector against Allah (13:34). What We report about ʿĪsā shows that true belief joins faith with righteous action. This is a remembrance for those who consider their deeds. Allah does not love those who do wrong.

### Phrase by phrase

**“And as for those who believe”** refers to those who believe in Allah, His scriptures, and His messengers, including both ʿĪsā and Muhammad ﷺ. The followers of ʿĪsā who accepted the later messenger are included in the faithful community.

**“and do good”** means that belief is joined with righteous deeds. The explanations mention prayer, charity, fasting, and maintaining family ties as examples. The deeds are done sincerely, not as a claim without action.

**“they will be rewarded in full”** means that Allah will not reduce the reward due to them. One explanation says their reward includes victory and security in this world and Paradise and high ranks in the Hereafter. Another says that the followers of the Messiah who believed before Muhammad ﷺ were rewarded for their faith, and that true faith also required accepting the messenger whose coming ʿĪsā had announced.

A report says that when a cloud raised ʿĪsā, Maryam held on to him in tears. He told her that they would be reunited at the Resurrection. The account places this at the sanctuary in Jerusalem on Laylat al-Qadr, when ʿĪsā was thirty-three, and says Maryam lived six years after him. This report accompanies the promise of future reunion and reward.

Hadith reports describe ʿĪsā’s return near the Hour. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim, in the Two Sahihs, report that he will descend and judge according to the law of Muhammad ﷺ. A report in Muslim says he will remain for seven years, while Abū Dāwūd al-Tayālisī gives forty years. One explanation suggests that the longer period may count his time on earth both before and after being raised. The reports differ in duration, while affirming his return and his role among the believers.

**“And Allah does not like the wrongdoers”** means that He does not love those who disbelieve, associate partners with Him, or reject His messengers. The greatest wrong is to ascribe divinity to a created being or deny the truth Allah has sent. The promise of full reward belongs to those who believe and act righteously, not to those who persist in wrongdoing.

### In short

- Allah gives believers the full reward for faith and righteous deeds.
- Their deeds include worship and good treatment of others.
- Allah does not love those who reject His messengers or associate partners with Him.

<<<VERSE 3:58>>>
The account concludes: “We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺ as one of the signs” (3:58). The story of ʿĪsā and the promises concerning him are revealed to Muhammad ﷺ as a wise reminder. They are a sign of the truth of the revelation and a guide to belief and action.

### Phrase by phrase

**“We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺”** means Allah reveals the account to Muhammad ﷺ through Gabriel. The Prophet was not present at the events surrounding Maryam and ʿĪsā. He receives this knowledge by revelation, not by ordinary observation or study.

**“as one of the signs”** means the account itself is evidence of Allah’s revelation. It recounts the birth, mission, miracles, opposition, and raising of ʿĪsā al-Masīḥ. The detail and truth of the story are a sign for the Prophet and his community. What We reveal also points to the promise that believers will receive security and authority when they worship Allah alone (24:55), while those who reject the truth have no protector against Him (13:34).

**“and ˹as˺ a wise reminder”** describes the Qur’an as a reminder that contains guidance about belief, lawful and unlawful conduct, and the truth of earlier revelation. It is a remembrance that calls people back to Allah and to the lessons of the story. One explanation says it confirms what is in the Torah and the Gospel. Another says it confirms what is recorded in the Preserved Tablet, al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ. This remembrance is not only history. It teaches people how to understand ʿĪsā, what to believe about him, and how to avoid exaggeration or rejection.

### In short

- Allah reveals the story to Muhammad ﷺ through Gabriel.
- The account is a sign of revelation and a wise reminder.
- The Qur’an guides belief and conduct and confirms earlier revelation.
