<<<VERSE 3:102>>>
> “O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺” (3:102)

The verse calls believers to live with taqwa, mindful awareness of Allah, and to remain in submission throughout life so that they meet death as Muslims. It links personal faith with the unity discussed in the next verse.

### Phrase by phrase

**“O believers!”** addresses those who have accepted faith and calls them to live up to that commitment. Belief is not only a claim. It shapes what a person chooses, avoids, remembers, and gives thanks for.

**“Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves”** calls for taqwa according to a person’s ability and responsibility. Ibn Masʿud explains this as obeying Allah rather than defying Him, remembering Him rather than forgetting Him, and showing gratitude rather than ingratitude. Taqwa is not simply fear. It is a steady awareness that Allah sees one’s conduct and that one will answer to Him.

Taqwa has degrees. At its foundation, a believer avoids disbelief and shirk. It grows through leaving what Allah has forbidden, fulfilling obligations, and avoiding conduct that harms others. The highest forms of taqwa call a person to watch over the heart and keep it occupied with remembrance of Allah and the desire to please Him. No one should imagine that they have mastered taqwa. The command encourages sincere effort, and another instruction in the Qur’an teaches believers to be mindful of Allah as much as they can.

Ibn Masʿud’s explanation gives practical measures: obey, remember, and give thanks. Anas also emphasizes guarding the tongue. A person’s speech can undo goodwill, spread suspicion, or injure another believer. Taqwa therefore reaches beyond private worship into ordinary dealings. The verse is not a demand for impossible perfection. It calls for a life directed toward obedience and a readiness to correct oneself after a lapse.

**“and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission”** does not command people to control the moment of death, which is beyond them. It tells them to remain in Islam throughout life, since the state in which a person lives is the state they may meet death upon. The believer should not postpone repentance or obedience on the assumption that there will be time later. Each day is an opportunity to return to Allah and renew one’s commitment.

Reports reinforce this concern without closing the door to hope. Jabir relates that a few nights before the Prophet ﷺ died, he urged people not to meet death except while trusting Allah sincerely. A report from Abu Hurayrah conveys Allah’s words that He is as His servant expects of Him. These teachings bring together hope in Allah’s mercy and seriousness about living faithfully. A Muslim asks Allah for a good end while continuing to act with care.

A report from Ibn ʿAbbas describes the Prophet ﷺ reciting this verse to people near the Sacred House. Other reports of its recitation stress that the command is not a private virtue detached from community life. The next verse calls believers to hold together and not divide. Taqwa gives unity its moral foundation, because people who are accountable to Allah cannot treat one another unjustly in the name of a group.

### What does mindful living require?

The verse calls for a settled direction, not an anxious attempt to predict the exact circumstances of death. A believer keeps faith active by obeying Allah, remembering Him, and being grateful. When a mistake occurs, the response is to repent and repair what can be repaired, not to despair or claim that the mistake no longer matters.

The account of Aws and Khazraj also illustrates the need for mindfulness. Before Islam, tribal rivalries had led them to war. The Qur’an’s call to fear Allah addresses the community before it celebrates their unity in the next verse. Taqwa restrains anger, boasting, and the desire to revive old harms. It asks each person to consider whether their actions are pleasing to Allah or merely satisfying pride.

A report from Abdullah bin Mas'ud gives another formulation: obey Allah, remember Him, and show gratitude. Related explanations in Al-Bahr al-Muhit and reports from the early community describe the same pattern as the heart of taqwa. When He calls believers to taqwa, He is calling them to a whole way of life, not a feeling that lasts only during worship. The Qur’an also describes the consequences of rejecting Allah’s covenant (3:112), warns against the Fire (3:131), and begins its counsel to families with mindfulness of their Lord (4:1). These reminders connect individual conduct with responsibility toward other people.

The command does not ask anyone to claim divine perfection. Some say that “as He deserves” means an absolute level no human can reach, while others explain it as making the fullest effort a person can sustain. The latter understanding is supported by the instruction to be mindful of Allah as much as one is able. A believer should not use human limits as an excuse for carelessness, but neither should they despair after a failure. They repent, repair harm, and continue along the path.

The reports about reciting this verse give a sense of its seriousness. Ahmad relates from Mujahid that Ibn Abbas was near the Kaʿbah when the Prophet ﷺ recited the command and warned about the danger of the Fire. Versions of the report were recorded by At-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah, Ibn Hibban, and Al-Hakim. The point is not to make believers live in terror, but to wake them from heedlessness. The Two Sahihs preserve Abu Hurayrah’s report that Allah says He is as His servant expects of Him. Hope and accountability belong together.

Another teaching reported by Abu Hurayrah warns against divisive talk that begins with “So-and-so said” and fuels suspicion, needless argument, or waste. This does not prohibit seeking information or correcting falsehood. It cautions people against repeating claims without responsibility. Taqwa asks a person to verify what they hear, guard their tongue, and consider how speech affects trust in the community.

A reflective account tells of Ahmad ibn Khiẓrūya seeing Abū Yazīd in a dream, where the distinction was made between seeking a gift from Allah and seeking Allah Himself. This story is not a legal definition of taqwa. It expresses an inward aspiration: worship should not be reduced to a transaction for reward, but should grow from love, gratitude, and recognition of Allah’s oneness. It is a call to sincerity, not a dismissal of the hope for Paradise or fear of punishment.

### In short

- Taqwa is obedience, remembrance, gratitude, and accountability to Allah.
- Believers should remain in faith throughout life and ask Allah for a faithful end.
- Mindfulness of Allah restrains conduct that could harm personal faith or communal unity.

<<<VERSE 3:103>>>
> “And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided. Remember Allah’s favour upon you when you were enemies, then He united your hearts, so you—by His grace—became brothers. And you were at the brink of a fiery pit and He saved you from it. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so that you may be ˹rightly˺ guided” (3:103)

The verse commands believers to cling together to Allah’s guidance, avoid destructive division, and remember how He changed former enemies into brothers. Their unity is a gift from Allah and a rescue from the consequences of disbelief and hostility. Together with the preceding command to practice taqwa as fully as one can (64:16), it presents unity as a daily responsibility rather than an occasional public slogan.

### Phrase by phrase

**“hold firmly to the rope of Allah”** means to remain attached to Allah’s covenant and guidance. The rope is explained as the Qur’an, the religion Allah revealed, and the bond that connects believers to their Lord. A rope also brings to mind people gripping a strong support so they do not fall. The image combines loyalty to Allah with the help believers give one another when they hold to the same guidance.

The command is not merely to admire revelation or quote it in disputes. Believers hold fast by learning it, acting upon it, and returning to it when they disagree. The Prophet’s teaching explains how the Qur’an is practiced. A report describes the Book of Allah as a rope extending from heaven to earth, a connection between Allah and His servants. Abdullah bin Mas'ud relates the saying that the Book of Allah is a rope extending from heaven to earth, and a report from Zayd ibn Arqam similarly identifies the rope with the Qur’an. The Qur’an also calls the believers to rely on Allah and on the bond of faith rather than on race, tribe, language, wealth, or political power as the deepest source of belonging. The People of the Torah and Gospel also received revelation, but this verse addresses the Muslim ummah and identifies the bond that should guide its shared life.

**“and do not be divided”** forbids the kind of separation that breaks the community into rival camps and makes people abandon revelation for group loyalty. The verse does not require every believer to hold the same opinion on every subsidiary question. Scholars may reach different conclusions while remaining committed to the Qur’an, the Prophet’s teaching, and good faith. Such difference is not the same as hostility, insult, or treating a secondary issue as the whole religion.

A hadith reported from Abu Hurayrah says that Allah loves for the community to worship Him alone, hold together to His rope, and give sincere counsel to those entrusted with authority. It warns against needless argument, excessive questioning, and wasting resources. Unity therefore requires both sound belief and responsible conduct. It is not achieved by suppressing every discussion, nor by following a leader in wrongdoing. It is built by shared commitment to Allah and by advice offered with sincerity. Muslim records this report from Abu Hurayrah. It describes holding together, worshipping Allah alone, and advising those with responsibility as things Allah loves. Its warning about “So-and-so said” cautions against rumor and factional gossip, not against principled counsel. Anas, Ibn Masʿud, and other early Muslims likewise connect taqwa with guarding one’s speech and keeping remembrance alive.

The Qur’an also speaks of a covenant that preserves people from disgrace (3:112). That bond is not a slogan. It is a commitment to Allah and a just relationship with people. Another verse calls believers to strive for Allah and hold fast to Him (22:78), while those who repent, reform, and hold firmly to Allah are promised a great reward (4:146). These passages show that unity is rooted in obedience and mutual responsibility, not simply in being placed under the same name.

The Qur’an also warns against following separate routes that lead people away from Allah’s path (6:153), and against turning religion into factions (6:159). These warnings concern division that abandons guidance and damages the community. They do not erase the legitimate work of understanding difficult questions. A healthy community can preserve unity of purpose while allowing careful discussion, humility, and difference in subsidiary matters.

### Remembering the gift of brotherhood

**“Remember Allah’s favour upon you when you were enemies”** calls believers to recall their history honestly. The Aws and Khazraj in Madinah had fought long wars and carried resentment into successive generations. Their rivalry could have continued to weaken them. The Prophet’s arrival and the acceptance of Islam changed the basis of their relationship.

**“then He united your hearts, so you—by His grace—became brothers”** makes clear that their reconciliation was Allah’s favor. The people themselves had to respond, but they could not create the depth of affection Allah placed between them simply by spending their wealth. The Qur’an says that Allah supported the Prophet through the believers and joined their hearts (8:62-63). Their brotherhood was not based on pretending that their past had never happened. It came from turning together toward Allah, leaving vengeance behind, and helping one another in righteousness.

This unity was tested after the conquest of Makkah at Hunayn. When the Prophet ﷺ distributed the spoils according to Allah’s guidance, some of the Anṣār felt that others had received more. The Prophet addressed them by recalling their earlier condition: they had been divided, poor, and without guidance, and Allah had brought them together through him. The Anṣār recognized that the greatest favor was not a share of worldly wealth but the guidance and fellowship Allah had given them. The incident shows that unity must be renewed when disappointment or competition threatens it. Accounts of the speech in the Two Sahihs preserve the Prophet’s reminder to the Anṣār, and Muslim records Abu Hurayrah’s report about holding to the rope and advising those in authority. One must not let a careless “So-and-so said” become a rumor that destroys the trust these teachings seek to protect.

The favor of brotherhood is not a reason to hide injustice or silence people who have been harmed. Rather, it gives the community a reason to settle disputes fairly, protect one another’s rights, and resist attempts to inflame old grievances. Remembering Allah’s favor makes people less willing to trade lasting solidarity for temporary pride.

### How does the rope create unity?

A community needs a common bond. Race, tribe, language, and place can shape identity, but none is a complete foundation for the whole ummah. People may share these features and still fight. Allah’s guidance offers a common reference that people from different backgrounds can choose to follow. In this sense, the Qur’an brings believers together without requiring them to erase their languages, histories, or lawful customs.

The rope connects people vertically to Allah and horizontally to one another. If a group holds firmly to the Qur’an but treats other believers with contempt, it has not carried out the full command. If people call for unity while setting aside Allah’s guidance, their agreement may last only while their interests coincide. The verse joins the two duties: hold fast to Allah’s bond together, and do not divide.

Muslim history includes differences of interpretation among the Companions, later scholars, and jurists. The existence of a considered difference does not by itself break the bond. What damages unity is turning interpretation into factional hatred, accusing others carelessly, or allowing a subsidiary matter to displace the foundations of faith. Believers should explain their views with evidence, accept correction, and leave room for others where the matter allows it.

The verse’s call is universal across the ummah. It is not a demand for a single political organization or for obedience to every person who claims leadership. It asks believers to unite upon Allah’s revealed guidance, maintain good counsel, and refrain from the kinds of division that destroy trust and cooperation. The bond is Allah’s, so no faction may claim exclusive ownership of it.

### Unity, accountability, and mercy

Unity grows from taqwa. Hasan al-Basri is among those who explain mindful fear of Allah as obeying Him, remembering Him, and showing gratitude. This personal discipline makes it harder to use religious language as a mask for envy, revenge, or a struggle for status. A community made up of muttaqin is not a community without disagreement. It is one in which people remember that Allah will question them about how they handled disagreement.

The Qur’an connects holding to Allah with striving in His cause (22:78), and says that those who repent, reform, and hold firmly to Him will receive a great reward (4:146). It also describes Allah creating affection for those who believe and do good deeds (19:96). These teachings place love and reconciliation within a moral framework. People should not demand unity by forcing the harmed to remain silent. They should seek justice, repair relationships, and avoid turning disagreements into lasting hostility.

The warning against division in 6:159 concerns people who split their religion into hostile sects and lose sight of the message. It does not erase the differences that arise when responsible scholars interpret details. The Qur’an itself distinguishes the central bond of revelation from disputes that should be handled with knowledge. Some differences of legal judgment are part of a living community. They become blameworthy when people use them to insult, exclude, or break relations without cause.

An inward reflection describes the Prophet ﷺ as the noble guide, ʿazīz, and sees Allah’s protection, ʿiṣma, as a gift that helps the community remain united. It calls those especially devoted, the khāṣṣ, to hold to the Book and the Prophet’s teaching with sincerity. These terms do not establish a privileged faction. They remind every believer that steadfastness is received from Allah, not achieved through pride in one’s own reasoning.

The verse’s command to hold together also echoes the covenant language of 3:112. Believers are responsible both to Allah and to the people with whom they live. The rope is therefore not an excuse to withdraw from society. It calls the ummah to keep its promise to Allah, deal justly with neighbors and authorities, and preserve trust among its members. The Qur’an offers a shared center that can cross the lines of tribe, language, and place, including the old rivalries associated with Quraysh and other Arab groups.

### “the brink of a fiery pit”

**“And you were at the brink of a fiery pit and He saved you from it”** recalls both the danger of disbelief and the destructive hatred that had brought the tribes close to renewed violence. The pit is an image of an imminent fall. Their old conflicts threatened their lives in this world, while disbelief carried a consequence in the Hereafter. Allah saved them by guiding them to faith and giving them a new relationship as brothers.

The verse does not say that believers are now incapable of falling into division. It asks them to remember how close the danger was and how they were rescued. Forgetting the gift can lead people to recreate the very hostility from which they were saved. Remembering it renews gratitude and encourages them to make peace before a dispute grows into violence.

### “so that you may be guided”

**“This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you”** points to the Qur’an’s instruction about belief, unity, and the consequences of division. The revelation explains the path and warns against what turns people away from it. Understanding these signs should lead to action: holding firmly to Allah’s guidance, repairing relationships, and choosing cooperation in good.

**“so that you may be ˹rightly˺ guided”** describes the purpose of this teaching. Guidance is not only knowing which direction is correct. It includes taking that direction together, with humility and care for the community. The verse makes taqwa the foundation of unity and Allah’s revelation the bond that sustains it.

### In short

- The rope of Allah is His guidance, especially the Qur’an and the Prophet’s teaching.
- Believers must unite on that guidance while allowing responsible discussion of subsidiary differences.
- Allah’s grace transformed the Aws and Khazraj from enemies into brothers.
- Remembering this rescue protects the community from renewed hostility and division.
