<<<VERSE 2:58>>>
Verse 58 recalls the moment the Israelites were finally given a city to live in, and the manner in which they were told to take possession of it. "Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please", Allah said, and then set the terms of entry: go in through the gate with humility, saying "Absolve us", and in return "We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers". The favour is a home and plenty. The condition is humility and a word of repentance. The next verse tells how both were thrown away.

### Phrase by phrase

**"when We said, 'Enter this city'"**. Scholars differ about which city this was and when the command came. The view most of them adopt is that it was the holy land, which Mūsā (Moses) had commanded his people to enter with the words, "Enter the Holy Land which Allah has destined for you" (5:21). As-Suddī, ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, Qatādah, Abū Muslim al-Aṣfahānī and others identified it with Bayt al-Maqdis (Jerusalem), and one early commentary specifies its gate. Others, including Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān ibn Zayd, said it was Jericho (Arīḥā). The background is the Israelites' refusal of jihād when they first came out of Egypt with Mūsā. They were ordered to fight the disbelieving Amalekites who held the holy land, but they would not, pleading weakness and exhaustion, and Allah punished them with the wandering recorded in Sūrat al-Māʾidah. When the forty years were over, Allah let them conquer the holy land in the company of Yūshaʿ (Joshua) ibn Nūn, on the eve of a Friday, and the sun was held back from setting for a little while until the victory was complete. It was then, having come out of the wilderness, that they received the command of this verse, delivered to them through Yūshaʿ, who was their prophet at the time.

A second view, held by Shāh ʿAbd al-Qādir, ties the verse to a different moment. When the Israelites tired of eating the same manna and quails every day and asked for the ordinary food they were used to (2:61), they were told to go to a certain city where they could have what they wanted, and this verse lays down the etiquette of action and speech for entering it. The two views place the event at different points in the story, and scholars note that this should not trouble the reader. The Qur'an does not tell stories for their own sake, as entertainment does. Its purpose is to draw lessons and illustrate spiritual principles, and since different episodes serve different lessons, it arranges them to suit the meaning it intends in a given passage rather than the calendar. This is a normal literary method. In any writing or speech it is the intention that governs the ordering of the material.

**"and eat freely from wherever you please"** means eat plentifully, with pleasure and without restriction, from the good things of the city wherever you find them.

**"enter the gate with humility"** translates sujjadan, literally "prostrating". The entry was to be an act of thanks to Allah for giving them victory, for restoring them to their land, and for rescuing them from being lost. Al-ʿAwfī reported Ibn ʿAbbās explaining sujjadan as "bowing", and Ibn Jarīr reported from him that they were to pass through a small door while bowing, a report al-Ḥākim also narrated and to which Ibn Abī Ḥātim added that they went through it backwards. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī held that they were told to prostrate on their faces as they entered, but ar-Rāzī rejected this, and others took sujūd here to mean submissiveness, since one cannot actually walk through a gate while prostrating. As for the gate, Ibn ʿAbbās, in Khaṣīf's report from ʿIkrimah, said it faced the qiblah, and ar-Rāzī also mentions the view that it was a door in the direction of the qiblah. Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, as-Suddī, Qatādah and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk said it was the gate of Ḥiṭṭah in Īliyāʾ, that is, Jerusalem. Ibn ʿAbbās also said the Israelites went in sideways, and ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd said that, told to enter bowing, they entered with their heads raised in defiance. Those details belong to the next verse.

**"saying, 'Absolve us'"**. The word is ḥiṭṭah. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as asking Allah for forgiveness, and al-Ḥasan and Qatādah as "relieve us of our errors". One commentary paraphrases it as "our request is to be exonerated from our transgressions", and another records a view that it meant saying there is no god but Allah.

**"We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers"**. This is the reward promised for obedience: if you do as We command, We will forgive your sins and multiply your good deeds. The verb has been read as naghfir, "We will forgive", and in two passive forms, yughfar and tughfar, "they will be forgiven". On the second view of the verse's setting, the sins to be forgiven include the Israelites' rejection of the manna and quails and their demand for ordinary food, an insolent demand that Allah nonetheless promised to overlook if they obeyed the new command. Either way, the pardon was offered to everyone willing to obey, while an extra reward was promised to those who gave themselves sincerely to good deeds.

### What the command amounted to

Taken together, the Children of Israel were being told, at the moment of victory, to submit to Allah in word and deed, to confess their sins and ask forgiveness for them, to thank Allah for His blessings and to hasten to the deeds He loves. The same pattern was set for the Prophet ﷺ in the sūrah revealed at the conquest of Makkah, which tells him, when Allah's help and the conquest come and people enter the religion in crowds, to glorify his Lord's praise and seek His forgiveness. A believer meets triumph with humility. The Israelites had been given a great city, and they were told to enter it not with swagger but in humility and repentance. Instead, as the sequel shows, they gave themselves over to celebrating their victory.

### The good-doers

The word rendered "good-doers" is muḥsinūn, and "sincere" or "wholehearted" is a weak translation of it. It comes from iḥsān, which means doing a thing beautifully, in the way that is proper to it. Beyond that lexical sense, iḥsān has a technical meaning fixed by a famous hadith: to worship Allah as though you see Him, knowing that if you do not see Him, He sees you.

### The arrows of the unseen

The devotional reading sums up the Israelites' conduct. They kept on neglecting what they were commanded. They were entrusted with a form of words to preserve and they altered it. They were commanded a posture of prostration on entering and they refused it. In doing so they laid themselves open to the arrows of the unseen, and when those arrows struck they could not bear the wound. Punishments they had not expected came upon them, and they could not stand up under the shock.

### In short

- The city was the holy land, Jerusalem or Jericho, conquered under Yūshaʿ after the forty years, or, on another view, a city they were sent to when they tired of manna and quails (2:61).
- They were told to enter the gate bowing in gratitude and to say ḥiṭṭah, "absolve us", with forgiveness promised to all who obeyed and more for the muḥsinūn.
- Victory is to be met with submission, confession and thanks, as the Prophet ﷺ was told at the conquest of Makkah.
- Iḥsān means doing a thing beautifully, and in religion it means worshipping Allah as if you see Him.

<<<VERSE 2:59>>>
Verse 59 records how the simple instruction of the previous verse was mocked. "But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say", it reports, "So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness". A gesture of humility and a single word of repentance were asked of them. They turned both into a joke, and a plague followed.

### Phrase by phrase

**"But the wrongdoers"**. The previous verse addressed the Israelites in the second person. Here the Qur'an switches to the third person and names them openly as "those who did wrong", and scholars observe that the change of person is meant to stress the depravity of what they did. They wronged themselves above all.

**"changed the words they were commanded to say"**. The Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī records from Abū Hurayrah the Prophet's ﷺ account: the Israelites were ordered to go in through the gate bowing and to say ḥiṭṭah, but they went in on their backsides and twisted the word, saying ḥabbah fī shaʿrah, "a seed in a hair". An-Nasāʾī recorded part of this from Abū Hurayrah with a chain to the Prophet ﷺ explaining the word ḥiṭṭah: "they deviated and said ḥabbah". ʿAbd ar-Razzāq recorded the like, and his route was also collected by al-Bukhārī, while Muslim and at-Tirmidhī gave similar versions, at-Tirmidhī grading it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. Other reports have them saying ḥinṭah, "wheat", or ḥinṭah fī shaʿīrah, "a grain of wheat in barley". The sum of what scholars say is this: the Israelites were commanded to submit to Allah in tongue and in deed, and they corrupted both. Told to enter bowing, they slid in on their rear ends with their heads held up. Told to say a word meaning "relieve us of our errors and sins", they mocked it with a word for grain. By doing so they mocked Allah's sacred law itself, and this is rebellion and disobedience of the worst kind. That is why Allah released His anger and His punishment upon them, all on account of their sin and their defiance of His commands.

**"So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them"**. The word is rijz. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported Ibn ʿAbbās saying that wherever rijz occurs in Allah's Book it means punishment, and Mujāhid, Abū Mālik, as-Suddī, al-Ḥasan and Qatādah explained it as torment. The punishment in this case was the plague. According to al-Qurṭubī it wiped out seventy thousand men, and another account says that in a very short time just under seventy thousand of them were dead. The link between rijz and plague rests on hadith. Ibn Abī Ḥātim narrated from Saʿd ibn Mālik, Usāmah ibn Zayd and Khuzaymah ibn Thābit that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ described the plague as a rijz, the very punishment Allah had inflicted on peoples before you, and an-Nasāʾī recorded it in the same way. Ibn Jarīr recorded from Usāmah ibn Zayd that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ called this disease a rijz with which some nations before you were punished. The basis of both reports is found in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, in the hadith that says: if you hear of the plague in a land, do not enter it. Another hadith, often recalled alongside these, says that plague is a punishment for the disobedient and a mercy for the obedient.

**"for their rebelliousness"** means because they trespassed over the limits of the law and strayed from obedience, changing the command they had been ordered to carry out.

### Can the words of a command be changed?

The Israelites were punished for replacing a word Allah had ordained with a phrase of their own, and distorting the meaning in the process. Jurists draw a legal lesson from this. By the consensus of the fuqahāʾ, altering the words of the Qur'an, of a hadith, or of a divine command in a way that invents or distorts the meaning is forbidden. That much is obvious.

The harder question is whether words may be changed when the meaning stays the same. Al-Qurṭubī answered that in certain kinds of text the words are as much intended as the meanings, and are equally necessary to convey the idea, so that in those cases the words may not be changed at all. The adhān is an example: no words may be used for the call to prayer except those laid down in the hadith. The same holds for everything recited in the ṣalāh, such as the opening Subḥānaka Allāhumma, at-Taḥiyyāt and the Qunūt, and the glorifications pronounced in rukūʿ and sajdah. All of these must be said in the exact words that have come down in the hadith, and substituting other words is not allowed even when the meaning is unaffected. This rule covers every word of the Qur'an. All the rulings about reciting the Qur'an attach only to the words Allah revealed to the Prophet ﷺ. The hadith promises that each letter recited earns the reward of ten good deeds, but a person who reads even a very accurate translation, or an Arabic text in which the original words have been swapped for others without harming the sense, has not, in the eyes of the Sharīʿah, recited the Qur'an, and earns none of that reward. The Qur'an is not its meanings alone. It is the name of the meanings as they live in the words Allah revealed to the Prophet ﷺ, and the two cannot be pulled apart. The present verse shows that Allah had commanded the Israelites to say this particular word, ḥiṭṭah, when offering their repentance, so that changing the appointed word was itself a sin, before they went further and distorted the meaning as well, drawing the punishment down on themselves.

In other kinds of speech, where the meaning is what is intended rather than the words, the masters of hadith and the jurists generally hold that the words may be changed provided the meaning is kept whole. Al-Qurṭubī cited Imām Abū Ḥanīfah, Imām Mālik and Imām Shāfiʿī as permitting a hadith to be reported by its meaning, on condition that the reporter has a perfect command of Arabic and knows the situation to which the hadith relates, so that he neither misreads the text nor distorts its sense. A stricter group of hadith scholars allowed no change whatever and insisted that a hadith be passed on in the very words in which it was received. Muḥammad ibn Sīrīn and Qāsim ibn Muḥammad were of this view, and some went so far as to say that if a reporter made a slip of language, the one who heard it from him must repeat it slip and all, merely noting what the correct word was likely to be. They cite a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ taught a man a prayer to say before sleeping, which included the words "I believe in Your Book which You sent down and in Your Prophet whom You sent". The prescribed word was nabiyy, Prophet, and when the man substituted rasūl, Messenger, the Prophet ﷺ told him not to change it but to say nabiyy. They also cite his words: may Allah make the man flourish who heard what I said and conveyed it just as he heard it.

Most jurists and hadith scholars hold a middle position. It is better to report a hadith in the exact words one heard, and never to alter them deliberately, but if the exact words cannot be recalled it is permissible to convey the meaning in one's own words. The hadith about conveying "just as he heard it" can mean conveying the meaning exactly and without alteration, which a change of wording does not necessarily violate. Al-Qurṭubī pointed out that this very hadith has reached us in different wordings in its different versions, which is itself proof that changing the words is allowed when necessary. As for the nabiyy and rasūl incident, two explanations are offered. The word nabiyy carries a loftier sense than rasūl, since rasūl can be used of any messenger at all, whereas nabiyy is reserved for those Allah has chosen to address directly through revelation (waḥy), who stand above all other men. The other explanation is that in prayers the words appointed by Allah or by His Prophet ﷺ carry a power that no other words have. This is why those who compose protective formulas, taʿwīdh or ʿawdhah, and those who recommend words of supplication, are so careful to keep to the authentically reported wording without the slightest change. Prayers considered especially effective therefore belong to the first category of speech, in which both the words and the meanings are intended and both must be guarded.

### Remorse too late

The devotional reading paints the aftermath. The Israelites could not ward off the opening of heaven above them by any stratagem, nor loosen the cords of affliction beneath them by relying on their usual ways. When the fangs of pain bit into them they gnashed the teeth of remorse, and what good was that once the situation was past help?

### In short

- Told to enter bowing and to say ḥiṭṭah, "absolve us", the Israelites crawled in on their backsides and said "a seed in a hair" or "wheat", mocking the command in deed and word.
- The rijz sent on them was the plague, which killed about seventy thousand. Hadiths in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs and elsewhere call the plague a rijz with which earlier nations were punished.
- Jurists agree that altering the words of the Qur'an, hadith or a divine command so as to distort the meaning is forbidden. Where the words themselves are intended, as in the adhān, the recitations of the prayer and the Qur'an, they may not be changed even if the meaning survives.
- Reporting a hadith by its meaning is permitted by most scholars for a reporter who knows Arabic perfectly, though some early scholars allowed no change at all.

<<<VERSE 2:60>>>
Verse 60 returns to the wilderness and to another favour, water from stone. "And remember when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, 'Strike the rock with your staff.' Then twelve springs gushed out", and each tribe knew which spring was its own. The verse ends with the same combination as before, a gift and a warning: "Eat and drink of Allah's provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land".

### Phrase by phrase

**"when Moses prayed for water for his people"**. This too belongs to the years of wandering. Parched with thirst in the wilderness, the Israelites asked Mūsā (Moses) to pray to Allah for water, and he did. The favour Allah asks them to remember is that He answered the supplication of their prophet when he asked Him to provide them with water.

**"We said, 'Strike the rock with your staff'"**. Allah told him to strike a particular rock with the staff he carried. The reports describe it as a rock Allah had given him, and one commentary identifies it as the rock that had once run off with his robe, a light, cube-shaped stone of marble about the size of a man's head. Ibn ʿAbbās described it as a square stone that Mūsā was commanded to strike. Another report pictures it with twelve protrusions resembling nipples, each of which released a spring when struck.

**"Then twelve springs gushed out, and each tribe knew its drinking place"**. Twelve springs burst from the stone, three on each side according to Ibn ʿAbbās, and each of the twelve tribes was assigned its own. The tribes were twelve because Yaʿqūb (Jacob) had twelve sons, each of whom had a large family that came to be reckoned a tribe with its own organisation and its own head. Each tribe knew which spring was its drinking place and did not share it with the others, so that there would be no quarrelling over water. Ibn ʿAbbās added that wherever they moved, they found the same bounty in the same manner as in the place they had left, so they never needed to travel in search of water. This report is part of the long hadith about the trials that an-Nasāʾī, Ibn Jarīr and Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded.

**"Eat and drink of Allah's provisions"**. The food is the manna and the quails, and the water is what came out of the rock. You eat and drink what I provided, Allah is saying, without any toil or hardship on your part, so worship the One who did this for you.

**"and do not go about spreading corruption in the land"** means do not repay the favour with acts of disobedience that cause favours to be taken away. Corruption here means disobeying Allah and overstepping His commands, or acting against what Mūsā told them. Grammatically, mufsidīn, "spreading corruption", is a circumstantial phrase that reinforces the verb lā taʿthaw, which comes from ʿathiya, to act corruptly.

### The same story in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf

The story appears again in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, which was revealed in Makkah, while this sūrah was revealed in al-Madīnah, and scholars note two differences in the telling. In al-Aʿrāf, Allah speaks about the Children of Israel in the third person, telling the Prophet ﷺ what He did for them, whereas here, in a sūrah addressed to the Jews of al-Madīnah, He speaks to them directly. And in al-Aʿrāf the verb says that the water trickled forth (7:160), describing the first moment when it began to flow, while here it says that it gushed out, describing what came next, when the water burst out in full force. Allah knows best.

### On denying miracles

Al-Qāḍī al-Bayḍāwī observed that denying miracles is a serious error, and even an error of logic. Allah has given one kind of stone the unusual property of drawing iron to itself. There is nothing logically or rationally impossible, then, in His giving another stone the property of absorbing water from the earth and releasing it again. That explanation is offered for people who look at things superficially. In truth there is nothing impossible in Allah producing the water inside the stone itself, and those who call it impossible do not understand what the word "impossible" technically means. The devotional reading adds a different observation. The One who can draw water out of solid rock can also quench thirst without any water. Yet He chose to make the effect of the miracle appear through Mūsā and to tie him to the place where help was sought, so that Mūsā would exert his own self (nafs) in going to the stone and bear the responsibility of striking it with his staff. In that way a measure of human effort was woven into the carrying out of the decree when he asked for water for his people.

### Praying for rain

The verse answers a practical question: in time of drought, must formal prayers be offered to ask Allah for rain? Mūsā simply prayed, and Allah provided miraculously, so the essential thing in asking for rain is the supplication itself. In the Sharīʿah of Mūsā a plain prayer sufficed, and Imām Abū Ḥanīfah held that this principle holds in the Islamic Sharīʿah as well. The Prophet ﷺ acted differently on different occasions. An authentic hadith reports that he once went out of the city to the open ground used for the ʿĪd prayers, offered formal prayers, delivered a khuṭbah and then prayed for rain. According to a hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from the Companion Anas, on another occasion he prayed for rain while delivering the Friday khuṭbah, and Allah sent rain. Whatever form the prayer takes, scholars agree that it will not be effective unless it is joined to repentance for one's sins, an admission of one's helplessness, sincere humility and an affirmation of one's servitude to Allah. Someone who persists in sin and transgression has no right to expect his prayer to be answered. If Allah in His mercy grants it anyway, that is His will, and He is able to do all things.

### Twelve drinking places

The devotional reading finds a further lesson in the arrangement of the springs. The Real wanted each people to follow its own way (sunnah), to keep within its own boundary (ḥadd) and not to dispute with its neighbours, so He gave each tribe a marker by which it knew its own drinking place and did not trespass on the others'. Once He had met their needs, He asked them to be grateful, to keep His command and to stop choosing sin, and so He said, "do not go about spreading corruption in the land". The drinking places differ, and each comes to its own. One is sweet and fresh, another salty and bitter, one clear and pure, another dried up to a few drops. Every people has a water carrier who leads it and a scout who drives it on. So it is that the lower selves arrive at the wells of craving and desire, the hearts at the wells of piety and obedient works, the spirits at the wells where unveiling and witnessing are found, and the innermost selves at the wells of the realities. These last are snatched away from created being and its outward markings, then from sensory awareness and human attributes, and finally pass away into the reality of finding and of the Essence.

### In short

- In answer to Mūsā's prayer, Allah had him strike a rock and twelve springs flowed, one for each of the tribes descended from Yaʿqūb's twelve sons, so that no tribe quarrelled with another over water.
- The report of Ibn ʿAbbās in the hadith of the trials describes a square stone with three springs on each side that travelled with them.
- Al-Aʿrāf tells the same story in the third person and in Makkah, and its verb describes the first trickle, while this verse describes the full flow (7:160).
- Al-Bayḍāwī argued that denying such miracles is irrational, since the One who made the magnet can make a rock give water.
- Asking for rain requires, above all, supplication joined to repentance and humility. Abū Ḥanīfah held a plain prayer sufficient, while the Prophet ﷺ sometimes prayed formally with a khuṭbah and once prayed from the Friday pulpit.
