<<<VERSE 2:258>>>
Royal power becomes an excuse for rebellion in the encounter described here: "Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship?" The king mistakes a gift for proof of his own divinity. Ibrāhīm (Abraham), Al-Khalil, answers by directing attention to life, death and the sun's movement. The king's authority over subjects does not make him master of creation.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship?"** The question asks the Prophet ﷺ to consider the event through knowledge and the heart, not personal observation of ancient history. The king is identified in the reports as Nimrod, also written Namrud, ruler of Babylon. Long rule and political power fed his arrogance where gratitude should have grown.

**"˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.”"** Allah creates life where none existed and ends it. The existence and passing away of creatures point towards the Creator. Another explanation includes His bringing life at resurrection and ending earthly life. The argument concerns real creative power, not merely deciding whether a prisoner is executed.

**"He argued, “I too have the power to give life and cause death.”"** One account says the king brought two prisoners, killed one and spared the other. Qatadah, Muhammad bin Ishaq and As-Suddi explain his words this way. Another explanation takes the claim more directly as an arrogant pretence to be the creator himself. These accounts differ in how they understand his assertion, but both expose its falsehood.

**"Abraham challenged ˹him˺, “Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west.”"** The One who truly governs existence also governs the heavenly bodies. The king is challenged to demonstrate comparable authority over a phenomenon he cannot control through an order to his servants.

**"And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people"** The king could not answer. His claim had no valid proof. The refusal of guidance here is connected with his injustice and rebellion, not with a weakness in the evidence. His false argument collapses and exposes him to Allah's judgment.

### What is said about Nimrod's claim?

Mujahid identifies Nimrod through a lineage passing from Canaan through Kush, Sam and Noah. Another reported lineage passes through Falikh, 'Abir, Shalikh and Arfakhshand before Sam and Noah. These are alternative genealogies, not one line assembled from all the names.

Mujahid also gives an account of four rulers extending power east and west. The believing pair are Sulayman bin Dawud and Dhul-Qarnayn, while the disbelieving pair are Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar. The report carries the reservation that Allah knows best.

Nimrod claimed that no deity besides himself existed. The discussion compares his pretence with Fir'awn (Pharaoh) in (28:38), where he says that he knows no god for his people besides himself. The royal office was acknowledged as a worldly position, but its holder's claim to divine authority was refused.

A historical explanation describes Nimrod as claiming to embody the sun-god. The people revered the sun as chief among their gods, and the king used that belief to support his sovereignty. Ibrāhīm's message was oneness, the worship of Allah alone, not a campaign initially concerned with administering the kingdom. Yet its denial of the sun's independent divinity undermined the king's political justification. That is given as a reason for his hostility.

As-Suddi places the debate after Ibrāhīm had been thrown into the fire, saying he had not met the king before then. That is a reported placement of the encounter, not a chronology supplied by the verse itself.

### Why move from life and death to the sun?

One explanation says the king failed to understand the first argument. Pardoning someone already alive is not creating life, and arranging an execution is not having unrestricted power to remove life from every creature. The second example made the distinction harder to obscure.

Another explanation rejects the idea that Ibrāhīm abandoned a weaker argument. It says that the solar challenge directly refuted the king's claim to be creator: anyone possessing the asserted power over life and death would also govern existence and its movements. The two proofs therefore answered two sides of the same pretence.

A further reading stresses compassionate communication. Ibrāhīm could have prolonged the dispute over the misuse of the words life and death, but he sought to convey truth rather than win a heated verbal contest. He chose an example the listener could not readily evade. The king was an addressee who needed guidance, not merely a rival to be humiliated.

The inward explanation similarly says the first proof was sound. The problem lay in the listener's deficient understanding. Continuing indefinitely with someone refusing to recognise a plain truth can waste time without benefit. Changing the example does not concede that the original evidence was false.

### Could the king have demanded a miracle in reply?

An objection asks why Nimrod did not demand that Ibrāhīm's Lord make the sun rise from the west. The answer given is that the king recognised his own helplessness and the divine character of the ordinary rising. He also recognised Ibrāhīm as a messenger whose prayer might be answered. Such an event could overthrow the claims sustaining his kingdom.

This explanation describes why he would not risk the counter-demand. It is not another miracle performed in the verse. The recorded outcome is his silence, not a reversal of sunrise.

### What lessons concern worldly authority?

The verse itself calls the opponent a recipient of kingship. It therefore permits acknowledging the worldly title or position of a nonbelieving ruler without accepting the ruler's religion. The debate also illustrates the permissibility of disputation intended to make truth distinct from falsehood.

Power and wealth can be read in two ways. A person may see them as entirely personal achievement and become proud, or recognise them as favours of Allah and become grateful. In the first case further gifts become occasions of deeper error. In the second they deepen recognition of the Giver. The king's experience shows how a blessing can be misused without ceasing to originate from Allah.

The inward warning treats separation from Allah as a punishment preceding bodily burning and, for an insightful person, more serious in its effect. A title, a throne and an apparent victory over subjects do not repair that separation. The defeat of the false argument exposes the poverty beneath royal arrogance.

### In short

- Kingship was Allah's gift, but Nimrod used it to support a false claim of divinity.
- Creating life differs from sparing a prisoner, and divine rule differs from issuing human orders.
- The sun's rising supplied a challenge the king could not answer.
- Different explanations of the change of argument preserve its soundness and emphasise the listener's error rather than a defect in the proof.
- Worldly honour should produce gratitude, not independence from the Lord who grants it.

<<<VERSE 2:259>>>
A ruined city prompts a question answered through direct experience: "How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?" Allah causes the traveller to die for a hundred years, restores him and shows him the restoration of his donkey. Food remaining unchanged beside decayed bones makes clear that time and ordinary expectation do not limit Allah's power.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins."** The account is joined to the preceding example by the word or. The buildings had collapsed, roofs and walls lay fallen and inhabitants were absent or dead. Many reports identify the traveller as 'Uzayr (Ezra). Another simply calls him a man from the Children of Israel. Jerusalem is a common identification of the city, while another account calls it Dayr Hiraql.

**"He wondered, “How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?”"** The sight appeared utterly beyond restoration. Several explanations understand this as wonder about the manner of revival, not denial of divine power. Another gloss describes it as challenging that power. Those emphases differ and should not be silently merged. The fuller inward reading expressly rejects attributing doubt to 'Uzayr as a prophet.

**"So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life."** The period was real, despite his later impression that little time had passed. In one account his eyes were restored first so he could witness the restoration of the rest of his body. His own life then became part of the demonstration he had asked about.

**"Allah asked, “How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?” He replied, “Perhaps a day or part of a day.”"** The speech is explained as conveyed through an angel. He had died earlier in the day and returned when the sun was still visible late in the day. He therefore judged by what appeared to be one day's familiar course.

**"Allah said, “No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink—they have not spoiled."** Reports describe figs, grapes and juice. They had not soured, rotted or otherwise changed as expected. The word for spoiling is discussed through two possible roots and a reading without its final consonant. Each reading preserves the meaning that the provisions remained unchanged.

**"˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity."** The donkey had decayed to scattered bones while the food remained sound. The same period had not imposed the same outcome on everything. The revived traveller was also a sign for other people, not only a private recipient of reassurance.

**"And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!”"** A reading of the verb concerns raising and assembling the bones, while another concerns reviving them. He watched the reconstruction rather than merely hearing that it had happened out of sight.

**"When this was made clear to him, he declared, “˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything.”"** Knowledge was strengthened through direct sight. Another reading makes the final word an imperative, know, spoken as a command from Allah. The conclusion affirms power unrestricted by the traveller's earlier inability to picture the process.

### Who is identified as the traveller?

Ibn Abi Hatim records 'Ali bin Abi Talib identifying him as 'Uzayr. Ibn Jarir also transmits that identification, with Sulayman bin Buraydah and As-Suddi supporting it. Qatadah, Al-Hasan and Ibn 'Abbas are also named in its transmission. Mujahid bin Jabr calls him an Israelite and identifies Jerusalem after its destruction by Nebuchadnezzar.

Another account names him 'Uzayr Ibn Shurahya and gives Dayr Hiraql as the town. These names and locations are reported particulars. the verse itself does not require the reader to settle their historical identification before understanding its evidence.

The discussion also says 'Uzayr is generally placed in the fifth century before Christ. The adjoining account of Ibrāhīm (Abraham) gives the years 2160 to 1985 before Christ. These are the chronological claims included with the narratives, not independently reconstructed dates. The events' central proof is the witnessed restoration, not a calculation from those dates.

### What happened during the century?

One account says the town was rebuilt seventy years after the traveller died. Its inhabitants increased and the Children of Israel returned. When he awoke, ordinary impressions could not reveal that intervening history. The answer about the hundred years corrected what his own limited perception suggested.

Another reported detail says he had died at forty and was restored still young while his son had reached 120. The contrast made him a lesson to the community. It illustrated returning in the state in which one had died rather than ageing through the period of death.

The details are not all expressed in the verse, and they remain reported explanations. They enlarge the picture without changing the explicit duration into a shorter sleep.

### What do the readings of the bones contribute?

Al-Hakim records through Kharijah bin Zayd bin Thabit that Zayd reported the Prophet ﷺ reading the word as nunshizuha. Al-Hakim calls the chain Sahih, authentic, and says Al-Bukhari and Muslim did not record it. This form is explained as moving, lifting and reassembling the bones.

Mujahid explains nunshiruha as bringing them back to life. Another related reading, nanshiruha, is also mentioned. The grammatical differences direct attention to different aspects of the same extraordinary restoration rather than supplying contrary outcomes.

As-Suddi describes a wind gathering the bones from their scattered positions. Each bone found its place. Flesh, nerves, veins and skin were restored, and an angel breathed life into the donkey's nostrils. It brayed while 'Uzayr watched. The return of ordinary animal life was the end of a process he could see.

### Why place unchanged food beside decayed bones?

The contrast prevents assuming that Allah is limited to one inevitable effect of time. Perishable fruit and juice remained good, while the living animal had died and decomposed. Both were within His power, and both states were then made part of the evidence.

The traveller's mistaken estimate showed the weakness of judging unseen history by a present sensation. Seeing late-afternoon sunlight did not tell him how many years had passed. Revelation and the sign corrected the impression rather than flattering it.

His final affirmation is therefore not merely a repetition. The explanation describes increased certainty: what he already knew through faith became something he had witnessed. In that respect the account prepares the next request to see how resurrection occurs.

### How is this related to the prophetic mission?

The shared reflection says that prophets teach realities ordinarily hidden behind the familiar chain of causes. Seeing such realities allows them to communicate with greater conviction. Their hearers also receive testimony from people known for honesty rather than a claim from someone with a history of deception.

The discussion describes prophets generally receiving their mission around forty, after a life of upright dealings. Their endurance under hardship supports the sincerity of their teaching. An invented message would not as readily explain that persistence or its agreement with the evidence of the world.

The inward reading applies this to 'Uzayr's request. It is a curious appeal for greater certainty, not ignorance of Allah. Allah permitted him to see the answer within his own body and then in his donkey. Seeking understanding is thus distinguished from an accusation against divine power.

### In short

- The traveller's restoration after a century and the donkey's revival show Allah's power over life and death.
- His impression of a brief interval was corrected by revelation and visible signs.
- Unchanged food beside decayed bones demonstrates that time's effects remain subject to Allah.
- Reports differ over the traveller's identity and location, while the verse's evidence remains clear.
- The fuller explanations understand his question as seeking the manner of revival and increased certainty.

<<<VERSE 2:260>>>
Ibrāhīm (Abraham) asks to see the manner of resurrection, not to replace unbelief with faith: "My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead." When Allah asks whether he believes, he answers yes. His request concerns the heart's further reassurance through direct sight. The restoration of the birds becomes a visible example of what Allah can do with scattered parts.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead.”"** The question asks how revival takes place. One explanation connects it with his earlier debate about life and death. Others describe a dead animal whose parts were consumed and scattered. In each, the request seeks a witnessed explanation beyond the knowledge he already possessed.

**"Allah responded, “Do you not believe?” Abraham replied, “Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured.”"** Allah knows his faith. The question makes its reality explicit for everyone hearing the account and closes the possibility of treating the request as denial. Iman means trusting belief or certainty in what is not seen. Itmi'nan is the settled peace of the heart. An increase in the latter need not imply absence of the former.

**"Allah said, “Then bring four birds, train them to come to you,"** Knowing and taming them prevents mistaking unrelated birds for the ones restored. Readings of the instruction, sirhunna and surhunna, are discussed as turning them towards him and cutting them. The explanation combines familiarising them with the subsequent preparation.

**"˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops."** The birds were slaughtered, their parts and feathers mixed, and portions set on hills. Reports mention four hills or seven. The number of birds is explicit, while the number of hills is supplied differently in the reports.

**"Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste."** The English translation describes their swift return. The detailed explanation reads the haste as coming on foot, with the parts first flying to their appropriate bodies. That reading keeps the returning birds within sight and avoids a suggestion that other birds replaced them while hidden overhead.

**"And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”"** No scattered part can defeat His power. His wisdom governs what is shown and what remains an object of faith in the unseen. The sign is possible through His might and granted according to His wisdom.

### What occasioned the request?

One explanation says that after answering Nimrod about life and death, Ibrāhīm wished to witness what he had affirmed. Another account from Qatadah describes him passing a dead beast surrounded by creatures of sea and land.

Al-Hasan, 'Ata' al-Khurasani, al-Dahhak and Ibn Jurayj give an account of a donkey at the shore. 'Ata' places it by Lake Tiberias. When water rose, sea creatures ate the remains and currents carried pieces away. When it receded, land animals fed, fragments became dust, and birds carried further parts into the air. Ibrāhīm knew Allah could gather them, but wanted to see how.

Ibn Zayd instead describes a dead whale partly in water and partly on land. Satan whispered about how parts dispersed through so many animals could be reunited. The request for reassurance dispelled that whisper. The accounts differ in animal and setting and are retained as alternatives, not assembled into one event involving every creature named.

Another explanation adds that the request sought reassurance of friendship with Allah and acceptance of prayer. The heart's longing may have several aspects without turning the explicit yes into a denial of belief.

### How is the report about doubt understood?

Al-Bukhari records from Abu Hurayrah a saying of the Prophet ﷺ that he and his community would be more liable to doubt than Ibrāhīm when asking this question. The explanation understands it as being more liable to seek certainty, not as an accusation that the prophet disbelieved.

The point distinguishes settled belief from a desire to see an unusual process. A person can be certain an event will happen while wondering about its mode. Such wondering can leave attention unsettled without destroying faith. Sukun, peace, is the rest sought when that mental distraction ends.

The question from Allah also serves a public clarification. Asking how can be innocent inquiry, or in a different setting can be a challenge expressing disbelief, as when someone asks a person to lift a load thought beyond his strength. Ibrāhīm's yes makes clear which kind of request this was. Allah did not ask because He lacked knowledge of the answer.

### What is said about the birds?

The Qur'an does not name their species. The discussion says disagreements over them are not necessary to the main proof. Yet the reported lists remain part of the material: one gives a cock, raven, duck and peacock. another a peacock, eagle, raven and cock.

As-Suddi and Al-Hasan explain the preparation as cutting the birds into pieces. Wahb bin Munabbih, Abu Al-Aswad Ad-Dili and Abu Malik also support that reading, along with Sa'id bin Jubayr, 'Ikrimah and Ibn 'Abbas. Feathers, blood and flesh were mixed so that ordinary bodily continuity could not account for their restoration.

Ibn 'Abbas says Ibrāhīm kept their heads. At the call, each part returned to its own body. The restored birds hastened towards him and accepted only their own heads. If another head was offered, the bird refused it. The account makes identification part of the demonstration, rather than leaving him to infer that any bird arriving was the original one.

Ibn al-Mundhir records from Hasan that bone, feather and flesh each found its place and that the birds came alive to Ibrāhīm. The accompanying explanation connects the sign with the gathering of human parts at the Resurrection. It is not merely the recovery of a scattered memory or reputation.

### Why is reassembling the scattered body not impossible?

A common objection imagines the body's dust carried through wind, water, plants and animals to distant places. Gathering every part seems impossible when judged by limited human power. The answer points to the creation and maintenance already occurring.

A person's own body is assembled from material drawn from many regions. Parents are sustained by food gathered from different places. After birth, nourishment continually becomes blood and flesh. Milk comes through animals fed by plants, which in turn are nourished through soil, rain and winds. Grain, fruit and medicine join the body through that wide network.

The person who thinks dispersion defeats resurrection already lives as an assembled body of formerly dispersed material. The One who brought it together the first time is not rendered powerless by its dispersion afterwards. The example asks the reader to recognise existing dependence before treating a future gathering as beyond possibility.

The tamed birds also protect the visual evidence. Ibrāhīm knew them beforehand. Their recognised return was not explained as another set of birds passing by. Their coming in view, in the detailed reading, removes the imagined exchange of identities.

### Does a request for more peace imply inferior faith?

A report from 'Ali says that lifting the veil of the unseen would add nothing to his certainty and peace. That raises the question of whether Ibrāhīm's request means less peace than a later follower's.

The answer distinguishes degrees. Friends of Allah and the sincerely truthful have a level of tranquillity. Prophets have a higher level, and specially granted direct sight is a further degree. Ibrāhīm already possessed the certainty described by 'Ali and the tranquillity belonging to his prophetic station. He requested an additional, exceptional gift, not repair of an absent foundation.

The Prophet ﷺ is also described as receiving visual reassurance through seeing Paradise and Hell. Its special character does not imply that believers denied such sight have no faith. 'Aziz, Almighty, affirms the ease of granting the vision. Hakim, Wise, explains why it is not given indiscriminately to everyone. Were every hidden matter universally exposed, the distinctive quality of faith in the unseen would not remain.

### Why did Ibn 'Abbas find this verse hopeful?

'Abdur-Razzaq records through Ma'mar and Ayyub Ibn 'Abbas describing this as a verse of extraordinary hope. In another account he asked Ibn 'Amr which passage gave him most hope. Ibn 'Amr chose (39:53), which calls those who have sinned against themselves not to despair of Allah's mercy.

Ibn 'Abbas chose Ibrāhīm's request and Allah's acceptance of his simple yes. The account relates it to passing thoughts and whispers that attack the heart. Their arrival does not by itself erase the believer's affirmation. Ibn Abi Hatim records the exchange through Muhammad bin Al-Munkadir. Al-Hakim also records it in Al-Mustadrak, calls its chain Sahih, authentic, and says the two principal collectors did not include it.

The consolation must remain with its distinction. Seeking assurance is not deliberate rejection. The Lord who knows the heart recognises the affirmation without demanding that the servant never experience a troubling thought.

The request of Prophet Ibrahim thus concerns Al-Khalil's desire for closer reassurance. Allah Almighty knew that desire without confusing it with a loss of belief.

### What longing is pictured in the inward reading?

One allusion understands Ibrāhīm as longing for Allah's address. His request opens the conversation, and the question about faith is itself the speech his heart yearns to hear. The narration imagines intense love driving out the ease of patient waiting.

A story illustrates the image through a man who buys a precious jewel for a woman unwilling to speak to him. He appears ready to break it, provoking her to ask what he is doing. He says that hearing that question was his purpose. The comparison concerns the desire to hear the beloved, not an instruction to manipulate Allah or deceive people.

Another reading concerns life of the heart and serenity of the secret core. Its goal is enduring remembrance, reflection, whispered prayer and love. Tongue, heart and spirit each have their form of participation. Here ruh means spirit, whose joy lies in delighting in Allah. One saying, attributed to Makki, describes the request as asking to see intimacy with Allah become established in the heart. Bodily resurrection remains the outward event. this further reading draws an inward application from it.

### What do the four birds signify inwardly?

The peacock pictures attachment to finery. Its death calls for refusing to make worldly beauty the place of ultimate rest. A verse contrasts the nightingale's calls and the peacock's colour with a deeper goal.

The crow represents avarice for what soon disappears. Poetry recalls the wealth or world that took Alexander's life and Darius's kingdom, asking why it should be loved as if it were faithful. The rooster pictures appetite that can block attention to Allah. Another poetic image contrasts the field of desire with the portico of intellect and Saturn in its seventh sphere.

The vulture pictures long wishes fixed on this world of distraction. Its death is an image of cutting that attachment short to reach the goodly life recalled in (16:97). These are spiritual analogies, not a claim that the Qur'an itself lists those meanings or species.

The numerical markers attached to the poems, 53 and 705, identify literary passages in the material and are not additional Qur'anic references. Their images are paraphrased without turning the markers into doctrines.

### How is the request connected with vision and sacrifice?

A further allusion understands Ibrāhīm as asking for vision indirectly, whereas Musa (Moses) asked explicitly. It therefore reads the answer concerning Allah's exaltedness as indirect too. The explicit reply to Musa in (7:143) is recalled to explain the contrast. The interpretation identifies an inward intention rather than replacing the words of the request.

Another saying turns the request back upon the servant: if he asks to see the dead brought to life, he is asked to show obedience in bringing the living Ishmael to death. The allusion recalls the gravity of surrender and sacrifice without supplying an extra narrative outside this job.

Together these readings call for living devotion through the death of dominating desires. The explicit lesson remains Allah's power to restore bodily life. His wisdom grants both the outward sign and the inward instruction drawn from it.

### In short

- Ibrāhīm affirmed belief and sought the greater peace of seeing how resurrection occurs.
- The known birds were cut, dispersed and restored, making the gathering visible and identifiable.
- Scattered matter already gathered into living bodies is evidence against limiting Allah by human ability.
- Passing whispers and seeking reassurance differ from disbelief, which is why Ibn 'Abbas found the verse hopeful.
- The allusive readings apply the sign to longing, surrender and the heart's release from finery, greed, appetite and prolonged wishes.
