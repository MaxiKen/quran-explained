The longest surah of the Qur'an opens with three letters of the alphabet, "Alif-Lãm-Mĩm", read one by one by their names. They are not a word and they are not a sentence. Twenty-nine surahs begin in this way, and from the time of the Companions onward the question of what these letters mean has been answered with a careful mixture of reverence and restraint: Allah knows what He meant by them, the scholars have offered readings, and everyone agrees that they were not sent down for nothing.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Alif-Lãm-Mĩm"** is three letters, the equivalents of A, L and M. Each is pronounced on its own, as the name of the letter, without a vowel joining it to the next. Because of that the technical term for them is al-muqaṭṭaʿāt, the cut-off or isolated letters. Other surahs open with other combinations, such as Ḥā Mīm, or Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād, or a single letter such as Nūn or Qāf, and some of these combinations have become the names of surahs: Ṭā Hā, Yā Sīn, Ṣād and Qāf. If the repeated letters are removed, the letters used at the openings of surahs come to fourteen: Alif, Lām, Mīm, Ṣād, Rā, Kāf, Hā, Yā, ʿAyn, Ṭā, Sīn, Ḥā, Qāf and Nūn, half of the alphabet of twenty-eight. Glory be to the One who made everything reflect His wisdom, even in so small a detail.

### What the letters mean

The oldest answer is that their meaning is something Allah kept to Himself. This was reported from Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān, ʿAlī and Ibn Masʿūd, and it is the view of most of the Companions, of the generation after them, and of the later authorities. Some add that the meaning may have been given to the Prophet ﷺ as a secret he was not to pass on, which would explain why no explanation of the letters has come down from him at all. Al-Qurṭubī adopted this position. He cited ʿĀmir ash-Shaʿbī, Sufyān ath-Thawrī and many masters of hadith for the view that every revealed book contains secret signs of Allah, and that these letters are His secret in the Qur'an. They belong to the elusive verses, the mutashābihāt, whose meaning is known to Allah alone, and it is not for anyone to argue over them. That does not make them useless. Believing in them and reciting them is itself a merit, and in reciting them a person receives blessings from the unseen world whether or not he is aware of it. The four caliphs and Companions such as Ibn Masʿūd held firmly that the letters should be believed in as having come from Allah and recited exactly as they came down, and that it is improper to pry into their meaning.

Other explanations exist beside this one. It was said that the letters are the names of the surahs they begin. It was said that they are the openings Allah chose for His surahs. It was said that they are symbols of His names. Mujāhid said that openings such as Qāf, Ṣād, Ṭā Sīn Mīm and Alif Lām Rā are simply letters of the alphabet, and some scholars of language explained that Allah named a few letters and meant the whole alphabet by them, the way a father says "My son recites Alif, Bā, Tā, Thā" and stops there while meaning all twenty-eight. Ibn Jarīr mentioned this opinion. Some held that the letters are an oath that Allah swore.

Ibn ʿAbbās is reported, in the chain that runs through al-Kalbī from Abū Ṣāliḥ, to have read the three letters as initials: Alif for Allah, Lām for Jibrīl, Mīm for Muhammad ﷺ. Other readings along the same line take Alif as His bounties (ālāʾuh), Lām as His kindness (luṭfuh) and Mīm as His dominion (mulkuh), or as the first letters of three of His names, Allah, al-Laṭīf, the Kind, and al-Majīd, the Glorious. Readings of this sort were given by great and reliable scholars, but their purpose was symbolic. They wanted to wake the reader to the endless meanings that lie in the Qur'an, or to make a difficult thing simple, and none of them claimed that this was what Allah Himself intended. For that reason it is not right to attack such efforts, and it is not right to treat them as the settled meaning either.

### Why the surahs begin with letters

Whatever the letters mean, the scholars agree on what they do. The Qur'an is made of the same letters that the Arabs used in their own speech, yet no one can produce anything like it. Opening surah after surah with bare letters of the alphabet puts that fact on display. This explanation was given by ar-Rāzī in his tafsīr, who traced it to al-Mubarrid and others. Al-Qurṭubī attributed it to al-Farrāʾ and Quṭrub. Az-Zamakhsharī supported it in al-Kashshāf. Abū al-ʿAbbās Ibn Taymiyyah held it, and al-Ḥāfiẓ Abū al-Ḥajjāj al-Mizzī held it too, and he reported that it was the view of Shaykh al-Islām Ibn Taymiyyah. A short gloss puts the same point simply: the letters have no meaning of their own, but they have a purpose, and that purpose is to point to the miracle of a Book built from ordinary letters.

Az-Zamakhsharī explained why the letters are not used once but again and again: repetition makes the challenge bolder, just as the Qur'an repeats its stories and repeats its dare to produce something like it. The openings use one letter, as in Ṣād, Nūn and Qāf, two letters, as in "Ḥâ-Mĩm" (44:1), three, as here, four, as in "Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ra" (13:1) and "Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ṣãd" (7:1), and five, as in "Kãf-Ha-Ya-’Aĩn- Ṣãd" (19:1) and "Ḥâ-Mĩm. ’Aĩn-Sĩn-Qãf" (42:1-2). Arabic words are built of one to five letters, so the openings cover every length a word can have.

There is a pattern that supports this reading. Almost every surah that opens with letters goes straight on to speak of the Book itself. Here the letters are followed by "This is the Book! There is no doubt about it" (2:1-2). In Āl ʿImrān they are followed by "He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth" (3:1-3). Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād is followed by "˹This is˺ a Book sent down to you" (7:1-2), Alif Lām Rā by "˹This is˺ a Book which We have revealed to you" (14:1), and Alif Lām Mīm in another surah by "The revelation of this Book is—beyond doubt—from the Lord of all worlds" (32:1-2). Ḥā Mīm is followed by "˹This is˺ a revelation from the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful" (41:1-2), and Ḥā Mīm ʿAyn Sīn Qāf by "And so you ˹O Prophet˺ are sent revelation" (42:1-3). The letters, then, come as a kind of signature before the mention of the Book they make up.

### How to hold the question

The scholars said that there is no doubt Allah did not send these letters down as idle play. Some ignorant people claimed that parts of the Qur'an mean nothing, and that is a grave error. The letters carry meaning. If a sound report from the Prophet ﷺ explaining them is found, it is to be accepted. If not, the believer stops where he has been made to stop and says, with those well grounded in knowledge, "We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord" (3:7). Since the scholars never settled on one explanation, whoever is convinced that one of them is right should follow it, and whoever is not should refrain from judging the matter. Allah knows best what He means by these letters.

### The inner reading

The letters have also been read as the private language of love. Lovers speak to each other in single letters so that no onlooker understands, and between friends there is a secret that words do not disclose and no pen records. Mūsā (Moses) heard a thousand words in a thousand tongues, but this secret was given to Muhammad ﷺ in the seclusion of the night journey, at the distance of "two arms-lengths away or even less" (53:9). Mūsā (Moses) heard the words and did not see the Speaker. Muhammad ﷺ heard the secret while looking upon the One who keeps it. Mūsā (Moses) found his joy in seeking and in hearing, and so much was said to him. Muhammad ﷺ had passed beyond hearing to the point of union, where remembrance is lost in the One remembered and love in the Light, and where a heart that has seen face to face has no more need of reports.

On this reading the three letters are a caress addressed to the best of creation, and they spell out three commands: isolate (afrid) your innermost self for Me, make your limbs supple (layyin) in My service, and stand (aqim) with Me, erasing your own traces and drawing near. The Prophet ﷺ is urged to pass beyond even Jibrīl's mediation so that love may lift the veil and show him the treasures of the unseen, and to drink in one waking moment what the Companions of the Cave drank in a sleep of three hundred and nine years, for the house is empty and the Friend is his. Another reading takes the letters as pronouns: Alif stands for "I" (anā), Lām for "Mine" (lī), Mīm for "from Me" (minnī). I: it is I who am the Lord and who join the servant to Myself in love, the light of the name and of the message, the "serenity" (56:89) of hearts and the rest of spirits. Mine: all that was, is and will be is My kingdom, held by My decree and kept by My protection. From Me: whatever has come has come from My power, My knowledge and My decree. The lesson drawn for the servant is to set aside his own cleverness and knowledge, and to leave the work to his Lord so that he may receive his share.

### In short

- The verse is three isolated letters, read by their names. Twenty-nine surahs open this way, using fourteen different letters in all.
- The four caliphs, Ibn Masʿūd and most scholars held that their meaning is Allah's own secret, to be believed and recited, not probed. Others took them as surah names, as the alphabet in brief, as symbols of His names, or as initials, and such readings were offered as symbols, not as Allah's intended meaning.
- Their purpose is agreed: a Book made of the letters everyone uses, which no one can imitate, and the letters are nearly always followed by mention of the Book.
- The believer accepts what is established, stops where he is made to stop, and says that all of it is from his Lord.
