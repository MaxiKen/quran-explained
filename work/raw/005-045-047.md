<<<VERSE 5:45>>>
The law given in the Torah required equal redress, not a scale favouring the powerful: "A life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear". The same principle continues with teeth and wounds. Yet the verse also honours voluntary pardon, joining the victim's right to justice with the possibility of charity and expiation.

This directly challenges the unequal arrangements described earlier. One tribe could not claim greater worth for its people and reduce the rights of a weaker neighbour merely because it possessed more power.

### Phrase by phrase

**"We ordained for them in the Torah"** identifies the rule as Allah's command to the Children of Israel. The criticism concerns their departure from a law already present in their scripture, not the absence of any instruction about equal treatment.

**"A life for a life"** concerns deliberate wrongful killing and its lawful retaliation. The offender's status does not give him automatic immunity from the consequence imposed for taking another life.

**"an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth"** extends equality to deliberately inflicted injuries. The corresponding injury sets the measure. The wording does not authorise additional harm because the victim belongs to a stronger group.

**"and for wounds equal retaliation.”"** states the wider principle of qisas, proportionate redress. Where exact equivalence is feasible, that is the rule. Where it cannot be achieved, the account leaves assessment to legal determination rather than uncontrolled imitation of the injury.

**"But whoever waives it charitably, it will be atonement for them."** commends voluntary surrender of the right to retaliation. The explanations differ over whether the expiation named concerns the victim who pardons or the aggressor who is pardoned. Both views are recorded below.

**"And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers"** condemns denying the oppressed the equal right Allah assigned. Disobedience harms others and exposes the wrongdoer himself to punishment.

### Which inequality is being rejected?

Banu An-Nadir demanded retaliation when one of their people was killed by someone from Qurayzah, but did not accept the same rule in reverse. The account describes the weaker tribe being required to pay both retaliation and enhanced blood money, while receiving only reduced diyah, compensation, when the stronger tribe killed one of its people.

The Torah's rule of equal life and injury exposed that arrangement as domination rather than revelation. Their request for the Prophet's ﷺ judgment could not honestly be a search for an unknown principle when the principle was already in their book.

The preceding verse called deliberate rejection of Allah's command disbelief (5:44). Here the emphasis is injustice: the victim's due is withheld and the aggressor protected by an unequal standard. The distinctions already reported between greater and lesser kufr, injustice and rebellion remain relevant. The label does not dispense with examining what a person believes and does.

### Does the principle also apply in Islamic law?

Abu Nasr bin As-Sabbagh states in Ash-Shamil that scholars agree upon implementing the verse's rule. He records agreement that a man is subject to retaliation for killing a woman, according to its general meaning.

An-Nasa'i records the Prophet ﷺ having that instruction written in the document given to 'Amr bin Hazm. Another hadith affirms the equal sanctity of Muslim blood. The victim's being a woman does not turn her life into something a man may take without the stated redress.

Ibn 'Abbas, in the account recorded by Ibn Jarir and Ibn Abi Hatim, also describes equality between free Muslim men and women in these injuries, and between male and female slaves within the category discussed. These are the historical legal categories supplied, not a new claim about every distinction debated elsewhere.

### What happened when Ar-Rabi' broke a girl's tooth?

Anas bin Malik relates that his aunt Ar-Rabi' broke a girl's tooth. Her relatives asked the injured girl's family to forgive, but they initially refused. The case came before the Prophet ﷺ, who ordered equivalent retaliation.

Anas bin An-Nadr, Ar-Rabi's brother, asked whether her tooth would really be broken. The Prophet ﷺ answered that Allah's book prescribed retaliation. Anas then swore that it would not happen. Later the girl's family voluntarily surrendered their right.

The Prophet ﷺ said that some servants of Allah have oaths He brings to fulfilment. Ahmad records the account, and it is also in the Two Sahihs. Its conclusion does not mean the court granted a powerful relative an exemption. The legal right remained until its holders chose to pardon.

### Why must the wound heal before retaliation?

The full extent of an injury may not be apparent immediately. Retaliating too early can therefore precede knowledge of the lasting harm. The stated rule is to wait until the victim's wound has healed.

Ahmad records an account through 'Amr bin Shu'ayb of a man whose leg was stabbed with a horn. He asked the Prophet ﷺ for retaliation and was told to wait for healing. He returned insisting, and permission was given. Later he complained that he had become lame.

He was reminded that he had been told to wait, and no further compensation was allowed for the resulting limp in that case. The report then states that the Prophet ﷺ prohibited retaliation before healing.

A further ruling reports the majority of the Companions and their followers as holding that, where lawful retaliation for a wound is authorised and the aggressor dies as a result, no compensation is due for that death. The account concerns authorised redress, not private violence or exceeding its lawful limits.

### For whom is pardon an expiation?

Ibn 'Abbas is reported as explaining that forgiveness brings expiation to the aggressor and reward to the victim. Ibn Abi Hatim records that interpretation through more than one account.

Jabir bin 'Abdullah instead identifies the expiation with the victim who forgives. Al-Hasan Al-Basri, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i and Abu Ishaq Al-Hamdani give the same view. A brief gloss preserves both possibilities.

Ahmad records 'Ubadah bin As-Samit reporting the Prophet ﷺ that a person who charitably gives up redress for a bodily wound receives forgiveness corresponding to what he relinquishes. An-Nasa'i and Ibn Jarir also record the report. This supports the explanation centred on the pardoning victim.

Pardon can be particularly generous where violence arose from an emotional lapse rather than a settled intent to harm. Such generosity can encourage a less vindictive society. But its being praised as charity means it remains the holder's voluntary act, not a favour a powerful offender may demand.

### In short

- Equal redress rejects rules that value one group's lives or injuries above another's because of power.
- Qisas is lawful, proportionate retaliation under legal authority, not unlimited revenge.
- Wounds should heal before redress so that their extent is known.
- Voluntary pardon is honoured, with reported interpretations connecting its expiation to either victim or aggressor.
- Neither status nor pressure should replace the victim's right or the sincerity of a freely given pardon.

<<<VERSE 5:46>>>
The line of revelation continues with 'Isa (Jesus): "We sent Jesus, son of Mary, confirming the Torah revealed before him." He does not arrive as an opponent of the earlier prophets. Allah gives him the Injil, the Gospel, as guidance and light, confirming earlier truth while providing the instruction appointed for his mission.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Then in the footsteps of the prophets, We sent Jesus, son of Mary"** places 'Isa son of Maryam within the succession of prophets to the Children of Israel. The sender is Allah, and the mission continues the earlier call to obedience.

**"confirming the Torah revealed before him."** He believes in the Torah and judges according to its truth. Confirmation includes Allah's oneness and the revealed laws, while allowing for particular provisions replaced by the new revelation.

**"And We gave him the Gospel containing guidance and light"** describes the Injil as a divine gift. Guidance directs people away from error, while light clarifies rulings, dispels doubts and resolves difficult questions.

**"and confirming what was revealed in the Torah"** emphasises continuity again. The two revelations do not differ over who is Lord or whether people owe Him obedience. A particular gloss also connects the confirmation with the legal ruling of stoning.

**"a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing"** identifies those who benefit from the instruction. Taqwa means mindfulness of Allah that restrains disbelief, idolatry and indecency. The lesson is meant to stop wrongdoing, not merely add information.

### How can confirmation include changed rulings?

The account says 'Isa adhered to the Torah except in certain matters clarified or replaced by the Injil. His statement that he came to permit some things previously forbidden shows that limited change (3:50).

The explanation therefore describes some Torah provisions as abrogated by the Gospel. This does not mean the original revelation was false. The Lord who gave the earlier rule can appoint a later one according to His wisdom.

Confirmation preserves the shared truth and authority of revelation. It does not require every practical ruling to remain unchanged through every prophetic mission. The same obedience that required following an earlier command requires accepting a genuine later command from Allah.

### Why single out the God-fearing?

The guidance is given, but its moral benefit depends upon a willing response. A person who fears Allah's warning allows the revelation to correct conduct, resolve doubts and restrain forbidden actions.

The preceding account has shown how people can possess a book while bending it to their desires. Here the description of the Gospel points toward the opposite response: allowing divine light to judge desire rather than allowing desire to edit the light.

### In short

- 'Isa son of Maryam continues the prophetic succession and confirms the Torah's truth.
- The Gospel is Allah's guidance and light, clarifying belief and conduct.
- Confirmation is compatible with Allah replacing some earlier practical rulings.
- The God-fearing benefit by allowing revelation to restrain and correct them.

<<<VERSE 5:47>>>
The gift of the Gospel carries a duty: "So let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed in it." Possession of revelation is not enough. Its people must believe and act upon its instruction rather than prefer falsehood when the command conflicts with desire.

### Phrase by phrase

**"So let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed in it."** directs the Christians to the truth entrusted to them. One explanation concerns their judgment by the Gospel during its appointed time before Muhammad ﷺ. Another emphasises believing all its contents and fulfilling its commands, including the announced coming of the final Messenger ﷺ.

**"And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed"** warns against setting aside or concealing the revealed instruction. A brief gloss particularly names the description of Muhammad ﷺ and the legal ruling they were to make known.

**"are ˹truly˺ the rebellious"** describes departure from obedience into falsehood. The original context is identified with Christians, but the warning concerns the act of rejecting Allah's command, not a privilege granted to another community to behave similarly.

### How does the command relate to the final Messenger ﷺ?

The explanation does not treat fidelity to the Gospel as permission to reject a messenger whose coming it announced. Obedience includes accepting that promise when fulfilled. The Qur'an describes people finding the unlettered Messenger written about in their scriptures and following him (7:157).

A related explanation reads the wording as expressing the purpose for which the Gospel was given: that its people should judge by it. The alternative reading presents a direct command to judge. Both connect revelation with practical obedience rather than ceremonial possession.

### What does rebellion mean here?

It means leaving what Allah has revealed for a preferred falsehood. The passage's earlier distinctions concerning rejection and sinful disobedience remain part of the wider treatment. The present verse does not supply a licence for casual judgments about particular individuals.

Its demand is straightforward: the rule a person professes to honour must govern the decision he makes. Faithfulness cannot be reduced to a label while contrary desires determine the outcome.

### In short

- The Gospel's people are commanded to follow the revelation entrusted to them.
- Its authority in its own time and its announcement of the final Messenger ﷺ are both preserved in the explanations.
- Replacing revealed guidance with preferred falsehood is rebellion against the One who gave it.
