<<<VERSE 5:5>>>
This verse opens with a general permission and then gives two particular ones. The general one is: "Today all good, pure foods have been made lawful for you." The first particular one allows the food of the People of the Book, meaning their properly slaughtered animals, and allows them to eat what the believers slaughter. The second allows a believer to marry a chaste woman from among the believers, and also a chaste woman from among those who were given the Scripture before, on condition that he gives her dowry and keeps to marriage. It forbids a man to have a woman in secret or to keep her as a mistress. The verse ends with a warning: whoever rejects the faith, all his good deeds are void, and in the Hereafter he will be among the losers. The verse therefore does three things: it opens the good foods, it makes lawful a marriage, and it reminds the reader that faith is the condition that makes any deed count.

The verse comes straight after the list of forbidden foods in 5:3 and the permission for trained hunting in 5:4. The material reads it as the completion of that discussion, and it says the word "today" refers to the day of 'Arafah in the tenth year after the Hijrah, the same day as the verse about the completion of the religion.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Today all good, pure foods have been made lawful for you"** begins with a general permission. The word translated as good and pure is at-tayyibat. The material explains that the word is set against al-khaba'ith, the foul and disgusting things, so that the verse states the principle that everything good and beneficial is lawful and everything foul and harmful is not. Muqatil said it covers everything Muslims are allowed and every legally earned provision. One reading argues that human beings are not like animals whose life is only eating and sleeping. Their purpose is higher, and that purpose requires high and pure morals. The material says that what enters the body becomes part of the person and so affects his conduct, which is why the lawful and the good matter so much. It gives unlawful income from theft, robbery, bribery, interest and gambling as examples of things that take a person away from humanity. The material also cites 23:51, which tells the messengers to eat of the good things and do righteous deeds, and it says the two commands are linked. Eating what is lawful is part of righteous conduct.

The material gives a further principle. Some things are judged good or evil by the sound taste of a person of good nature. Carrion and blood are judged disgusting by such people in every period. But where custom has blunted that taste, or where the evil is hidden, the decision of the prophets is the guide. The prophets, the material says, were protected and raised by Allah so that they could tell good from evil. Shah Waliyyullah is quoted as saying that the forbidden animals fall under two principles. One is an animal that is evil by its nature, and the other is an animal that was not slaughtered in the right way, so that it counts as carrion.

**"Similarly, the food of the People of the Book is permissible for you and yours is permissible for them."** This is the permission for the slaughtered animals of the People of the Book. The material says that a long line of early authorities read "food" here as animals properly slaughtered. Among them are Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, al-Hasan and as-Suddi, together with several others. The reason given is that the People of the Book, like the believers, say the name of Allah over the animals and hold that slaughter for anyone else is forbidden. The material says the scholars agreed on this. Ibn Hatim and other reports say that 'Abdullah bin Mughaffal reported that during the siege of Khaybar someone threw a leather bag of fat, which the believers took as booty. The material says the fat was taken before the division of the booty, which the scholars use as evidence that a person may eat what he needs from the booty first. The Hanafi, Shafi'i and Hanbali scholars took it as evidence for eating parts of the Jewish slaughter that the Jews themselves avoid, such as the fat. The Maliki scholars disagreed. A stronger proof, the material says, is the report of the poisoned leg of sheep that the people of Khaybar offered the Prophet ﷺ. He took a bite, the animal told him it was poisoned, and he spat it out. Bishr bin al-Bara' bin Ma'rur died from eating it, and the Prophet ﷺ had the woman who poisoned it put to death. The material notes that the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions ate from that animal without asking the Jews whether the fat had been removed.

The material explains the second half of this phrase, "and yours is permissible for them", as permission for believers to give their own slaughtered meat to the People of the Book. The Prophet ﷺ gave his robe to 'Abdullah bin Ubayy bin Salul to wrap him when he died, because 'Abdullah had once given his own robe to al-Abbas. A report says do not befriend anyone but a believer, nor let anyone but a righteous person eat your food, and the material says it is meant to encourage such conduct.

The later scholars then take up the question of who counts as the People of the Book. The material says the term applies to those who hold a scripture that is certainly from Allah, such as the Torah, the Gospel and the Psalms of David, and to the followers of those scriptures. It does not apply to peoples who claim a book without proof, such as the idolaters of Makkah, the Magians, the Hindus, the Buddhists or others. The Sabians are described as unclear, and some scholars include them and others do not. The material says that the Jews and Christians are the People of the Book by universal agreement. Those who distorted their scriptures, and who took 'Uzayr or the Messiah as sons or partners of Allah [9:30], remain called the People of the Book, because the Qur'an keeps this name for them even while it describes their distortions [5:13]. The material gives the example of a governor who asked 'Umar about people who read the Torah and kept the Sabbath but did not believe in the Last Day. 'Umar replied that they should be treated as a sect of the People of the Book. The material then says that people who are listed as Jews or Christians in census records, but who do not believe in God or accept Musa or 'Isa as prophets, do not count under this rule.

Ibn al-Jawzi reports that 'Ali said not to eat from the animals slaughtered by Christians of Bani Taghlib, because they had taken nothing from Christianity except drinking wine. Al-Shafi'i is said to have transmitted this with sound authority. The majority of the Companions and Successors, however, found that these people were ordinary Christians who had never left their faith. So they ruled that their slaughter was lawful too. Al-Qurtubi says the majority of jurists hold that the slaughter of Christians is lawful, whatever tribe they belong to, and the same is true of every Jew. The material concludes that Christians who are known for certain not to believe in God or in Musa and 'Isa as prophets are not covered by the rule.

The material also explains what "food" means. Literally it means anything eaten. But most scholars hold that here it means meat slaughtered by the People of the Book, because dry foods like wheat, chickpeas, rice and fruit are open to all and need no slaughter. As for food that has been handled by non-Muslims, the careful practice is to avoid it where there is no need, because the purity of their utensils and hands cannot be relied on. Al-Qurtubi argues that food that needs an act of production, such as bread or pressed oil, may be refused out of simple distaste, but that slaughter is different because it requires faith and intention. Ordinarily a slaughter by an unbeliever would not be valid, but the Qur'an makes the slaughter of the People of the Book lawful as an exception, as Ibn 'Abbas explained.

**"And ˹permissible for you in marriage˺ are chaste believing women"** begins the marriage permission. The material says that this covers free, chaste believing women. It refers to women who do not commit fornication, which the word "chaste" makes clear. It is set against 2:221, which forbids marrying idolatresses until they believe, and against 4:25. The material reports that 'Abdullah ibn 'Umar advised against marrying Christian women, because he said the worst form of association with Allah is a woman's claim that 'Isa is her lord, and he quoted 2:221. Ibn Abi Hatim recorded that Ibn 'Abbas said that when 2:221 came down, the people stopped marrying pagan women. When this verse came, they married women from the People of the Book. Some Companions married Christian women and saw no problem in it, reading this verse as an exception to 2:221, while others held that 2:221 covered the People of the Book as well. The material says the two verses do not contradict, because the People of the Book were named separately when the other idolaters were named [98:1] and [3:20].

**"and chaste women of those given the Scripture before you"** gives the wider group of women. The material explains that a Jewish or Christian woman may be married on the same condition, that she is chaste.

**"as long as you pay them their dowries in wedlock, neither fornicating nor taking them as mistresses"** lays down the conditions. The dowry, mahr, must be paid with a good heart. The material notes the view of Jabir bin 'Abdullah, 'Amir ash-Sha'bi, Ibrahim an-Nakha'i and al-Hasan al-Basri that if a woman commits adultery before the marriage is consummated, the marriage is annulled and she returns the dowry. Men as well as women must be chaste, and a man must not take a woman as a secret lover, as the Qur'an forbids in 4:25 and as the material explains in its discussion of Surat an-Nisa'.

**"And whoever rejects the faith, all their good deeds will be void ˹in this life˺ and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers"** closes the verse. The material explains that without faith good deeds have no true value. A deed counts only when it is done for Allah, and a deed done for oneself does not. One reading says that the believer who fears God keeps the law in its true spirit, and that the law does not guard a person who has no inclination of his own. One reading takes "losers" to mean that the disbeliever is deprived of Paradise and enters the Fire. Another reads the voiding of deeds the same way, saying that the condition for good deeds to be accepted is true faith.

### Why a Muslim man may marry a woman of the People of the Book

The material gives a reason. Muslim women may not marry non-Muslim men, but Muslim men may marry women from the People of the Book on certain conditions. One reading says this is because a woman is more easily influenced. Once she enters married life, she may be drawn to the faith and the society of her Muslim husband, so the marriage may become the start of her entry into Islam. One scholar adds that the two communities share the same rules on forbidden degrees of marriage, on public marriage and on the presence of two witnesses, so the marriage is not a matter of breaking the law.

### What the slaughter requires

The material also explains why the name of Allah must be spoken over slaughter. The Qur'an makes it obligatory to say the name at slaughter, but it only recommends saying "Bismillah" when eating. The reasoning is that an animal's life is taken, so the act must be marked by gratitude to Allah, while grain and fruit grow for human use and do not need such a mark. The material also says that Islam replaced the pagan practice of naming idols at slaughter with the naming of Allah, which was a practical way to wean people from their old customs.

### The People of the Book and the idolaters

The material compares the slaughter of the People of the Book with the slaughter of idolaters. Idolaters do not claim any scripture or any prophet sent by Allah, so their slaughter is not lawful, while the People of the Book claim a scripture, and their rules on slaughter and marriage are still close to Islam's. The material records a view that the verse makes the food of the People of the Book lawful because their own religion still requires the name of Allah at slaughter, and it forbids slaughter for anyone else. The material says the verses on slaughter in the earlier surahs, al-Baqarah and al-An'am, remain in force, and that this verse is not an abrogation of them. Ibn Jarir and Abu Hayyan are reported to have held that there was no abrogation, and the majority of the Companions held the same view. The material says that some Successors allowed the slaughter of the common People of the Book even when they named Masih or 'Uzayr, and this was based on their knowledge that the wider community had not left its original faith.

The material records Ibn al-Arabi asking his teacher Abu al-Fath al-Maqdisi about Christians of his time who named Masih or 'Uzayr when they slaughtered. Al-Maqdisi replied that the rule that governs them is like that of their forefathers. Allah knew this condition, and He made them subordinate to their forefathers.

### In short

- The verse opens the good and pure foods and makes lawful the slaughter of the People of the Book, on the understanding that they still say the name of Allah and reject slaughter for others.
- A believing man may marry a chaste believing woman or a chaste woman of the People of the Book, on condition that he gives her dowry and keeps to marriage.
- The verse links lawful food and good conduct, and it says that faith is the condition under which any good deed has value.
- The material notes that the scholars differ over who counts as the People of the Book and over the slaughter of certain Christian groups, and it reports the view of the majority in each case.
