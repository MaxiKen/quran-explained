<<<VERSE 5:5>>>
Allah confirms the permission of wholesome food and explains two forms of lawful relationship with the People of the Book: sharing food and marriage under the stated conditions. "Today all good, pure foods have been made lawful for you." The verse particularly includes the animals slaughtered by Jews and Christians, and allows Muslims to give them their own food in return. It also permits marriage to chaste women from those communities, with dowries and an intention of genuine wedlock.

These permissions are not an invitation to treat faith, lawful slaughter or sexual responsibility as unimportant. The verse excludes both open fornication and secret affairs, and ends with a warning that rejection of faith makes a person's works fruitless. Outward permission must remain joined to inward belief and obedience.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Today all good, pure foods have been made lawful for you."** At-tayyibat means what is good, wholesome and pleasing to sound human nature. The declaration is connected with the day of 'Arafah during the Farewell Hajj in the tenth year after the Hijrah. Its explanation does not imply that these foods had all been forbidden the previous day. It confirms the lasting permission as the process of revelation approaches its end.

**"Similarly, the food of the People of the Book is permissible for you"** concerns especially their properly slaughtered animals. Ordinary foods such as grain and fruit do not require this particular distinction between religious communities. Jews and Christians are the People of the Book included by agreement, because of their relationship to the revealed Torah and Gospel. The permission does not include things already forbidden, such as a prohibited species or meat falling outside the required conditions.

**"and yours is permissible for them."** Muslims may give them food from their own lawful slaughter. The preferred explanation reads this as permission addressed to Muslims to feed them, matching the permission to eat their food. Another explanation takes it as information about what is lawful for the People of the Book themselves. In either case, the verse permits an exchange rather than imposing complete separation at the table.

**"And ˹permissible for you in marriage˺ are chaste believing women"** permits marriage to women who believe and preserve chastity. The explanation also understands al-muhsanat here as free women. The marriage must be an honourable bond, not a means of dressing an illicit relationship in respectable words.

**"as well as chaste women of those given the Scripture before you"** extends that permission to women of the Jewish and Christian communities, subject to the stated conditions. Some Companions married Christian women on the strength of this verse. A more restrictive response from Ibn 'Umar is also reported and must not be overlooked.

**"as long as you pay them their dowries in wedlock"** requires the mahr, the woman's marriage due. It is to be given willingly and honourably. A dowry is not payment for prostitution. It belongs to a marriage undertaken for chastity and the obligations of married life.

**"neither fornicating nor taking them as mistresses."** The first expression excludes open illicit intercourse, and the second excludes a concealed sexual relationship with a particular lover. A secret affair is not made lawful by being exclusive. Chastity is required of the man as well as of the woman.

**"And whoever rejects the faith, all their good deeds will be void ˹in this life˺"** warns against denying Allah, His oneness or the laws He has given. One explanation particularly applies it to someone who abandons previously accepted faith. Good deeds require true belief for acceptance. Their outward appearance cannot replace their foundation.

**"and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers"** states the final consequence for someone who dies in that rejection. The loss is not merely a disappointing worldly result. It is deprivation of Paradise and entry into the Fire.

### Why connect wholesome food with human character?

The distinction between at-tayyibat and al-khaba'ith, good and impure things, is stated explicitly elsewhere (7:157). This explanation sees the law of food as concerned with the whole person. What is consumed may affect the body, but also the dispositions and behaviour through which a human being lives.

Human life is not reduced to eating, drinking, sleeping and eventually dying. People have a purpose requiring moral refinement. The Qur'anic comparison of some people to cattle, or worse in their wandering, is used to warn against allowing the appetites to become the measure of life.

Environment and companionship influence conduct. The argument then asks why food entering and becoming part of a person's body should be treated as morally irrelevant. It therefore connects lawful consumption with righteous action. The messengers are commanded to eat good things and do good deeds (23:51).

Lawfulness includes the way something was acquired. Food purchased from theft, robbery, bribery, interest or gambling cannot be separated from the wrong that supplied it. The discussion describes such income as drawing a person away from humane conduct and toward a satanic disposition. The concern is not simply a list of animal species.

Meat receives particular attention because it becomes part of the body and may also carry physical harm. The explanation mentions disease as well as harm to character. Its general religious argument is that what Allah declares wholesome nurtures bodily and moral life, while what He forbids is harmful to the body, the soul or both. This does not turn a particular reader's taste into an independent authority to repeal His rulings.

### Who decides what is wholesome?

Sound human nature is described as recognising many obvious examples of what is repellent or harmful, such as carrion and blood. Yet habits and inherited ignorance can distort that recognition. Harm may also be hidden. The explanation therefore does not leave the final ruling to appetite or custom.

The prophets are the guiding authority where personal judgment is unreliable. They are described as possessing sound minds, balanced dispositions and moral purity under Allah's special care. His protection kept corrupt surroundings from determining their hearts and conduct. Their instruction provides the dependable application of the general principle.

Shah Waliyyullah, in Hujjatullah al-Balighah, groups forbidden animals under two broad reasons. Some are prohibited because of their own nature. Others would be lawful in themselves but have not been killed through a valid method of slaughter. A good species does not make every death lawful, and a proper cutting method does not make every species lawful.

Swine is placed in the first group. Most of the other forbidden cases discussed in the preceding list concern the second group, such as death through a blow or the absence of proper slaughter. The general rule about wholesome and impure things gives the framework, while the Qur'an and the Prophet ﷺ supply its applications.

### Which animal characteristics are discussed?

The explanation points to the punishment of certain people by transformation into swine and monkeys as a sign of the low character associated with those forms. On that reasoning, those animals remain unlawful even if slaughtered. The point is about their classification in the stated legal argument, not permission to mistreat them.

Predatory behaviour supplies another group of examples. Animals such as wolves and lions tear other animals apart with their teeth. A report describes the Prophet ﷺ responding to a question about a wolf by asking whether a human being would eat it. Birds such as hawks and falcons hunt with their talons. The rule given here forbids beasts that tear prey with their teeth and birds that hunt through their claws.

Other examples include creatures whose behaviour is associated with harm, filth or scavenging. Snakes, scorpions, house-lizards and flies are mentioned, together with rats, carrion-eaters and donkeys. Kites also appear among the birds. The discussion treats these examples as illustrations of the kind of aversion recognised by a sound disposition, while relying on the revealed rule rather than personal dislike alone.

The other route to prohibition is an invalid killing. A lawful animal can become carrion by being killed through shock or a blow rather than slaughter. Invoking a name other than Allah's, or deliberately omitting His name, is also treated in this account as invalidating the slaughter. The later discussion records disagreement about how these conditions apply to the People of the Book.

### Why is Allah's name required at slaughter?

All food is a gift from Allah, yet the invocation at slaughter receives a special legal status in this explanation. Saying bismillah, in Allah's name, at an ordinary meal is described as mustahabb, recommended and rewarded without punishment for omission. Invoking His name when slaughtering is described as wajib, a binding duty.

The reason offered is that taking an animal's life requires particular recognition of the One who permits it. Living creatures share the possession of life. The permission for one living being to kill another for food is therefore a major gift, not an authority people possess independently. Naming Allah expresses gratitude and acknowledges the limit under which the act is allowed.

Grain and fruit are contrasted with this. Their growth serves human needs without the same act of slaughter. The invocation over ordinary food is still good, but its legal placement is not identical to the invocation when taking an animal's life.

There is also a historical reason. People before Islam named their idols over slaughtered animals. Replacing those names with Allah's name transformed an established custom into worship of Him alone. A positive practice displaced the false one rather than leaving the old religious association untouched.

### Why does food here especially mean slaughtered animals?

Ta'am ordinarily means whatever is eaten. In this verse, however, the distinctive ruling concerns dhabihah, an animal properly slaughtered for food. Grain, rice, lentils, beans, flour and fruit are lawful when obtained through lawful means regardless of whether the seller belongs to the People of the Book. They do not become forbidden merely by being owned by someone of another religion.

The explanation of food as slaughtered animals is reported from Ibn 'Abbas, Abu Umamah, Sa'id bin Jubayr and Mujahid. It is also given by 'Ikrimah, 'Ata', Makhul, Al-Hasan, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i, As-Suddi and Muqatil bin Hayyan. The related discussion also names Abu ad-Darda', Qatadah and Dhahhak. This meaning is presented as agreed upon among the Companions and their Successors.

There is a distinction between an activity that does not involve a religious rite and one that does. Making bread from dough or pressing oil from olives is not an act of sacrifice. In al-Qurtubi's explanation, refusing such products solely because a non-Muslim subject of the Islamic state produced them would be a matter of personal aversion, not proof that the food is forbidden.

Another passage recommends caution about prepared food where the cleanliness of the utensils or hands is uncertain, especially without need. That is stated as a precaution concerning purity. It is not a special distinction between idolaters and the People of the Book, since the same concern could arise with either. Nor does it cancel the separate explanation that ordinary food is not made unlawful merely by its owner's religion.

Slaughter is different because its legal effect involves belief and intention. Without a specific permission, the argument says, one might have expected a non-Muslim's ritual act not to have that effect for Muslims. The verse supplies the permission expressly for the People of the Book. This, rather than bread or fruit, explains their particular mention.

### Who belongs to the People of the Book?

The word "Book" does not mean any written text claimed to be sacred. It refers here to a scripture whose revelation from Allah is established through the Qur'an and the Sunnah, the Prophet's ﷺ teaching and practice. Examples include the Torah and Gospel, the Zabur or Psalms of Dawud (David), and the scriptures of Musa (Moses) and Ibrahim (Abraham).

Jews and Christians are included by agreement through their connection with the Torah and Gospel. A community's own claim to possess a divine book is not by itself treated as sufficient. The legal discussion therefore distinguishes the People of the Book from the pagan Makkans, Magians or Zoroastrians, and the Hindu, Buddhist, Aryan and Sikh communities it lists.

The Sabians are discussed separately because their identity is uncertain in this account. Those who identify them as followers of the Psalms include them among the People of the Book. Those who understand them as star-worshippers place them with idolaters and Magians instead. That disagreement is tied to identifying their beliefs, not to a denial of the agreed position concerning Jews and Christians.

The question then arises whether only people who follow an unchanged original scripture qualify. The answer given is no. The Qur'an calls people the People of the Book while also accusing them of altering scripture (5:13). It also mentions the claims about 'Uzayr and about the Messiah as sons of Allah (9:30). Their errors do not cause the Qur'an to stop using the category.

The discussion applies this to beliefs about 'Isa (Jesus) and Maryam (Mary) as well. However far such beliefs depart from Allah's oneness, a continuing claim to Judaism or Christianity and its scripture is distinguished from complete abandonment of that religion. Being included in the legal category does not mean that all the included person's beliefs are approved.

Al-Jassas reports a governor asking 'Umar about people who read the Torah and observed the Sabbath but denied Qiyamah, the Last Day. 'Umar instructed him to treat them as a sect of the People of the Book. The account illustrates that serious doctrinal error and total renunciation of scriptural religion are not treated as the same thing.

### What if someone has abandoned the religion entirely?

The explanation distinguishes an actual religious affiliation from a name retained only in a register or inherited family description. A person known with certainty to deny Allah's existence, reject the scripture and deny the prophethood of Musa or 'Isa is not included merely because a record labels him Jewish or Christian.

The discussion raises this as a concern about nominal religious identities in Europe and elsewhere. Its legal distinction depends on established information about the person or group, not on automatically assuming that everyone from a region lacks faith. A name alone cannot prove a religious commitment which is expressly rejected.

The case of Bani Taghlib illustrates the issue. 'Ali prohibited eating their slaughtered animals because, according to his information, they had taken nothing from Christianity except drinking wine. Ibn al-Jauzi reports the statement, and Ash-Shafi'i's transmission is described as having sound authority.

Most Companions and Successors reached a different conclusion about the group. They understood its members to remain Christians rather than to have rejected the faith altogether. They therefore allowed their slaughtered animals. Al-Qurtubi states the majority permission for Christian slaughter, whether from Bani Taghlib or another group, and for Jewish slaughter likewise.

The difference is explained through the judgment about the people involved. 'Ali treated them as people without a real religious commitment. The majority did not accept that description of them. Both sides' reasoning must be retained rather than turning one account into an undisputed ban on all nominally similar people.

### Why give this particular permission to Jews and Christians?

The principal explanation is that their inherited law recognises slaughter in Allah's name and rejects sacrifice to another being. Their beliefs about Allah may be gravely mistaken, but those mistakes do not by themselves remove every point of agreement concerning slaughter.

A parallel explanation is offered about marriage. The discussion describes agreement in prohibited degrees of relationship and in marriage being publicly pronounced with witnesses, rather than a secret sexual arrangement. These are the points of legal correspondence used to explain the permission. They are presented as the account's reasoning, not as a fresh investigation of the practices of every present-day community.

The permission is therefore not read by the majority explanation as a repeal of the earlier food prohibitions. The instructions concerning unlawful dedication and omission of Allah's name, discussed in Surahs Al-Baqarah and Al-An'am, remain operative. Properly slaughtered food from the People of the Book falls within them rather than outside all restraint.

Practices of uninformed members of a community do not automatically establish what its religion teaches. The discussion compares this with customs among Muslims that violate Islam. A custom does not become part of the religion simply because some adherents practise it.

### What if another name is invoked or no name is said?

Here there is a real disagreement. One group of early authorities applies the permission broadly even to errors in the slaughtering practices of the People of the Book. They reason that Allah already knew those practices when He permitted their food. Their members remain governed by the ruling attached to their forefathers and religious community.

Ibn al-Arabi reports asking his teacher Abu al-Fath al-Maqdisi about Christians who invoked the Messiah or 'Uzayr over their slaughter. How could such meat be lawful? Al-Maqdisi answered that they were treated according to their forefathers' standing, a condition already known to Allah when He gave the permission.

On this view, the permission in Al-Ma'idah qualifies, or is described as in some sense superseding, the general prohibitions concerning another name or an omitted invocation. Those taking this position recognise the practices as deviations from the original religious rule, but do not remove the erring people from the communal permission on that account.

The majority position described here rejects that conclusion. It regards slaughter with another name, or deliberate omission of Allah's name, as contrary both to Islamic law and to the religious rule whose correspondence explains the permission. Such meat is therefore not included in the permitted food merely because the person slaughtering belongs to the People of the Book.

This position also rejects making their wrongdoing a cause for naskh, abrogation, of the Qur'anic restrictions. Ibn Jarir and Abu Hayyan are among the authorities named in support of retaining those restrictions. The supplied argument favours this view, while recording the broader permission and its reasoning honestly.

The passage ends while restating that conclusion. Its incomplete final sentence does not justify inventing an additional ruling. The substantive difference is already clear: whether communal status includes irregular slaughtering practices within the permission, or whether the other Qur'anic conditions exclude those practices from it.

### Can Muslims eat parts the Jews themselves avoid?

A report in the Sahih describes 'Abdullah bin Mughaffal at the attack on Khaybar's fortress. Someone threw down a leather bag containing fat. He hurried to take it and declared that he would not share any of it that day. Turning around, he saw the Prophet ﷺ behind him, smiling.

The report is used to allow eating needed food from spoils before their formal distribution. Hanafi, Shafi'i and Hanbali jurists also use it to permit parts of Jewish-slaughtered animals that Jews themselves prohibit, such as fat. The Maliki position mentioned disagrees about that latter permission.

A stronger supporting example is drawn from the roasted sheep offered to the Prophet ﷺ by the people of Khaybar. It had been poisoned. He liked its leg and took a bite, but the meat disclosed the poison to him, and he discarded the bite. The poison affected his palate, while Bishr bin Al-Bara' bin Ma'rur died after eating. The account states that Zaynab, the Jewish woman who poisoned it, was then killed on the Prophet's ﷺ order.

The legal point drawn from this report is not the poison itself. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions had intended to eat the offered sheep without first asking whether the portions prohibited to Jews had been removed. That conduct supports the permission argued by those jurists. It does not erase the recorded disagreement.

### Why explicitly permit giving them Muslim food?

The verse's reciprocity allows Muslims to feed the People of the Book just as they may eat their lawful food. The preferred explanation treats this as fair dealing and return of benefit, rather than a message addressed only to the other community about its own obligations.

An example of returning a kindness is given from the Prophet's ﷺ dealings with 'Abdullah bin Ubayy bin Salul. He gave his garment for 'Abdullah's burial. It is said that this repaid the garment 'Abdullah had earlier given Al-'Abbas when he arrived in Madinah. The example shows that reciprocating a worldly kindness is not confined to intimate religious friendship.

A report advises keeping the company of a believer and having a pious person eat one's food. Here that is understood as encouragement toward good companionship, not as an absolute prohibition contradicting the verse's permission to feed the People of the Book. A recommendation about close company and a legal allowance to share food answer different questions.

### How does the marriage permission relate to the ban on idolatrous marriage?

The earlier instruction forbids marriage to idolatrous women until they believe (2:221). Ibn 'Umar used that instruction to advise against marrying Christian women. He regarded the claim that 'Isa is their lord as a particularly grave form of shirk, associating another with Allah. His warning is a genuine part of the discussion.

Ibn Abi Hatim records Ibn 'Abbas explaining that Muslims first refrained from marrying pagan women under the earlier prohibition. When the present permission concerning chaste scriptural women was revealed, they married women from the People of the Book. Some Companions accordingly married Christians without regarding the marriage itself as forbidden.

There are two ways of relating the verses. If the earlier word for idolatrous women includes the People of the Book generally, the present verse supplies a specific exception. Alternatively, the Qur'an itself distinguishes the People of the Book from other idolaters, so the two commands need not cover exactly the same group.

The separate mention of disbelieving scriptural communities and idolaters illustrates that distinction (98:1). Another passage addresses those given scripture and those without it separately when calling them to submission (3:20). These distinctions do not approve false beliefs, but they matter for the legal category being discussed.

### What does chastity require of both spouses?

The permission concerns honourable marriage. The woman receives her mahr, and the man seeks a chaste relationship rather than unrestricted sexual access. The explanation refers to the corresponding distinction between marriage, illicit intercourse and lovers in the treatment of women elsewhere (4:25).

Men are expressly bound by the same moral standard. The verse does not ask women to be chaste while leaving men free to maintain casual or secret affairs. Both open fornication and a continuing concealed relationship fall outside the permission.

Jabir bin 'Abdullah, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i, Al-Hasan Al-Basri and 'Amir Ash-Sha'bi are reported to hold a further ruling. If the woman commits illicit intercourse after the marriage contract but before consummation, they regard the marriage as annulled and require return of the paid mahr. This is their stated ruling in the discussion, not a claim that no juristic difference could exist beyond it.

A brief report connects part of the verse's setting with women of Makkah boasting to women of Madinah. It supplies no fuller story. That limited notice should not be expanded into invented details about the people or their claims.

### Is the interfaith permission the same for Muslim women?

The explanation states that a Muslim woman may not marry a non-Muslim man. The permission under discussion is for Muslim men to marry women from the People of the Book under the stated conditions. It is not described as symmetrical in both directions.

One explanation gives as its rationale the expectation that a wife may be influenced by her husband and his surrounding society. It hopes that marriage into a Muslim household may become a way for her to accept Islam. This is the supplied rationale, expressed through its view of marital influence, not a new empirical claim about every woman's character or independence.

Whatever hope is attached to the marriage, its lawfulness depends on the conditions actually given. The verse does not permit an illicit relationship in anticipation that it might later produce a good result. Faith, chastity and the marriage due are not optional details.

### Why end these permissions with a warning about faith?

Written law does not enforce itself within a person's heart. Someone determined to evade it can seek outward loopholes while disregarding its purpose. Fear of Allah makes obedience more than a search for the least inconvenient wording. The nearby warning of swift reckoning therefore remains relevant to these permissions.

The final sentence also makes the foundation of accepted deeds explicit. The rejection of faith empties works of their religious value, and a person dying in that state faces loss in the Hereafter. One gloss particularly describes apostasy and the failure of previously performed good deeds.

The inward explanation asks for whose sake the deed is done. A deed not directed toward Allah may instead serve the self. Its outward usefulness does not give the person authority to demand divine reward while rejecting the One from whom that reward is sought. The verse's closing warning keeps permission in food and marriage from being mistaken for indifference about belief.

### In short

- The verse confirms wholesome food and permits the properly slaughtered animals of the People of the Book, together with reciprocal sharing of Muslim food.
- Jews and Christians remain within that legal category despite the doctrinal errors discussed. Total abandonment of their religion is treated separately from error within it.
- The application to irregular slaughter is disputed. The favoured explanation retains the prohibitions concerning another name or deliberate omission of Allah's name, while the broader early view is also recorded.
- Marriage to chaste scriptural women is permitted to Muslim men under the stated conditions, including the dowry and genuine wedlock. Neither open fornication nor secret affairs are allowed.
- Intention, fear of Allah and faith govern the use of these permissions. Rejection of faith brings the loss of accepted works and final reward.
