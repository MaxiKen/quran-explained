<<<VERSE 4:11>>>
This is the verse of inheritance, one of the three passages of this surah from which the whole science is derived: "Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female". In one sentence the verse gives the general rule for children, then works through the cases: daughters alone, a single daughter, parents with and without grandchildren, the mother's share when siblings exist, and the order of payment that governs every estate. Its closing words, "˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise", fix the whole distribution as His decision, not ours.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah commands you regarding your children"** opens the law. The word translated "commands" is a waṣiyyah, a charge, and it means a plain command. The shares that follow are not counsel to be weighed. They are the ruling.

**"the share of the male will be twice that of the female"** is the universal rule for children. Where a man leaves sons and daughters together, the estate splits so that each boy receives twice each girl's portion. One boy and two girls, for example, divide the property into four parts: two for the boy, one for each girl. The rule entitles boys and girls both, against the practice of the Jāhiliyyah, which gave the males and nothing to the females. The doubled share is not a grading of worth. Men carry the financial duties of the family, the dependants, the commercial transactions, the work and the obligations, and so they carry the double portion.

**"If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate"** handles the family of daughters. Where there is no son and the daughters are two or more, they share two-thirds of what is left equally among them, and the remaining third goes to the other rightful heirs, the parents of the deceased, or the wife or husband. The words "or more" were weighed word by word. Some said the verse means two daughters only and the "more" is padding, which is not so. Nothing in the Qur'an is useless or redundant. Had the verse been about two women alone, the wording would have been that both of them together share two-thirds. Two daughters follow the same rule as more than two, and this is proven twice over: in the Book, where two sisters are given two-thirds in the verse about the final case of inheritance (4:176), and in the sunnah, in the Prophet's ﷺ command that two-thirds go to the two daughters of Saʿd ibn ar-Rabīʿ. The argument runs by precedence: if a female is entitled to a third when she stands with a male, she is all the more entitled to the same share standing with another female. One grammarian note says the word "more than" simply introduces a relative clause. Another says it guards against a wrong impression, lest people think that the more females there are, the larger their total share grows beyond the fixed two-thirds.

**"But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half"** gives the only daughter half the estate when there is no other child, the rest passing to the other inheritors. A detail of the wording was read two ways, wāḥidatan and wāḥidatun, one making the construction complete and one leaving it suspended, both meaning the same.

**"Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring"** moves to the parents. Where the deceased leaves a child, male or female, the father and mother receive a sixth each. The phrasing "to each one of the two" was pointed: the parents do not share one sixth between them, they receive one sixth apiece. The word for child covers a grandchild as well, and the word for parent covers a grandparent. Where the deceased leaves one daughter only, she takes her half, the parents their sixth each, and the sixth that remains returns to the father. In the terminology of the science of inheritance this is the entitlement of taʿṣīb, agnatic kinship, by which the residue reverts to the nearest male relative.

**"But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third"** gives the second case. No children, no brothers or sisters, and the parents alone inherit: the mother receives a third and the father the remaining two-thirds, so that the father's share stays twice the mother's. If the deceased also leaves a spouse, the spouse's share comes out first, half for a husband or a fourth for a wife, and from what remains the mother takes her third and the father two-thirds, for the remainder is treated just as the whole estate would be for the parents' shares.

**"But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth"** is the third case. Where the deceased leaves no child but leaves two or more siblings, whether full brothers and sisters or half siblings from either side, the mother's share falls from a third to a sixth, and, absent other heirs, the remaining five-sixths go to the father. The siblings themselves inherit nothing here, because the father is nearer than they are. In the terminology of the science this is called ḥajb an-nuqṣān, the blocking that reduces. Qatādah commented on this part of the verse: their presence reduces the share of the mother, but they will not inherit. A single surviving brother does not reduce the mother's share. It stays a third unless the siblings are two or more. The people of knowledge explain the reduction this way: the father is the one who helps the deceased's brothers and sisters to marry, spending from his own money for the purpose, while the mother does not spend from hers, so her sixth leaves room for duties that will fall on him. That is a sound opinion.

**"after the fulfilment of bequests and debts"** sets the order of every estate. The scholars of the salaf, the early generations, and the khalaf, the later ones, agree that debts are paid before the will is executed, and this is apparent to anyone who reads the verse carefully. The full order under the sharīʿah: first the shrouding and burial of the deceased, done without extravagance or stinginess. Then the debts. If the debts equal or exceed the property, nothing is distributed and no will is applied. Then the will, up to a third of what remains, and a will for the entire property takes effect only to the extent of a third. Leaving more than a third is not appropriate, and where it is done with the intention of cutting out the inheritors, it becomes a sin. What remains after debts and the applied will is distributed among the heirs, and where there is no will at all, the whole of what remains after the debts is distributed as inheritance.

**"˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you"** gives the reason for fixing the shares rather than leaving them to judgement. One may draw benefit in this life or the next from one's parents that one could never draw from one's children, and the opposite equally. The verse removes the guesswork: since benefit could come from either, a fixed share is appointed to each. One reading takes the benefit as the benefit of the Hereafter, in rank. Another takes it as benefit in this world with regard to inheritance. Read inwardly, children benefit a person with their service and parents with their compassion: parents stand by in the weakness at the beginning of one's life, children in the weakness at its end.

**"˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise"** closes with authority. These appointed shares, some larger and some smaller, are a commandment Allah has decided and ordained. He places everything in its rightful place and gives each one his rightful due.

### Half of all knowledge

The knowledge of al-Farāʾiḍ, the shares of inheritance, is derived from this verse, the verses after it, and the last verse of this surah, together with the hadiths that explain them. Ibn ʿUyaynah said that the knowledge of al-Farāʾiḍ was called half of knowledge, because it touches all people. Muslim jurists collected and codified every rule of it from the Qur'an and the hadith, and gave it the standing of a permanent science, known in Islamic terminology as ʿIlm al-Farāʾiḍ, the science of the laws of inheritance.

### The occasions of the verse

Two reports carry the verse's revelation. Al-Bukhārī recorded that Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh said: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came visiting me on foot with Abū Bakr at the dwellings of Banū Salmah, and found me unconscious. He asked for water, performed the ablution, and poured some of it on me, and I came to. I said: "O Messenger of Allah, what do you command me to do with my money?" And this verse was revealed. Muslim and an-Nasāʾī recorded it the same way, and the rest of the six compilers collected the hadith as well. It is apparent, though, that this incident belongs to the last verse of the surah (4:176), for at that time Jābir had sisters, and no daughters, parents or offspring to inherit from him. It is mentioned here just as al-Bukhārī mentioned it.

The second report fits the verse itself. Aḥmad recorded from Jābir that the wife of Saʿd ibn ar-Rabīʿ came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and complained that her husband had fallen as a martyr at Uḥud, and his daughters' uncle had seized their money and left them nothing, though no one would marry them without some assets. He said: "Allah will decide on this matter." Then the verses of inheritance came down, and the Prophet ﷺ sent word to the uncle commanding him: "Give two-thirds of Saʿd's money to his two daughters, one eighth to their mother, and whatever is left is yours." Abū Dāwūd, at-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah collected this hadith. In another version of the same event, the woman is the widow of Thābit ibn Qays, who fell as a martyr at Uḥud, and she came with her two girls to complain that their uncle had taken possession of the whole fortune. The Prophet ﷺ told her Allah would judge for her, the surah was revealed, and he then sent for the woman and the uncle and delivered the same division: two-thirds for the girls, an eighth for the mother, the remainder for the uncle.

### What the rule replaced

Ibn ʿAbbās described the old world the verse entered. The custom in the early days was that the property of the deceased was inherited by his offspring, while the parents inherited only if the will named them. Allah cancelled from that custom whatever He willed, and ordained instead that the male receive twice the portion of the female, that each parent take a sixth, that the wife take an eighth or a fourth, and the husband a half or a fourth. The share of parents was thus moved from the uncertainty of wills to the certainty of law.

The doubling of the male's share is not a licence to favour sons. The verse itself testifies that Allah is more merciful to children than their own parents are, since He commands the parents to observe justice with their own children. Al-Bukhārī records the hadith of the captured woman who was searching for her child. On finding him she clasped him to her, put him to her breast and nursed him. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ asked his Companions whether they thought she would willingly throw her child into the fire. They said no. He said: "By Allah, Allah is more merciful with His servants than this woman is with her own child."

### Why the shares are fixed and not chosen

Every human law of inheritance leans somewhere. In the ancient tribal age the boy was the strength of the tribe, so he took the sole right of inheritance and the girl was left nothing. That is now resented, and the modern answer declares boy and girl equal in share. But if the ancient principle was unjustified, the modern one is unrealistic. A law free of every bias can be expected only from God, to whom infinite knowledge and wisdom belong.

The verse says as much in its own words. If the distribution were left to opinion, usefulness would become the criterion, and who will deliver the real benefit, from a parent or a child, in this life or the next, is exactly what a person cannot know with any certainty. Left to personal judgement, a person might think well of someone and leave everything to him, or think badly and leave nothing, and be wrong both times. So Allah, in His exalted majesty, took the responsibility into His own hands, that justice and equity reign over the estate and the capital of the deceased circulates among competent inheritors in a manner that is just. His determination needs no justification. It is not built on analogy. Indeed, if the matter were decided by qiyās on the pattern of the verses around it, where the weak are given preference, the female would be favored, for her weakness and her inability to move about freely. The ruling stands on the wisdom of the Wise, and the shares are settled injunctions to be accepted wholeheartedly, with no one entitled to enforce opinion on them, increase them or decrease them.

### The Qur'an's care for daughters

Look at how the verse words the rule. It could have framed the rule the other way round, naming the females' portion as a fraction of the male's. Instead it says the male receives the equivalent of two females, making the daughters' share the stated basis from which the sons' share is derived. The Qur'an shows visible concern that girls be given their share. That concern indicts a practice still alive: brothers who keep the whole estate and say the sisters forgave their shares. Usually they forgave nothing. They waived reluctantly, knowing they would never be paid anyway and asking themselves why create bad blood between brothers and sisters. Such a waiver is not valid under Islamic law. The sisters' claim remains due against the brothers, and those who usurp inheritance are terrible sinners. Where the heirs include minor girls, the sin doubles: usurping the share of a legal heir and devouring the property of an orphan in one act.

### The two principles of the law

The whole law rests on two principles, the farḍ, the fixed obligatory portions, and the taʿṣīb, the inheritance by the group of agnate relatives. The taʿṣīb is the stronger of the two, for it can draw the entire estate to itself, while the fixed portions together never exceed two-thirds of it. The division therefore begins with the holders of the fixed portions, whose entitlement is weaker, and then proceeds to the ʿaṣaba, whose right is stronger. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whatever remains of the portions goes to the nearest male relative." That is His way, a sunna forever. It is the same order of generosity seen in the Book itself, for of the servants who inherited the Book, "Then We bequeathed the Book to those of Our servants whom We chose" (35:32), He began with the wrongdoer, who has the weaker claim, because the wrongdoer is broken-hearted and unable to withstand a prolonged deferral.

### In short

- A son's share is twice a daughter's. Daughters alone, two or more, share two-thirds. A single daughter takes half. These are fixed obligations from Allah, derived from this verse, the following verses, and the last verse of the surah, the science called al-Farāʾiḡ, half of all knowledge because it touches everyone.
- Parents take a sixth each when there are children, with the residue reverting to the father by taʿṣīb. With no children, the mother takes a third and the father the rest. Two or more siblings reduce the mother to a sixth without inheriting themselves, a reduction called ḥajb an-nuqṣān, explained by the father's duty of marrying off the deceased's siblings.
- Debts come before bequests, and bequests take effect only up to a third. Burial comes first of all.
- The shares are fixed because no one can know who will prove more beneficial, parent or child, in this life or the next. Human laws lean toward the strong tribe or the fashionable principle. Only God's law leans nowhere.
- The verse words the rule so the daughter's share is the basis of the son's, and the forced waiver of a sister's share is no waiver at all.
