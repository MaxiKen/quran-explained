<<<VERSE 2:103>>>
Verse 103 closes the long passage on the sorcery of the Jews by pointing to the road they could have taken. "If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew". Having described what they bought and what it cost them, Allah states what they refused, and why the refusal was so foolish.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺"**. The Jews are meant. Had they believed in Muḥammad ﷺ and the Qur'an, and been fearful of Allah's punishment, turning from Judaism and sorcery and giving up acts of disobedience to Him, of which sorcery was the prime example, the outcome would have been altogether different. Faith here means really having faith in Allah, and mindfulness means taking care to obey His sacred law and not to rebel against it. The grammarians note that the answer to the conditional "if only" is left unspoken. It is understood to be "they would have been rewarded", and the rest of the verse makes that plain.

**"there would have been a better reward from Allah"**. Had they believed in Allah and His Messenger ﷺ and avoided what He prohibited, His reward for those good deeds would have been better for them than what they chose and preferred for themselves. It would have been better than Judaism and sorcery, and better than the thing for which they sold their souls in the previous verse. The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere through the people of knowledge who rebuked those dazzled by Qārūn's wealth: "Allah’s reward is far better for those who believe and do good" (28:80). The phrase is framed as an oath, "verily a reward from Allah", with "would have been better" as its predicate. The reward is the subject, and the comparison is deliberately open, since nothing they could have gained by magic bears comparison with it.

**"if only they knew"**. Had they known that this is better, they would never have preferred the other thing to it. One gloss reads the ending as "if only they believed in Allah's reward, but they neither know nor believe", while another says that they did in fact know all this from their own Book. Both readings fit the pattern of the preceding verse, which first says that the Jews "knew" that the buyer of magic has no share in the Hereafter and then ends with "if only they knew". Knowledge that is neither acted upon nor thought through properly counts for nothing, and so it is treated as if it did not exist. If they had truly grasped what would benefit them they would never have done anything so disgraceful and so plainly misguided.

### What the whole passage comes to

Read together, verses 102 and 103 set out an exchange. On one side is the magic the devils fathered on Sulaymān (Solomon), the books dug up from beneath his throne, the formulas the two angels in Babylon explained only under protest and with a warning, and the spells that broke up marriages. On the other side is faith in the Messenger who confirmed the Torah, and the God-fearing life that follows from it. The Jews of al-Madinah chose the first and threw the second behind their backs, although they knew the price. This verse says that the price was not only miserable but needless, since a far better reward stood ready for them in return for belief and piety. The faith and the fear of Allah it mentions are precisely the things that sorcery destroys, for magic in the strict sense is won by pleasing the devils through unbelief, impurity and sin, and no one keeps both. That is why the verse joins "faithful" and "mindful" and sets the pair against the sorcery of the previous verse as its exact opposite.

The modern reflection on the passage observes that once superstition and apathy had settled on the declining community, the profiteers who sold magical prescriptions in Sulaymān's name found a ready market, and even the angels' plain warning that they were only a test did not stop people practising magic eagerly and for illicit ends. The remedy was never more knowledge of the formulas. It was belief in the clear signs that had come, and a fear of Allah that kept people away from what they had been told to shun.

### The treasure of the two abodes

The devotional reading sees in the verse the whole difference between turning towards God (iqbāl) and being distracted from Him (ishtighāl). Had they only preferred drawing near to God over being preoccupied with other things, they would have gained the treasure of both abodes (dhukhr ad-dārayn) and reached the glory of both worlds (ʿizzat al-kawnayn). Instead the blows of subjugation pressed down upon them and held them fast in the lands of abandonment (mawāṭin al-hajr). Reward "from Allah" is set against everything that distracts from Him, and the one who sells his soul for the lesser thing ends up losing both.

### In short

- Had the Jews believed in the Prophet ﷺ and the Qur'an and feared Allah enough to abandon sorcery and disobedience, Allah's reward would have been far better than anything they gained.
- The unspoken answer to "if only" is "they would have been rewarded", and the reward is framed as an oath that admits no comparison (28:80).
- "If only they knew" repeats the lesson of the previous verse: knowledge not acted upon is as good as none.
- Turning towards God wins the treasure of both worlds, while being distracted from Him leaves a people in the lands of abandonment.
