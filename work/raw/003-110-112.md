<<<VERSE 3:110>>>
> “You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah. Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them. Some of them are faithful, but most are rebellious” (3:110)

The verse describes the Muslim ummah as a community raised to benefit humanity through faith and moral responsibility. Its distinction is tied to encouraging good, preventing wrongdoing, and believing in Allah. It is a trust to fulfil, not a reason for pride or a claim that every Muslim acts well.

### Phrase by phrase

**“You are the best community ever raised for humanity”** praises the ummah of Muhammad ﷺ for its potential and mission. Surah al-Baqarah elsewhere describes it as a balanced community (2:143). This honor does not come from ancestry, political power, or numbers. It is connected to the service described in the rest of the verse. A community loses the meaning of this praise if it abandons faith, justice, and concern for others.

The phrase “for humanity” matters. The community is not praised merely for its own advantage. Its task is to bring benefit to people by making Allah’s guidance known and protecting what is good. It should serve rather than exploit, teach rather than coerce, and uphold justice even when that requires correcting its own members. The best form of daʿwah is consistent conduct joined to truthful speech.

Reports of the Prophet’s teachings describe this community as among the last in historical order yet honored on the Day of Judgment. Abu Hurayrah and other companions transmit accounts in which the Prophet ﷺ expressed hope that many of his followers would enter Paradise. These reports encourage gratitude, not complacency. The community’s honor remains tied to the conditions Allah names here.

**“you encourage good”** means that believers promote what Allah has made right and beneficial. The Arabic term maʿruf describes recognized good within the revealed law and sound moral understanding. It includes worship, keeping promises, supporting the vulnerable, speaking truthfully, and treating neighbors with fairness. The instruction follows the previous verse’s call for a group to guide people, but it applies to the whole community according to each person’s knowledge and ability.

**“forbid evil”** means resisting what Allah has prohibited and what clearly harms people. The Arabic term al-munkar names this rejected conduct. This is not permission to police every private matter, impose personal preferences, or accuse people over disputed questions of Islamic law. The community’s duty must be carried out with knowledge, proportion, and respect for people’s rights. It should give priority to clear wrongs and real harms rather than letting minor disputes consume all its attention.

**“and believe in Allah”** identifies the foundation for the community’s work. Faith in Allah’s oneness gives its members a shared direction and reminds them that they are accountable to Him. Their belief should be visible in humility, obedience, and rejection of shirk. It prevents service from turning into a campaign for status or control. The actions named in the verse arise from faith and must remain guided by it.

The verse’s praise is therefore conditional in practice. ʿUmar ibn al-Khattab is reported to have recited it during pilgrimage and reminded people that the community must fulfil the qualities Allah set out. Ibn Jarir transmits this report. The verse does not authorize Muslims to look down on others. It asks them to examine whether they are actually serving humanity through faith and justice.

### Reports about the ummah’s responsibility

Several hadith reports describe the honor and hope given to the ummah, while connecting that honor to the Prophet’s message. Ahmad, At-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah, and Al-Hakim record a report from Hakim ibn Muʿawiyah ibn Haydah in which the Messenger ﷺ calls this community the last of seventy nations and the best in Allah’s sight. At-Tirmidhi describes the report as hasan. A similar meaning is related from Muʿadh and Abu Saʿid. These reports describe a blessing, not a guarantee that every Muslim is righteous.

Other narrations in the Two Sahihs speak of believers from the ummah entering Paradise. In one, Ukkashah ibn Mihsan al-Asadi asks the Messenger to supplicate that he be among a group promised Paradise, and another man from the Anṣār asks the same. The Prophet responds to Ukkashah first. Reports from Jabir and Abdullah bin Masʿud also convey the Prophet’s hope that many of his followers will enter Paradise. Ahmad records a version from Jabir. Buraydah transmits another account about the community’s share among the people of Paradise, which At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah also record. Such reports should encourage faith and gratitude, not complacency or a claim to judge who is saved.

A report attributed to Ali ibn Abi Talib lists gifts given to the Prophet ﷺ that were not given to earlier messengers. It mentions the earth made a place for prayer and a means of tayammum when water is unavailable, and the ummah being made the best community. The report links this distinction to the Law brought by Muhammad, not to a superiority of race. It also points to the convenience and mercy in Islamic law.

Abdur-Razzaq transmits from Abu Hurayrah a report that Muslims are the last community in the sequence of revealed nations but will be foremost on the Day of Judgment. The report connects this with Friday, the weekly gathering that Muslims observe, while Jews and Christians have their own appointed days. The point is that Allah gave this ummah guidance and responsibility. It is not a reason to disparage people of other faiths.

Earlier scholars including Ar-Rabi, Ad-Dahhak, Muqatil, Qatadah, Ibn Abbas, and As-Suddi explain the praise through the duties named in the verse. Al-Bukhari records Abu Hurayrah’s interpretation of the verse, and a report from Abu Saʿid also describes the community’s standing. Another account names Abdullah bin Masʿud, Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, Muʿadh, and Salim, the freedman of Abu Hudhayfah, among believers who stood by the Prophet. The People of the Book are not all treated alike. Abdullah ibn Salam and others accepted Muhammad ﷺ, while others remained in rejection. The Qur’an’s mention of their different responses gives Muslims a model for fair speech about religious communities. Some reports describe Malik ibn al-Dayf and Wahb ibn Yahudha disputing with early believers about whose community was better. The answer of the verse is that merit rests on faith and action, not inherited status. The ummah believes in the messengers, including Isa (Jesus), and follows the final guidance brought by Muhammad al-Mustafa. This call to haqq, truth, is open to all nations, al-umam, rather than a privilege of one lineage.

The opening of this passage has joined taqwa and unity to service. The community’s jihad includes the effort to restrain the nafs, teach what is good, and prevent clear injustice. It must not turn the word into a license for domination. The Prophet, the Messenger, taught that the mission of the ummah is to invite people to Allah’s oneness and the good, not to boast about identity.

### The People of the Book

**“Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them”** addresses Jews and Christians who rejected the message of Muhammad ﷺ despite recognizing its signs. Belief would have brought them good in this life and the next. The statement refers to those who knowingly refused the truth, not to every individual in those communities.

**“Some of them are faithful”** explicitly recognizes that there were sincere believers among them. Abdullah ibn Salam and others accepted the Prophet ﷺ after recognizing his message. The verse distinguishes those who believed from the majority who remained rebellious. It does not treat a religious community as a single undifferentiated group.

**“but most are rebellious”** describes those who crossed the limits by rejecting revelation and resisting what they knew to be true. The Qur’an elsewhere criticizes people who failed to restrain one another from wrongdoing (5:79). This warning applies to Muslims too if they neglect their responsibilities. The ummah cannot use the failures of other communities to excuse its own.

### In short

- The ummah is honored through faith and service, not ancestry or power.
- Encouraging good and resisting evil must be done with knowledge, justice, and care.
- The verse acknowledges sincere believers among the People of the Book and warns against generalizing about them.

<<<VERSE 3:111>>>
> “They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance. But if they meet you in battle, they will flee and they will have no helpers” (3:111)

The verse reassures the early Muslim community that its opponents would not achieve the lasting harm they sought. It acknowledges that believers might still face verbal injury or other limited hardship, while describing the outcome of armed confrontation in the historical setting of the revelation.

### Phrase by phrase

**“They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance”** does not promise that believers will never be insulted, threatened, or hurt. It distinguishes temporary injury from the destruction of faith and community that hostile opponents hoped to bring about. Earlier verses describe attempts to create doubt and revive divisions among the Muslims. The message is that such schemes cannot erase Allah’s guidance.

Some reports connect this warning with Jewish leaders in Madinah who mocked or pressured people who had accepted Islam. A report attributed to Muqatil names al-Nuʿman, Abu Rafi, Abu Yasir, and Ibn Surya among leaders who confronted Abdullah ibn Salam and other converts. The verse tells believers that painful words do not determine the truth of their faith or the outcome of Allah’s plan. Ibn Abbas and Qatadah explain that the harm in view included verbal attack, while the promise of safety does not cancel the need for patience and lawful protection. It does not require people to ignore abuse or leave wrongdoing unaddressed. They may seek protection and justice through lawful means.

**“But if they meet you in battle”** refers to armed conflict in the circumstances faced by the early Muslim community. The verse is not a general command to attack Jews, Christians, or any other people. It speaks to a specific historical confrontation and assures the believers that hostile forces would not prevail over them as they expected. The Qur’an’s broader commands to uphold covenants and justice remain in force.

**“they will flee and they will have no helpers”** foretells that the hostile forces in view would retreat and lose support. The verse offers reassurance in a time of danger, not permission to mistreat civilians or break agreements. Protection, treaty, and safe conduct remain serious obligations. A military outcome does not make every action lawful.

The history of the early community included conflict and political change in Madinah, Makkah, and later regions such as Ash-Sham. Reports mention Jewish tribes in Madinah and Christian forces under Byzantine rule. These historical events should not be turned into a timeless accusation against entire peoples. The verse describes those who fought or plotted against the believers in that period. It does not erase the distinction between opponents, peaceful neighbors, and people protected by a treaty. The previous verse describes the ummah’s duty to resist clear munkar, wrongdoing, but it must do so within Allah’s law. Reports in al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah and Abdullah bin Masʿud speak of the Prophet’s hope for his followers. That hope offers courage without promising that believers will never face hardship. The Messenger, ﷺ, called people to patience, faith, and reliance on Allah.

### In short

- The verse promises that hostile schemes will not destroy the believers’ faith.
- It acknowledges limited hardship and addresses battle in its historical setting.
- It does not authorize aggression against peaceful people or violation of covenants.

<<<VERSE 3:112>>>
> “They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go, unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people. They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations” (3:112)

The verse describes the consequences of persistent rebellion against Allah and names covenant and treaty as forms of protection. Its accusations concern people who rejected revelation and attacked prophets. It must be read with care: it is not a license to humiliate Jews or any other community, nor to ignore the protection owed to people under a covenant.

### Phrase by phrase

**“They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go”** describes a state of political and social vulnerability that followed the conduct named in the verse. The passage addresses a historical community in the context of its rejection of Allah’s signs and the killing of prophets. It does not declare that every Jewish person in every time is guilty of those acts. The previous verse has already acknowledged that some among the People of the Book believed.

**“unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people”** names two ways safety may be secured: a commitment grounded in Allah’s law and a pact made with people. The wording makes protection an obligation, not a favor that can be withdrawn arbitrarily. A treaty binds those who make it. People who enter a covenant must be treated according to its terms and protected from injustice.

Classical discussions connect this verse with agreements that granted protection to non-Muslim communities, including the jizyah arrangements of earlier Muslim polities. Such historical legal arrangements should be understood in their own political context. The verse’s basic principle is that covenants matter and that people are not to be harmed merely because they belong to another faith. A treaty with people is explicitly recognized as a means of security.

**“They have invited the displeasure of Allah”** means that their conduct brought them under divine anger. The following clauses explain the reason. The verse does not portray Allah as unjustly imposing disgrace. It connects consequences to deliberate rejection and transgression.

**“for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly”** refers to grave historical crimes attributed to some of the Children of Israel. Killing a prophet is an extreme violation, and the verse stresses that it was done without right. The Qur’an condemns the wrongdoing itself. It does not permit later people to transfer collective guilt to descendants or to individuals who did not commit those acts.

**“This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations”** repeats that the outcome follows from repeated conduct. They disobeyed Allah and crossed the limits He set. The warning calls people to reject rebellion and protect the messengers’ guidance. Such violations are munkar, actions rejected by Allah’s law. It also reminds Muslims that they too are accountable if they disobey Allah or violate the rights of others.

### Covenant, justice, and responsibility

The reference to a covenant with Allah and a treaty with people makes this verse relevant to the conduct of the Muslim community. Muslims must not invoke scripture selectively to justify broken promises. The Prophet’s Sunnah and Islamic law require honoring covenants, protecting those entitled to safety, and judging individuals by their own actions. The verse’s force lies in its moral warning, not in permission for collective punishment.

The people addressed in the broader passage include Jews and Christians who rejected Muhammad ﷺ, but the Qur’an repeatedly distinguishes between those who believed and those who did not. Abdullah ibn Salam and other former members of the People of the Book accepted Islam. Ibn Abbas and Qatadah transmit explanations of the verse’s historical setting, while Al-Bukhari records reports about Abu Hurayrah’s account of the ummah’s responsibilities in the preceding verse. This recognition prevents a reader from flattening the passage into hostility toward a whole religious population. Surah al-Baqarah also distinguishes among people by their deeds rather than by inherited claims.

### In short

- The verse links disgrace to specific acts of rejection and transgression.
- It recognizes covenants with Allah and treaties with people as sources of protection.
- It does not assign collective guilt to all Jews or permit Muslims to violate agreements.
