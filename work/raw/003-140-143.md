<<<VERSE 3:140>>>
> “If you have suffered injuries ˹at Uḥud˺, they suffered similarly ˹at Badr˺. We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers, choose martyrs from among you—and Allah does not like the wrongdoers—” (3:140)

The verse consoles the believers after Uhud by reminding them that the Quraysh had also suffered at Badr. Victory and defeat alternate in worldly life. Hardship tests the believers and brings some to the honor of martyrdom, while injustice remains hateful to Allah.

### Phrase by phrase

**“If you have suffered injuries at Uhud, they suffered similarly at Badr”** recalls the two battles. At Uhud the Muslims lost companions and were wounded. At Badr, the Quraysh had suffered casualties and defeat. The verse does not treat either side’s pain as unreal. It puts the believers’ loss within a longer history in which neither side has always had the upper hand.

The Quraysh were from Makkah, while many of the believers had migrated to Madinah. The earlier victory at Badr had been a great relief, but it did not guarantee that every later encounter would be easy. Uhud revealed the costs of mistakes, including leaving the position assigned to the archers. The believers were asked to study what happened rather than assume that faith meant immunity from injury.

**“We alternate these days of victory and defeat among people”** means worldly fortunes change. One community may gain strength for a time, and another may face a setback. A temporary defeat does not prove that the defeated are abandoned by Allah, and temporary success does not prove that the powerful are morally right. The pattern is a test of conduct and character.

The alternation also makes people examine causes. The believers could grieve for the dead and wounded, then identify the errors that contributed to the setback and take corrective action. This is not fatalism. Trust in Allah can coexist with honest assessment, preparation, and responsibility.

**“so that Allah may reveal the true believers”** means that faith becomes visible through actions under pressure. Allah already knows what people conceal. The trials make sincerity manifest in the world, so the community and the believers themselves can see who remains faithful and patient. The verse is not permission to test or persecute one another.

**“choose martyrs from among you”** means that some believers are honored with martyrdom in Allah’s cause. Their deaths are grievous to their families and companions. The verse gives meaning to their sacrifice without saying that the community should seek death or disregard life. The Qur’an’s promise of reward comforts the mourners and affirms that loss is not forgotten by Allah.

**“and Allah does not like the wrongdoers”** warns against oppression and misconduct. The verse does not make every person who suffers a casualty automatically righteous, nor every victor an oppressor. Allah judges by knowledge of deeds and intentions. The believers should respond to loss with patience and justice, not turn grief into revenge against innocent people.

### Mourning and learning from Uhud

A report from Rashid ibn Saʿd describes women whose husbands and sons were killed at Uhud grieving openly. The Prophet ﷺ also grieved for what had happened. The verse does not forbid mourning. It teaches the believers to hold grief together with perspective and trust in Allah.

### In short

- Badr and Uhud show that worldly fortunes alternate.
- Trials make faith and conduct visible, and some believers are honored as martyrs.
- Grief is human, but it should lead to patience and learning rather than injustice.

<<<VERSE 3:141>>>
> “and distinguish the ˹true˺ believers and destroy the disbelievers” (3:141)

This verse continues the explanation of why fortunes change. The trial purifies the believers and distinguishes their sincerity, while the defeat and exposure of aggressors weakens the forces opposed to them.

### Phrase by phrase

**“and distinguish the true believers”** means the trial separates sincere commitment from claims that have not been tested. At Uhud, the believers had to face fear, wounds, loss, and the consequences of disobeying instructions. Some were strengthened by the experience, while others saw the need to correct their conduct. Allah’s knowledge is complete, but the test makes what was hidden evident in people’s actions.

The process is compared in a spiritual reflection to smelting metal: impurities are removed so that what is valuable becomes clear. The Qur’an uses a related image in another context, where scum passes away while what benefits people remains (13:17). The comparison is about moral purification, not a claim that every hardship is easy or that a wounded person is blameworthy.

**“and destroy the disbelievers”** refers in this battle context to the defeat and weakening of those who fought against the believers. It does not authorize indiscriminate harm or turn a temporary victory into a general license to punish people. The Qur’an elsewhere asks whether people expect to enter Paradise without facing trials like those before them (2:214). The believers’ path includes faith and patience through difficulty.

The alternation of days discussed in the preceding verse meant the Quraysh could gain an apparent advantage at Uhud, while the Muslims had prevailed at Badr. A setback revealed the believers’ mistakes and tested their resolve. The community was to return to obedience and correct its strategy, not give up or seek revenge beyond justice.

The verse says that the trial distinguishes and purifies. The muttaqin, those mindful of Allah, respond by renewing their commitment to the right path. It is not for human beings to declare that another person’s hardship proves Allah’s anger. The final judgment belongs to Allah, who knows the unseen and the true state of every heart.

### In short

- Trials distinguish sincere faith and can purify believers.
- The defeat of aggressors is described in the context of Uhud, not as permission for indiscriminate harm.
- The believer responds to hardship with sabr and renewed obedience.

<<<VERSE 3:142>>>
> “Do you think you will enter Paradise without Allah proving which of you ˹truly˺ struggled ˹for His cause˺ and patiently endured” (3:142)

The verse asks whether believers expect Paradise without being tested. It connects striving for Allah’s cause with patience, especially in the difficult circumstances of Uhud.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Do you think you will enter Paradise”** challenges the assumption that faith is a claim with no demands. Paradise is Allah’s gift, but the believer’s sincerity is shown through choices, obedience, and perseverance. Earlier communities also faced severe trials before relief came (2:214), and people are not left untested merely because they say they believe (29:1).

**“without Allah proving which of you truly struggled for His cause”** means the trial makes the believer’s commitment evident through action. In the context of Uhud, this includes jihad, striving to defend the Muslim community under the Prophet’s command. The word also has a broader moral sense of exerting effort for what Allah approves. It does not encourage people to seek conflict or to call every personal dispute a battle.

**“and patiently endured”** means remaining steady when obedience is hard, when one suffers loss, or when the result is not what one hoped. Sabr is not passive acceptance of injustice. It includes continuing to do what is right, resisting the urge to disobey, and learning from mistakes without despair.

The Qur’an’s use of “proving” does not mean Allah lacked knowledge and needed to discover who was sincere. His knowledge is complete. The test brings sincerity into action and makes it visible to the people who witness the event. It also gives each person an opportunity to choose faithfulness rather than merely claim it.

The test at Uhud involved more than combat. The believers had to follow instruction, resist the temptation to leave their assigned place, and remain loyal when rumors and fear spread. Their conduct mattered as much as their courage. The passage asks them to continue on the path of Allah with patience, not to chase martyrdom or place themselves carelessly in danger.

### In short

- Faith is tested through action and perseverance.
- Jihad in the Uhud setting is tied to the Prophet’s leadership and defense of the community.
- Sabr means remaining faithful and correcting mistakes, not seeking harm.

<<<VERSE 3:143>>>
> “You certainly wished ˹for the opportunity˺ for martyrdom before encountering it, now you have seen it with your own eyes” (3:143)

The verse reminds some believers that they had wished for the chance to attain martyrdom before they faced the danger of Uhud. When battle came, they saw its reality and were tested in what they had hoped for.

### Phrase by phrase

**“You certainly wished for the opportunity for martyrdom before encountering it”** recalls the believers who had longed to meet the enemy and gain the honor they saw in those who had died at Badr. Some had not been present at that earlier battle. Their wish was sincere, but they had not yet experienced wounds, fear, confusion, or the sight of death.

The verse does not scold people for wanting Allah’s reward. It confronts the gap that can exist between imagining a trial and actually facing it. A believer may admire courage in another person but feel fear when danger comes close. The lesson is to be honest about one’s limits, ask Allah for well-being, and remain patient if the test arrives.

**“now you have seen it with your own eyes”** means the believers had witnessed the battle and its consequences directly. They had seen the swords and injuries, the deaths of companions, and the confusion that followed the rumor about the Prophet ﷺ. Their wish had become a real situation demanding action and obedience.

Ibn Abbas is cited in reports about the believers’ earlier desire to meet the enemy as those at Badr had. The verse reminds them that wishing for martyrdom is not the same as being patient in danger. The Prophet ﷺ had taught believers not to wish for an encounter with the enemy, but to ask Allah for well-being. If they did meet the enemy, they were to remain patient. This hadith is preserved in the Two Sahihs.

The statement that Paradise is under the shade of swords in that hadith is about steadfastness when a battle has come upon the believers. It is not a call to seek war. Courage belongs alongside restraint, obedience, and care for life. The believer can ask for a peaceful outcome and still act faithfully if conflict becomes unavoidable.

### In short

- Some believers had wished for martyrdom before experiencing battle.
- Facing danger revealed the difference between imagining a trial and enduring it.
- The Prophet taught believers to seek well-being and be patient if battle could not be avoided.
