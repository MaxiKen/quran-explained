<<<VERSE 5:12>>>
Allah recalls an earlier community's pledge so that believers understand the seriousness of their own: "Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel and appointed twelve leaders from among them". The covenant required prayer, payment of the poor due, faith in the messengers, support for them and sincere spending for Allah. His help, forgiveness and Paradise were promised in connection with those duties, not merely with ancestry.

The verse ends by warning that disbelief after accepting such a covenant is a deliberate departure from a clearly marked way. The appointment of leaders made the commitment a shared responsibility with practical oversight, not simply a declaration left without anyone to uphold it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel"** recalls a binding promise to obey Him. One explanation includes their acknowledgment in the Torah of Muhammad ﷺ, worship of Allah alone and rejection of partners with Him. The covenant has definite requirements, which the verse proceeds to name.

**"and appointed twelve leaders from among them"** means one representative from each of their twelve tribes. Each undertook responsibility for his people as well as for himself. The narration shifts from speaking about Allah to His saying "We", drawing attention to His appointment. Some glosses call the twelve messengers or kings, while the main explanation describes tribal chiefs responsible for keeping the pledge.

**"and ˹then˺ said, “I am truly with you."** promises Allah's protection, assistance and support if the covenant is fulfilled. It also reminds them that He watches everything connected with it. Their intentions, private plans and outward actions cannot escape His knowledge.

**"If you establish prayer, pay alms-tax"** names salah and zakah, worship and the obligatory due from wealth. Prayer is to be maintained properly, not merely acknowledged. Giving the poor due discharges rights others have in one's possessions. These duties were laid upon the people of Musa (Moses) before the coming of this community.

**"believe in My messengers, support them"** requires accepting all the messengers Allah sends, honouring them and helping their work of true guidance. A gloss includes armed assistance against an enemy within that support. Belief is not to be restricted to a preferred messenger while later messengers are rejected.

**"and lend to Allah a good loan"** calls for spending in His cause with a sincere heart. The money is not given to supply a need in Allah. It is called a loan to assure the giver of a return. Its goodness includes sincerity and giving what is valued rather than seeking an easy disposal of worthless things.

**"I will certainly forgive your sins and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow."** promises pardon and entry into Paradise. Sins are covered and erased rather than punished. One gloss qualifies the sins mentioned as other than the enormities. The Gardens include dwellings and trees with rivers beneath them, and a fuller gloss names water, milk, wine and honey. The promise joins removal of what is feared with attainment of what is hoped for.

**"And whoever among you disbelieves afterwards has truly strayed from the Right Way.”"** warns against rejection after accepting the pledge. Al-sawa' originally conveys the middle way. Here it is the clear road of guidance leading to Paradise, from which the covenant-breaker knowingly turns aside. The wording makes the assurance solemn through the form of an oath attached to its conditions.

### Why place this after the believers' own covenant?

The earlier reminder calls Muslims back to their declaration of hearing and obeying (5:7). The statement of faith that Allah alone is worthy of worship and Muhammad ﷺ is His Messenger binds its speaker to obedience. The following command requires justice toward both friend and enemy (5:8). The reminder of protection shows another side of Allah's favour (5:11).

The present account shows that taking a covenant is not unique to Muslims. Earlier communities received duties, promises and warnings too. Their history therefore gives the present hearer a reason to take the same kind of commitment seriously.

Help is connected with taqwa, awareness of Allah, and tawakkul, trust in Him. The discussion stresses that victory does not depend on outward equipment alone. Fear of Allah keeps a person faithful when breaking a pledge would otherwise be tempting. Trust in Him prevents just treatment of an enemy from appearing pointless merely because it is not immediate revenge.

The argument also answers the fear that tolerance will simply allow a defeated enemy to recover and attack again. Justice and courtesy, joined to faithfulness and reliance on Allah, may instead draw hearts toward Islam. This is the religious basis of the confidence described, not a claim that every outward arrangement guarantees safety by itself. Iqbal's poetic image expresses it by calling for the spirit of Badr if people seek help like the angelic help of that occasion.

### What were the twelve chiefs responsible for?

Ibn 'Abbas and Muhammad bin Ishaq connect their selection with Musa's campaign against the powerful people in Palestine. Allah commanded a representative to be chosen from each tribe. They pledged obedience to Allah, His messenger and His Book on behalf of their tribes.

Representation did not release the rest of the people from duty. The chiefs were to obey themselves and help ensure their people's obedience. The collective arrangement supported continued prayer, payment of others' rights, allegiance to the messengers and spending for the religion.

This reveals the difference between the use of family knowledge in administration and a claim of inherited superiority. A familiar member of a group may understand its circumstances and enjoy its trust. Giving him responsibility can help maintain order without declaring his ancestry better before Allah.

The Prophet's ﷺ final Hajj address rejected superiority based on being Arab rather than non-Arab, white rather than black, or of a supposedly higher caste. Membership in Islam brought brotherhood. Jami's poetic reminder makes the same point: the servant travelling in love must leave boasts of parentage behind. Being someone's son does not establish spiritual rank.

Ancestry can therefore help identify people for practical arrangements without becoming the measure of their worth. The tribal structure is used here to secure responsibility, not to replace obedience with privilege.

### How does the account of twelve springs illustrate this?

When the Children of Israel faced shortage of water, Musa prayed and was commanded to strike a rock with his staff. Twelve springs flowed, providing for the twelve communities. The Qur'an mentions their division into tribes and the springs that supplied them (7:160).

The arrangement gave each group its provision and reduced grounds for conflict. It is another example of recognising social groupings for a useful purpose rather than treating them as competing spiritual ranks. The same practical concern explains the appointment of a chief for each tribe.

### Which twelve leaders represented the Ansar?

At 'Aqabah the Prophet ﷺ likewise received a pledge through twelve leaders from the Ansar, the helpers of Madinah. Three represented Aws and nine represented Khazraj. They undertook allegiance and obedience on behalf of their people.

From Aws were Usayd bin Al-Hudayr and Sa'd bin Khaythamah. The third is given as Rifa'ah bin 'Abdul-Mundhir, with Abu Al-Haytham bin At-Tayhan named as an alternative in the account.

The Khazraj representatives included the two Sa'ds, Sa'd bin 'Ubadah and Sa'd bin Ar-Rabi', and 'Ubadah bin As-Samit. Also named are Abu Umamah As'ad bin Zurarah, Al-Bara' bin Ma'rur and Rafi' bin Malik bin Al-'Ajlan. Completing the nine are 'Abdullah bin Rawahah, Al-Mundhir bin 'Umar bin Khunays and 'Abdullah bin 'Amr bin Haram.

Ibn Ishaq records Ka'b bin Malik's poem naming these men. Their representative pledge is given as a parallel to the earlier appointment, not as a claim that the two events were identical in every detail.

### Must twelve rulers follow one another without interruption?

A report from Jabir ibn Samurah in Al-Bukhari and Muslim speaks of people's affairs remaining sustained while twelve ruling authorities lead them. The discussion cautions that the report does not state that all twelve must succeed one another continuously. They may be separated by intervals.

The first four are named in their order: Abu Bakr, 'Umar al-Faruq, 'Uthman al-Ghani and 'Ali al-Murtada. After an interval, 'Umar ibn 'Abdul-'Aziz is described as accepted by the community as a fifth rightly guided ruler. The account does not supply a completed list of twelve, and one should not be constructed from outside it.

The comparison draws attention to the recurring number while retaining the limited claim actually made. It is not a reason to force every later ruler into an invented sequence.

### Why are prayer and zakah mentioned before faith in the messengers?

Iman, faith, is foundational to action. The order here does not reverse that relationship. The explanation distinguishes duties already to be performed from the future arrival of further messengers. Prayer and zakah were immediate obligations, while accepting and supporting later messengers would be tested as they came.

The passage also stresses that prayer and zakah were not invented for this final community. They belonged to earlier prophetic guidance, not only to the Children of Israel but to the successive revealed laws. The covenant names basic religious responsibilities that join worship of Allah to the rights of other people.

The promise of Paradise therefore rests on faithfulness, not racial connection. A community cannot preserve the claim to Allah's support while discarding the duties attached to that claim.

### What makes a loan to Allah good?

The good loan is expenditure offered sincerely for His pleasure, without a concealed worldly bargain. Giving only what is unwanted while retaining everything valued contradicts the generous spirit described. The image of a loan assures the giver that the expenditure will not be lost.

A loan is ordinarily expected to be returned under law, custom and conscience. Applying that language to spending for Allah strengthens confidence in His reward. The giver is encouraged to regard the return as certain, though its granting is Allah's generosity.

Zakah has already been named separately. The good loan therefore includes other charity and communal needs, rather than merely repeating the same obligation. Paying zakah does not settle every financial responsibility that can arise among Muslims.

The discussion gives examples such as building a needed mosque or supporting religious education when the authorities do not provide it. Institutions teaching children and adults still need resources. Their needs cannot automatically be dismissed by saying that one's annual zakah has been paid.

### How do individual and communal financial duties differ?

Zakah is described as fard 'ayn, an obligation resting upon each individual who owes it. Other needs may be fard kifayah, a collective obligation. If enough people fulfil a collective duty, the remainder are released from its burden. If nobody undertakes it, the neglect leaves everyone responsible.

The criticism here is directed at two failures. Some people do not pay zakah at all, or pay it without properly calculating the full amount. Others do pay it but imagine that no further financial duty can ever arise. The passage particularly describes the hardship faced by people maintaining religious schools with insufficient support.

It also criticises the habit of offering only zakah money whenever a mosque or educational institution needs help, without recognising distinct duties of support beyond zakah. The point is to preserve the difference between the obligatory due already named and the wider charitable expenditure named afterward. The verse is not reduced to a single payment that ends concern for every communal need.

### What happens if the covenant is rejected?

The promised forgiveness and lasting comfort depend on fulfilling the stated responsibilities. Rejection after the pledge means turning away from instructions already made clear. The wrong is therefore not merely lack of information but departure from acknowledged guidance.

A brief report says that they disbelieved except for five. It supplies neither the names nor a fuller explanation of that count. Its qualification is retained without using it to construct an additional historical account.

The warning prepares for the next verse's description of the covenant's breach and its consequences. The promise and warning are parts of the same offer, not two unrelated messages.

### In short

- The covenant required worship, payment of others' rights, acceptance and support of the messengers, and sincere spending for Allah.
- Twelve tribal representatives were made responsible for helping their people keep it. Practical organisation does not make ancestry a measure of spiritual superiority.
- Prayer and zakah belonged to earlier revelation too. Faithfulness, not lineage, is the basis of the promised help and Paradise.
- The good loan includes financial duties beyond zakah, including communal needs that someone must fulfil.
- Rejecting the covenant after accepting it is a knowing departure from the way of guidance.

<<<VERSE 5:13>>>
The consequence of breaking the covenant is stated directly: "But for breaking their covenant We condemned them and hardened their hearts." The verse describes punishment that reaches understanding and character, not only outward circumstances. Distortion of scripture, neglect of its demands and repeated treachery follow that inward hardness.

Yet the instruction to the Prophet ﷺ does not simply mirror their wrongdoing with contempt. It ends by commanding pardon and good conduct. Their failure does not release him from the conduct Allah loves.

### Phrase by phrase

**"But for breaking their covenant We condemned them"** makes their breach the cause of the punishment. The grammatical form reinforces that causal connection. La'nah, condemnation or curse, is explained as removal from Allah's mercy and its effects. Another gloss includes the imposition of a capitation tax among the punishments described.

**"and hardened their hearts."** Their hearts became resistant to faith and good advice. Words of guidance no longer found a receptive place. This is a spiritual consequence of disobedience, not a claim that sincere obedience first caused an innocent heart to be rejected.

**"They distorted the words of the Scripture"** includes changing words, their recitation or their intended meanings. They moved revelation away from its proper setting and interpreted it to suit desire. Particular examples include the Torah's description of Muhammad ﷺ and its ruling on stoning married people who commit illicit intercourse.

**"and neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold."** Forgetting here includes deliberate abandonment in practice. The explanation particularly mentions the instruction to follow Muhammad ﷺ and make his description known. Possessing a text is not the same as acting upon its reminder.

**"You ˹O Prophet˺ will always find deceit on their part, except for a few."** The Prophet ﷺ would continue discovering broken promises and plots against him and his Companions. Mujahid mentions the attempt to kill him. A gloss identifies Banu Qurayzah in this connection. The exception is real: 'Abdullah Ibn Salam and other sincere believers are given as examples of those who did not share the betrayal.

**"But pardon them and bear with them."** He is told to overlook wrongdoing and refrain from taking them to task in the manner covered by this command. The instruction calls for restraint rather than letting knowledge of their conduct corrupt his own.

**"Indeed, Allah loves the good-doers"** gives the positive reason. Kindness and moral excellence remain loved by Allah even when their immediate recipients are difficult or unresponsive. The believer's conduct has a value before Him beyond the reaction it receives.

### How can punishment take place within the heart?

The discussion distinguishes visible punishment from inward punishment. Visible examples include destructive stones or the overturning of land. Inward punishment concerns damaged judgment and feeling, leaving a person increasingly unable to recognise truth or respond to it.

Surah al-Mutaffifin describes the stain placed upon hearts by wrongdoing. A report from the Prophet ﷺ explains the process through a dark mark left by sin. At first the mark is painful and noticeable, like a stain on clean cloth. Repentance and a resolve to leave the wrongdoing remove it.

If the person becomes careless and continues sinning, the marks accumulate until darkness covers the heart. The image then becomes that of an overturned vessel: what is poured toward it cannot remain inside. Good counsel cannot settle there because the disposition receiving it has become resistant.

The person may eventually reverse moral judgments, calling a vice a virtue or a defect a merit. This is already a punishment within worldly life, before the final judgment. Repetition makes the wrongdoing easier to repeat.

Some early teachers express the corresponding pattern on both sides. One good action is rewarded with tawfiq, Allah-given ability, to perform another. One sin can draw the heart toward another sin. Obedience and disobedience each acquire a momentum, which is why a first failure should lead to repentance rather than indifference.

### How does distortion replace the covenant?

When understanding becomes corrupt, the person no longer allows revelation to correct desire. Instead, revelation is made to appear to support whatever is desired. Words may be changed, passages detached from their meanings or explanations shaped to avoid their demands. The discussion also notes that some acknowledgments of textual alteration have come from European Christians themselves, without supplying further examples here.

The wider warning concerns a community constructing its own religion while retaining a revealed name. Prayer becomes a subject of irrelevant disputes rather than obedience. People seek salvation without discharging the rights of others. Worldly activity is called service of truth, and ordinary self-interested expenditure is given religious excuses.

Once that pattern dominates, the guidance is no longer being followed. The community recognises the language of its desires more readily than the demands of its covenant. Death then brings people before Allah despite the religious labels they used to justify themselves.

This is why the exception for a few must not be ignored. The verse does not erase those who remain faithful or accept the truth. Its judgment concerns breach and distortion, not a refusal to recognise any sincere person among the addressed people.

### Why command pardon after exposing treachery?

A natural reaction to repeated betrayal would be contempt and refusal to have anything further to do with the offenders. The verse directs the Prophet ﷺ toward a better response. Courtesy may reach a person whom argument alone has failed to move.

Even where that hope is not realised, maintaining one's own good conduct remains necessary. Another person's hard heart is not a reason to spoil one's own character. The good action still brings the doer nearer to Allah's pleasure.

An early saying puts the principle in terms of responding to disobedience with obedience. When someone violates Allah's command in treating a believer, the finest response is to keep Allah's command in treating that person. Pardon may gather hearts around truth and become a means of guidance.

A differing legal account is also recorded. Qatadah regards this command as abrogated by the later instruction to fight those who do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Another gloss calls it superseded by the sword verse, but its printed reference is malformed. That opinion is preserved without adding a precise verse number from outside the material. The explanation praising pardon and the stated abrogation view should both remain visible.

### In short

- Breach of the covenant brought distance from mercy and hearts resistant to guidance.
- Repeated sin can corrupt judgment, while repentance removes its stain and obedience opens the way to further good.
- Distorting scripture includes bending its meaning to desire and neglecting duties while retaining religious language.
- The faithful few are explicitly excepted. The command to pardon teaches good conduct despite betrayal, alongside a recorded view that the command was later superseded.

<<<VERSE 5:14>>>
The warning extends to another scriptural community: "And from those who say, “We are Christians,” We took their covenant". They too abandoned part of what they had been instructed to uphold. The consequence described is hostility and enmity among them, continuing until the final judgment when Allah will disclose and repay their deeds.

The verse therefore distinguishes a religious claim from faithfulness to its obligations. Calling oneself a follower of 'Isa (Jesus) does not by itself fulfil the covenant attached to that claim.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And from those who say, “We are Christians,” We took their covenant"** recalls their pledge in the Gospel. It included worshipping Allah alone, accepting every messenger He sends and following Muhammad ﷺ when he came. The explanation also includes acknowledging and making known his description. A particular gloss identifies Christians of Najran.

**"but they neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold."** They abandoned part of the instruction concerning faith and practice. This is not described merely as failure to remember a sentence. It is a failure to fulfil a binding reminder, like the preceding covenant-breaking.

**"So We let hostility and enmity arise between them until the Day of Judgment"** describes the result of schism and competing desires. One reading places it among Christian sects. Another includes hostility between Jews and Christians. The explanation mentions mutual accusations of unbelief, fighting and destruction as expressions of that enmity.

**"and soon Allah will inform them of all they have done"** threatens final disclosure and repayment. Their opposition, concealed truth, betrayal and hatred will not disappear behind claims of religious loyalty. Allah knows the actions for which they will answer.

### How is the sectarian conflict described?

The account describes groups condemning one another's beliefs and excluding one another from places of worship. It names the Monarchist or Melkite group, Jacobites, Nestorians and Arians. Another gloss mentions the Nestorians of Najran together with Jacobites, Marqusiyya and Melkites.

A brief classification cited through al-Baydawi gives three formulations concerning 'Isa. It describes the Fisturyah, Nestorians, as calling him Allah's son. It describes the Ya'qubiyah, Jacobites, as identifying him with Allah, and the Malka'iyah, Malkites, as treating him as one of the Trinity. These are the classifications supplied in that account, not an independent historical survey of each group's full theology.

The shared objection is to attributing a companion or son to Allah. He is one and self-sufficient, neither born nor begetting, with no equal. The warning concerns claims opposed to that oneness and the conflicts sustained through them.

### What if some nominal Christians appear united?

The discussion answers by distinguishing religious adherence from a merely inherited or national label. It applies the statement about religious dissension to people who continue professing and practising Christianity. Those who have left religion for secular or atheistic commitments are not treated as proof that religious schisms have ended simply because records still call them Christian.

The explanation is about the basis of the hostility being described. If religious commitment has been abandoned, the absence of a particular theological dispute among those people does not answer a statement about divisions within continuing religious adherence. This is the distinction made in the discussion, not a claim about the personal beliefs of every person using a Christian identity today.

### How can neglect harden into a lasting sect?

The inward account describes communities turning from clear, enduring commands to matters of merely local or temporary interest. Dissatisfied with the guidance received, people seek new controversies concerning law, spiritual life or political activity. Worldly desire is then allowed to decide what should count as religion.

Changes may be defended as keeping up with the times or correcting what is imagined to be an incomplete religion. Each new version gathers people with similar preferences. Later generations inherit it and defend it as the sacred possession of their ancestors.

The passage of time then gives the invention an appearance of permanence. Loyalty to the inherited version becomes a religious duty in people's eyes. Hostility to others can even be presented as obedience to Allah. What began as departure from a common covenant becomes a continuing justification for division and violence.

The final judgment exposes that substitution. A venerable name or long history cannot conceal what people actually did with the instruction they received.

### In short

- Christian communities also received a covenant of worship, faith and obedience, and are judged by fidelity rather than their claim alone.
- Neglect of the reminder is linked to continuing hostility and competing sectarian loyalties.
- Inherited custom can be mistaken for revelation and used to sanctify hatred.
- Allah will disclose and repay the deeds hidden behind religious claims.
