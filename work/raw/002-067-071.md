<<<VERSE 2:67>>>
Verse 67 opens the story that gives this sūrah its name, al-Baqarah, the Cow. Mūsā (Moses) told his people, "Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow". They answered, "Are you mocking us?", and he replied, "I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!" The favour that the Israelites are being asked to remember here is the miracle at the end of the story, when a murdered man was brought back to life by means of the cow and named his killer.

### The murder behind the command

The reports fill in what the verse assumes. Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded ʿUbaydah as-Salmānī's account. There was a wealthy man among the Children of Israel who had no children, and whose only heir was his nephew. The nephew killed him for the inheritance, carried the body by night and left it at the door of another man. In the morning he raised the cry for vengeance, and the people took up their weapons and came close to fighting one another. The wiser among them asked why they should kill each other while the Messenger of Allah was in their midst, so they went to Mūsā and laid the matter before him, and he told them that Allah commanded them to sacrifice a cow. Ibn Jarīr reported something similar. Other accounts give a different motive. According to the Mirqāt, a commentary on the Mishkāt, a man wanted to marry a girl, her father refused, and the suitor was so enraged that he killed the father and disappeared. The Maʿālim at-Tanzīl cites al-Kalbī for the statement that no ruling about killing had yet been sent down by Allah, which, if correct, would place the incident before the revelation of the Torah. In any case, the killer was unknown, and the Israelites asked Mūsā to pray to Allah to reveal him, or, as another account has it, simply asked him how the culprit could be traced. Under Allah's command, he told them to sacrifice a cow.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow"**. The command, as first given, meant any cow at all. Had they simply obeyed, as Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿUbaydah said, slaughtering any cow would have been enough.

**"Are you mocking us?"**. Instead of hurrying to obey, they answered stubbornly, asking whether he was making fun of them by answering a question about a murder with an order about a cow.

**"I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!"** means I take refuge with Allah from being one of the ignorant (al-jāhilīn), those who indulge in mockery. God forbid, Mūsā is saying, that he should ever mock the believers, or be counted among the ignorant who lie about Allah and ridicule people. A prophet does not joke about Allah's commands.

### How the story ended

Since the following verses dwell on the Israelites' questions, it helps to know where the story goes. Had they not quibbled, any cow would have served, but because they quibbled the matter was made harder for them, until they were sent looking for one particular cow. They found it with a man who swore he would sell it for nothing less than its hide filled with gold, and they paid that price. They slaughtered the cow and struck the dead man with a piece of it. He stood up, and when they asked him who had killed him he said, "that man", pointing to his nephew, and then died again. The nephew was denied his inheritance, and from then on anyone who murdered in order to inherit was barred from inheriting. The Israelites' habit of raising doubts and objections is on full display in the exchanges that follow, and the hadith notes that had they obeyed without so many questions, no strict conditions would have been laid on them.

### The cow as a mirror

The devotional reading treats the story of the cow and the listing of its qualities as one of the subtle wisdoms and precious pearls of the Qur'an, which is an all-encompassing ocean with kingly pearls and night-brightening gems in its depths. The reader is invited to take all that the Exalted Lord says here about the Israelites' cow as an allusion to attributes of his own. Whoever does so becomes worthy to dive into that ocean and is given access to its hidden wonders and unseen pearls. Three attributes are set out in the verses: first, the cow is neither old nor young, second, it is golden and bright in colour, and third, it is not broken in to plough the earth. The first describes the seeker whose feet go straight on the circle of the Ṭarīqah once the intoxication and greed of youth no longer veil him and the weakness of old age does not yet hamper him. Revelation reached al-Muṣṭafā ﷺ when he was neither a raw youth nor had reached the most feeble stage of life that the Qur'an describes (16:70), and had there been a more complete state, revelation would have come at that time. When longing for God is joined to the intoxication of youth, there is fear of highwaymen on the road, and it is rare for a young man in his first ardour to pass them safely. That is why al-Muṣṭafā ﷺ said that your Lord marvels at a young man who has no youthful recklessness. The second attribute describes the seeker who steps onto the field of the Ṭarīqah in the fullness of his nature and stands straight on it: Unity dyes him in the colour of love, and the colour of love is colourlessness, washing out every mixture, as the Qur'an promises that Allah will strip away whatever rancour is in the breasts of the people of Paradise (7:43). The third attribute is taken up under verse 71.

### In short

- An Israelite was murdered, by his nephew for the inheritance according to the main report, and the people asked Mūsā to find the killer.
- Allah's answer was to sacrifice a cow, and any cow would have done had they obeyed at once.
- They accused Mūsā of mocking them, and he took refuge in Allah from the foolishness of joking about His command.
- Their endless questions made the task hard, until they paid a cow's hide full of gold for the one cow that fitted, and the dead man rose to name his killer.
- The cow's three attributes are read inwardly as the marks of a true seeker.

<<<VERSE 2:68>>>
Verse 68 begins the Israelites' interrogation. They said, "Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type" of cow it should be, and Mūsā (Moses) relayed the answer: "The cow should neither be old nor young but in between. So do as you are commanded!"

### Phrase by phrase

**"Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type of cow it should be"**. Once they realised that Mūsā was in earnest, they asked him to pray to his Lord to explain the nature of the cow, whether, as one commentary puts it, it should be large or small. Allah mentions here the obstinacy of the Children of Israel and the many needless questions they put to their messengers, and because they were obstinate He made the ruling harder on them. Had they slaughtered any cow it would have sufficed, as Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿUbaydah said, but they made the matter difficult, and so Allah made it more difficult for them.

**"The cow should neither be old nor young but in between"**. The Arabic says neither fāriḍ, an aged cow, nor bikr, a young one that has not yet calved, but ʿawān, in between. This is the explanation of Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, al-Ḥasan, aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, Wahb ibn Munabbih, ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī, ʿIkrimah, Mujāhid, as-Suddī and Abū al-ʿĀliyah. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported Ibn ʿAbbās adding that she was at the age when a cow is at its strongest and fittest, neither old nor young.

**"So do as you are commanded!"** means carry out the sacrifice, and ask no further questions.

### The seeker's age

In the devotional reading, the Israelites were bound to carry out the command directly, but they frittered away their time on outward details, imagining that they could afford to linger in digressions from the duty their covenant imposed. Their misery was doubled as a result, and the disgrace they had been warned of overtook them. The description "neither old nor young, but in between" is read as an allusion to the person suited to the spiritual path, the Ṭarīqah: someone neither swept along by the impetuosity and drunkenness of youth nor paralysed by the weakness and frailty of age, but alert, sobered from his intoxication while still keeping some of the vigour of youth.

### In short

- Taking Mūsā seriously at last, the Israelites began asking for specifications instead of obeying.
- The first answer: a cow in the prime of life, neither aged nor too young to have calved.
- Each needless question narrowed the field and made the duty harder, which is the pattern of the whole passage.
- Inwardly, the "in between" cow pictures the seeker who has outgrown youthful recklessness without yet losing his strength.

<<<VERSE 2:69>>>
Verse 69 records the second question. They said, "Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour", and the answer came: "It should be a bright yellow cow", pleasing to those who look at it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour"**. Still debating and still stubborn, they asked what colour the cow should be.

**"It should be a bright yellow cow, pleasant to see"**. The Arabic describes a yellow cow whose colour is fāqiʿ, intense. One commentary calls it golden, a very deep yellow. Al-ʿAwfī reported Ibn ʿAbbās describing the colour as a deep yellow with a whitish cast. As-Suddī explained "pleasant to see" as pleasing to anyone who looks at it, which is also the view of Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Qatādah and ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas. Wahb ibn Munabbih said that if you looked at the cow's hide you would think the rays of the sun shone out through it. One early commentary gives a different picture, a cow with yellow hooves and horns and a black body, and a light colour overall. The modern text of the Torah says the cow was red, but scholars regard this as an error, unless the cow was so deeply yellow that it looked blackish or reddish. Allah knows best.

### Those who remind others of God

The devotional reading connects the cow's striking colour, which no eye could miss, with certain people in the story of the spiritual path whose very presence overwhelms the hearts of others, because they have been clothed in the robe of divine invincibility (jabarūt) and raised up from witnessing the unseen. Such a person is seen to be heedless of ordinary human states and overtaken by the remembrance of the Real. Hence the tradition describing God's friends as people whose very sight brings God to mind.

### In short

- The second specification: a cow of intense, radiant yellow that delights the eye, so bright, in Wahb's words, that the sun seemed to shine through its hide.
- The later Torah's "red heifer" is judged a mistake, or a description of a yellow so deep it looked red.
- The cow's unmissable colour is read as a figure for God's friends, whose sight reminds people of God.
<<<END>>>

<<<VERSE 2:70>>>
Verse 70 brings the third question, and with it a small saving grace. They said, "Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow", adding by way of excuse, "for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided".

### Phrase by phrase

**"Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow"**. The Israelites persisted in their obstinacy and asked Mūsā (Moses) to have Allah describe the cow still further, this time asking, as scholars understand it, whether it grazed freely or was used for labour, whether it ploughed the soil or not.

**"for all cows look the same to us"**. Their excuse was that cows answering the description so far were plentiful and they could not tell one from another, so they had not managed to find the one intended and needed more detail.

**"Then, Allah willing, we will be guided"**. If you describe it further, they said, we shall find it, or, on another reading, we shall be guided to the right description. One early commentary records the view that they meant being guided to the killer, naming the murdered man as ʿĀmīl. A hadith notes the importance of the proviso they added: had they not said in shāʾa Llāh, "if Allah wills", the matter would never have been made clear to them.

### The cost of hair-splitting

A modern commentator draws the lesson of these exchanges. One of the Israelites' recurring mistakes was to destroy the simplicity of divine religion by entangling themselves in complicated theological wrangling. They needed to learn to understand God's will in a straightforward way and obey it at once. The murder case became the occasion for teaching them that hair-splitting over the exact scope of a divine command only makes things difficult for everyone, and that piling on conditions complicates the carrying out of what began as a simple instruction.

### In short

- The third question asked whether the cow was a working animal, on the excuse that too many cows fitted the description.
- Their words "Allah willing" were, according to a hadith, the reason the matter was finally made clear to them.
- The passage teaches that religion is simple until people complicate it with needless questions.

<<<VERSE 2:71>>>
Verse 71 gives the final specification and the reluctant obedience that followed. Mūsā (Moses) relayed Allah's words: "It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish". They said, "Now you have come with the truth". And the verse closes with a telling remark: "Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly".

### Phrase by phrase

**"It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields"**. The Arabic describes a cow that is not dhalūl, not broken in or subdued for labour, neither for turning the earth in ploughing nor for drawing water to irrigate the tilled land. It was not a farm animal at all but an honoured and handsome creature, unyoked and unfettered. Grammatically, the clause about ploughing describes the word dhalūl and forms part of the negation.

**"wholesome"** translates musallamah. ʿAbd ar-Razzāq related from Maʿmar that Qatādah explained it as a cow free of any defect, and Abū al-ʿĀliyah, ar-Rabīʿ and Mujāhid said the same. ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī specified that its legs and body were free of physical faults. One commentary adds that it bore none of the marks of toil.

**"and without blemish"**, lā shiyata fīhā, means that it carried no patch of any colour other than its own yellow, no spots or flecks of white.

**"They said, 'Now you have come with the truth'"** means now you have explained it clearly, now we know the cow's proper description. The words carry an unintended confession, since Mūsā had been bringing them the truth from the first word.

**"Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly"**. The Arabic says they slaughtered it "and they nearly did not do it". They searched for the cow and found it, according to one account, in the possession of a boy who was exceptionally dutiful to his mother, and they bought it for its weight in gold, or, in the other report, for its hide filled with gold. ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān ibn Zayd ibn Aslam, Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Wahb ibn Munabbih, Mujāhid and ʿUbaydah all said that the Jews bought it for a very large sum, though there is a difference of opinion about the details. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported Ibn ʿAbbās explaining the final clause simply: they did not want to slaughter it. Even after all the questions and answers they were still reluctant, and the Qur'an criticises them for it, because their whole aim had been obstinacy. Some commentators attribute the hesitation to the enormous price, others to the arguing and stubbornness that had marked the affair from the start. A hadith sums the passage up: any cow they sacrificed would have been enough for them, but they were hard on themselves, and so Allah was hard on them.

### The cow that was never yoked

The devotional reading completes its account of the three attributes. Just as this cow was never broken in for work nor worn down in the pursuit of profit, and bore no colour inconsistent with the grandeur of its own, so the people of divine friendship (walāyah) exert themselves for no one and nothing other than God. They do not try to obtain things through secondary causes, do not rest their hearts on outward appearances and images, and do not trust their own choices and schemes. Desire cannot claim them as its prey, nor can the present world catch them in its claws. Carnal appetite does not rule them and no human authority controls them. They make no effort to grasp what they want and are not anxious to reach what they wish for. No mark of others is on them and no trace of reliance on causes, for they are upheld by God and annihilated from everything else, effaced, turned this way and that by God alone. God has conquered their hearts. Just as the one they worship (maʿbūd) is God, so their only aim (maqṣūd) is God. As their aim is God, so what they witness (mashhūd) is God and what they find (mawjūd) is God. Rather, they are effaced in God and God alone remains after them. Their spokesman put it in verse: if you want both of us to be pleased and want to hold our shared life's reins, then see the world with my eyes, hear with my ears and speak with my tongue. As for "they nearly did not do it", the Israelites looked for a way out for themselves, and only when their devices failed did they submit to the decree and find relief from the burden of the demands. Had they done what they were told at the outset, their misery would not have been doubled.

### In short

- The last specification: a cow never put to ploughing or watering, free of every defect and of any patch of a second colour.
- "Now you have come with the truth" was an admission that Mūsā had been truthful all along.
- They found the cow, paid a fortune for it, and still barely brought themselves to slaughter it, out of obstinacy and, some say, because of the price.
- The hadith's verdict: any cow would have sufficed, but they made it hard on themselves and Allah made it hard on them.
- Inwardly, the unyoked, unblemished cow pictures God's friends, who labour for nothing but Him.
