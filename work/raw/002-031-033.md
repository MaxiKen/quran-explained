<<<VERSE 2:31>>>
Allah had told the angels, "I know what you do not know." This verse begins the demonstration that proved it. "He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, 'Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?'" The angels had assumed that no creature could outrank them in knowledge, and that beings who glorify and sanctify Allah without pause were better suited to be His vicegerents than a creature who would shed blood. Instead of simply overruling them, Allah set a test that showed them, in a way they could not dispute, that Ādam (Adam) possessed a kind of knowledge they lacked. The verse therefore states the virtue of Ādam over the angels: Allah taught him, and not them, the names of everything. One view holds that this teaching actually took place after the angels prostrated to him, and that it is mentioned first only to underline the importance of his rank and to show how little the angels knew about the vicegerent when they asked about him.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He taught Adam the names of all things"** is explained in several ways, which build on one another. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported that Ibn ʿAbbās said it means the words people use for things: human, animal, sky, earth, land, sea, horse, donkey, and so on through every other kind of creature. Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Ibn Jarīr reported through ʿĀṣim ibn Kulayb, from Saʿīd ibn Maʿbad, that Ibn ʿAbbās was asked whether Allah taught Ādam the names of the plate and the pot, and he answered, "Yes, and even the words for breaking wind." So Allah taught Ādam the proper names of everything, the names of their qualities, and the words for what they do, down to the homeliest words in the language. Others say the names were those of all his descendants, or of all the animals and other things including a cooking pot, a plate and a saucer. Taken together, the teaching covered living things and objects, their pronunciation and their meanings, and the knowledge was placed directly into his heart. The Arabic is doubly emphatic: "names" is a general term that already implies comprehensiveness, and "all of them" adds that nothing was left out and that each meaning was fully verified.

A hadith confirms that the teaching embraced the names of all creatures. In his Ṣaḥīḥ, in the Book of Tafsīr, al-Bukhārī explained this verse with a report from Anas ibn Mālik that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ described the great intercession on the Day of Resurrection. The believers will gather and agree to seek someone to intercede for them with their Lord. They will come to Ādam and say: you are the father of the whole human race, Allah created you with His own Hand, had the angels prostrate to you and taught you the names of everything, so will you not intercede for us so that we are relieved of this standing place? Ādam will remember his error, feel ashamed, and tell them he is not the one for this. He will send them to Nūḥ (Noah), the first messenger Allah sent to the people of the earth. Nūḥ will recall having asked Allah about what he had no knowledge of, feel ashamed, and send them to Khalīl ar-Raḥmān, the intimate friend of the Most Merciful, Ibrāhīm (Abraham). Ibrāhīm will send them to Mūsā (Moses), the servant whom Allah addressed directly and to whom He gave the Tawrāh. Mūsā will remember having killed a man without right and will send them to ʿĪsā (Jesus), Allah's servant and messenger, His Word and a spirit from Him. ʿĪsā will send them to Muḥammad ﷺ, a servant whose earlier and later errors have been forgiven. The Prophet ﷺ said that he will then go to Allah, ask His permission, fall in prostration when he sees his Lord, and remain so as long as Allah wills, until he is told: Muḥammad, raise your head, ask and you will be given, intercede and your intercession will be accepted. He will praise Allah with praise that Allah inspires in him, intercede, and be granted a number of people to admit to Paradise, and he will return and do the same a second, a third and a fourth time, until he says that no one remains in Hell except those whom the Qur'an has confined there for eternity. Muslim, an-Nasāʾī and Ibn Mājah also recorded this hadith. The part that matters here is the believers' words to Ādam, "Allah taught you the names of everything," which show that the names were the names of all created things.

**"then He presented them to the angels"** means that He displayed the objects themselves, the things that had been named, before the angels. ʿAbdur-Razzāq narrated from Maʿmar that Qatādah said that Allah paraded the objects in front of them. Most of the things named were intelligent beings, which is why the Arabic uses the pronoun for rational beings. On the reading that the names were those of Ādam's descendants, the scene is this: Allah brought the whole progeny of Ādam before their first father and said to the angels, in effect, "These are the children of Ādam. Can you name each of them and say what sort of people they will be?" The word "then" (thumma) marks a pause or interval between the teaching and the presentation.

**"and said, 'Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?'"** was said in reproach. The point was to prove the angels wrong, and the question is directed at the very angels who had asked, "Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood?" It is as if Allah said: you asked whether the vicegerent would be from among you or from some other creation, and you pointed to your praise and glorification. If you speak the truth in claiming that a non-angel vicegerent and his offspring will disobey Me, cause corruption and shed blood, while you, if I made you the vicegerents, would obey Me, carry out My commands and glorify Me, then tell Me the names of what I have set before you. But since you do not know even the names of these objects that are present before you, you have still less knowledge of what will happen on an earth that does not yet exist. "If what you say is true" refers to their first claim, their belief that Allah would not create anything more knowledgeable than they were, or that they were more deserving of the vicegerency, or that they were a nobler and better creation than Ādam. The answer to the conditional "if" is understood from what precedes it.

### Who gave language to human beings?

Imām al-Ashʿarī drew a conclusion from this episode: language as such was created by Allah Himself and not invented by man, and its use by different peoples later produced the many forms of language. The first speaker learned his words from his Maker.

### The names of created things and the names of the Real

Some scholars find a second layer in the verse. Just as Allah taught Ādam the names of created things, all of them, as Ibn ʿAbbās and others explain, He also taught him the names of the Real, that is, knowledge of Allah's own names and attributes. To the angels, however, He disclosed only the place He had chosen for knowledge of the names of created things, and through that disclosure the measure of Ādam's superiority over them. Ādam's uniqueness in knowing the names of Allah was a secret of which no angel, however near to Allah, was aware. If a being does not even match Ādam in knowing the names of created things, what hope has it of drawing near to the names of the Real or of grasping the unseen's secrets? And if being chosen to know the names of created things made it fitting for the angels to prostrate before him, what must be thought of being chosen to know the names of the Real, and what is owed to the one honoured with that?

On this view the special excellence of the angels lies in glorification and sanctification. Those are acts of obedience, and obedience befits created beings, since it is the mark of servants and of servants alone. Knowledge, by contrast, is a form of praise that is necessarily true of the Real and belongs properly to no one else, and among created beings none honours Allah more perfectly than the one who honours Him through Allah's own explanation of His attributes. It is also said that Allah first honoured Ādam privately with what He taught him, and then made His choice of him and his precedence public on the day he was presented to the angels.

### What the angels learned about human beings

If the things presented were the descendants of Ādam, then the demonstration also answered the angels' worry about corruption and bloodshed. When Ādam, taught by Allah, explained to the angels the names and characters of the human race, they realised that besides the wicked and the corrupt there would be great, righteous and pious souls among them. The mixture of good and evil in humanity, which had alarmed them, turned out to include the very people for whose sake the whole enterprise was worthwhile.

### In short

- To prove that He knew what the angels did not, Allah taught Ādam the names of all things, from the greatest creatures to the plate, the pot and the coarsest everyday words, then set the things before the angels and challenged them to name them.
- The challenge answered the angels' assumption that no being could surpass them in knowledge or deserve the vicegerency more than they did.
- The great intercession hadith (al-Bukhārī, Muslim, an-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah) confirms that Ādam was taught the names of everything.
- Imām al-Ashʿarī concluded that language itself is Allah's creation, and the Sufis add that Ādam was also given knowledge of the names of the Real.

<<<VERSE 2:32>>>
Confronted with things they could not name, the angels did not argue. "They replied, 'Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.'" The verse records how perfectly obedient creatures respond when their understanding is shown to be limited: with praise first, then an admission, then an affirmation of Allah's knowledge and wisdom.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Glory be to You!"** declares Allah's holiness and His perfection above every deficiency. In context it carries several meanings at once. The angels exalt Allah above any objection anyone might raise to Him. They repent of what they had said earlier. And they acknowledge that His judgment and His sacred law cannot be doubted. They offered this praise before they made their apology, and by it they affirmed the rightness of His decree over any objection of their own. Their words also clear Allah of any suggestion that He had wronged them by asking the incapable to do what He knew they could not do, for the question had been put in order to teach, not to blame.

**"We have no knowledge except what You have taught us"** is the admission. No creature acquires any share of Allah's knowledge except by His permission, and none knows anything except what He teaches or inspires. The angels recognised their shortcoming and recognised that everything comes from Allah. They were saying: we have no knowledge of what You have asked us about, and You taught Ādam what You did not teach us.

**"You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise"** ends the reply with two names. The Arabic innaka anta is emphatic, "You, You alone." Allah knows everything about the angels and about the things presented, and nothing escapes His knowledge. He is Wise in His decrees and laws, in the angels' affairs and in the affairs of human beings, and He makes the wisest decisions, granting knowledge to whom He wills and withholding it from whom He wills. Whatever He does is true and right, no one can pass judgment over Him, and nothing foolish or ugly comes from Him. His wisdom and justice in every matter are perfect.

### What the angels' reply shows

The angels' earlier question, as the previous verses explained, was a humble inquiry and an offer of service, not a protest. Their reply here proves it. The moment another being was shown to be more suitable for the task by his special kind of knowledge, they acknowledged the fact just as humbly and withdrew their opinion. The phrase "Glory be to You" also implies that Allah is free of any charge of having withheld from the angels the knowledge He gave to Ādam, because, being All-Knowing and All-Wise, He gives each creature the kind and degree of knowledge and understanding that suits its particular nature, and He alone knows what that is.

### In short

- The angels answered their failure to name the things with praise, repentance and an admission that their knowledge is only what Allah has taught them.
- Their reply affirms that Allah's judgment and law are beyond objection and that His knowledge and wisdom are perfect.
- It models the right response to being corrected: no argument, no resentment, only glorification and surrender.

<<<VERSE 2:33>>>
The test now reaches its conclusion. "Allah said, 'O Adam! Inform them of their names.'" Once Adam names them, Allah reminds the angels that His knowledge embraces the hidden realities of heaven and earth. It also embraces both what they disclose and what they keep inwardly. Ādam (Adam) does what the angels could not, his superiority in knowledge becomes plain, and Allah reminds the angels of His earlier answer, "I know what you do not know," adding that He knows not only the unseen in the heavens and the earth but also what the angels had said aloud and what they had kept to themselves.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Allah said, 'O Adam! Inform them of their names'"** turns the demonstration over to Ādam. Zayd ibn Aslam said that Ādam told the angels, "You are Jibrīl, you are Mīkāʾīl, you are Isrāfīl," and so on, until he came to the name of the crow. Mujāhid said he gave the name of the pigeon, the crow and everything else, and similar explanations are reported from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, al-Ḥasan and Qatādah. He named each thing by its proper name and also mentioned the wisdom behind its creation. On the reading that the things presented were his descendants, he passed on to the angels what Allah had taught him about the nature of the human race.

There is a subtle point in the choice of words. When Allah asked the angels, He said, "Tell Me." When He commanded Ādam, He said, "Tell them." The difference shows that Ādam stood as a teacher while the angels sat as pupils, which is itself a sign of his superiority. The episode also shows that the knowledge of the angels can increase or decrease, for through Ādam they received at least an elementary knowledge of something they had not known before. Another trace of Allah's kindness to Ādam appears here. When Allah said to the angels, "Tell Me," they were seized with fear of speaking, especially because they were being asked about something their kind of knowledge did not reach. Ādam, because of what he knew, was directed to address the angels, and he was not overcome by fear in speaking to them.

**"Then when Adam did, Allah said, 'Did I not tell you that I know the secrets of the heavens and the earth'"** is spoken in rebuke once Ādam's merit had become evident. It means: did I not say that I know the seen and the unseen alike, everything hidden in the heavens and on the earth, and everything that will take place in them? It also means that Allah knows the different kinds of knowledge of created beings, which the angels do not. Allah says elsewhere, "Whether you speak openly or not, He certainly knows what is secret and what is even more hidden" (20:7), and He reports the words of the hoopoe to Sulaymān (Solomon) about those who do not prostrate to Allah, "Who brings forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth, and knows what you all conceal and what you reveal. He is Allah! There is no god worthy of worship except Him, the Lord of the Mighty Throne" (27:25-26).

**"and I know what you reveal and what you conceal"** has been explained in several ways. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reported that Ibn ʿAbbās said it means: I know secrets just as I know what is apparent, such as the arrogance and pride that Iblīs concealed in his heart. Abū Jaʿfar ar-Rāzī narrated that ar-Rabīʿ ibn Anas said that what the angels revealed was their question, "Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood?" while what they concealed was the thought, "We have more knowledge and honour than any creation our Lord will create," and they then learned that Allah had favoured Ādam over them in both knowledge and honour. Others say that what they revealed was the obedience they showed their Lord in bowing to Ādam and what they hid was the inward side of that obedience, or that the verse refers to what Satan disclosed and what he kept hidden from them. One further reading is that Allah knows the acts of obedience the angels displayed and the belief in their own superiority over Ādam that they kept hidden.

### Allah's care and Allah's decree

The whole episode shows Allah's solicitude for Ādam. When the Real wished to choose him, He guarded him, instructed him, and let him see the effects of His care, until Ādam could say what he said about the names. Yet when Allah wished to carry out His decree concerning him, He let forgetfulness enter him, so that he forgot the covenant made in Allah's presence and overstepped His limit: "And indeed, We once made a covenant with Adam, but he forgot, and so We did not find determination in him" (20:115). The moment favoured by divine care brought knowledge and goodness to their fullness, while the moment when the decree took effect returned him to forgetfulness and disobedience. Such are the decrees of the Real in what they bring and what they take away. The servant is humbled by His decree, and He is the "Doer of whatever He wills" (85:16), for "surely your Lord does what He intends" (11:107).

The angels had imagined that their glorification and sanctification would win them preference. Allah let them know that the carpet of His majesty is too holy to be adorned by the obedience of the obedient or stained by the errors of stubborn deniers. Their return to prostration before Ādam made plain that Allah has no need of the agreement or the opposition of any creature.

### Corruption and bloodshed

Since the angels' question about corruption and bloodshed hangs over these verses, one lesson about human conduct follows. After denial of one's Lord, humanity's greatest crimes are spreading corruption and shedding blood. No individual and no group has God's permission to do anything that disrupts the order of nature He established, and no one may take another's life. Actions of this kind disqualify people from God's mercy. To preserve the system of nature is to reform the earth, and to upset it is what the Qur'an calls spreading corruption.

### In short

- Ādam named everything the angels could not, from Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl and Isrāfīl down to the crow and the pigeon, and so his superiority in knowledge was proved.
- "Tell Me" to the angels and "tell them" to Ādam made Ādam the teacher and the angels the pupils.
- Allah reminded the angels that He knows the unseen of the heavens and the earth and also what they had said and what they had silently thought, including the pride hidden in Iblīs.
- Ādam's knowledge came from Allah's care, and his later lapse came from Allah's decree (20:115). Both show that Allah needs neither the agreement nor the opposition of His creatures.
