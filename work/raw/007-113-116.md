<<<VERSE 7:113>>>
Pharaoh's summons went out, and the magicians arrived from all over the country: "The magicians came to Pharaoh, saying, “Shall we receive a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?”" Allah describes the exchange between Pharaoh and the magicians he gathered to defeat Mūsā (Moses).

### Phrase by phrase

**"The magicians came to Pharaoh"**: Pharaoh (Firʿawn) sent people to call the magicians, and they came. One explanation puts their number at 70. Historical reports differ widely on how many there were, giving figures from nine hundred to three hundred thousand. The staffs and ropes used in the contest are said to have filled three hundred camel-loads.

**"saying, “Shall we receive a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?”"** They asked whether he would give them a reward, a gift, if they won and beat Mūsā with their magic. In the Arabic, "shall" (a-inna) may be read with both hamzahs pronounced, or with the second softened and an alif inserted between them.

### What does their first question reveal?

The first thing the magicians did was to secure a promise of payment for their performance. Worldly people are always after worldly gain, and they do nothing until they are sure of their profit. The prophets and their followers, by contrast, always declare, "I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds" (26:109). The prophets of Allah preach and deliver His message only for people's good and guidance, and seek no payment for it. They look for their reward from Allah alone.

After this exchange with Pharaoh, the time and place of the great contest were fixed. An open piece of land was chosen, and the time was set for after sunrise on their festival day, as Mūsā had said (20:59).

### In short

- The magicians gathered from all over Egypt, in numbers the reports give anywhere from 70 to many thousands.
- Their first concern was payment, the mark of worldly people. The prophets ask no reward from anyone but Allah.
- The contest was set for after sunrise on the festival day, in an open place.

<<<VERSE 7:114>>>
Pharaoh answered the magicians generously: "He replied, “Yes, and you will certainly be among those closest to me.”" He promised them a reward and great provision. He made them hope for whatever they wished, and to be among his inner circle and closest companions.

### Phrase by phrase

**"He replied, “Yes"**: yes, you will have your reward and a gift.

**"and you will certainly be among those closest to me.”"** On top of your reward, you will be brought near to me in rank and given important positions.

### Did Mūsā speak to the magicians first?

Some reports say Mūsā (Moses) had a short conversation with the chief of the magicians. He asked whether they would accept the faith if he defeated them. The chief replied that there was no question of their being defeated, for they had such great magical powers that no one could overcome them. Yet, he said, if Mūsā did defeat them, they would declare their faith openly before Pharaoh.

### What happened once they were reassured?

Once the magicians were assured of the cursed Pharaoh's promises, they turned to the contest itself, as the next verse tells. They came confident of victory and of the rich rewards Pharaoh had dangled before them.

### In short

- Pharaoh promised the magicians not only a reward but a place among those closest to him.
- Reports say their chief told Mūsā they would openly believe if he defeated them.
- Reassured of their reward, they went into the contest sure of winning.
<<<END>>>

<<<VERSE 7:115>>>
On the day of the encounter, the magicians challenged Mūsā (Moses): "They asked, “O Moses! Will you cast, or shall we be the first to cast?”" Proud and certain of winning, they offered him the choice.

### Phrase by phrase

**"They asked, “O Moses! Will you cast"**: the Arabic word ilqāʾ means to drop something down. Will you throw your staff first? Choose, O Mūsā, whether you want to be the first to throw.

**"or shall we be the first to cast?”"** Or shall we throw what we have before you? In another verse they say, "O Moses! Either you cast, or let us be the first to cast" (20:65).

### What lay behind their question?

The question was perhaps meant to show how sure they were of their art. The way they worded it suggests that they wanted to go first. But to give the impression of strength to their opponent, they put it as a question. Since Mūsā had nothing to fear, he invited them to throw first.

### In short

- At the contest, the magicians offered Mūsā the choice of throwing first or letting them begin.
- Their wording showed their wish to start and their pride in their art.
<<<END>>>

<<<VERSE 7:116>>>
Mūsā (Moses) let them go first: "Moses said, “You first.” So when they did, they deceived the eyes of the people, stunned them, and made a great display of magic". Trusting in Allah to give him victory, he was not worried about them, and told them to throw their ropes and staffs.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Moses said, “You first.”"** Throw first whatever you are going to throw. This was a command permitting them to begin, as a means of bringing the truth to light.

**"So when they did"**: when they threw their ropes and staffs. One explanation says they threw 70 staffs and 70 ropes. Ibn ʿAbbās said they threw thick ropes and long sticks, which seemed to be crawling, an illusion produced by their magic.

**"they deceived the eyes of the people"**: literally "they bewitched the people's eyes". They deceived the eyes and made people think the trick was real, when it was only an illusion. They kept people from seeing the true state of those ropes and staffs. Things looked different from what they really were.

**"stunned them"**: they frightened and overawed the people, making the ropes and staffs look like slithering snakes.

**"and made a great display of magic"**: they produced a mighty sorcery. One explanation calls it a plain lie.

### Why did Mūsā let them go first?

The wisdom of letting them throw first may have been this, and Allah knows best: the people would see the magicians' sorcery first. Then, once the magicians had cast their spell and captured every eye, the clear and undeniable truth would come, just when everyone was waiting for it, and strike their hearts all the more. That is exactly what happened. It has also been said that Mūsā's courtesy in giving them the first turn had its effect, for they accepted the faith after their defeat.

A question arises here. Sorcery is forbidden, and when it is used to oppose a prophet of Allah it becomes disbelief. How then could Mūsā give the magicians permission by saying "You first"? A little thought gives the answer. It was certain that the magicians would show their magic in any case. The only question was who would begin, and Mūsā let them begin. This had a further advantage. The people could watch their whole performance while the magicians had all the time they wanted to make their staffs look like snakes. Then Mūsā's staff would become a serpent and swallow all their snakes, so that the defeat of magic would be displayed openly before everyone.

### Why does a prophet not strike first?

Prophets are given miracles suited to the mindset of their contemporaries. In ancient Egypt, magic held people's minds captive, so Mūsā was given a miracle that would outdo magic. As Pharaoh had planned, all the great magicians gathered at the national festival. When they asked Mūsā who should begin, he asked them to show their magic first, and they agreed.

This shows that a prophet never takes the initiative in moving against his enemies. Up to the last moment he leaves his opponents the chance to start hostilities. Only once they have taken that responsibility on themselves does he use every power at his command to overcome them. When it comes to setting out the teachings of Islam, the prophet is the one who begins. Where the first move could mean an attack, he never makes it.

### Was it real change or only illusion?

Allah describes what they did elsewhere:

> "So Moses concealed fear within himself. We reassured ˹him˺, “Do not fear! It is certainly you who will prevail. Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go.”" (20:67-69).

This verse shows that their display was only a bewitching of people's eyes. It made them see the staffs and ropes as snakes leaping on the ground, while the ropes and staffs did not change at all. It was a kind of mesmerism that took hold of the onlookers' minds.

That does not mean sorcery is limited to this kind, or that it can never change one thing into another, for there is no proof either way. Many forms of magic have been taken as real. The words magic and sorcery are usually applied to anything that seems to happen in an unusual way. Sleight of hand, telepathic influence and mesmerism are sometimes called magic. Turning one thing into another by magic may be possible, but there is no sound reason or proof for or against it.

### In short

- Mūsā let the magicians throw first, and their ropes and staffs seemed to become slithering snakes.
- It was an illusion cast on the eyes. The ropes did not change, though people were deceived and frightened.
- Letting them begin let everyone see the magic fully before the truth overwhelmed it, and his courtesy is said to have helped win them to faith.
- A prophet never starts hostilities. He lets opponents begin, then overcomes them with what Allah gives him.
