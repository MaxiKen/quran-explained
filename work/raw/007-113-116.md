### Phrase by phrase

**"The magicians came to Pharaoh"** records the gathering that the summons through the provinces had produced. They were seventy in number by one count, brought in from the towns and the districts of the kingdom, and they came to the king at his call.

**"saying, “Shall we receive a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?”"** is what they said when they arrived. The question is a plain request for a wage, and the words put it as a condition, that there would be a gift for them if they came out of the contest as the victors.

Pharaoh met them with what he could offer without giving away anything of his own. He told them that they would have their reward, and that he would give them tremendous provisions. He set that hope before them, and he added the promise of a place among his private audience and his closest associates, which in a court of that kind carried more weight than the money did.

What their opening words say is that the outcome was to them a matter of business. The day set for the meeting was the day of the festival, with the people gathered when the sun had risen (20:59). One report adds a conversation that took place before the contest. Musa spoke with the chief of the sorcerers and asked him whether they would accept the faith if he defeated them. The man answered that there was no question of their being defeated, since their powers were such that no one could overcome them, and he added that if the outcome went the other way, they would declare their faith openly in the presence of Pharaoh. The bargain struck with the king and the wager offered by the messenger were both on the table before a single staff had been thrown.

### In short

- The magicians gathered at Pharaoh's call, and one count gives their number as seventy.
- Their first words were a bargain, since they asked what reward would be theirs if they won.
- Pharaoh promised them a reward, generous provision, and a place among those closest to him.
- One report has Musa asking their chief beforehand whether a defeat would bring them to the faith.
- The chief answered that defeat was not a thing he could imagine, and that if it came they would declare their faith openly before the king.

<<<VERSE 7:114>>>
The king's answer is given in one short sentence. "He replied, “Yes, and you will certainly be among those closest to me.”"

### Phrase by phrase

**"He replied, “Yes"** settles the bargain without any haggling. He confirmed the reward they had asked about, and the promise was given in full.

**"and you will certainly be among those closest to me"** goes past the money. The words mean that they would belong to the circle brought near, and the Book describes the whole exchange in a few sentences. In another account the promise was put in the same terms, that they would surely be among those brought near (26:109). The point for the record is the weight of the offer and the smallness of what it was offered for, since a court place and a private standing were being used to buy a verdict in a matter of truth.

**What his promises were for.** Pharaoh had collected the magicians to defeat Musa, and everything he said to them was directed at that end. He told them there would be a reward and gave them large assurances, so that their hopes would be fixed on what they wished for, and he made them among his closest associates and his best companions, so that their standing in the court would be tied to his project. When they were assured of his promises, they went to the contest with the whole of their craft and with the whole of their ambition in the same scale.

The matter was the easier for him to arrange because the land had already decided what it thought of the signs. The people of Pharaoh had given the lie to Musa, had called him a great sorcerer, and had said that he wanted to take the rule of the country from them. So the man who set the reward was the man who had already framed the charge, and the magicians were brought in to confirm a verdict that had been reached before they arrived.

### The sign in the bargain

There is a lesson in the shape of this exchange. The truth came to those people with proofs that no craft could match, and the men who were asked to answer it were offered money and rank to speak against it. The offer worked on them, and the reason it worked is written plainly in the account of what happened afterwards. A person who sets his hope on what a king can give him will meet the clearest proof in the world and still stand against it.

### In short

- Pharaoh granted the reward at once, without any negotiation.
- He added a place among those brought near him, which was worth more in that court than the money.
- The promises were made for one purpose, to secure a victory over Musa.
- He gave them long assurances so that their hopes would be fixed on what they wished for.
- The men who promised the reward had already called Musa a sorcerer and accused him of wanting the kingdom, so the verdict was bought in advance.

<<<VERSE 7:115>>>
The two sides meet, and the courtesy of the messenger gives the first move away. "They asked, “O Moses! Will you cast, or shall we be the first to cast?”"

### Phrase by phrase

**"They asked, “O Moses! Will you cast"** puts the choice to him. The word used here for casting means to drop something down, and the question is about which side would drop what it carried first.

**"or shall we be the first to cast?”"** is the other half of the question, and in another place the same words are given as or we be the first to throw (20:65). Their manner of speaking shows what was in their minds. They were certain they would win, since their pride in their craft had made the result a settled thing in their eyes, and the question was put as one who expects to be answered in his own favour. The wording also shows that they wanted to begin. By putting the choice to their opponent, they kept up the appearance of giving him the honour of the first move, while the flow of the sentence made their own wish plain.

**The size of the thing they were about to throw.** On the day of the meeting they threw down their ropes and their staffs, and the count given in one account is seventy staffs and seventy ropes. The same number is given again in the account of the staffs alone, seventy of them thrown at once. Their hope was that the mass of them, moving together, would settle the matter before Musa had even begun.

**What a prophet does when he is opposed.** The answer Musa gave is treated as a model in the account. A prophet does not take the first step in a hostile action against the people who oppose him. Even at the last moment he gives his opponents the chance to open the fighting, and once they have taken that responsibility on themselves, he uses the power he has been given to bring them down. It is worth putting the two sides of that side by side, since it applies to the message as much as to the contest. In setting out the religion and calling people to it, the prophet is the one who begins, and he takes the first step every time. When the matter turns into an attack, the first move is left to the other side.

### In short

- The magicians put the choice to Musa, of who would drop what they carried first.
- The phrasing shows both their confidence and their wish to begin.
- Their pride in their art had made victory a settled thing in their own minds.
- One count of what they threw comes to seventy staffs and seventy ropes.
- A prophet does not start a fight, and he leaves the first hostile move to the other side, though in calling people to the truth he is always the one who begins.

<<<VERSE 7:116>>>
The messenger gives way, the craft puts on its full display, and then everything is answered. "Moses said, “You first.” So when they did, they deceived the eyes of the people, stunned them, and made a great display of magic"

### Phrase by phrase

**"Moses said, “You first.”"** is the answer, and it is a command that lets them go before him. Musa behaved towards them with courtesy by inviting them to take their turn first, and the effect of that courtesy was that they accepted the faith after they were defeated. So the giving of the first move was not a concession made out of weakness. It was the manner of a man who had nothing to fear.

A question comes up at this point. Sorcery is an unlawful act, and when it is used against a prophet sent by Allah it becomes an act of disbelief. How then did Musa give permission for magic by telling them to go ahead? The answer follows from what the contest was. It was certain that they would put their magic on show by one means or another, and the only matter in question was who would begin. Since the point of the conversation was the order of turns and nothing else, permitting them to go first was no part of the magic itself.

There was an advantage in it besides. The people could watch the performance to the end, and the sorcerers had all the time they needed to work their craft and to turn their staffs into snakes. Only after that was the staff of Musa to become a serpent and swallow the whole of what they had made, so that the defeat of magic stood out openly in front of everyone. The truth came at the very moment the crowd was waiting for it, and that made it more striking to their hearts than it would have been at the start.

**"So when they did, they deceived the eyes of the people, stunned them, and made a great display of magic"** describes what they produced, and the number of things thrown was seventy of the staffs alone. The words say that they cast a spell on the eyes of the people, that they frightened and overawed them, and that they came out with a mighty sorcery, which in one reading means a plain lie. Ibn Abbas said that they threw thick ropes and long sticks, and that these appeared to be crawling, an illusion that their magic created.

**Where the craft could reach and where it could not.** The verse makes plain that the display was a bewitching of the eyes of the onlookers. The people were made to see the staffs and the strings as snakes leaping on the ground, while the things themselves did not change in their nature. It was a form of influence on the minds of those watching. The fear that Musa felt at that moment is recorded in the Book, together with the answer given to him. He was told not to fear, that he would have the upper hand, that he should throw what was in his right hand, that it would swallow up what they had made, and that what they had made was only the trick of a magician, and no magician is successful however much skill he may reach (20:67-69).

None of that settles the question of what magic can do in general. The account here does not prove that sorcery is confined to the deceiving of eyes, and there is no proof against the other forms either. Many kinds of magic are taken to be real in their effects, and the words magic and sorcery are applied to all acts that appear to happen in an unusual way, whether that is sleight of hand, or an influence on a distant mind, or the holding of a gaze. The change of one thing into another by such means may be possible, and no valid evidence has been produced either for it or against it.

### Why the display was allowed to run its course

The whole scene had been arranged so that the contrast could be seen. The sorcerers were given the festival day, the assembled people, and the time to do everything their art could do. Their craft was the pride of Egypt at that time, and the minds of the people were held by it, which is why the miracle Musa was given was of that same field, so that the master of it could not be mistaken for the truth. The sticks and the ropes were turned into what looked like snakes before the eyes of a nation, and then a real serpent opened its mouth and swallowed them on the ground. What the people saw with their own eyes in that hour settled what no argument could have settled, and the men who had been bought with a court place were the first to bow.

### In short

- Musa told them to throw first, and his courtesy towards them was among the things that later brought them to the faith.
- His permission had nothing to do with the magic itself, since the contest was settled and the only question was the order of turns.
- Their display came first so that the defeat of their craft would be open and unmistakable in front of the whole nation.
- What they did was a bewitching of the eyes, since the staffs and ropes only appeared to be snakes, and Ibn Abbas described them as thick ropes and long sticks made to look as though they crawled.
- Fear came over Musa at the sight, and he was told not to fear, that what they had made was a trick, and that no magician succeeds, however great his skill (20:67-69).
- The passage does not decide how far sorcery can really go, and the kinds of it that are taken to have real effects are left undecided for lack of proof either way.
