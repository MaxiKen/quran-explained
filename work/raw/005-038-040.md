<<<VERSE 5:38>>>
"As for male and female thieves, cut off their hands for what they have done—a deterrent from Allah. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise"

This verse sets out the punishment for theft. The material places it among the Hudud, the punishments that the Qur'an itself fixes and that no ruler may change. The verse follows the rules on robbery in 5:33 and 5:34, and it moves from the crime against the road to the crime against property taken in secret. The material notes that the rule is stated as the right of Allah, which is why it is called the Hadd of Sariqah, the punishment for theft.

The material also explains that the rule is harsh only in its outline. Its application is bounded by many conditions, so that in practice the hand is cut off only in a narrow set of cases.

### Phrase by phrase

**"As for male and female thieves."** The material notes that the Qur'an normally addresses men, and that women are understood to be included. In the matter of theft and adultery the text names both sexes separately. The reason given is that a Hadd may be dropped on the slightest doubt, so the Qur'an leaves nothing implied. The material also notes that in the verse on adultery in 24:2 the order is reversed, with the woman first. The reason given there is that a man who steals has many lawful ways to earn, so his theft is the graver fault, while a woman is protected by her natural modesty and by the people around her.

**"Cut off their hands."** The material explains that the cut is made at the wrist, and that the right hand is the one taken. The Sunnah describes a further penalty for repeat offences, with the left foot, then the left hand and the right foot, after which a discretionary punishment applies.

**"For what they have done."** The word translated as the reward is a requital. The material says that the hand is cut because it was the tool used to take what belonged to others. It is fitting that the tool of the wrong is the part that is punished.

**"A deterrent from Allah."** The Arabic word is nakal, a punishment that serves as a lesson to others and holds them back. The material says that the rule is meant to remove the crime from society by making an example of the offender. The phrase "from Allah" marks a second aspect of the crime. Theft is an injustice to the victim, and it is also an act against the command of Allah. Because of the second aspect, the punishment is a Hadd and not a Qisas, a crime against the public order. The victim cannot forgive it, and only Allah can.

**"And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise."** The material reads this closing phrase as an answer to those who call the rule savage. It says that the severity of the rule belongs to the mighty and wise will of Allah, and that its purpose is also wise.

### The limits of the rule

The material sets out the conditions for the Hadd of theft. The first condition is that the property belongs to someone privately. If the thief has a share in it, or if there is doubt about ownership, or if it is shared by the public, the Hadd does not apply. The second condition is that the property is kept in a secure place, such as a locked house or a guarded store. Property left in the open is not covered. A doubt about security also removes the Hadd. The third condition is that the thief has no permission to take the item. A person who was allowed to enter the house and take an item does not fall under the Hadd, even if he steals. The fourth condition is that the theft is done in secret. Open seizure is robbery, which has its own rules.

The material gives examples of thefts that do not carry the Hadd. Items taken from public places such as a mosque, a park or a station are not in a secure place. Fruit hanging on trees and honey are not covered. Pickpocketing, snatching, fraud and breach of trust are all unlawful, but they are punished by a discretionary penalty. Stealing from a grave is also unlawful, but the shroud is not owned by the dead, so the Hadd does not apply. A theft from property held in joint ownership is also not covered, because the thief has a share.

### The minimum value and the hadith

The material gives the hadith on the value of stolen goods, which is the basis for the minimum. Abu Hurayrah reported a saying of the Prophet ﷺ that cursed the thief who steals an egg or a rope and loses his hand over it. A'ishah reported, in the two sahihs and in Muslim's collection, that the hand is cut off only when the theft reaches a quarter of a dinar or more. The material says this hadith fixes the value. Some reports give three dirhams, and the material reconciles them, since a dinar then equalled twelve dirhams, so three dirhams were a quarter of a dinar.

The material records that this view was held by Umar bin al-Khattab, Uthman bin Affan and Ali bin Abi Talib. It also records that Umar bin Abdul-Aziz, al-Layth bin Sa'd, al-Awza'i and Ash-Shafi'i held it, with his companions. Ahmad bin Hanbal and Ishaq bin Rahwayh in one narration, and Abu Thawr, also held it. Dawud al-Zahiri took the same view. The material records that Abu Hanifah and his students Abu Yusuf, Muhammad and Zufar, and Sufyan Ath-Thawri, held that the minimum is ten dirhams. The material prefers the first view as the sounder one, and it says the low limit was set so that people would refrain from theft.

### The woman, the ransom and Makkah

The material says the story of the woman from Makhzum took place during the conquest of Makkah. Az-Zuhri reported it from Urwah from A'ishah, and the two sahihs record it. The Quraysh feared for the woman and sent Usamah, the beloved of the Prophet ﷺ, to speak for her. This is the same narration that is set out in the entry for verse 38 above.

### The Hudud as the law of the land

The material says that the Hudud are part of Islamic law, the shariah, and that the fiqh of the Hudud is set out in the Hanafi books of jurisprudence. It notes that the Hadd of theft is a Hadd and that its conditions are bound up with the rules of the ruler and the judge. The material also notes that the same law governs acts of worship such as salah, sawm, hajj and zakah, but in the case of theft and adultery the Qur'an names both sexes, because the Hadd must be applied only in clear cases.

### Repentance and the path to Allah

The material links repentance here with the path that leads to Allah. Taqwa is the fear of Allah that keeps a person from crime. Jihad is the struggle against the self and against wrong. The material says that a thief who gives up his sin and corrects his life is treading the path back to Allah, and that the path is open to anyone who turns to Him.

### The law of evidence

The material explains that the proof required for a Hadd is stricter than for other matters. For theft two male witnesses are needed, and their testimony must be given promptly. A witness who gives evidence after a long delay is not accepted. The judge cannot accept the testimony of a person known to sin in a Hadd case, even when he might accept it in other matters. Where the proof is incomplete, the punishment falls to the discretionary level.

The material says that the outcome of these conditions is that the Hadd is applied only rarely, because the crime and its proof must both be complete. In the cases where both are found, the punishment is severe, and it serves as a warning that discourages others from the same act.

### The tradition of the woman from Makhzum

The material gives the account that Ahmad, Abdullah bin 'Amr and the two sahihs record in various forms. A woman from the tribe of Makhzum stole during the time of the Prophet ﷺ. Her people asked for a ransom, and the Prophet ﷺ refused and ordered the hand cut off. She asked whether repentance was open to her, and he told her that she was free of her sin that day. The verse on repentance in 5:39 was then revealed. The matter became a concern to the Quraysh, who asked Usamah to intercede. The Prophet ﷺ said that intercession in a punishment prescribed by Allah is not permitted, and he gave a speech in which he warned that earlier nations fell because they punished the weak and let the powerful go. He said that if Fatimah had stolen he would have cut her hand off. A'ishah reported that the woman repented sincerely, married, and came to her with her needs.

### The modern objection

The material responds to those who call the Hudud harsh. It says that in the places where the Hudud are applied, the number of thefts is low, and that shops are left open without fear. It reads the rule as an act of mercy to the many, and it says that showing mercy to a criminal can be cruelty to society. The material also contrasts this with the position of those who object to these rules while holding weapons that have killed many civilians. It adds that God revealed the Hudud alongside the instruction to not show mercy in enforcing them, and that the law of retaliation is described as a life for those who understand. The material ends with a prayer that such people may be guided to the true path of peace.

### In short

- The verse sets out the cutting of the hand for theft as a Hadd, a right of Allah and a deterrent.
- The rule applies only when strict conditions are met, including secure property, private ownership, no permission and secret taking.
- The proof is strictly required, and any doubt replaces the Hadd with a discretionary punishment.

<<<VERSE 5:39>>>
"But whoever repents after their wrongdoing and mends their ways, Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness. Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"

This verse follows the rule on theft and opens a door of repentance. The material compares it with the exception in 5:34 for those who repent before the robbers are taken. The two verses use forgiveness in different ways.

### Phrase by phrase

**"But whoever repents after their wrongdoing and mends their ways."** The material explains that repentance here is a turning away from the sin and a correction of conduct. It is not enough to say sorry. The person must stop stealing and make right what he has done.

**"Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness."** The material says that this forgiveness is in the Hereafter. A thief who repents is forgiven by Allah from the punishment of the next life. The forgiveness does not remove the worldly Hadd. Once the crime is proved before a judge, the ruler cannot drop the punishment because of repentance.

**"Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."** The material links this to the verse on the power of Allah in 2:284, which says He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills.

### The difference from the robbery exception

The material explains that the exception in 5:34 applies to the worldly punishment. A robber who repents before he is overpowered is excused the Hadd. The thief in 5:39 is treated differently. Repentance does not stop the hand from being cut, once the case is proved. It does bring deliverance in the Hereafter. Most jurists agree that the Hadd for robbery is not applied to a robber who repents before arrest. For theft, the material says that a thief who repents after the theft, whether before or after arrest, still faces the Hadd. This is the reading of al-Shafi'i, who held that a thief pardoned before being brought to the ruler escapes the amputation. The material records that once the matter has reached the ruler, the victim's right to restitution and the Hadd remain.

### The path back and the schools

The material says that a person who repents has taken the path of taqwa, which is the fear of Allah, and of jihad, which is the struggle against his own desires. The path is open to any thief who gives up his theft and corrects himself. The material notes that Ash-Shafi'i and other jurists held that a thief who is pardoned before he reaches the ruler escapes the Hadd. The hadith on the egg and the rope, which is recorded by Abu Hurayrah, and the report of A'ishah in Muslim, are the points on which the schools rest their views on value.

### In short

- Repentance and correction bring forgiveness from Allah in the Hereafter.
- Repentance does not stop the worldly Hadd for theft once the case is proved.
- The verse is read against the exception in 5:34, which does excuse the robber who repents before capture.

<<<VERSE 5:40>>>
"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah ˹alone˺? He punishes whoever He wills and forgives whoever He wills. And Allah is Most Capable of everything"

This verse closes the passage on theft and robbery. It states that Allah alone owns the heavens and the earth, and that He alone decides who is punished and who is forgiven. The material reads it as a reply to those who think that the Hudud violate human dignity.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah alone?"** The question is an affirmation. The material says that the owner of all things is the one who sets the law. Human judgement is therefore bounded by His command.

**"He punishes whoever He wills and forgives whoever He wills."** The material says that punishment and forgiveness both come from the wisdom of Allah. It cites the verse in 2:284 that gives the same pattern.

**"And Allah is Most Capable of everything."** The power of Allah reaches all things, including punishment and forgiveness. The material says that the limits of His wisdom cannot be measured by human reason, but enough of it is open to human understanding to bring peace to the heart.

### The Hudud and the modern objection

The material gives a full account of the five Hudud. The punishment for robbery is the cutting of the right hand and left foot. For theft it is the cutting of the right hand at the wrist. For adultery it is a hundred lashes, or stoning in some cases. For false accusation of adultery it is eighty lashes. For drinking wine it is eighty lashes, by the consensus of the Companions. All other offences are left to the discretion of the ruler and the judge, who may set penalties in line with the crime and the circumstances. The material says that in many modern systems the law is passed by an assembly and the judges apply it within set limits.

Even for the five Hudud, the material says, the punishment is not applied where the proof is missing or the conditions are incomplete. In those cases a discretionary penalty is given instead. The benefit of any doubt goes to the accused. The result is that the Hadd is rare and that most cases end in discretionary punishment.

The material gives examples of how strict the definition of theft is. Taking from public places, from trees, or from a house where one was permitted to enter does not meet the definition. Pickpocketing and fraud are punished, but not by amputation. The material notes that the Hadd is not applied when the thief has a share in the property. It adds that in such cases, the conditions of security and permission and proof are all considered before the Hadd can apply.

The material also argues that a society where crime is punished severely can be more peaceful than one where criminals are treated gently, and it offers the example of certain places where the Hudud are applied and shops are left open without fear. It addresses the objection that these punishments are savage by comparing them with the conduct of states that have built and stored weapons of mass destruction. It says that deterrence works in both cases, and it asks why what human beings can do in their own interest cannot be done by God in His wisdom. The material ends with a prayer that those who oppose Islamic punishments may be guided to the true way of peace.

### The shariah, fiqh and the Hudud in Makkah

The material explains that the shariah rules for the five Hudud are set in the fiqh books. It notes that Makkah, Madinah and the lands of the early Muslims had rules that followed the Qur'an and the Sunnah, and that the Hajj and Umrah bring Muslims from many countries to see these rules in practice. Many who have visited report that shops are left open during the prayer times, and that owners leave their stock without fear of loss. The material says that the Hudud are the reason for that order, and it argues that this order is a benefit to the whole community, not only to the thief who would otherwise be shown mercy.

### The consensus on wine

The material records the consensus of the Companions on the fifth Hadd, the eighty lashes for wine. It says that this consensus, like the Qur'an, is a source of law, and that the other four Hudud were fixed by the text itself.

### The rules of the Hadd of theft in the fiqh

The material notes that the jurists who wrote on the Hadd of theft, including the authors of the Hanafi jurisprudence, set the conditions in detail. The rules of the Hadd are stated in a way that makes the Hadd rare, and this is in keeping with the wisdom of the shariah. The material says that the crime and the proof must both be complete, and that any doubt causes the Hadd to be replaced by a discretionary punishment.

### In short

- Allah owns the heavens and the earth, and His power and wisdom govern punishment and forgiveness.
- The five Hudud are fixed by the text or by consensus, and all other offences are left to the judge.
- The strict conditions for the Hadd of theft mean that it is applied rarely, and the material defends it as a deterrent that protects the public.
