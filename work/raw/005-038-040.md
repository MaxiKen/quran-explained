<<<VERSE 5:38>>>
The verse returns from the call to faith and obedience to the legal protection of property: "As for male and female thieves, cut off their hands". The punishment is described as recompense and deterrence from Allah. Its application belongs to those in authority and depends on the legal definition, conditions and proof of theft, not simply on any accusation that something was taken.

The ending joins Allah's might to His wisdom. The penalty is not presented as force without purpose. The legal explanation connects it with protecting property, deterring repeated crime and restricting the punishment to cases that meet demanding requirements.

### Phrase by phrase

**"As for male and female thieves"** explicitly includes both sexes. The construction has the force of a conditional: when a person commits the legally defined offence, its consequence follows. Ordinary usage of the word theft is broader than the offence qualifying for this fixed punishment.

**"cut off their hands"** is explained as the right hand at the wrist. This is a command to the responsible authorities after legal establishment of the offence, not to private individuals. The Sunnah supplies further conditions, including a minimum value, over which different legal positions are reported.

**"for what they have done"** identifies the penalty as a consequence of their own unlawful taking. The instrument used to seize another person's property is itself subjected to the stated punishment.

**"a deterrent from Allah"** describes nakal, an exemplary consequence intended to restrain both the offender and others. The phrase from Allah also marks it as a hadd, a fixed penalty involving His right, rather than merely a private claim that the property owner can always cancel.

**"And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise"** affirms power that cannot be defeated and wisdom in command, prohibition, legislation and judgment. The severity is understood within that combination, not as a denial of wisdom or care for society.

### Why are men and women mentioned separately?

Many commands address men in a form that includes women, as in prayer, fasting, pilgrimage and zakah. Here women are named expressly, just as both sexes are named in the punishment for unlawful intercourse. Since doubt can prevent a hadd, their inclusion is not left merely implicit.

The order differs: the man is named first in theft, whereas the woman comes first in the corresponding sexual-offence wording (24:2). One explanation associates the first order with the man's greater opportunities and strength for earning, making his resort to theft especially blameworthy. It associates the other with the modesty and protection expected of women.

That is a supplied explanation of the wording, not a rule that every man or woman has identical circumstances, nor a different penalty based on sex. Both are expressly accountable for the offence.

### Which reported occasion is connected with the verse?

Al-Kalbi identifies Tu'mah ibn Ubayriq, who stole armour, as the person connected with its revelation. A brief gloss likewise names Tu'mah when explaining the male thief. The material here does not supply the remainder of that story, so it is not expanded with details from elsewhere.

The account also says hand-cutting existed during the period before Islam and was retained with defined legal conditions. Other inherited practices, such as blood money, were retained with modifications. Continuity in a broad form does not mean every earlier practice was accepted without qualification.

### What is sariqah in this legal discussion?

Sariqah is explained as secretly taking another person's privately owned property from its secured place without permission. The lexical definition cited from Al-Qamus and the legal definition are treated as corresponding. Several conditions follow from each part of it.

First, the property must belong to someone else, individually or jointly. The accused must not have a share in it or a legally relevant doubt of ownership. Property in which the public have equal rights, such as the belongings of a public-welfare institution, is treated differently for this fixed penalty.

Second, the property must be secured, for example within a locked house or under a guard. Taking something from an unsecured place does not meet that condition. Even a relevant doubt about the security can prevent the hadd.

Third, the taking must be without permission. Where the accused had permission to take and use the property, but then wrongfully kept it, the legal character differs. Doubt concerning such permission likewise matters.

Fourth, it must be taken secretly. Open seizure belongs to another category, and armed robbery has its own ruling. The legal distinctions prevent every wrongful taking from being treated as the same offence.

### Does the absence of a condition make the taking lawful?

No. The question is whether a particular worldly punishment applies, not whether another person's property may be taken. The prohibition against consuming one another's wealth through false means remains (2:188).

A missing condition can prevent the hadd while leaving sin, an obligation concerning the property and a possible discretionary penalty. Ta'zir, punishment determined by the relevant authority, may apply where legally warranted. The account includes corporal penalties among those possibilities.

It is therefore mistaken both to impose the fixed penalty on every common theft and to conclude that whatever falls outside its technical definition is permitted. The legal category and the wider moral prohibition answer different questions.

### What minimum value is required?

'A'ishah reports in Al-Bukhari and Muslim that the penalty applies to theft worth a quarter of a dinar or more. Muslim's wording explicitly excludes what is less. This is presented as the basis of one principal position.

A value of three dirhams is reconciled with that amount by taking the dinar in question as twelve dirhams. The two figures then express the same threshold. The account favours this explanation and sees the relatively small threshold as a deterrent against unlawful taking.

This position is reported from 'Ali bin Abi Talib, 'Uthman bin 'Affan and 'Umar bin Al-Khattab. Among later authorities named with it are Al-Awza'i, Al-Layth bin Sa'd, 'Umar bin 'Abdul-'Aziz and Ash-Shafi'i with his followers. Ahmad ibn Hanbal, Abu Thawr and Dawud bin 'Ali Az-Zahari are also named, as is one narration from Ishaq bin Rahwayh.

Abu Hanifah and his students Abu Yusuf, Muhammad and Zufar, together with Sufyan Ath-Thawri, instead give ten dirhams as the threshold. The disagreement is retained rather than hidden behind the favoured quarter-dinar position.

### How does the warning about small stolen objects fit?

Abu Hurayrah reports in the Two Sahihs the Prophet's ﷺ condemnation of a thief whose stealing of an egg or rope leads to losing his hand. The report supplies a severe warning about the course of theft.

The same discussion expressly sets out the minimum-value hadith and juristic disagreement. The warning therefore must not be isolated as though it cancelled every stated condition or established a separate universal rule for any object called an egg or rope. No further explanation of those objects is supplied here.

### What is said about repeated offending?

One legal gloss describes a sequence after the right hand: the left foot, then the left hand, then the right foot, followed by discretionary punishment for later offences. This is the particular position recorded in that gloss.

The sequence is not presented here as an independently established consensus of all jurists. It belongs with the supplied legal account and its insistence that authority and proof govern application. The verse's brief wording is explained through law, not through personal improvisation.

### Why distinguish hadd from qisas here?

A theft harms the owner and also violates Allah's command. The phrase from Allah is understood as making the latter aspect predominant in this fixed penalty. Once the hadd is established in its proper setting, the owner's forgiveness alone does not necessarily cancel it.

Qisas, proportionate redress for killing or injury, gives a different place to the victim's private right and its pardon. Confusing the two would falsely transfer the rules of one category into the other.

The separate question of pardon before a case is brought to the ruler is addressed with the next verse. Likewise, repentance can obtain Allah's forgiveness without automatically removing an established worldly penalty. These qualifications are part of the explanation, not exceptions to be ignored in the name of severity.

### In short

- The punishment belongs to legal authority and requires the defined offence, its conditions and sufficient proof.
- Ownership, security, permission and secrecy distinguish sariqah from other wrongful takings.
- The reported minimum values differ, with the quarter-dinar position favoured in one explanation and ten dirhams given by another school.
- A case outside the hadd can still involve sin, restoration of rights and a discretionary penalty.
- Deterrence is joined to Allah's wisdom, while questions of repentance and pardon require their own distinctions.

<<<VERSE 5:39>>>
The offender is not shut out from mercy: "But whoever repents after their wrongdoing and mends their ways, Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness." Repentance requires leaving the theft and correcting one's conduct, not merely wishing its consequences away. Allah accepts a genuine return even after a serious offence.

The promise concerns forgiveness before Allah. Its relation to the worldly legal penalty is a separate question, and the explanations distinguish this verse from the special exception for highway robbers who repent before capture.

### Phrase by phrase

**"But whoever repents after their wrongdoing"** opens the way back after theft. One gloss describes repentance after the offence and its punishment. The person acknowledges the wrong and turns from it rather than continuing to justify it.

**"and mends their ways"** requires changed action and repairing the relationship with Allah. The repentance should be visible in abandoning the practice, seeking pardon and refraining from a return to the offence.

**"Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness."** promises acceptance by Allah through His grace. The wording does not expressly exempt the offender from the earthly penalty in the way the earlier robbery exception does.

**"Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** assures the repentant person that the past crime does not make forgiveness impossible. Mercy and accountability are not mutually exclusive.

### Does repentance cancel the legal punishment?

The principal legal explanation says that repentance alone does not remove an established theft hadd, whether it occurs before or after arrest. The earlier provision for robbery explicitly excepts those who repent before capture (5:34). This verse instead promises Allah's favourable turning toward the repentant person.

On that reading, the worldly sentence may remain while the punishment of the Hereafter is forgiven. A person should not infer that Allah rejected sincere repentance simply because a court still applies a legally established consequence.

Another gloss preserves the victim's claim and the duty to restore property. It adds that pardon before the offender is brought to the imam, the ruler, can prevent the cutting penalty, and attributes that view to Al-Shafi'i. This qualification concerns the procedural stage and the owner's pardon, not simply a declaration of repentance after the case reaches court.

### What happened to the woman from Makhzum?

Ahmad records 'Abdullah bin 'Amr describing a woman brought before the Prophet ﷺ for theft. Her people offered a ransom, eventually proposing five hundred dinars, but he ordered the prescribed penalty. After her right hand was cut, she asked whether repentance remained open.

He assured her that it did, describing her release from sin through the image of the day her mother bore her. The report connects the verse's revelation with that assurance. The penalty did not mean she could never again belong among the forgiven.

### Why was Usamah's intercession rejected?

The Two Sahihs record 'A'ishah's account through Az-Zuhri of concern among Quraysh over the Makhzumi woman's case at the conquest of Makkah. They sought someone close enough to the Prophet ﷺ to intervene and chose Usamah bin Zayd.

When he spoke, the Prophet's ﷺ displeasure became visible. He challenged intervention in a punishment prescribed by Allah, and Usamah asked him to seek forgiveness for him.

The Prophet ﷺ later addressed the people. Earlier communities had been ruined by allowing influential thieves to escape while punishing the weak. He declared that even his own daughter Fatimah would not receive such immunity if she committed the offence. The point is equal accountability, not the social worth of the accused.

'A'ishah says the woman later repented sincerely, married and came to her to convey needs to the Prophet ﷺ. Muslim records that wording. Another Muslim narration describes her borrowing things and denying having received them. That description is retained alongside the theft account rather than silently replaced with a new legal harmonisation.

### What hope does reform give?

The narrative joins refusal of privileged exemption with recognition of the offender's later life. The woman was not reduced forever to her offence. Her sincere repentance and ordinary needs remained worthy of acknowledgment.

Reward and forgiveness belong to Allah. A person who reforms later in life need not imagine that the lateness of that return makes every earlier sin unforgivable. The required response is genuine change, while legal and private claims are dealt with according to their own rules.

### In short

- Repentance after theft requires abandoning the wrong and correcting conduct.
- Allah's forgiveness does not automatically cancel a worldly penalty already established in law.
- The reported qualification about pardon before presentation to the ruler remains distinct from repentance alone.
- The Makhzumi woman's story combines equal accountability, rejection of privileged intercession and the reality of sincere reform.

<<<VERSE 5:40>>>
The passage grounds punishment and forgiveness in Allah's sovereignty: "the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah ˹alone˺". No part of creation lies outside His ownership, and no power can overturn His judgment. He punishes with justice and forgives through grace, with complete power over both.

The question is a reminder of something already known, not a request for information. It addresses the Messenger ﷺ and directs the hearer back to the Lord whose law and mercy have just been explained.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah ˹alone˺?"** affirms His complete dominion. The treasures and resources of both belong to Him. His authority is not borrowed from another ruler or limited by a rival claimant.

**"He punishes whoever He wills and forgives whoever He wills."** Both actions belong to His judgment. The deserving sinner can be punished, and Allah can also forgive through His grace. Nothing external forces Him to punish or prevents His mercy.

**"And Allah is Most Capable of everything"** includes carrying out judgment and forgiving sin. No one can resist His decree. The same joining of dominion, pardon, punishment and power appears elsewhere (2:284).

### Why does sovereignty follow these legal passages?

The severe penalties for robbery and theft can raise questions about dignity and mercy. The answer given here begins with Allah's ownership, wisdom and power. He knows the whole creation and the consequences of conduct more fully than human judgment can encompass.

That does not prevent reflection on the legal safeguards and purposes already supplied. It sets the limits of that reflection: the human mind does not possess an exhaustive measure of divine wisdom. What it can understand through the stated principles can nevertheless reassure the heart.

The verse also prevents a one-sided picture. Allah does not only punish. He forgives, and both judgment and pardon are His. A repentant person should not despair, nor should an offender imagine that someone else can compel a favourable outcome through influence.

### How much of the penal system is fixed in this account?

The legal discussion distinguishes a small set of fixed penalties from the much wider field of discretionary punishment. It names robbery, theft, unlawful intercourse and false accusation of unlawful intercourse as expressly treated in the Qur'an, with wine-drinking added through the Companions' ijma, consensus.

Its outline includes cutting on opposite sides for the relevant robbery case, the right hand for qualifying theft, one hundred lashes or stoning in different unlawful-intercourse cases, eighty lashes for false accusation, and eighty for wine in the consensus-based account given. The different robbery cases were already explained in their own passage and are not erased by this brief list.

For other offences, the relevant ruler and judge can assess the act, offender and circumstances. A government may establish a regulated schedule of penalties through consultation and bind its judges to it. The discussion compares this with legislation delimiting judicial sentencing.

Even within offences that can carry a hadd, a missing condition or insufficient proof prevents that fixed penalty. Another lawful discretionary penalty may then apply if the offence is otherwise established. The distinction makes the system more qualified than a list of severe sentences considered without their conditions.

### Which theft examples show the importance of security and ownership?

The detailed account is drawn from Hanafi fiqh, jurisprudence, and names Bada'i' al-Sana'i' as its legal reference. Its examples should therefore be read as that legal exposition, not as a claim that every school gives every example identically.

It treats taking from commonly accessible places differently from breaking the required security of private property. The illustrations include a mosque, the open prayer ground used at 'Id, parks, clubs, stations and waiting rooms, as well as trains and aircraft. It also mentions fruit still on trees and honey.

The conclusion in these examples is that the theft hadd is not imposed under the described conditions, while an appropriate discretionary penalty can remain. The examples do not make taking such things lawful.

Permission to enter also affects the security condition in this account. A servant, hired worker, contractor, friend or relative admitted into a house is not in the same position as a person who breaks its protection from outside. Wrongfully taking something after such admission may still be punished, but the specified hand-cutting penalty is not applied in the example.

The account likewise treats pickpocketing, snatching cash or jewellery, obtaining property by fraud and denying a trust as unlawful takings governed here by discretionary penalties rather than the technical theft hadd. The classifications are reported as supplied, not expanded into a general survey of all juristic differences.

### What about shrouds and jointly owned property?

The theft of burial clothes is described as gravely unlawful, yet outside the fixed penalty in this Hanafi explanation. It gives two reasons: the absence of the required secured place and the fact that the shroud is not owned by the dead person in the relevant legal sense. A discretionary punishment remains possible.

Taking from property in which the accused holds a share also creates a relevant ownership doubt. The examples include a joint inheritance and a commercial partnership. Wrongfully appropriating more than one's due does not become permitted, but the mixed ownership prevents the hadd in the account given.

These cases illustrate why the bare word theft cannot settle every legal question. The particular right, manner of taking and condition of the property have to be established.

### What additional safeguards govern evidence?

The discussion distinguishes evidentiary rules for hudud from those for ordinary cases. Unlawful intercourse requires four eyewitnesses meeting the stated standard, while theft requires two. The proof must be sufficiently definite to leave no relevant doubt in what the witnesses attest.

For the fixed penalties, the account requires two male witnesses and does not allow the substitution used in some ordinary matters of a man and two women. It also excludes testimony from a person whose sinful conduct disqualifies him, even where a judge might otherwise be satisfied that he does not lie.

Delay is another qualification in this Hanafi exposition. In ordinary matters, the passage of time need not prevent testimony. For hudud, it describes testimony delayed for a month or more as unacceptable. This is a stated legal position, not a new universal claim about every court or school.

The essential argument is that strict penalties are joined to strict proof. A court must establish both the complete offence and the complete evidence. Compassion toward an accused person appears through those safeguards as well as through the opportunities for repentance and appropriate pardon already explained.

### What is the argument about deterrence?

The defence of these laws says that rare application under exacting conditions can still create a strong public warning. It contrasts that with systems in which habitual offenders treat imprisonment as an interruption and plan further crimes while confined.

A historical illustration describes pilgrims seeing shops in Saudi Arabia left open during prayer while owners went to the Sacred Mosque. It also describes amputations and stoning as infrequent and theft as greatly restrained. These are observations and claims used in the argument, not current crime statistics independently verified here.

The reasoning is that pity focused only on the offender can neglect people exposed to repeated predation. A punishment intended to deter further harm is defended as protection for the wider community. The account relates this to the teaching that qisas preserves life.

That defence does not remove the preceding safeguards. The argument depends upon proper legal conditions and proof, not upon treating every suspicion as an opportunity for an exemplary sentence. Severity without the rules would not be the system described.

### How does the discussion compare penal severity with warfare?

The explanation insists that concern for mercy should be consistent. It points to restraints in warfare concerning women, children, the elderly and religious worshippers not participating in the fighting. Those protections are used to answer the charge that the legal tradition is simply indifferent to human life.

It then contrasts condemnation of these punishments with the mass killing of civilians at Hiroshima and the continued development of nuclear weapons. The criticism is that societies cannot claim compassion solely from rejecting a penal rule while tolerating destruction of noncombatants on a vastly greater scale.

An added argument questions the assumption that powerful states are automatically responsible custodians of such weapons. Possession of destructive power does not prove that it will never be abused. Calling nuclear arms deterrents while dismissing every severe religious penalty as inherently beyond consideration is presented as an inconsistent standard.

These are the moral comparisons made in the discussion. They do not require claiming that all critics are personally guilty of wartime acts, nor do they establish current military facts beyond the historical examples supplied. Their concern is the relation between deterrence, self-interest and a consistent regard for human life.

### Where does personal reform fit beside the legal argument?

A person sometimes faces a choice between truth and the apparent safety of ego, status or possessions. Following truth can require accepting difficulty and surrendering self-importance. Taqwa, fear of Allah, and jihad, sustained effort for His cause, direct the person through that test.

Nearness to Allah may then be tasted inwardly in this life, while its full reality belongs to the Hereafter. Refusing that struggle can instead deepen distance and expose a person to consequences from which worldly resources cannot rescue him.

The penal system is explained through recompense and deterrence, but it does not make repentance useless. Someone who sincerely abandons crime can hope for Allah's forgiveness. No independent force can alter the final judgment against His will, and no late return is automatically rejected merely because an earlier life contained serious wrongs.

The verse thus holds power and mercy together. The sovereign Lord is neither unable to punish nor unable to forgive. The appropriate response is to respect His limits, fulfil the rights of others and seek His pardon through genuine reform.

### In short

- Allah alone owns creation and has complete power to punish or forgive.
- Fixed penalties operate within defined offences and strict proof, while discretionary punishment covers a wider field.
- The detailed examples here belong to a Hanafi legal exposition and should not be mistaken for an unqualified survey of every school.
- The defence of deterrence stresses protection of the wider public and consistency in judgments about violence and mercy.
- Personal reform and hope of forgiveness remain essential beside legal accountability.
