The surah settles a contest of sanctuaries: "Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people". Mujahid reports mutual boasting. Jerusalem's defenders prized its prophets' migrations and sacredness, Muslims answered that the Kaʿbah was better. Revelation gives Bakkah's House three distinctions: priority, blessing and guidance for everyone.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah"**: it was appointed for all people and their ritual life. Around it they perform Tawaf, beside it they pray, and within its area they remain in Iʿtikaf. Ibrahim Al-Khalil built it at Allah's command and invited people to Hajj there, although Jews and Christians who claim his religion do not make that pilgrimage. Its honour is not confined to one nation. An aura placed by Allah draws human hearts generally.

**"a blessed sanctuary"**: sanctified and full of blessing, the ground holds forgiveness and mercy.

**"and a guide for ˹all˺ people"**: guidance reaches Al-ʿAlamin, mankind and jinn. Every prophet, messenger, veracious one (siddiq) and believer takes it as Qiblah.

### First house in time, or first for worship?

One reading makes the Kaʿbah the world's first house of any kind, set apart for divine worship before residence existed elsewhere. Adam's prophethood and vicegerency make it plausible that he built God's House right after reaching earth, before his own dwelling. ʿAbdullah ibn ʿUmar, Mujahid, Qatadah and successor authorities therefore called it the world's first house. Another reading allows homes to precede it while preserving priority as the first building devoted exclusively to worship, that view is reported from ʿAli.

Al-Baihaqi's Dala'il al-Nubuwwah reports ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs: after Adam and Hawwa' arrived in the mortal world, Jibra'il conveyed a command to build Allah's House. Once they obeyed, Tawaf was prescribed for them, and they learned that they were the first people just as this was the first House appointed for people.

Abu Dharr's questions establish a second chronology:

> "I said, 'O Allah's Messenger! Which Masjid was the first to be built on the surface of the earth?' He said, 'Al-Masjid Al-Haram (in Makkah).' I said, 'Which was built next?' He replied 'Al-Masjid Al-Aqsa (in Jerusalem).' I said, 'What was the period of time between building the two?' He said, 'Forty years.' He added, 'Wherever (you may be, and) the prayer becomes due, perform the prayer there, for the whole earth was made a Masjid.'"

Imam Ahmad recorded this, and Al-Bukhari and Muslim collected it too. Angels reportedly built the House before Adam's creation, with the Aqsa following forty years later. Another report calls the first visible thing at creation a white foam over which the earth was unrolled, beneath the House's site.

The forty years can refer to Ibrahim's new edifice and Bayt al-Maqdis's initial construction forty years afterward. Sulayman's later Bayt al-Maqdis was also a rebuilding on Abrahamic foundations rather than a brand-new foundation. Reports thus need not conflict.

### Rebuilding on ancient foundations

Adam's structure is reported to have survived into Nuh's time and vanished in the Flood. Ibrahim rebuilt after its traces disappeared, on the same foundations. Successive collapses produced further rebuilding by Jurhum and then the Amalkites. Near the Prophet's era, Quraysh rebuilt it, and the Prophet ﷺ participated, helping place the Black Stone with his blessed hands.

Quraysh nonetheless altered the Abrahamic plan. They excluded the section known as Hatim. Two doors, entrance and rear exit, became one eastern door. They raised that door high above ground level, restricting entry to people they permitted.

The Prophet ﷺ told ʿA'ishah he wished to demolish the current structure and raise it exactly on Ibrahim's foundation, correcting Quraysh's arbitrary deviation. New Muslims' imperfect knowledge and potential misunderstanding made him leave it temporarily, and death came before implementation.

His nephew through ʿA'ishah, ʿAbdullah ibn Zubayr, heard the statement. As Makkah al-Mukarramah's ruler after the Khulafa al-Rashidin, he demolished and rebuilt it according to the Prophet's wish and Ibrahim's original foundation. His rule over Makkah al-Muʿazzamah was brief. Hajjaj ibn Yusuf invaded, ʿAbdullah was martyred, and Hajjaj, hostile to the martyr gaining fame from the feat, smeared him as wrong and claimed the Prophet had intended the Quraysh form to remain. The structure was demolished again and rebuilt in that earlier Jahiliyyah form.

Later rulers considered restoring the Prophet's stated preference. Imam Malik ibn Anas, master-jurist of that period, warned that repeated demolition and rebuilding would turn God's House into a plaything for rulers chasing fame. The ummah accepted his fatwa. Hajjaj's structure therefore remains, repaired over centuries as damage and dilapidation required.

Scripture itself points to older foundations. Ibrahim and Ismail were "raising up the foundations of the House" (2:127), implying that the Qawa'id were already present. God had also "settled for Ibrahim the site of the House" (22:26), suggesting a predetermined location. Reports add that an angel led Ibrahim to foundations buried under sand dunes.

### Why Bakkah?

Bakkah names Makkah. One derivation says it brings Buka', crying or weeping, upon tyrants and the arrogant, humbling them nearby. Another says crowds gather (do Buka') around it. It crushes the necks of tyrants (tabukku). Some explain Bakkah as Makkah al-Muʿazzamah by the familiar substitution of mīm for bā, others treat Bakkah simply as an alternative name.

Makkah carries many names: Bakkah, Al-Bayt Al-ʿAtiq, the Ancient House, Al-Bayt Al-Haram, the Sacred House, Al-Balad Al-Amin, the City of Safety, Al-Ma'mun, Security, Umm Rahm, Mother of Mercy, Umm Al-Qura, Mother of the Towns, Salah, and more.

### Blessing measured in usefulness

Barakah means growth and sustaining. Quantity can visibly multiply, but blessing can also make a limited quantity do what much more would ordinarily be required for. The second kind also counts as increase.

Outward blessing is conspicuous in a dry and barren desert. Makkah receives fruits, vegetables and necessities in every season. Residents alone do not exhaust them, visitors arrive from the whole world. Hundreds of thousands during Hajj can outnumber residents four or five to one and remain for months. Outside the season, thousands continuously enter and leave. Markets do not historically run out during the pilgrimage crowds. Sacrificial animals, at least one per pilgrim on average and sometimes more, remain available without special import arrangements. "Brought toward it are fruits of everything" (28:57) captures the phenomenon.

Inward blessing is too large to enumerate. Hajj and ʿUmra revolve around Baytullah. Prayer values also climb through the sacred mosques:

- 1 salah at home.
- 25 salahs in a local masjid.
- 500 salahs in a Jami', a large congregational mosque.
- 1,000 salahs in al-Masjid al-Aqsa.
- 50,000 salahs in the Prophet's Masjid.
- 100,000 salahs in al-Masjid al-Haram.

Ibn Majah and Tahawi are among this narration's reporters. Correct Hajj also cleanses a Muslim of past sins as if he had been born that day, pure and pristine. These blessings and its role as guidance belong to all worlds.

### In short

- Bakkah's House is the world's first House, or first exclusively for worship, and it stands blessed as guidance and Qiblah for all.
- Adam, Ibrahim, Jurhum, the Amalkites, Quraysh, Ibn Zubayr and Hajjaj built successively on ancient foundations (2:127, 22:26), giving Makkah's aura to all humanity.
- Barakah shows outwardly through provision for millions in a barren valley (28:57), and inwardly through prayer rewarded up to one hundred thousandfold in al-Masjid al-Haram.

Inside the first House, meaning gathers: "In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe. Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people. And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation". Baytullah is distinguished by manifest signs, safety for entrants, and Hajj required from anyone able to reach it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham"**: manifest signs demonstrate that Ibrahim built the Kaʿbah and that Allah honoured and blessed it. Clear signs include the acts of worship performed in it and its landmarks. Alongside the Abraham standing place stand Ishmael's semi-circular white marble wall, Hatim, and the black stone.

**"Whoever enters it should be safe"**: entry brings security from violence. The Haram is a refuge for anyone in fear, and no harm comes to an entrant there.

**"Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people"**: Hajj to the House is a duty mankind owes Allah for those able to undertake the journey. Its verbal noun can be read hijj al-bayt or hajj al-bayt, "the intention to journey there". Ability defines the people addressed: anyone able to make his way there and perform the acts.

**"And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation"**: whoever disbelieves in Allah, Muhammad, the Qur'an and the obligation of pilgrimage brings no loss to God. He stands Independent of all creatures, humans, jinn and angels, and needs neither their belief nor their devotions.

### The Maqam: footprints, movement and casing

When Kaʿbah walls rose, Ibrahim stood on the Maqam to build higher while Isma'il handed up stones. It originally stood beside the House. During his reign, ʿUmar bin Al-Khattab moved it farther east. Tawaf performers could then circle easily without disturbing worshippers praying beside the Maqam after finishing. Allah commands, "And take you (people) the Maqam (station) of Ibrahim as a place of prayer" (2:125).

Reports explain its sign quality. Al-ʿAwfi transmits Ibn ʿAbbās: "Such as the Maqam and Al-Mash'ar [Al-Haram]." Mujahid points to Ibrahim's feet impressions remaining on the Maqam. ʿUmar bin ʿAbdul-ʿAziz, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, As-Suddi, Muqatil bin Hayyan and others said similarly. The House has endured all this length of time and the constant passing of hands over it.

The stone reportedly possessed responsive movement. It raised itself with the construction level and descended when required. Footprints still mark it. An inert stone gaining sudden volition to respond functionally, or assuming wax-like plasticity beneath a perfect foot impression, signposts Allah's power and Baytullah's merit.

Its position migrated over time. From the door area, it was removed after the command in 2:125 for Tawaf convenience, then placed in front of Baytullah outside the mataf, the Tawaf area, close to Bi'r Zamzam. That well's original site is now marked with a black marble circle on Baytullah's floor with an Arabic legend inlaid. Later, the stone was secured in a small structure behind which the two post-tawaf rak'at were offered. Today, a strong metal-crystal casing encloses it, but the particular stone inside remains the Maqamu Ibrahim. Post-Tawaf salah of two rak'at behind or close to it is more merit-worthy. Yet Maqamu Ibrahim in its literal sense covers the entire al-Masjid al-Haram, so Muslim jurists allow the two rak'at anywhere within the Sacred Mosque.

### More signs: birds, protection, rain and disappearing pebbles

Reward for good deeds multiplies in the House, birds never fly over it, and people of Makkah have received protection from enemy attacks because of its blessings. Abrahah's elephant army, invading Makkah, was destroyed by birds.

Rainfall provides another reported sign: rain falling on one side of Baytullah seems to favour countries situated on that side with substantial rainfall. The Jamarat's pebbles provide a more testable one. Every pilgrim throws seven pebbles at each pillar for three days, with a couple million or more pilgrims every year. Without removal, the Jamarat would be buried under a mountain of pebbles. Yet after three days, only scattered pebbles are visible. According to the Prophet ﷺ, angels pick up the thrown pebbles, scattered ones belong to people whose Hajj was not accepted for some reason. Picking up pebbles lying near the Jamarat for throwing is forbidden because they belong to unaccepted pilgrimages.

Shaykh Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti, in al-Khasa'is al-Kubra, counts this among miracles of the Holy Prophet ﷺ that still live, stand and will continue until the Day of Judgment for everyone to see. The Holy Qur'an's unmatched presence is another: no force in the world can jointly match it, an inability that persisted in the Prophet's time and will persist to Judgment. Every Muslim in every period can challenge the whole world, "Then, produce a Surah like it" (10:38). Similarly, the statement about angels invisibly collecting accepted pebbles has stood the test of time for centuries. It is a continuing prophetic miracle and a major sign in the Kaʿbah.

### Safety declared from creation

Even Jahiliyyah felt the Haram's refuge. Al-Hasan Al-Basri describes a murderer wearing a piece of wool around his neck before entering. The murdered man's son could meet him inside and would make no move until he left the sanctuary. Scripture frames it universally: "Have they not seen that We have made (Makkah) a secure sanctuary, while men are being snatched away from all around them?" (29:67). "So let them worship (Allah) the Lord of this House (the Ka'bah). (He) Who has fed them against hunger, and has made them safe from fear" (106:3-4).

Hunting, driving game from its den to be hunted, cutting trees and picking grass are prohibited in the Haram. At the conquest, the Prophet ﷺ announced, as recorded in The Two Sahihs with wording from Muslim and attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās:

> "There is no more Hijrah (migration to Makkah), only Jihad and good intention. If you were mobilized, then march forth."

He also said:

> "Beware! Allah made this town (Makkah) a sanctuary when He created the heavens and earth, and it is sacred by Allah's decree until the Day of Resurrection. Fighting in Makkah was not permitted for anyone before me, and it was made legal for me for only a few hours or so on that day. No doubt it is at this moment a sanctuary by Allah's decree until the Day of Resurrection. It is not allowed to uproot its thorny shrubs, hunt its game, pick up its lost objects, except by announcing it, or to uproot its trees."

Al-ʿAbbās intervened for lemongrass, used in their houses and graves. The exception came: "Except lemongrass."

Abu Shurayh Al-ʿAdawi spoke to ʿAmr bin Sa'id while troops were being sent toward Makkah against ʿAbdullah bin Az-Zubayr. The Two Sahihs recorded his plea. His ears had heard it, his heart memorised it, and his eyes saw the Prophet ﷺ speak after glorifying and praising Allah:

> 'Allah, not the people, made Makkah a sanctuary. Therefore, anybody who has belief in Allah and the Last Day, should neither shed blood in it nor cut down its trees. If anybody argues that fighting in it is permissible on the basis that Allah's Messenger fought in Makkah, say to him, "Allah allowed His Messenger and did not allow you." Allah allowed me only for a few hours on that day (of the conquest), and today its sanctity is as valid as it was before. So, those who are present, should inform those who are absent of this fact.'"

ʿAmr answered: "O Abu Shurayh! I know better than you in this respect, Makkah does not give protection to a sinner, a murderer or a thief."

Jabir bin ʿAbdullah heard a simpler prohibition from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ: "None of you is allowed to carry a weapon in Makkah." Muslim recorded it.

Affection had a voice as well. Abdullah bin ʿAdi bin Al-Hamra' Az-Zuhri heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say while standing at Al-Hazwarah in Makkah's marketplace:

> "(By Allah! You are the best of Allah's land and the most beloved land to Allah. Had it not been for the fact that I was driven out of you, I would not have left you.)"

Imam Ahmad collected this Hadith and this is his wording. At-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah also collected it. At-Tirmidhi said, "Hasan Sahih."

### Security under law and in human instinct

Legally, an entrant cannot be molested or killed there. A person who kills someone or commits another crime, then enters the sanctuary, should not be punished inside. He is compelled out first. Punishment follows departure. Divine law thus protects the entrant while he remains within.

A second security is factual. Divine creation planted awe and reverence for Baytullah in people's hearts. Wild, warring Jahiliyyah Arab tribes were ready to sacrifice their lives to uphold Baytullah's honour. Esteem for the Haram was so deep that a son could encounter his father's killer there, say nothing, and quietly move away despite a burning rage for revenge.

Only a few hours were ever excepted. Revelation at the conquest of Makkah allowed the Holy Prophet ﷺ alone to fight so Baytullah could be cleansed for an important objective of faith. His express announcement soon made the original prohibition of fighting permanently valid.

Hajjaj ibn Yusuf's later armed action against ʿAbdullah ibn Zubayr, with killings and terror, was a grave sin and a flagrant violation of law and the Kaʿbah's sanctity, hated by the entire community, and it does not dent the divine declaration. Hajjaj himself knew the action was unlawful and a serious crime, but political and administrative considerations overwhelmed him. The wider community has always held the Haram and Baytullah in highest esteem and has regarded fighting or quarrelling in sacred precincts as one of the ugliest sins. That esteem is Baytullah's universal and exclusive mark.

### Hajj: a duty once, for whoever can reach it

Hajj is obligatory by this verse, a fundamental pillar mentioned by many Hadiths and agreed upon by Muslims. Text and scholarly consensus limit the adult Muslim to one obligatory performance in a lifetime. Abu Hurayrah reported in Imam Ahmad that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ once delivered a speech saying:

> "O people! Hajj has been enjoined on you, therefore, perform Hajj."

A man asked, "Is it every year, O Allah's Messenger?" The Prophet ﷺ remained silent until the man repeated the question three times and He then said:

> "Had I said yes, it would have become an obligation and you would not have been able to fulfill it."

He said next:

> "Leave me as I leave you, those before you were destroyed because of their many questions and disputing with their Prophets. If I command you with something, perform it as much as you can. If I forbid something for you, then refrain from it."

Muslim recorded similarly.

Ability has multiple categories, personal physical ability and ability related to other things, elaborated in jurisprudence. Abu ʿIsa At-Tirmidhi records Ibn ʿUmar answering three questions. Who is the pilgrim? "He who has untidy hair and clothes." Which Hajj is better? "The noisy (with supplication to Allah) and bloody (with sacrifice)." What is the ability to undertake the journey? "Having provision and a means of transportation." Ibn Majah collected the narration. Al-Hakim narrated that Anas said that the Messenger of Allah was asked about Allah's statement "for those who are able to undertake the journey" [3:97]: "What does 'able to undertake the journey' mean?" The Prophet ﷺ answered, "Having sufficient provision and a means of transportation." Al-Hakim considered Anas's chain authentic by Muslim's guidelines, though the Two Sahihs did not collect the report. Ahmad also recorded Ibn ʿAbbas: whoever intends Hajj should rush to perform it. Abu Dawud collected that Hadith too.

"Means" means surplus resources beyond basic needs. They should cover travel to Baytullah and back, expenses during stay in the Holy Land, and family expenses until return, since that is a standing obligation on him. Physical handicaps that prevent going that far and completing Hajj's many requirements, inability to see, or to use hands and feet, negate ability. As women are not legally permitted to travel without a Mahram (marriage with whom is prohibited), they would be considered "able" to embark on their Hajj if they are travelling with a Mahram making his Hajj, whether the Mahram bears his own expenses or the woman pays for his expenses as well. Similarly, route security is part of "ability": acute danger to life and property means an absence of ability to perform Hajj.

Literally, Hajj means "to intend". What it means in terms of prescribed religious observance is already stated in the Holy Qur'an itself: the tawaf of the Kaʿbah, the stay in ʿArafat, and in Muzdalifah. Remaining details have been made clear by the Holy Prophet ﷺ through his words and deeds.

### Whoever disbelieves after capability

Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujahid and several others commented on this verse: "Whoever denies the necessity of Hajj becomes disbeliever, and Allah is far Richer than to need him." Al-Hafiz Abu Bakr Al-Isma'ili recorded that 'Umar bin Al-Khattab said: "Whoever can afford Hajj but did not perform it, there is no difference in his case if he dies while Jew or Christian." This has an authentic chain of narration leading to 'Umar.

Intentional rejection of Hajj's obligation is undoubtedly included. Such a person cannot be considered a Muslim. He is a disbeliever indeed, for the description "And if one disbelieves" fits him clearly and comprehensively. A different case is the believer who accepts the obligation, has means and ability, and still does not perform it. He is no less a denier of the Divine command in practice. In his case, "and if one disbelieves" applies by admonition and warning, since he acts like disbelievers who do not perform Hajj. Muslim jurists therefore treat the verse as a severe warning to capable non-performers: heedlessness makes them resemble disbelievers. Refuge with Allah is sought from such a fate.

### The House of stone, and the heart it draws

Start with visitors, not masonry. Bodily selves, nufūs, circumambulate the House, while hearts, qulūb, seek the Real. What joins servant to sanctuary is imaged as mud bound to stone: the servant is madara, a bit of mud, the House is ḥajara, a bit of stone, and rabaṭa al-madara bi-l-ḥajara (the mud is bonded to the stone) lets mud, madar, remain with stone, ḥajar, while the Eternal is magnified. Relics are stones, but their traces point backward to the people who stood there.

Response differs by heart. One stone can cool and delight, muthlij wa-mubhij, while another experience of the same stone stirs and arouses, munfij wa-muzʿij. Lovers awaken, muzʿij, and poor souls, fuqarāʾ, leap, munfij. Visit a House as a mazār, a place for loved ones, and traces, āthār, or news, akhbār, may be sought. Through separation, the innermost self returns wasted, kharāb. Through connection, approach and approval, ījāb, are won. Houses remain in pact with lovers who stop at them, even in silence. Bodily visitation finds kindness. Heart visitation finds unveiling. God calls it "My House" (2:125 and 22:26).

### In short

- In the House are manifest signs, among them the Maqam stone that rose with the walls and kept Ibrahim's footprints, moved east by ʿUmar and still prayed behind (2:125).
- Whoever enters is safe under a sanctity declared at creation, lifted only for hours at the conquest, so that killing, hunting, cutting and weapons are all barred.
- Hajj to the House is a duty once in a lifetime for whoever has provision, transport, bodily ability, a secure route and, for women, a Mahram, and denying its obligation is disbelief.
