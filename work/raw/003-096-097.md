<<<VERSE 3:96>>>
“Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people” (3:96). The verse identifies the Kaʿbah in Makkah as the first House appointed for people to worship Allah. It is a place of blessing and guidance for all humanity.

### Phrase by phrase

**“the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity”** means the first sanctuary designated for people’s worship and religious rites. It is not simply an ordinary building. The Kaʿbah is the focal point of prayer, pilgrimage, and circumambulation. Its place in worship belongs to all people, rather than to one tribe or nation alone.

The verse’s declaration was especially relevant when Muslims and Jews disputed which direction of prayer was more worthy. Some Jews pointed to al-Bayt al-Maqdis in Jerusalem, while the Muslims faced the Kaʿbah. The verse directs attention to the House at Bakkah and its ancient role as a sanctuary. It does not deny the sacred history of Jerusalem. It establishes the special distinction of the House in Makkah and answers those who claimed that the earlier qiblah made the Kaʿbah inferior.

Reports differ about the earliest construction of the House. Some accounts connect it with Adam, and others say that its foundations were known before Ibrahim and Ismail raised its walls. The Qur’an describes Ibrahim and Ismail building the foundations (2:127) and Allah showing Ibrahim the site of the House (22:26). These passages establish their role in rebuilding and raising it. They do not require us to claim certainty about every detail of an earlier structure or its history through the time of Nuh (Noah). Ibrahim, al-Khalil, the close friend of Allah, is remembered as the one who called people to pilgrimage by Allah’s command. Reports collected by al-Baihaqi in Dala'il al-Nubuwwah tell of Adam building at the site, then of the House being rebuilt after the flood. Such reports preserve early Muslim understandings of its history, though the Qur’an itself gives the firm account of Ibrahim and Ismail raising its foundations.

A report from Abu Dharr asks which mosque was first established on earth. The Prophet ﷺ answered that it was al-Masjid al-Haram in Makkah, then named al-Masjid al-Aqsa in Jerusalem, with forty years between them. Al-Bukhari and Muslim recorded this report in their Sahih collections. It distinguishes the first sanctuary from the later sacred mosque in al-Maqdis. Both places are honored, but the Kaʿbah has the distinction described in this verse. This report is transmitted from Abu Dharr through the Prophet, Allah’s Messenger, and is also included in accounts cited by Ahmad. It connects the history of al-Masjid al-Haram with al-Aqsa without confusing the two sanctuaries or their places in the story of prophethood, al-nubuwwah.

**“the one at Bakkah”** identifies the location of the House. Bakkah is a name for Makkah, and it is also explained as referring particularly to the place where people gather around the Kaʿbah. The sanctuary is also called al-Bayt al-ʿAtiq, the Ancient House, al-Bayt al-Haram, the Sacred House, and al-Balad al-Amin, the City of Safety. Other names in reports include al-Maʾmun, Umm Rahm, Umm al-Qura, and Salah. These names emphasize its antiquity, sacredness, security, and central place among human settlements. The name al-Kaʿbah refers to the House itself, while al-Masjid al-Haram includes the sacred mosque around it.

### How does the House bless and guide people?

**“a blessed sanctuary”** means that Allah placed barakah, blessing and lasting good, in the House and its surroundings. The blessing is not only material. Makkah is a dry land, yet it receives pilgrims and visitors from distant places, and people find provision there. The Qur’an mentions that people around the sanctuary were made secure and that fruits are brought to it (28:57, 29:67, 106:3-4). Its spiritual blessings include the rites of hajj and ʿumrah, prayer in al-Masjid al-Haram, and the opportunity to turn to Allah in repentance and supplication.

The verse calls the House blessed without suggesting that the stones themselves act independently. Its honor comes from Allah, who designated it and taught people how to worship there. The acts of worship performed around it have their meaning because they follow His guidance. This is why the House is both a sign of mercy and a test of obedience.

**“and a guide for ˹all˺ people”** means that the House is guidance for Al-ʿĀlamīn, all worlds, and points people toward worship of Allah alone. It is the qiblah for prayer and a destination for pilgrimage. The word guidance also includes the signs connected to the House, the remembrance of Ibrahim and Ismail, and the rites that bring Muslims together. The House gathers an ummah from many lands without erasing their different languages and customs. Its direction is shared by people who may never have seen Makkah.

The Kaʿbah’s guidance is not limited to Muslims who can travel there. A person unable to perform pilgrimage is still directed by its example toward sincere worship, prayer, and care for others. The House reminds people that religious devotion has a history and a source. Ibrahim’s call, the rebuilding with Ismail, and the Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ teaching connect the community to a shared worship of Allah.

### How has the Kaʿbah’s structure changed?

The Quraysh rebuilt the Kaʿbah before the Prophet’s mission. Reports say that they shortened the structure and left the Hatim outside its walls because they lacked the resources to complete it on the foundations Ibrahim had raised. They also raised the entrance so that access could be controlled. The Prophet ﷺ later told ʿAʾishah that he wished to rebuild the House upon Ibrahim’s foundations, with two doors level with the ground, but he did not want to unsettle people who had recently entered Islam.

After the Prophet’s death, ʿAbd Allah ibn al-Zubayr rebuilt it according to what he understood from the Prophet’s words. After his death during the conflict with al-Hajjaj, the structure was changed again. Accounts connect this with the rulers who followed the Khulafa al-Rashidin. Later, Imam Malik advised against repeated rebuilding by rulers seeking distinction, since the House should not become a tool of political rivalry. These reports explain why the structure seen in Makkah al-Mukarramah has not always matched the earliest reported form. They do not change the House’s sanctity or its role in worship.

### In short

- The Kaʿbah at Bakkah is the first House appointed for humanity’s worship.
- Bakkah is Makkah, a blessed sanctuary with an ancient and honored place.
- The House guides people to worship Allah alone and is the qiblah and destination of pilgrimage.

<<<VERSE 3:97>>>
“In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe. Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people. And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation” (3:97). This verse names signs of the House, establishes the sanctity of its precinct, makes pilgrimage obligatory for those able to undertake it, and reminds people that Allah needs nothing from creation.

### Phrase by phrase

**“In it are clear signs”** means that the House and its rites point to Allah’s power and to the history of His messengers. The station of Ibrahim is named explicitly, and other signs include the Black Stone, the ancient foundations, and the rites performed around the House. The Qur’an also connects the sanctuary with the safe provision of the people of Makkah (29:67, 106:3-4). These signs direct attention to Allah rather than making the building an object of worship.

**“and the standing-place of Abraham”** refers to Maqam Ibrahim, the stone associated with Ibrahim’s building of the Kaʿbah. Reports say that Ibrahim stood upon it as he raised the walls while Ismail handed him the stones. The marks on the stone are remembered as a sign of this work. The Qur’an elsewhere tells worshippers to take the station of Ibrahim as a place of prayer (2:125). ʿUmar ibn al-Khattab moved it away from the immediate path of tawaf to make room for worshippers. Its location has been adjusted over time so that people can perform tawaf without obstructing those who pray. The sign is honored, but worship is directed to Allah alone.

The House was rebuilt and repaired at different times. Reports describe Adam’s early building, the loss of traces in the flood during Nuh’s time, and Ibrahim and Ismail rebuilding upon the foundations. Other reports recount later work by the Quraysh, including the Prophet ﷺ helping to place the Black Stone before his prophethood. People trusted him as al-Amin, the trustworthy, when they needed a fair resolution. These historical accounts should be distinguished from the verse’s clear teaching: the House is sacred, its signs recall Allah’s guidance, and Ibrahim’s role is honored. Reports from Ibn ʿAbbas and Mujahid mention the footprints on Maqam Ibrahim, and As-Suddi and other early authorities likewise describe it as a sign.

### How is safety protected in the Haram?

**“Whoever enters it should be safe”** declares the inviolability of the Haram, the sacred precinct around Makkah. The statement has a legal meaning: people must not be attacked or killed there, and its sanctuary is not to be treated as an ordinary place of conflict. The Prophet ﷺ announced that Allah made Makkah a sanctuary and that its sanctity would remain. Reports in the Two Sahihs relate this declaration. The rules also protect the area’s plants and game, with detailed rulings about cutting vegetation, hunting, and lost property.

This protection does not turn the sanctuary into a place where wrongdoing is approved or where justice ceases to matter. Jurists discuss how a person who commits a crime and then enters the Haram should be dealt with, while preserving the sanctity of the precinct. The principle is that the sacred space is not to be violated by private revenge or violence. It is not permission to harm a person outside it, nor a declaration that people are immune from all lawful accountability.

The verse’s promise also recalls the security associated with Makkah. The people of the pre-Islamic period, despite their conflicts, recognized the sanctuary’s special status. The Qur’an asks whether they have not seen that Makkah was made secure while people around it were seized (29:67), and it calls them to worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and protected them from fear (106:3-4). The sanctuary’s protection is a sign of Allah’s favor, not a guarantee that no hardship or violation will ever occur. Al-Hasan al-Basri describes how even during the pre-Islamic period, a person who entered the Haram could be protected from the victim’s family. The practice did not make bloodshed outside the sanctuary acceptable. It illustrates the awe and security that Allah placed around the House.

Several reports stress the continuing sanctity of Makkah. Ibn ʿAbbas relates the Prophet’s declaration on the day of the conquest. Al-ʿAbbas asked about the exception of a plant used in homes and graves, and the Prophet ﷺ permitted that exception. Abu Shurayh reminded a commander of the Prophet’s words that Allah, not the people, made Makkah sacred. Jabir reports a warning against carrying weapons there. Another report describes the Prophet ﷺ standing at al-Hazwarah in Makkah and saying that Makkah is the best land of Allah and the most beloved to Him. Ahmad, al-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i, and Ibn Majah transmit versions of this report. These reports explain how the community is to honor the sanctuary. A report about hijrah says that migration to Makkah ended after its conquest, while jihad and good intention remained. This does not cancel the sanctuary’s safety. It concerns the changed circumstances of migration after Makkah returned to Muslim rule.

### Who must make the pilgrimage?

**“Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah”** establishes hajj as a duty for Muslims who meet its conditions. The word hajj carries the sense of intending or setting out for the House. Its rites include tawaf around the Kaʿbah, standing at ʿArafat, the stay at Muzdalifah, and the other prescribed acts taught by the Prophet ﷺ. The hajj is a pillar of Islam and, for a person who is able, is obligatory once in a lifetime. The verse does not require a person to repeat it every year.

A report from Abu Hurayrah says that the Prophet ﷺ announced the obligation of hajj. When a man asked whether it was required every year, the Prophet remained silent, then explained that if he had said yes it would have become obligatory and people would not have been able to fulfill it. He taught the community not to burden itself with needless disputation, to carry out commands as well as it can, and to refrain from what is prohibited. The report teaches both the seriousness of hajj and the mercy in its limits.

**“upon whoever is able among the people”** makes ability a condition of the duty. The person needs sufficient provision and a means of reaching the House, along with the ability to return and meet essential responsibilities. The needs of dependents must not be ignored. Health, a safe route, and practical access matter. Classical jurists discuss details such as transport, a mahram for a woman where required, and the security of travel. Their rulings consider a person’s circumstances rather than demanding an impossible journey.

Reports explain ability as having provisions and a means of transportation. Ibn ʿUmar narrates a description of a pilgrim and asks what it means to be able to undertake the journey. Al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah record this report. Al-Hakim relates a report from Anas on provision and transport, and Abu Dawud records a report from Ibn ʿAbbas urging the person who intends hajj not to delay without reason. These narrations clarify practical ability while leaving the duty tied to the person’s real circumstances.

The pilgrimage also expresses equality and unity. People arrive from different regions and social conditions, enter the same sacred space, and perform the same rites. They make supplication, remember Allah, and offer sacrifice. Some accounts mention the jamarat as part of these rites. Hajj is not a display of status or wealth. It is an act of worship performed according to the Prophet’s guidance and within one’s means.

**“And whoever disbelieves”** is a grave warning after the command. It includes rejecting Allah’s obligation and refusing His guidance. Some early explanations apply the phrase to denying that hajj is obligatory. A person who believes the duty is true but is unable to travel is not described by that denial. Neglect by someone able is a serious sin, but the verse should not be used carelessly to declare a particular individual outside Islam. Judgment belongs to Allah, who knows a person’s knowledge, intention, and circumstances.

**“then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation”** reminds people that hajj benefits the worshipper, not Allah. Allah is independent of the worlds, the ummah, and every human act of devotion. He does not need the House, the pilgrim, or the rites. People need the guidance and mercy that worship brings. The warning therefore combines the dignity of the obligation with a reminder of human dependence on Allah.

### In short

- The House contains signs of Allah’s guidance, including Maqam Ibrahim.
- The Haram is a sanctuary whose safety and sacred limits must be honored.
- Hajj is obligatory once for a person who is able, with ability judged in light of real means and circumstances.
- Allah needs nothing from creation. The benefit of worship returns to the worshipper.
