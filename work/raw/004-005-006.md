<<<VERSE 4:5>>>
The previous verses ordered the property of orphans returned and the dowries of women paid in full. This verse adds the other half of the rule: "Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly". A right to property is not the same as an ability to manage it. Where the person entitled is not yet capable, the property stays in the hands of the one who can guard it, while the incapable person is fed and clothed from it and treated with gentleness.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth"** forbids the handover. The word behind "the incapable" is the foolish, the weak of judgement. Who exactly is meant was answered in several ways. Ad-Daḥḥāk reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that it refers to children and women. Ibn Masʿūd, al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUyaynah, al-Ḥasan and ad-Daḥḥāk said women and boys. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said the unwise are the orphans. Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah and Qatādah said they are women. In one reading the foolish are the squanderers found among men, women and children alike. Behind the disagreement stands a shared idea: whoever is likely to spend wrongly, whether from immaturity or inexperience, is not to be given free use of wealth.

**"which Allah has made a means of support for you"** explains the stakes. Property is what stands people up, the beam the household rests on. The words can also be read as "that by which property is valued", and both readings say the same thing: this wealth is the substance of livelihood, for the guardian and for the children alike, and it must not be wrecked.

**"but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly"** gives the guardian his alternative duty. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported from Ibn ʿAbbās: do not give to your wife or children the wealth Allah has made your responsibility and your means of sustenance. Keep the money in your own care, look after it, and let the spending on their food, clothes and provision come from you. If they demand the property, explain honestly and gently, in a way that neither breaks their heart nor wastes the wealth. Ibn ʿAbbās gave the very words a father might use: "All this is there for you. Just grow up a little more and you will have it all." The same explanation is reported from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, and al-Ṭabarī adopted this view. The kind words are part of the command itself: Mujāhid read the closing phrase as kindness and the keeping of good relations. Where there is nothing to give at the moment, even a promise to clothe and provide, spoken kindly, is within the verse.

### Who the ruling covers

The immediate setting is the orphan, but the words are general. The verse reaches all children, orphaned or not, and all whose judgement is weak, and the form of address, "your wealth", carries a deliberate breadth. It includes the property of the guardians themselves and, for as long as the orphans remain immature, the property of the orphans too, which stays in the guardian's safe custody and under his responsibility as though it were his own. Verse 2 has already settled the ownership beyond doubt, that the orphan's property goes to the orphan alone, so no confusion arises.

The same rule extends past childhood. It applies in insanity and erratic behaviour, in weakness of intellect or of religious practice, and in bankruptcy, where creditors ask that the property of a man who cannot pay his debts be placed in escrow. Wherever handling fails, custody holds.

### Why property is guarded so strictly

Property is meant neither for luxury nor for display. It is the means by which a person lives, and its protection matters. Human love can do the very thing greed does: out of natural affection, people hand properties to inexperienced children or ill-informed women, and the usual result is waste and the quick poverty that follows it. A person who squanders what was supporting a household has torn a hole in the arrangement by which Allah gives His servants their livelihood, and that is true whoever the owner is. When the property belongs to an orphan, the weakest member of society, the duty of protection is at its strongest and the dealing must be fair and just.

### The worth of lawful wealth

A cluster of reports fixes the value the verse places on property. The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever is killed defending his property is a shahīd (martyr), counted with the martyrs in divine reward, just as the one killed defending his life earns the great reward of shahādah (martyrdom). Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record it. He also said that the best asset a good man can hold is property that is good and clean, and that being rich does no harm to one who fears Allah, because such a person keeps his wealth away from sin. The anti-wealth sayings of many saints apply only to wealth earned in lust and spent on sin, which becomes its owner's punishment. Still, since people who grow rich tend to drift from moderation, the earliest generations earned about what they needed, gave thanks, and were glad to be spared the final audit of every dirham. In an age when people care little for faith and will drop it rather than fall out with a passing fashion, lawful earning and careful conservation matter all the more. The Prophet ﷺ said that poverty can push a person to the edge of disbelief. Sufyān al-Thawrī put the two sides together: once, keeping wealth was thought blamable, but today wealth is the shield of the believer. Whoever holds wealth in such times should make it serve him well, for when a need presses, a person without means is liable to spend his faith to meet it.

### The inner reading

Read inwardly, the foolish are whoever turns a servant from the Real and busies him away from the Lord: the dependents and children who prefer their worldly portion over the rights of Allah most high. The wealth assigned as your maintenance is to be handled without the disgrace of begging and contriving: better to stay with pleasing actions, generosity over withholding, patience over grasping. Rather than form the intention to beg, or make yourself and your dependents a burden on others, hold on to a sufficient amount of what Allah has assigned you, and be generous with what exceeds your needs. Two further counsels follow. If you have more than enough to help your dependents today, do not withhold what you know they need out of fear of future poverty. And if your hand is too tight to support them, do not let your tongue loose in speaking ill of them. When the lower self calls a person to spend in a useless way, that person is the most foolish of the foolish, and should not yield to it.

### In short

- The incapable, whether understood as children and women, as women and boys, as orphans or as squanderers of any kind, are not to be given free use of wealth, theirs or anyone's.
- The guardian holds the property, spends from it on their food and clothing, and speaks to them gently, promising good without breaking hearts.
- The rule extends to every kind of weak judgement: insanity, erratic behaviour, weak intellect or religion, and bankruptcy escrow.
- Property is the means of livelihood, not luxury or pride. Squandering it is a sin, defending it can earn martyrdom, and wealth in God-fearing hands does no harm.
- Read inwardly, the foolish are whatever pulls a servant from his Lord, and the command is to hold enough, give the excess, help today, and never spend uselessly.

<<<VERSE 4:6>>>
The surah now comes to the moment of return. The orphan's property, guarded through childhood, must go back at the right time and in the right way: "Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner". The verse was revealed over a real question. Rifāʿah died leaving his son Thābit ibn Rifāʿah al-Anṣārī very young, and the boy's uncle came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ to ask what was lawful for him from his orphan nephew's wealth and when he should hand it back. The answer came as this verse: test, then return, with witnesses, and Allah watching.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans"** is a command of practical education. What is tested is intelligence, in the words of Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, as-Suddī and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, and the test is conducted by doing: the child is given small assignments of buying and selling well before maturity, and the guardian watches how he manages himself in actual transactions.

**"until they reach a marriageable age"** is the age of puberty. When a child has become eligible for marriage through the signs of adulthood, the special assessment begins. The majority of scholars hold that puberty arrives with the first wet dream.

**"Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them"** names the true finish line, which is not a birthday but demonstrated judgement. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said it means finding them good in their religion and wise with their money, and the same was reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and others among the leading scholars. The scholars of fiqh (Islamic jurisprudence) therefore state that when a child becomes sound in religion and competent with money, the money kept for him is surrendered.

**"And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺"** warns the guardian against two distinct sins while he waits: spending above the orphan's normal needs, and spending early, in a hurry, as though racing the day the boy grows up and the property must leave the guardian's hand.

**"If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation"** makes guardianship an unpaid duty for the rich man. The service is an obligation on him, and receiving payment for it is not permissible.

**"but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision"** permits the poor guardian a share. He may consume from the orphan's property in fairness, a measure just about right to cover his basic needs, or, as some added, in proportion to the work he does for the estate, or as a loan he intends to repay.

**"When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses"** protects both sides. The handover happens in front of reliable witnesses so that no one can later deny receiving what he received, and the guardian stands absolved with a clear proof.

**"And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner"** closes the account. Allah is enough as Witness, Reckoner and Watcher over the guardians' work, whether the property they return is complete and whole or deficient and less. He knows all of it. A small point of the wording carries the emphasis: the preposition in the phrase is extra, and the verse means, plainly, Allah suffices as the One who counts everything.

### How the testing works

Children move through three stages in these verses. First, minority, before puberty. Second, after puberty. Third, self-reliance and discretion in conducting one's own affairs, rushd as opposed to safāḥah, sound judgement as opposed to folly. In the first stage the guardian educates and trains the child with hands-on experience, letting him grow smarter through small dealings in buying and selling on his own. That is what the command to test the orphans means. From it Abū Ḥanīfah deduced the ruling that transactions of buying and selling entered into by minor children with their guardian's permission are sound, valid and operative. In the second stage, when the child is mature and of marriageable age, the guardian examines his growth in experience, intelligence and dealing. When it is clear that the child understands his profit and his loss and handles his affairs satisfactorily, the property is handed over.

### When a child counts as grown

Real maturity, the verse indicates, is not tied to a count of years but to the indicators and signs of adulthood. When those signs show and the person is fit to marry, maturity is established, even at thirteen or fourteen. Puberty by wet dream is the marker held by the majority. ʿAlī said he memorized from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ the words: "There is no orphan after the age of puberty, nor vowing to be silent throughout the day to the night." Abū Dāwūd recorded it. Another hadith from ʿĀʾishah and other Companions says that three kinds of person have no deeds recorded by the pen: the child until he reaches puberty, the sleeper until he wakes, and the senile until his mind returns.

Where the signs do not appear, age steps in. The two Ṣaḥīḥs record Ibn ʿUmar saying that he was presented to the Prophet ﷺ on the eve of Uḥud at fourteen and was not allowed to join the battle, and presented again on the eve of al-Khandaq, the battle of the Trench, at fifteen, and was allowed. ʿUmar ibn ʿAbdul-ʿAzīz, when this hadith reached him, said: "This is the difference between a child and an adult." Fifteen completed years is also the mark fixed by al-Shāfī. Muslim jurists differ where signs never appear: some set eighteen years for boys and seventeen for girls, some fifteen for both, and the Ḥanafī ruling, given as the operative fatwa, is that boy and girl are both considered mature under Islamic law once they have completed their fifteenth year, whether or not the signs of maturity are found.

One further sign was debated: pubic hair. The correct opinion is that it does count as a sign of adulthood, and the sunnah supports it. Imam Aḥmad recorded from ʿAṭiyyah al-Quraẓī that on the day of Quriẓah the captives were presented to the Prophet ﷺ, and whoever had grown pubic hair was executed while whoever had not was left to go free. ʿAṭiyyah was one of those set free. The four compilers of the Sunan recorded something similar, and at-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ.

### Sound judgement, and how long to wait

The Qur'an commands the handover when proper understanding is perceived, but sets no final age for it. Some jurists leaned toward holding the property until full understanding is certain, keeping it in the guardian's custody for life if need be. Abū Ḥanīfah, however, verified the issue differently: the absence of rushd that the verse speaks of is the effect of childhood, and that effect is gone within ten years after maturity. Fifteen years for bulūgh, maturity, and ten years for rushd bring the age of presumed understanding to twenty five. By then the barriers of childhood are past, and the property goes over.

The verse also says "sound judgement" in an indefinite form, and the indefiniteness softens the standard. Perfect sense is not an absolute condition. A reasonable measure of understanding is enough, on the strength of which the property is given. Some people remain simple, innocent, slow and shy in their practical dealings their whole lives, and they are not to be deprived of their property for it. Only the totally insane fall under a separate rule: such a person stays in the category of the immature child, and his property is never handed over unless his mind returns, even if his whole life passes in insanity.

Read inwardly, to perceive maturity in someone is to perceive decency and integrity in him: generosity and the preservation of honour, keeping company with elders, striving toward good and performing acts of devotion as the command decrees. The rashīd, the mature one, is a person whom Allah has guided to his Lord, so that when a need presses on him he leans neither on his own strength and power, nor on his own plans and choices.

### The two temptations of the waiting guardian

The verse names the guardian's two temptations precisely. The first is extravagance, spending above the orphan's normal needs out of the property in one's care. The second is haste, starting to spend from the orphan's property long before there is any need, as if in a race against the boy's growing up, the day the property would have to be returned and the guardian's control would end. One reported reading of the phrase hears the guardian's own fear in it: consuming in haste lest they grow up and put a stop to the devouring of their wealth. Both are forbidden, and the boundary is simple: the orphan's wealth is used only when there is genuine need.

### The guardian's wage

May a man who spends his time and labour raising an orphan and protecting his property take payment for it from that property? The rule comes in the verse's own words. The rich guardian, who can meet his needs by other means, takes nothing, because the service is an obligation already on him. The poor guardian, who has no other livelihood, may consume from it in fairness, an amount just about right for his basic needs. ʿĀʾishah said that the verse about the rich abstaining and the poor consuming fairly concerns the orphan's guardian, and covers whatever labour he performs for the orphan's estate. Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded it and al-Bukhārī collected it. A man once told the Messenger of Allah ﷺ that he had no money but had an orphan in his care. The answer was:

> Eat from your orphan's wealth without extravagance or wastefulness, without mixing it, and without saving your own money by spending his.

Imam Aḥmad recorded it in the report of ʿAmr ibn Shuʿayb from his father from his grandfather.

### The same rule for public trusts

The verse yields a wider juristic principle by extension. It covers those who supervise awqāf (religious endowments and trusts), manage mosques and religious schools, head the institutions of Muslim states, or render similar service to country and community, service known in Islamic terminology as fard ʿalal-kifāyah, an obligation that absolves the rest when some discharge it. The superior conduct for such people, if they have the means to maintain their families, is to take nothing from those institutions or from the public treasury. If they lack means of their own and give their earning time to the service, they may take from it in proportion to their need. The condition is exactly that: proportion to need. Plenty of people fix a small official salary to satisfy the letter of the law and then spend on themselves and their families by a dozen other routes far beyond it. For that there is no remedy except the fear of Allah, and the verse's last words are addressed to everyone alike, ruler and ruled: the man untouched by unlawful acquisitions is the man who fears the final accounting.

### Witnesses, and the Reckoner

When the testing is done and the property goes back, witnesses are called to be present, so that no dispute can arise later and the guardian's responsibility is honourably discharged with a clear proof. Some add that the settlement should be recorded in writing as well, which serves the same purpose. The person whose affairs are being settled may be weak and unable to defend himself, but the Arbiter is not weak. Allah will catch hold of everyone who administered another's affairs unjustly on the Day of Reckoning and punish the unjustified settlement severely. None can escape it.

For all of that, the true Guardian of the orphan is Allah. In his Ṣaḥīḥ, Muslim records the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying to Abū Dharr:

> O Abū Dharr! You are weak, and I love for you what I love for myself. Do not become a leader of two, and do not assume guardianship of an orphan's property.

### In short

- Orphans are tested for competence through real dealing, small buying and selling, from before maturity. Abū Ḥanīfah deduced from this that a minor's transactions with the guardian's permission are valid.
- Maturity comes by its signs, the wet dream, or pubic hair, or by age: fifteen, the age at which Ibn ʿUmar was allowed at al-Khandaq, and the mark fixed by al-Shāfī and by the Ḥanafī ruling.
- The property is returned when sound judgement appears, good religion and competence with money. By the Ḥanafī reckoning the latest presumption is twenty five, since the word "sound judgement" is indefinite and a reasonable measure suffices. The permanently insane never take charge of property.
- Wasteful and hasty spending from the orphan's wealth while waiting is forbidden. The rich guardian takes no wage. The poor guardian takes according to need, or the measure of his work, and the same rule governs everyone paid from public trusts.
- The handover is witnessed, so no right is lost and no dispute remains, and Allah is sufficient as Reckoner, for even the weak orphan's cause is in His hands.
