<<<VERSE 4:5>>>
The earlier verses commanded that orphans be given their property and that women be given their dowries. Someone might conclude that property must be handed over in every case, even to a person who has no idea how to look after it. This verse removes that misunderstanding: "Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly." The property is to be watched over and kept safe. It is to be handed over only when the owner can protect it and spend it wisely.

The verse treats money as something to protect, not something to be thrown away through misplaced kindness. Some people, out of natural love, put their wealth into the hands of inexperienced children or poorly informed women. The usual result is that the wealth is wasted and poverty follows quickly.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth"**. The Arabic word is sufahāʾ, the unwise or foolish. Allah forbids giving such people the freedom to do as they please with wealth. Early scholars named different groups. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report of aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk, said it means children and women. Ibn Masʿūd, al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUyaynah, al-Ḥasan and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk said "women and boys". Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said it refers to orphans, while Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah and Qatādah said it means women. Shorter explanations speak of those who do not know the rights of women and children, or the squanderers among men, women and children, or anyone who cannot manage property well.

The words "your wealth" also say something. They speak of the property as belonging to the guardians, although the property in question is the dependants' own and merely held by the guardian. The wording may be wise on purpose, since it covers both the guardian's property and the orphan's. Until the orphans are mature and sensible, their property stays in the guardian's safe keeping and responsibility, as though it were his own.

**"which Allah has made a means of support for you"**. The Arabic word is qiyām. It is a verbal noun from qāma, to stand, and a variant reading has qiyam, the plural of qīma, value, meaning that with which property is valued. Either way the sense is that wealth is what keeps your life and the well-being of your children standing, so it must not be spent improperly. Another short explanation says Allah gave it to you to live on.

**"but feed and clothe them from it"**. Ibn ʿAbbās said, in the report of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah, that you should not hand your wealth, which Allah made you responsible for and a means of support, to your wife or children. Keep hold of it and look after it, and spend on their clothes, food and needs yourself. Others put it this way: you are responsible for feeding and clothing them, because you know better than they do what is right in matters of spending and giving.

**"and speak to them kindly"**. Mujāhid said it means kindness and keeping good relations. The verse commands kind treatment in deed toward the family and those under one's care. One should spend on their clothes and provisions and be good to them, and good words are part of that goodness. Another explanation says that if you have nothing to give them, you should promise that you will clothe them and give to them later. Others say the "decent words" are a firm promise to give them their property when they reach maturity and show sound judgement.

Ibn ʿAbbās described how to deal with a dependant who keeps demanding the property. Explain honestly, fairly and reasonably, in a way that neither breaks the person's heart nor causes the property to be wasted. For example: "All of this is for you. Grow up a little more and you will have it all."

### Who counts as incapable, and what happens to their property

This ruling applies sometimes because of youth, since young people cannot make wise decisions. It also applies in cases of insanity, erratic behaviour, and a weak mind or weak religious practice. It applies in cases of bankruptcy, when the creditors ask that a bankrupt person's property be placed in escrow because his debts cannot be paid from his money.

According to the explanation of Ibn ʿAbbās, which was also reported from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī and was adopted by aṭ-Ṭabarī, the verse covers all women, children and others who are weak in judgement and inexperienced. It is dangerous to give them property, since they may lose it. It makes no difference whether they are one's own children or orphans, or whether the property belongs to them or to the guardian. The surrounding verses may suggest that this order concerns orphans only. But the wording is general and includes all children, orphans or not. Nor is there any room for doubt that an orphan's property must be given to the orphan, because that was made clear in the second verse of this surah (4:2).

### What property is for

Property is not meant for luxury or for showing off. It is a means of livelihood that supports a person's existence in the world. It is not right to make acquiring it the goal of life. At the same time, wasteful use of it must be checked, and when it falls into the wrong hands, earnest efforts must be made to bring it back to its rightful owner. Taking anyone's belongings wrongly is like making a breach in Allah's arrangement for giving His servants their livelihood.

Guarding wealth is a duty, and squandering it is a sin. If someone dies while defending his property, he counts as a shahīd, a martyr. This is like being killed in defence of one's life, which earns the great reward of shahādah. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "Whoever is killed while protecting his property is a martyr."

Al-Bukhārī (page 337) and Muslim recorded this. The Prophet ﷺ also said:

> "For a good man, clean and lawful wealth is the best asset of his life."

> "Being rich does no harm to one who fears Allah, the Mighty, the Exalted."

These two sayings are in Mishkāt. They tell us that the wealth of a righteous, God-fearing person does not harm him, because his fear of Allah keeps him from spending it on sin.

### Wealth, mystics and our own times

Many Muslim saints and mystics taught against wealth, but those teachings are aimed only at people who spend greedily earned wealth on sinful purposes, and so make it the cause of their punishment in the Hereafter. There is a second reason why keeping away from wealth was seen as desirable. Once a person becomes rich, he is naturally inclined to give up moderation in spending and to lose his concern for staying safe from other sins.

The early generations earned about as much as they needed. They were grateful to Allah, and they were glad to have escaped being questioned about how they earned and spent whatever wealth they had. Today people care little about matters of faith and are drawn to material things. They are quick to give up their faith at the slightest provocation. It is not hardship that moves them. They fear going against a passing fashion, a trend or a borrowed norm of the society around them. So it matters that people earn lawfully and keep what they earn. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "Poverty can bring a person close to disbelief."

Sufyān ath-Thawrī explained this by saying that in earlier times, owning and keeping wealth was not considered good, but today that wealth is the shield of the true Muslim. He also said that whoever has some of it in his hands should make it work for him, because these are times when a person in need is likely to spend his faith first in order to meet that need. In other words, the wish to meet one's need has become more important than the duty to follow one's faith. These sayings are in Mishkāt too.

### The inner reading

On this reading, the foolish are whatever pulls you away from the Real and turns your attention from the Lord. They are the dependants and children who put their share of this world above the rights of Allah. Allah says that He has made the property a means of support for you. It is better for you to keep to what pleases Him in that situation than to expose yourself to disgrace by petitioning, begging and scheming. For the freedom of the heart, it is better to be generous than to hold back, and better to rely on patience. But if the aim is to beg, or to make yourself and your dependants a burden on others, it is more fitting to hold on to a sufficient amount of what Allah gave you. Then you can give freely out of whatever exceeds your needs.

The words "feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly" mean this as well. If you have a surplus today, do not keep back what you know they need, out of fear of being poor tomorrow. If you cannot afford to support them, do not let your tongue run wild with bad words. It is also said that when your lower self calls you to waste money on something useless, you are the most foolish of the foolish, so refuse it.

### In short

- The property of those who cannot manage money, whether children, orphans, women or anyone weak in judgement, is to be kept safe by the guardian and not handed over to be wasted.
- The guardian feeds and clothes them from it and speaks to them kindly, with a promise that they will have their property when they are ready. Hands-on care and good words both count.
- Property is a means of livelihood, not a goal or a source of pride. Protecting it is necessary, and the wealth of a God-fearing person does him no harm.
- Inwardly, the truly foolish are those who put this world before Allah's rights, and a person should be generous out of what exceeds his needs while not exposing himself to begging.

<<<VERSE 4:6>>>
This verse takes the guardian from the day the orphan is a child to the day the property is handed back: "Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation, but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner."

Once the previous verse established that property must not be given to the unready, this verse says how to find out when they are ready. The guardian is to educate and test the child, judge his understanding, hand over the property in full, take nothing for himself beyond a fair wage if he is poor, and do it all before witnesses.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age"**. Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, as-Suddī and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said that the test is a test of the orphans' intelligence. The "marriageable age" is the age of puberty, as Mujāhid said. Most scholars hold that puberty arrives when a boy has a nocturnal emission.

The testing is to begin well before puberty. Children should be given small tasks of buying and selling, to see whether they can handle transactions on their own. This practical trial should go on right up to the age of marriage, when they become mature, and that is the time of special assessment. The guardian then checks whether they have become smart and independent in their affairs. One short explanation says that the guardian should hand them a portion of their wealth and watch how they handle it. Another says the test covers the duties of religion as well, before they are allowed to manage their own affairs.

**"Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them"**. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said this means: when you find them good in religion and wise with their money. The same was reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and other leading scholars. The scholars of fiqh, Islamic law, said that once a child is good in religion and sensible with money, the money his guardian has been keeping must be surrendered to him. Short explanations say the same: righteousness in religion and a tendency to protect one's wealth, or right judgement in matters of religion and property. If the orphans manage well and show sound judgement, the property is handed over in full.

**"And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺"**. Allah commands that the orphan's money must not be spent without need, and not in haste for fear that the orphan may grow up. One explanation says "wastefully" means in transgression and unlawfully. Another says the guardian must not hurry to spend it for fear that the orphan will become an adult and take it back. Others say: use it only when necessary, and do not rush to spend it, afraid that they will claim it once they come of age. So the guardian is warned against two things. First, spending the orphan's property extravagantly, which means over and above normal needs. Second, starting to spend from it long before there is any need, in a hurry, with the day in mind when the wards will grow up, the property will have to be given to them, and his control will end.

**"If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation"**. A rich guardian, who has no need of the orphan's money, must not take any of it as pay. Looking after the child is a duty he owes, and payment for it is not permitted. Another explanation says he should not even reduce the orphan's wealth by the smallest amount.

**"but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision"**. The Arabic says "what is just and reasonable". ʿĀʾishah said, in a report recorded by Ibn Abī Ḥātim and also collected by al-Bukhārī, that this verse was revealed about the guardian of an orphan and concerns the work he does for the orphan's estate. A guardian who is poor, needy and without any other source of income may take a fair amount from the orphan's property for his basic needs. The amount must be just about enough to cover them. Different explanations tell what the measure is. It may be in proportion to the work he does on the property. It may be what keeps him from needing the orphan's wealth. It has also been said that he may take it as a loan to be paid back.

Aḥmad recorded that ʿAmr ibn Shuʿayb reported from his father, from his father, that a man asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, "I have no money, but I have an orphan under my care." The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

> "Eat from your orphan's wealth, without extravagance or waste, without mixing it with yours, and without saving your own money by spending his."

**"When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses"**. Allah commands the guardians to hand over the property of the orphans who have become adults and are seen to be wise, and to do so in the presence of witnesses, so that none of them can deny having received his money. Some say the witnesses should be reliable and pious people. A short explanation says it is also so that you are cleared of the duty and, if a dispute arises, you can point to clear proof. That same explanation calls the command one of guidance. It is also necessary to put a fair settlement with orphans in writing and have it witnessed, so that the executor's duty is honourably fulfilled with no question of later disputes or complaints.

**"And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner"**. Allah is enough as a Witness, a Reckoner and a Watcher over what guardians do for orphans and over the way they hand over the money, whether the property was complete and whole or deficient and short. Allah knows all of it. Another explanation says He guards the deeds of His creatures and takes account of them. A person who takes someone's affairs in hand must go forward knowing that any injustice he commits is known to Allah. The person whose affairs are being settled may be weak and unable to resist unfair treatment. But Allah will seize the one who settled them unjustly on the Day of Reckoning and punish him severely. That is certain, and no one escapes it.

### The occasion: Thābit ibn Rifāʿah and his uncle

The verse was revealed about Thābit ibn Rifāʿah al-Anṣārī and his uncle. Thābit's father Rifāʿah died while the boy was still very small. Thābit's uncle went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and said, "My brother's son is an orphan in my care. What is lawful for me to take from his wealth? And when should I give it back to him?" Allah then revealed this verse.

### Three stages of a child

Children go through three stages. The first is minority, before puberty. The second is after puberty. The third is after self-reliance and sound discretion in handling one's own affairs, which the Arabic calls rushd, the opposite of safāhah, foolishness. In the first stage, guardians are told to educate and train their wards by giving them hands-on experience, that is, small dealings in buying and selling done on their own. This is what "test the orphans" means. From it Imām Abū Ḥanīfah drew the ruling that buying and selling done by minor children with their guardian's permission is sound, valid and effective.

When children have grown up, reached puberty and become fit for marriage, the guardian checks how they have developed in experience, intelligence and dealings. Once it is clear that they understand profit and loss and handle their affairs satisfactorily, their property is handed over.

### When does a child become an adult?

Abū Dāwūd recorded in his Sunan that ʿAlī said he memorised these words from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ:

> "There is no orphanhood after puberty, nor a vow of silence from day to night."

In another hadith, ʿĀʾishah and other Companions reported that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> "The pen does not record the deeds of three: the child until he reaches puberty, the sleeper until he wakes, and the senile until he is sane."

A second view takes the age of fifteen as the age of adolescence. The two Ṣaḥīḥs record that Ibn ʿUmar said, "I was brought before the Prophet ﷺ on the eve of Uḥud, at fourteen, and he did not let me fight." He went on, "I was brought before him again on the eve of al-Khandaq, the Trench, at fifteen, and he let me join." When the hadith reached ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, he remarked, "This is the difference between a child and an adult."

Scholars differ on whether pubic hair is a sign of adulthood. The correct view is that it is, and the Sunnah supports it. Aḥmad collected a hadith from ʿAṭiyyah al-Qurazī, who said: "We were presented to the Prophet ﷺ on the day of Qurayẓah. Whoever had pubic hair was killed, and whoever had none was let go. I was one of those who had none, so I was let go." The four Sunan compilers recorded something similar, and at-Tirmidhī called it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ.

The Qur'an ties maturity to being fit for marriage and not to any count of years. Real maturity depends on the signs that adults show when they reach the threshold of adulthood. When these signs make a person fit to marry, he is regarded as mature, even if he is only thirteen or fourteen. If the signs never appear in a child, he is considered mature by age, and here jurists differ. Some fix eighteen for boys and seventeen for girls. Others fix fifteen for both. Among the Ḥanafiyyah, the fatwā is that boys and girls are both mature under Islamic law once they have completed their fifteenth year, whether or not the signs have appeared.

### When is "sound judgment" reached?

The Qur'an does not set a final time limit for rushd, sound understanding. So some jurists leaned toward keeping a child's property in the guardian's custody until full understanding is proved, even if that lasts the rest of his life. Imām Abū Ḥanīfah looked into the matter and concluded that the lack of understanding in question is the lack caused by childhood. Within ten years after puberty, the effect of childhood is gone. So fifteen years is the age of maturity and ten more years is the age of sound understanding. By the time a person has reached these 25 years, sound understanding is most likely to be there, something that was not possible while the barriers of childhood and youth stood in the way.

The Qur'an also uses the word "sound judgment" in an indefinite form, which suggests that full understanding and perfect sense are not strict conditions. A reasonable measure is enough to justify handing over the property. So even if perfect understanding has not come after waiting 25 years, the property is still handed over. Some people never reach perfect understanding and wisdom in their whole lives. They stay simple, innocent, rather shy and slow in practical dealings. They are not to be deprived of their property for that. A person who is wholly insane is under a separate rule. He stays in the category of immature children, and his property is never handed over to him until his insanity ends, even if his whole life passes in it.

### What a guardian may take for his work

The rule on payment for a guardian carries over to others. It applies to people who supervise awqāf (religious endowments, trusts and estates), manage mosques and religious schools, head the institutions of Muslim states, or serve their country and community in similar ways. Such services are called farḍ ʿalā al-kifāyah, a duty which, if some people carry it out, clears the rest. For these people too, the better and more virtuous course, if they have enough assets of their own to cover the necessary expenses of their families, is to take nothing from these institutions or from the public treasury. If they do not have funds of their own and they give their earning time to such service, they have the right to take funds from these institutions in proportion to their need.

The condition is "in proportion to need". A lot of people fix a small monthly salary on paper to satisfy legal requirements, while they spend far more on themselves and their families by other means, recklessly. There is no cure for this carelessness except the fear of Allah. The final words of the verse, "sufficient is Allah as a Reckoner", invite everyone, ordinary people and leaders alike, to see that the person who stays clear of unlawful gain is the one who fears the final reckoning with Allah. And the ability to do so comes from Allah.

### A warning from the Prophet ﷺ to Abū Dharr

Muslim recorded in his Ṣaḥīḥ that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said to Abū Dharr:

> "O Abū Dharr, you are weak, and I love for you what I love for myself. Do not become the leader of two people, and do not take on the guardianship of an orphan's property."

### The inner reading

To perceive maturity, on this reading, means to perceive decency and integrity. It is generosity, protecting one's honour, keeping company with elders and striving to see the good and to perform acts of devotion as the command lays down. It is also said that the mature person, the rashīd, is the one whom his Lord has guided. When a need comes upon him, he does not lean on his own strength and power, or on his own planning and choice.

### In short

- Orphans are to be tested through small dealings well before puberty, and their property is handed over in full once they are mature and show sound judgment, which means good in religion and wise with money.
- Maturity is tied to the signs of puberty and fitness for marriage. Where signs are absent, jurists set an age, most commonly fifteen, and sound judgment is needed only in a reasonable measure, with Abū Ḥanīfah allowing a further ten years.
- The guardian must not spend the orphan's wealth wastefully or hastily. A rich guardian takes nothing, and a poor one takes a fair amount for his needs and his work. The same principle applies to people who run endowments and community services.
- Witnesses are called when the property is handed over, so that no dispute arises, and Allah is enough as the Reckoner.
- The verse came down about Thābit ibn Rifāʿah and his uncle, and the Prophet ﷺ told Abū Dharr, a weak man, not to take on an orphan's property as a guardian.
