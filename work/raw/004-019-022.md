<<<VERSE 4:19>>>
The passage that opened with orphans and inheritance now guards the woman herself: "O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery. Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing". Three commands stand in the verse: no inheriting of women, no squeezing of their dowry, and honourable companionship even when the heart has gone cold.

### Phrase by phrase

**"It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will"** abolishes a practice of the Jāhiliyyah. Al-Bukhārī recorded that Ibn ʿAbbās said that when a man died, his male relatives held the right to do whatever they pleased with his wife. If one of them wished, he married her, or he passed her in marriage to another man against payment, or barred her from marriage altogether, for they claimed a better right to her than her own family. The verse came down on exactly that.

**"or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺"** forbids the second abuse. The husband is forbidden to use harshness until the woman hands back all or part of her dowry, or surrenders one of her rights under coercion and oppression.

**"unless they are found guilty of adultery"** is the one exception, and its exact width was debated, as the section below shows.

**"Treat them fairly"** is the standing command of companionship, and **"If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing"** forbids the husband to act on dislike alone, since the disliked thing may be carrying a blessing he cannot see.

### The world the verse ended

The root of all the old abuses was one assumption: that a man owned not only the property of the woman but her very life. A woman taken in marriage was passed on, after her husband's death, like a piece of property to his heirs, who became the new owners of the estate and of the widow together. They could marry her themselves or give her in marriage to somebody else against payment. The son of the husband from another wife could marry her himself once his father died. From this one social ill grew hundreds of others. Whatever a woman received by inheritance or gift from her parents was devoured by the men of her husband's house. If she did come into possession of property, her relatives by marriage would block any remarriage, so that her share never left the house, and they waited for her to die where she was. A husband who tired of his wife on a whim, with no shortcoming on her side, would neither live with her nor divorce her, but harass her until she returned her jewellery and dower, or forgo the dower still unpaid. Even after divorce, some husbands blocked the woman's remarriage to squeeze the dower out of her.

The verse names the case that stood for all of it. Before Islam, the wife of a deceased father was inherited by the eldest son like any property. The report of the occasion connects it with Kabshah bint Maʿān al-Anṣāriyyah and with Muḥṣin ibn Abī Qays al-Anṣārī. If the inherited woman was rich and beautiful, the son married her without giving any dowry. If she was neither rich nor young nor beautiful, he left her untouched, sometimes for life, unless she ransomed herself with her own money. Allah forbade the practice.

The Qur'an could have used a simple prohibitive order, and instead it said: it is not lawful for you. The negation of lawfulness itself carries a ruling. Should a man proceed to take an adult woman as a wife with no consent and no permission from her, the marriage entered upon is null and void, and no husband-wife relationship is established from it, nor do the injunctions of inheritance or lineage follow from it. Likewise, if a man forces a woman to return the dower or to forgo the outstanding one, a return extracted by force, or a forgiveness forced, counts for nothing in the sight of the sharīʿah, and what is taken that way never becomes lawful, for both are patently ḥarām (forbidden). The word "against their will" is a statement of fact rather than a condition, as al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ notes. A woman in her normal senses would never agree to being owned, and the Islamic law will not concede the position even if some woman, out of her mind, approves of it. What was given to the wife as a gift, or as her own property, cannot be taken back by husband or heirs, though an article lent for temporary use, like jewellery she does not own, remains askable.

### What counts as open lewdness

The exception was read two ways. Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib and ash-Shaʿbī, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Muḥammad ibn Sīrīn, Mujāhid with ʿIkrimah, ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Zayd ibn Aslam with Abū Ṣāliḥ, as-Suddī and ad-Daḥḥāk, Abū Qilābah, and Ibn Masʿūd with Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd ibn Abī Hilāl all said it refers to illicit sex: if the wife commits adultery, the husband may take back the dowry he gave her, and may press her until she returns it in exchange for a khulʿ, a release bought with compensation. Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrimah and ad-Daḥḥāk said the fāḥishah here is disobedience and defiance. In one of the later commentaries, Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿĀʾishah and ad-Daḥḥāk are given for disobedience and indecent abuse against the husband, while Abū Qilābah and al-Ḥasan take it as immodesty and adultery in this place. Ibn Jarīr chose the general view: the word covers adultery, disobedience, defiance and rudeness together, so that the husband may press the wife who does any of these until she forfeits all or part of her rights, and then he separates from her. The anchor for the whole discussion stands in Sūrat al-Baqarah: "It is not lawful for you to take back any of what you have given them, except when both parties fear that they would be unable to keep the limits ordained by Allah" (2:229).

### Live with them honourably

The command of companionship was spelled out in practice: kind words, kind treatment, and an appealing appearance, as much as a man can manage, the same as he likes for himself from her. "And they have rights similar over them to what is reasonable" (2:228). The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "The best among you is he who is the best with his family. Verily, I am the best one among you with my family." His way with his wives was kindness, cheerfulness, play, compassion, spending and laughter. He raced with ʿĀʾishah, the Mother of the Faithful, as a kindness to her. She said: "The Messenger of Allah ﷺ raced with me and I won the race. This occurred before I gained weight, and afterwards I raced with him again, and he won that race. He said, 'This is for that.'" When he was at the home of one of his wives, sometimes all his wives would meet there and eat together, then return to their homes. He and the wife whose night it was slept in one bed, and he would remove his upper garment and sleep in his lower garment, and he would talk with her after the ʿIshāʾ prayer before sleeping. "Indeed in the Messenger of Allah you have a good example to follow" (33:21). In his Ṣaḥīḥ, Muslim recorded that Jābir said that in the speech the Prophet ﷺ gave at the Farewell Ḥajj he said: "Be kind with women, for you have taken them by Allah's covenant and earned the right to have sexual relations with them by Allah's word."

A deeper justice stands under the command. Property is inanimate, without feeling, and so a proper object of inheritance. A human being has a sensate, independent existence, and so possesses the right to decide her future by her own choice. The widow is not part of the estate to be exploited as the successors decide.

### The disliked wife

The verse ends by holding the husband to patience. Ibn ʿAbbās commented that the husband may come to feel compassion toward a wife he had disliked, and Allah gives him a child with her, and that child carries tremendous goodness. An authentic hadith states: "No believing man should hate his believing wife. If he dislikes a part of her conduct, he would surely like another." One reading of the closing words says plainly that God may provide a righteous child through the very companion whose company the man resents, and the patience of keeping a disliked wife carries reward in this life and the Hereafter. The counsel runs wider than marriage. Living collectively is the condition of human life, and no one survives alone. With tempers differing, irritants crop up, and the only workable approach is to ignore complaints, avoid friction, and give each member of the household the chance to exercise his or her abilities. When one decides to separate instead, one tends to exaggerate the other's faults and fabricate allegations so the weaker party gives ground. That is against the covenant, and a covenant, written or unwritten, is sacred and binds both parties alike.

### The inner reading

Read inwardly, the verse's address is a whole liturgy in itself: the "O" is the call, the "you" the admonishment, the "who have faith" the bearing of witness, and the ruling that follows. Women are considered weak, imprisoned beneath severity, so beware of tormenting them. Neither rule over them through scheming and deceit, nor be severe with them, nor demand of them anything the sharīʿah does not approve. Live with them honourably, by the teachings of the religion and the courtesy of the submitted. Show them the road of the religion, teach them the courteous acts of being Muslim, and protect them from the Fire, as Allah says elsewhere: "Shield yourselves and your families from a Fire" (66:6). Preserve the courtesies of companionship and tolerate suffering from them, but do not lay on them the burden of your service. And though the outward address is to husbands, the allusion reaches every Muslim: never see yourself as having a mandatory due from, or an excellence over, any other Muslim, do not push yourself on those beneath you, and do not use force against the weak, but give them comfort and draw near them. A revelation came to Dāwūd (David): "O David, if you see someone broken in My road, or someone whose heart has been lost in My work, take care to serve him."

### What the passage does next

The surah does not leave the subject here. Verses 20 and 21 tighten the dowry rule from the other side: even a qinṭār, the word whose meaning was given in the commentary on Āl-ʿImrān, may not be reclaimed, a ruling ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb once tested from the minbar with a four-hundred-dirham cap, until a woman of Quraysh stood him down with the Qur'an itself. The two Ṣaḥīḥs then carry the case of the imprecated spouses over the dowry. And the passage closes by sealing one marriage forever: the wife of the father, whose prohibition ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ read as earning the hatred of Allah, and whose violator was dealt with by the mission of Abū Burdah, as the last verse of this group shows. The whole set of rulings is law for this entire ummah, and its shared principle, that the shameful acts named al-fawāḥish are not even to be approached (6:151), runs from the first verse of the group to the last.

### In short

- Women are not estate property. The heirs of a deceased man have no ownership of his widow, and a marriage contracted without the woman's consent is null and void.
- Harassing a wife to recover the dowry is forbidden, with one debated exception: open lewdness, read either as adultery or as defiant disobedience, and the general view covers both.
- Companionship must be honourable, on the pattern of the Prophet's ﷺ own household, from the race he lost and won with ʿĀʾishah to the Farewell Ḥajj command to be kind with women.
- Dislike is not a ground for cruelty, for the disliked wife may carry a blessing, and the believing man is told never to hate her outright.
- Read inwardly, the verse forbids every Muslim to claim excellence over another, and serves the broken in God's road, as Dāwūd was told.

<<<VERSE 4:20>>>
The verse of the qinṭār takes the dowry rule to its extreme case: "If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully". The rule is now absolute. Where the previous verse allowed recovery only in the case of the wife's open lewdness, this one removes every excuse based on the size of the amount.

### Phrase by phrase

**"If you desire to replace a wife with another"** covers the man who, following his own desire, divorces one wife to marry another in her place, or adds a wife to the one he has. The cause is his wish alone, with no fault on the woman's side.

**"and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺"** names the qinṭār, the vast weight of gold, to say that the size of the dowry changes nothing. The verse itself is clear evidence that a dowry may be substantial.

**"do not take any of it back"** is the prohibition, without exception for the amount, and **"Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully"** is the rebuke. The taking would be calumny and open sin, and the rebuke is meant as a disavowal, not a question.

### Why the claim is an imputation

The logic of the closing words reaches back to verse 19. The only situation in which a husband may claim the dowry back is the wife's shameful act, on the reading the previous verse allowed. So a man who takes it back without that cause is, by his own act, imputing a shameful act to her, and since she has committed none, his claim is a false imputation and an open sin. Where the woman has shown no contumacy and no immodesty, nothing may be claimed from her in exchange for divorce, however much was given, and the dower still outstanding cannot be forced from her either. The shameful acts, the fawāḥish, are of the family of sins the Qur'an commands not even to approach, whether committed openly or secretly (6:151), and unlawful sex itself is named "a Fahishah and an evil way" (17:32). The rights run both ways, for the wives "have rights similar over them to what is reasonable" (2:228), and the exception in Sūrat al-Baqarah stands only where both parties fear they cannot keep the limits of Allah (2:229).

### The caliph corrected

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb used to discourage large dowries, and then changed his view. Imam Aḥmad recorded that Abū al-ʿAjfāʾ as-Sulamī heard ʿUmar say: "Do not exaggerate with the dowry of women." Had the practice been an honour in this world, or a part of taqwā, the Prophet ﷺ would have had more right to it than anyone. Yet: "The Messenger of Allah ﷺ never gave any of his wives, nor did any of his daughters receive, a dowry more than twelve uqiyyah." Men would sometimes pay a huge dowry precisely to hide enmity toward the wife inside it. Aḥmad and the collectors of the Sunan collected it through various chains, and at-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ.

Then came the minbar. Al-Ḥāfiẓ Abū Yaʿlā recorded that Masrūq said that ʿUmar stood on the minbar of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and asked the people why they exaggerated concerning the dowry, when the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions used to pay up to four hundred dirhams or less. Had paying more been taqwā or honour, they would not have been outdone in it. He said he did not want to hear of a man paying more than four hundred dirhams. Coming down, he was met by a woman of Quraysh who asked whether he had prohibited more than four hundred dirhams in dowry. He said yes. She asked whether he had not heard what Allah sent down, giving a man who has given one of his wives a qinṭār. ʿUmar said: "O Allah, forgive me." He went back up and told the people to pay what they liked of their money. The chain of the hadith is strong. The dowry may be large, and it may not be clawed back.

### The way of the generous

Read inwardly, the verse embodies generosity itself within the religion of friendship, and lays smooth the foundation of chivalry. The counsel given is that cruelty of separation must never be joined to the snatching back of livelihood, for such is not the work of the generous. The scar of separation is already cut into the poor woman's heart, so do not cut off the hand of her expenditure, for taking back what was given only lays a second scar upon the first. Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī divorced a wife and then sent her plentiful wealth, saying: "The tribulation of our separation is enough. I must not put upon her the suffering of neediness as well." The wealth was 40,000 dirhams. The woman dumped it on the ground and said: "Paltry goods from a departed lover!" So it is with what is given grudgingly, even a lot of it, after the khulʿ and harassment the earlier verses forbade. The standard for the believing man is the example of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ (33:21), whose way with his wives was cheerfulness and spending, and against it the claim on a sinless wife's dowry, with the imputation it carries, stands as far from his way as the case the two Ṣaḥīḥs record of the spouses who imprecated each other, or the closing prohibition on the wives of fathers, whose enforcement was given to Abū Burdah, and the wider list of prohibited women that begins with two sisters in wedlock at the same time (4:23).

### In short

- A husband replacing one wife with another may reclaim nothing of the dowry, not from a qinṭār of gold, for the woman is at no fault.
- The claim without cause is itself an imputation of lewdness, and so a calumny and an open sin.
- ʿUmar capped dowries at four hundred dirhams until a woman of Quraysh corrected him from the Qur'an, and the cap was withdrawn.
- The generous add gifts at separation rather than take back, as Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī did, for the way of the Prophet ﷺ is the standard.

<<<VERSE 4:21>>>
The question that ends the argument: "And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment". Verse 20 said the taking would be sinful. Verse 21 asks how it is even conceivable.

### Phrase by phrase

**"after having enjoyed each other intimately"** refers to sexual intercourse, as Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, as-Suddī and several others said. The marriage has been consummated, the couple have come together under one cover, and it is precisely that consummation which makes the dowry due and owned. The word carries more than the act. What has occurred between husband and wife is relationship, love, enjoyment and the sharing of secrets, and greed for the wealth of a woman after all that is shameful as well as forbidden.

**"and she has taken from you a firm commitment"** is the marriage tie itself, according to Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr. The covenant is sworn with the name of Allah, witnessed by a gathered company of people, and preceded by a khuṭbah. It is a binding pledge with two branches: that the wife be retained honourably or released with kindness. Through this covenant and the word of Allah she became lawful to her husband, by His decree and His sacred law.

### The case of the imprecated spouses

The two Ṣaḥīḥs record the ruling that fixes what the dowry pays for. In the case of the spouses who pronounced the mutual imprecation, the mulaʿanah, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said three times:

> Allah knows that one of you is a liar, so would any of you repent?

The man said: "O Messenger of Allah, my money," meaning the dowry he had given his wife. The Messenger said:

> You have no money. If you are the one who said the truth, the dowry is in return for the right to have sexual relations with her. If you are the one who uttered the lie, then this money is even farther from your reach.

The dowry stands or falls with the consummation the covenant licensed, and the man who destroys the marriage by a lie loses the furthest claim of all.

### The farewell address

Jābir, in the account his Ṣaḥīḥ carries from Muslim, reported that in the speech of the Farewell Ḥajj the Prophet ﷺ said: "Be kind with women, for you have taken them by Allah's covenant and earned the right to have sexual relations with them by Allah's word." The verse and the farewell address say one thing. The right the husband holds was granted by a covenant taken in the name of Allah, and the only fitting response to a covenant of that rank is kindness, not extortion.

### The weight of the covenant

A covenant stands sacred with God. Written or unwritten, it binds in essence, holding the two parties alike and leaving neither free to walk out of it. The command of verse 19 to live with them honourably and the question of verse 21 stand on the same covenant, and the previous verse's allowance, in the single case of the wife's open lewdness, was the exception the law itself drew, not a licence for appetite. Read inwardly, the prior companionship has a firm inviolability, and respect for its due is to stop and keep to what the covenant requires. The Companions understood the gravity. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, the same caliph who yielded on the minbar to the woman of Quraysh, held the marriage covenant in awe, and the household of the Prophet ﷺ, from the race with ʿĀʾishah to the gathering of his wives at one table, modelled what the covenant demands. The passage will close with the severest breach of covenant respect there is, the marriage of a father's wife, an act classed with the fawāḥish that may not even be approached (6:151), with the imputation of the imprecated spouses condemned in the two Ṣaḥīḥs, and with the mission of Abū Burdah against the man who committed it, just before the surah lists the women prohibited forever, beginning with two sisters in wedlock at the same time (4:23), all of it under the same principle of rights held and honoured, "for they have rights similar over them to what is reasonable" (2:228), and the example of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ (33:21).

### In short

- The intimacy of marriage is what makes the dowry due, and the covenant of marriage is what made the intimacy lawful. To enjoy both and then reclaim the payment is theft from one's own oath.
- In the imprecation case the Prophet ﷺ ruled that the dowry is the price of the consummated right, and the lying spouse has no claim on it at all.
- The Farewell Ḥajj address names the covenant of Allah as what grants the husband's right, and kindness as its only fitting use.
- The covenant binds both parties, and its gravity is the measure of every breach, from dowry extortion to the marriage of a father's wife.

<<<VERSE 4:22>>>
The passage on women closes its first movement with a prohibition that restores the honour of fatherhood: "Do not marry former wives of your fathers—except what was done previously. It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice". One sentence ends a practice of the Jāhiliyyah and stamps it with three names of condemnation.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Do not marry former wives of your fathers"** prohibits the women whom the father married, in honour and respect to the fathers, so that no son may ever approach the wife of his father, in the father's lifetime or after his death. As soon as the father's marriage contract is conducted, the woman is closed to the son of her husband, on which ruling the scholars hold consensus. The prohibition needs no consummation by the father to take effect.

**"except what was done previously"** forgives what happened in the time of ignorance before Islam. The maxim that the past is past applies: orders based on things now irrelevant breed endless problems, so the past is left to Allah and the present reformed.

**"It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice"** gives the three weights of the act. Shameful, an obscenity. Despicable, the word maqtan: offensive and hated. ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ said it means Allah will hate him who does it. And an evil way, the worst path to follow.

### The world it closed

During the Jāhiliyyah the people prohibited what Allah prohibits concerning marriage, except in two matters: marrying the stepmother, and taking two sisters as rival wives. Ibn Jarīr recorded that from Ibn ʿAbbās, and similar reports come from ʿAṭāʾ and Qatādah. This verse and the next closed both: "And marry not women whom your fathers married," and "and two sisters in wedlock at the same time" (4:23). How can someone keep calling a woman his mother for years and then stoop to make her his wife once the father dies? The act is itself a sin, and it breeds hate, for a man who marries a woman tends to resent whoever was married to her before him, and this sin teaches the son to resent his own father. The same logic protects the Mothers of the Faithful, who were not allowed to any man after the Messenger ﷺ. He is as a father to the believers, and far more, and his right comes before each person's love of himself. The act belongs to the family of sins the Qur'an fences off entirely: "Come not near to al-Fawāḥish (shameful acts) whether committed openly or secretly" (6:151), and "And come not near to unlawful sex. Verily, it is a Fahishah and an evil way" (17:32).

### The occasions

Several cases were named with the verse's revelation. Hisn ibn Abī al-Qays took as wife Kubayshah bint Maʿān, who had been his father's wife. Al-Aswad ibn Khalaf married the wife of his father. Ṣafwān ibn Umayyah married his father's wife Fakhītah bint al-Aswad ibn al-Muṭṭalib. Manẓūr ibn Zabbān married the wife of his father Mulaykah bint Khārijah. And Ashʿat ibn Sawār told of the case that brought the question: Abū Qays, one of the righteous people of Madinah, died, and his son Qays proposed to his father's wife. She said to him: "But I consider you like my son. I will consult the Messenger of Allah, Allah bless him and give him peace, to see what he commands." She went and informed him, and Allah revealed this verse. Another report names the verse's subject as Muḥṣin ibn Abī Qays al-Anṣārī.

### The punishment

The offence was treated as reversion from the religion. Those who commit this practice deserve capital punishment and confiscation of their property, which is given to the Muslim treasury. Al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, in a report Aḥmad and the Sunan collectors carry, told how the Messenger of Allah ﷺ despatched his uncle Abū Burdah to a man who had married his stepmother, with orders to execute him and confiscate his money. The verse was law for the whole ummah, and the law was enforced.

### Rulings from the verse

The verse introduces the muḥarramāt, the women prohibited in marriage, whose list the coming verses will give in full. Some are prohibited forever, by kinship, by fosterage, or by relationship through marriage, and never become lawful under any condition. Others are prohibited only until they are bound in marriage with someone else. From this verse the jurists drew its edges both ways. The bare fact that the father married a woman forbids her to the son, with no need for consummation. By the same principle the father may not take the wife of his son, even where the son's marriage went no further than the formal nikāḥ, with the wife not yet having come to live with him. Whoever holds to taqwā holds to the limits of respect the verse guards.

### The inner reading

Read inwardly, the verse alludes to the protection of honour and staying within the limits of respect. Since a man naturally hates that another should bed his wife, sons are barred from trampling the father's rights in pursuit of the father's wife. The same jealousy of honour, turned upward, is why the believers' mothers were sealed after the Prophet ﷺ, whose example the Qur'an commands: "Indeed in the Messenger of Allah you have a good example to follow" (33:21). And the covenant-honour of this whole passage, from verse 19 through the farewell address recorded by Muslim from Jābir at the Ḥajj, to the dowry ruling of the two Ṣaḥīḥs, reaches its peak here, for the father's bed is the first covenant of all.

### In short

- The wife of one's father is prohibited by the marriage contract alone, forever, and the father's wife is equally closed to no son by any device of the Jāhiliyyah.
- The prohibition was new law for the ummah: the pre-Islamic custom that allowed it, along with rival sisters, was ended, the past forgiven.
- The act is obscene, hated by Allah, and an evil way, classed among the fawāḥish not even to be approached, and it was punished in this world by execution and confiscation, as Abū Burdah's mission shows.
- The verse opens the surah's list of prohibited women and guards, above all, the honour of the father and the sanctity of the covenant.
