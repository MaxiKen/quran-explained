<<<VERSE 3:149>>>
> “O believers! If you yield to the disbelievers, they will drag you back to disbelief—and you will become losers” (3:149)

The verse warns believers, in the crisis after Uhud, not to follow voices urging them to abandon faith. It is a warning about surrendering religious conviction under pressure, not a command to treat every non-Muslim as an enemy.

### Phrase by phrase

**“O believers!”** addresses the companions who had just endured a frightening setback. A rumor said the Prophet ﷺ had been killed, and the community was shaken. In that moment, some voices urged the Muslims to return to their former religion and leave the conflict behind.

**“If you yield to the disbelievers”** means accepting advice that would pull believers away from the truth and the commitments they had made to Allah. The surrounding reports describe hypocrites exploiting the confusion at Uhud and encouraging a return to the old faith. The warning concerns that pressure and its aims. It should not be turned into a blanket judgment about every person outside Islam or a reason to refuse fair dealings and peaceful counsel.

**“they will drag you back to disbelief”** describes the consequence of abandoning faith and following a call to renounce it. The expression of turning back on one’s heels pictures a reversal after having recognized and accepted the message. The verse warns believers to examine where advice leads, rather than being persuaded by fear or an apparently convenient solution.

**“and you will become losers”** means that abandoning faith would harm the people who turn away, not Allah. They would lose the guidance and the lasting reward of the Hereafter. The temporary setback at Uhud did not prove Islam false, nor did the rumor about the Prophet ﷺ invalidate the message.

### A warning for the moment of confusion

The people who encouraged reversion used the Prophet’s apparent absence as an argument to abandon the religion. The verse answers that the message does not belong to one person or one military outcome. The believers were to hold to what Allah had revealed and to follow the Messenger’s teaching, rather than mistake a crisis for evidence that faith had failed.

The passage does not say that believers should never listen to advice from outside their community. It asks them not to accept counsel that requires disbelief or betrayal of their obligations. Advice is weighed by its truth and consequences, not merely by the identity of the speaker.

### The battle behind the warning

Al-Baraʾ’s report in Al-Bukhari recalls Abu Sufyan calling out after Uhud to ask whether Muhammad, Abu Bakr, and Umar were present. Abu Bakr was the son of Abu Quhafah, and Umar the son of Al-Khattab. The Prophet ﷺ first told the companions not to answer, then directed them to affirm that Allah is their protector. The exchange also recalls Badr, when Quraysh had invoked Hubal and the Muslims affirmed Allah’s greatness. It shows that the rumor of the Prophet’s death was not a reason to return to kufr or shirk.

The confusion at Uhud was not a settled rejection of faith. Some companions retreated in the moment, then returned to the Prophet and continued their commitment. Allah’s warning addresses the danger of treating fear as a reason to abandon the path of Islam. The Qur’an’s call is to remain grateful and steadfast, not to deify the Prophet or make worldly safety the highest aim.

### In short

- The verse addresses pressure to abandon faith after Uhud.
- It is not a blanket condemnation of every non-Muslim or a ban on fair relations.
- Believers should not let fear turn a setback into a reason to renounce their faith.

<<<VERSE 3:150>>>
> “But no! Allah is your Guardian, and He is the best Helper” (3:150)

This verse answers the pressure described in the previous one: believers are to rely on Allah, their protector, rather than surrender their faith to those who would lead them away from it.

### Phrase by phrase

**“But no!”** rejects the idea that the believers should turn back because of their losses or because the Prophet ﷺ was rumored to have been killed. Their security is not ultimately dependent on a human leader’s continued presence. The Prophet is followed because he conveys Allah’s guidance, while Allah remains the protector of the community.

**“Allah is your Guardian”** means He cares for the believers, guides them, and supports them through trials. This does not mean they should neglect practical means. At Uhud, the Prophet ﷺ organized the army and appointed archers. Trust in Allah accompanied preparation and obedience.

**“and He is the best Helper”** means that Allah’s support is greater than any power people can offer. The community asks Him for help, follows His commands, and does not compromise its faith in the hope of gaining support from those urging it to disbelieve.

A hadith from Jabir bin Abdullah, preserved in the Two Sahihs, describes five favors given to the Prophet ﷺ: he was aided with awe affecting the enemy from a month’s distance, the earth was made a place of prayer and a means of purification, lawful spoils were permitted to his community, he was granted intercession, and his message was sent to all humanity. These favors show that Allah supported the Prophet’s mission in ways previous communities had not received. They do not mean Muslims should seek war. The same report presents the earth as a place for worship and cleanliness, and describes the Prophet’s universal mission.

### Help during the crisis at Uhud

The companions’ reliance on Allah was tested when Abu Sufyan’s Quraysh army attacked and the Muslims were pushed back. The Prophet ﷺ was defended by people including Talhah ibn ʿUbayd Allah and Saʿd ibn Abi Waqqas. Reports from Sahl bin Saʿd and Abu Uthman an-Nahdi describe Talhah shielding the Prophet and Saʿd receiving arrows to defend him. The believers did not depend on their own strength alone. They used the means available and asked Allah for help.

The exchange after the battle, reported by Al-Baraʾ, recalls Abu Sufyan asking whether Abu Bakr, whose father was Abu Quhafah, and Umar, son of Al-Khattab, were present. The companions’ response was to affirm that Allah is their protector. This is the meaning of the verse: believers can value leaders and companions while relying ultimately on Allah, who does not die and is the best Helper.

### In short

- Allah is the believers’ protector when they face pressure and loss.
- Reliance on Him goes together with preparation and obedience.
- The Prophet’s mission and the community’s help come from Allah.

<<<VERSE 3:151>>>
> “We will cast horror into the hearts of the disbelievers for associating ˹false gods˺ with Allah—a practice He has never authorized. The Fire will be their home—what an evil place for the wrongdoers to stay” (3:151)

The verse describes Allah placing fear in the hearts of the Quraysh after Uhud, preventing them from returning to attack Madinah. It condemns shirk, associating partners with Allah, and warns that no one can invent divine approval for it.

### Phrase by phrase

**“We will cast horror into the hearts of the disbelievers”** refers in the immediate setting to the Quraysh force after the battle of Uhud. They withdrew toward Makkah, then considered returning to finish off the Muslims. Reports say Allah placed fear in their hearts, and they abandoned the plan. The Muslim community was spared a renewed assault at a moment when it was injured and vulnerable.

The verse describes this outcome as help from Allah. It does not promise that every opponent will always be frightened or that believers will never face danger. Nor does it ask Muslims to cultivate terror among peaceful people. The event concerns an army that had fought them and was considering another attack.

**“for associating false gods with Allah”** names shirk as a grave wrong. The Quraysh worshipped idols and attributed divine status to them. **“a practice He has never authorized”** means that their worship had no revelation or valid proof from Allah. Human beings cannot invent permission to worship others alongside Him.

The verse condemns the belief and practice of shirk. It does not authorize coercion or injustice against people. Judgment belongs to Allah, who knows each person and their circumstances. The believers are called to worship Allah alone and to remain just in their dealings.

**“The Fire will be their home”** warns that persistent rejection and wrongdoing have consequences in the Hereafter. **“what an evil place for the wrongdoers to stay”** makes the warning vivid. The verse does not invite believers to pronounce the final fate of named individuals. Allah knows who repents and who persists until death.

Al-Suddi relates that Abu Sufyan and the Quraysh regretted leaving the Muslims alive and considered turning back. Allah’s gift of fear prevented their return. This report places the verse in the vulnerable aftermath of Uhud and shows the help described in 3:150.

### The aftermath of the battle

The report about the Quraysh’s retreat comes after the loss of the Muslim position at Uhud. The Prophet ﷺ remained in danger. Musʿab ibn ʿUmayr, from Banu Abd Ad-Dar, was killed while shielding him from Ubayy ibn Khalaf. Urwah ibn Az-Zubayr relates this encounter. Ubayy later died from his wound, and the report cites the warning that the wrongdoers will recognize their end (67:11). These details show the human danger of the battle, while the verse’s central emphasis is Allah’s protection and judgment.

The Quraysh had advanced from Makkah under Abu Sufyan. Their decision not to return was not due to a peaceful agreement or a change in their beliefs. They were deterred by fear Allah placed in their hearts. The Muslims were given a chance to recover and prepare, and this restraint did not absolve either side of responsibility for its actions.

### In short

- Allah’s help at Uhud included preventing a renewed attack.
- Shirk has no authority from Allah, and the verse warns of its consequence.
- The verse concerns hostile forces in a battle and does not permit injustice against peaceful people.

<<<VERSE 3:152>>>
> “Indeed, Allah fulfilled His promise to you when you ˹initially˺ swept them away by His Will, then your courage weakened and you disputed about the command and disobeyed, after Allah had brought victory within your reach. Some of you were after worldly gain while others desired a heavenly reward. He denied you victory over them as a test, yet He has pardoned you. And Allah is Gracious to the believers” (3:152)

The verse explains the turning point at Uhud. The Muslims initially gained the advantage, but many archers left their assigned position after disagreeing about the Prophet’s command. The setback tested them, and Allah nevertheless forgave them.

### Phrase by phrase

**“Allah fulfilled His promise to you”** recalls the early success at Uhud. The Muslims drove back the Quraysh by Allah’s permission. The victory was real, but it was not a guarantee that the battle was over or that the believers could disregard their orders.

**“then your courage weakened”** describes the moment when some companions hesitated as the battle changed. Ibn Abbas explained the Arabic term as losing courage. The word does not mean the believers stopped being Muslims. It describes a human lapse during a dangerous event.

**“and you disputed about the command and disobeyed”** refers especially to the archers placed on the rise under Abdullah ibn Jubayr. The Prophet ﷺ ordered them to stay at their post whether the Muslims seemed to be winning or losing. When the Quraysh appeared to retreat, some archers thought the battle had ended and wanted to join those collecting spoils. Abdullah ibn Jubayr reminded them of the order. Most left, while he and a small group remained.

Al-Baraʾ reports that the Prophet ﷺ had appointed Abdullah ibn Jubayr commander of the archers and told them not to abandon their position. Al-Bukhari records the report. Khalid ibn al-Walid led cavalry around the hill and attacked the Muslim rear after the archers left. The Muslims were thrown into confusion, and the Quraysh regained the initiative. The event shows why following a clear command matters even when one’s own judgment suggests the situation has changed.

**“after Allah had brought victory within your reach”** means the believers had seen success and the opposing force was withdrawing. It was precisely at that moment that some assumed they no longer needed to remain at their post. The verse does not say that gathering lawful spoils was inherently forbidden. It says the archers should not have left the responsibility entrusted to them.

**“Some of you were after worldly gain while others desired a heavenly reward”** distinguishes between those who left to pursue spoils and those who remained with Abdullah ibn Jubayr. The verse does not say that every companion who moved had a corrupt heart or that collecting spoils was unlawful. Their share was governed by the rules of the campaign. Yet the desire to take part in collecting them contributed to neglecting the Prophet’s explicit instruction.

Muhammad ibn Ishaq transmits an account from Az-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwam about seeing the archers leave the hill and the Quraysh cavalry attack from behind. A woman of the Harithiyyah clan then raised the Quraysh standard after its bearers had fallen. This detail helps explain how the battle’s momentum changed, but the main lesson remains the failure to hold the assigned position.

**“He denied you victory over them as a test”** means the change in fortune exposed the believers’ conduct and allowed them to learn. It was a test, not proof that Allah had abandoned them. The verse makes this explicit through the next statement.

**“yet He has pardoned you”** is a declaration of mercy. The companions’ mistake was corrected, but it did not erase their standing as believers. Allah’s pardon also teaches the community not to turn this event into a pretext for condemning every person who faltered.

**“And Allah is Gracious to the believers”** means His favor continued even after their disobedience. He granted victory at first, tested them, and forgave them. The believers were to respond with gratitude, renewed discipline, and greater care in following the Messenger.

### What the verse does and does not say

The verse is honest about the archers’ mistake. It does not conceal the fact that disobedience had consequences. At the same time, it acknowledges that some remained at their post and that Allah forgave the companions. It does not call the whole army hypocritical or declare that those who left had abandoned Islam.

The distinction between seeking worldly gain and seeking the Hereafter is a warning about intention. It should not be used to deny that people have ordinary needs or lawful rights to the proceeds of a campaign. The key failure was leaving a specific military position against a clear order. The lesson for any community is to distinguish between personal preference and an entrusted responsibility.

Reports mention that Abu Sufyan called out after the battle and asked whether Muhammad, Abu Bakr, and Umar were present. The Prophet ﷺ first told the companions not to answer, then permitted a response affirming that Allah is greater and the believers’ protector. Abu Sufyan also invoked Hubal and al-ʿUzza, while the Prophet directed the Muslims to affirm Allah’s greatness. The exchange shows the contrast between worship of idols and reliance on Allah, without changing the fact that the Muslims had suffered a serious setback.

### The companions’ actions after the lines broke

Al-Baraʾ’s hadith records Abu Sufyan asking about Abu Bakr, the son of Abu Quhafah, and Umar, the son of Al-Khattab. The companions answered that Allah is greater and is their protector. The story also recalls that the Quraysh had fought at Badr and that the early victory at Uhud did not last once the rear was exposed.

Other reports describe Anas ibn an-Nadr, who had missed Badr and hoped to fight beside the Prophet. At Uhud he advanced when others retreated and was killed. Al-Bukhari records this account. Saʿd ibn Abi Waqqas later recalled receiving arrows from the Prophet, and Sahl bin Saʿd described Talhah’s injured hand as he shielded him. Abu Uthman an-Nahdi reports that Talhah and Saʿd stayed near the Prophet. The accounts show different forms of loyalty and courage, while the verse still acknowledges the error of leaving the assigned position.

A report from Saʿid ibn al-Musayyib gives Saʿd’s memory of the Prophet encouraging him to shoot. Another report attributed to Az-Zuhri and transmitted through Urwah ibn Az-Zubayr describes Ubayy ibn Khalaf’s attack and death, which is linked to the warning in 67:11. Ibn Marduwyah preserves different explanations from Ibn Abbas, Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf, Qatadah, and As-Suddi about the order of the two griefs. These reports help explain why the battle remained painful even after Allah declared His pardon.

### In short

- The archers’ departure from their post turned the battle’s course.
- Some pursued spoils, while others stayed, but the error was disobeying the Prophet’s order.
- Allah describes the event as a test and expressly says He pardoned the believers.

<<<VERSE 3:153>>>
> “˹Remember˺ when you were running far away ˹in panic˺—not looking at anyone—while the Messenger was calling to you from behind! So Allah rewarded your disobedience with distress upon distress. Now, do not grieve over the victory you were denied or the injury you suffered. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do” (3:153)

The verse recalls the confusion and retreat at Uhud. Some believers fled toward Madinah or climbed the mountain while the Prophet ﷺ called them back. Their distress was intensified by the rumor of his death, but Allah later forgave them and taught them not to remain trapped in grief.

### Phrase by phrase

**“when you were running far away”** describes the companions who fled from the battlefield after the Muslim lines broke. Some headed toward Madinah, while others climbed Mount Uhud. The verb can describe going uphill, and the accounts include both movements. The verse openly rebukes the retreat, but it does not say that every person who ran had renounced Islam. It describes panic and confusion in a battle.

**“not looking at anyone”** means that fear narrowed their attention. They did not stop to see what had happened to their companions or to follow the Prophet’s call. Their actions were a response to the sudden reversal, but the verse still holds them accountable for leaving the Messenger behind.

**“while the Messenger was calling to you from behind”** reminds the believers that the Prophet ﷺ was still alive and urging them to return. As-Suddi reports that he called, “Come to me, servants of Allah.” Some could not hear him through the noise and confusion. Others did not recognize his voice. The call continued even while the Muslims were dispersing.

The Prophet ﷺ was defended by companions who remained close. Qays ibn Abi Hazim saw Talhah’s hand become injured while he shielded the Prophet. Abu Uthman an-Nahdi reports that Talhah and Saʿd ibn Abi Waqqas stayed with him. Saʿd remembered the Prophet giving him arrows and encouraging him to shoot. Reports also describe two figures in white defending the Prophet, understood by the companions as Jibril and Mikail. These accounts show both the human courage around the Prophet and the belief that Allah’s help was near.

**“So Allah rewarded your disobedience with distress upon distress”** describes the successive griefs of that day. Commentators differ over their order. Ibn Abbas says the first was the defeat and the rumor that Muhammad ﷺ had been killed, followed by the enemy’s advance up the mountain. Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf identifies the rumor as the greater second distress. Mujahid and Qatadah mention the report of the Prophet’s death and then the casualties and injuries, while As-Suddi gives another ordering connected to the lost spoils and the enemy’s renewed attack. The reports agree that sorrow came in waves.

The phrase does not trivialize the injuries or blame every grieving companion. It explains the painful consequences of disobedience and the shock that followed. The verse also speaks of the incident as a lesson, not as a declaration that Allah had rejected the believers.

**“do not grieve over the victory you were denied or the injury you suffered”** means do not let regret over missed spoils and grief over wounds and deaths consume the community. It does not mean that the dead did not matter or that families should not mourn. It directs the believers to accept what has passed, learn from it, and return to responsible action.

**“And Allah is All-Aware of what you do”** means that Allah knew who fled in fear, who stayed to defend the Prophet, and what each person intended. No detail of the battle or the companions’ efforts was hidden from Him. The verse closes with accountability and reassurance: Allah’s knowledge includes both the mistake and the courage shown amid it.

### Grief, correction, and mercy

One grief was the loss of victory and spoils, while another was the rumor about the Prophet’s death, the injuries, and the death of companions. The exact order differs in the reports, but both material loss and fear for the Prophet weighed heavily on the believers. The Qur’an teaches them not to remain consumed by what they could no longer change.

The following passage describes the believers’ return to security after the battle. Here, the emphasis is on the Prophet’s call, the companions’ retreat, and Allah’s awareness of their actions. The community must learn from the event without turning it into a blanket accusation against the Ansar, the Muhajirun, or all who fled momentarily.

### In short

- The verse recalls a real retreat and the Prophet’s call for the companions to return.
- Distress came in successive forms, and commentators differ on their exact order.
- The believers are told to learn from the event, not remain trapped in grief or condemn one another.
