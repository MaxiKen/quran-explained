<<<VERSE 4:77>>>
The verse remembers a moment of human weakness: "Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who had been told, 'Do not fight! Rather, establish prayer and pay alms-tax.'?" (4:77). It is addressed to men who had once begged for the very command they now dreaded. Two eras of the young community stand behind it, the years of patient endurance in Makkah and the years of strength after the Migration, and the verse asks what changed in a few hearts when the order they had prayed for finally came.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who had been told"** means to be told about, to know of. Those spoken of are Companions in Makkah, among them al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad, Qudāmah ibn Maẓʿūn, Ṭalḥah ibn ʿAbdullāh, Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ and ʿAbdur-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, as al-Kalbī names them. A gloss ties the verse to the expedition of the lesser Badr, when some of them disliked joining the Prophet ﷺ on the march.

**"Do not fight! Rather, establish prayer and pay alms-tax"** belongs to the Makkan years. The Muslims then lived under persecution: assault, destruction of their livelihoods, exclusion from the Kaʿbah, and pressure to abandon the faith and return to the religion of their fathers. In principle that oppression had already made arms lawful for them, and they came again and again to the Prophet ﷺ asking leave to fight and end the kāfir reign of terror. He always held them back. He had not been commanded to fight, he told them, but to be patient, to forbear and forgive, and to keep the ṣalāh and the zakāh already given them, devoting themselves to these, for the charity comforted the poor among them. The restraint had its reasons. The Muslims were few against many, and their city was sacred ground, the most honoured spot on earth, which is why the command to fight was not revealed in Makkah. Premature and untimely action is not the way of Islam. A practical step waits until its preparations exist, and a clash of arms at that stage would have handed the Makkans a justification for open war instead of stray tyranny at the individual level. Until then the faithful carry the personal obligations that hold under all circumstances: to seek nearness to Allah, to pay others their rightful dues, and to endure hardship along the path of religion.

ʿAbdur-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf and a group of his companions once came to the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah and complained:

> O Prophet of Allah, we were mighty when we were idolaters, but now that we are believers we have become humiliated.
>
> I am commanded to pardon. Do not fight these people.

Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded the report on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās, and an-Nasāʾī and al-Ḥākim both collected it. The words about pardon were his answer in Makkah. When Allah moved him to Madinah, the command to fight the idolaters came, and some of them held back, and this verse came down about them.

**"Then once the order came to fight"** marks the change. After the Migration the community had a town of its own, strength, power and support, and jihād was legislated. The command was exactly what they had asked for through the years of harm. Yet when it arrived, a section of them grew weary and deeply afraid of facing the idolaters in battle.

**"a group of them feared those ˹hostile˺ people as Allah should be feared"**, or even more. They feared death at the hands of the Makkans as they feared the punishment of Allah, or with greater fear still. One reading counts Ṭalḥah ibn ʿAbdullāh among this group.

**"Our Lord! Why have You ordered us to fight? If only You had delayed ˹the order for˺ us for a little while!"** is the wish itself. They wanted postponement, a little more time to live and to enjoy, because fighting means bloodshed, orphans and widows. The Qur'ān had already described this wavering: "Those who believe say: 'Why is not a sūrah sent down for us?' But when a decisive sūrah is sent down and fighting is mentioned" (47:20), the eagerness drains away.

**"The enjoyment of this world is so little"** turns their eyes from the length of life to its worth. There is no gain in this world for one who has no share in the eternal abode from Allah. If it still attracts anyone, its enjoyment is short-lived and its decline is near, for once the eyes close in death, the Hereafter is there to see.

**"whereas the Hereafter is far better for those mindful ˹of Allah˺"** promises the person of taqwā a destination better than this whole life. Tafsir Kabīr sets out four differences between the two sets of blessings. This world's are few, while the Hereafter's are many. These perish, while those endure eternally. Worldly blessings come laced with every kind of botheration, while the blessings of the Hereafter come free of such impurity. And no one is certain of gaining the world's goods, while the blessings of the Hereafter most certainly reach everyone who fears Allah, the observer of taqwā.

**"And none of you will be wronged ˹even by the width of˺ the thread of a date stone"** seals the promise. The faṭīl is the thin thread upon the pit of a date. Not one deed will be cut short by even that much, and the full rewards will be earned. A promise of this kind pulls the believer's hope away from this life, makes him eager for the Hereafter, and steadies him for jihād.

### A wish, not a rebellion

The wish for delay was not an objection to the command of jihād. It was a benign and subtle complaint, and human nature explains it. People hurt past endurance burn to strike back, while people grown used to comfort lose the taste for marching out. In Makkah they had known nothing but pain from the disbelievers and looked forward most eagerly to the command. In Madinah they found the peace they had missed for long, and when the order came in that state it found them without their old fire, so they simply wished the call had not come at that hour. To read this as defiance, or to charge those Muslims with sin for it, is not correct. The excuse holds only if they pronounced the complaint aloud. If nothing passed their lips and it was only a thought crossing the mind, then thoughts in the mind and doubts in the heart are not counted as sins by the Sharīʿah of Islam. The word "they said" need not mean speech, for saying can happen within the hearts. This is abridged from the Bayān al-Qur'ān of Mawlānā Thanāʾī. Others say the verse speaks of the hypocrites, in which case no explanation is called for at all, as Tafsir Kabīr has it.

### Self-correction before reform

The verse keeps an order. First come the prescribed prayers and the fixed zakāh, the medium of self-correction. Then comes jihād, the medium of collective and political correction, through which the forces of coercion and injustice are eliminated and peace prevails in the country. The lesson is that well before worrying about correcting others, one should start correcting oneself. Seen by relative importance, the first duty is farḍ al-ʿayn, an absolute obligation on every individual, while jihād is farḍ al-kifāyah, an obligation which, when some perform it, absolves the rest, as al-Mazharī notes, which makes the precedence of self-correction obvious. The order also trains. Unless a person is conditioned to fight against his own evil desires in obedience to Allah's command, and used to bearing physical pain and financial sacrifice, he will find joining jihād and staking his life a very difficult proposition. That is the wisdom of the Makkan years of prayer, charity and patience.

### The trifling world

The people of the heart hear the same words inwardly. He gave you power over this world and then pronounced the enjoyment of it trifling, of no account for you. Give alms from it as little as half a date and you would be saved from the Fire and reach the Garden. That is generosity beyond measure. Caring little about what comes to you for the sake of the Beloved is the strongest indicator of your relationship with Him. When He wants a servant to forsake the present world, He makes it seem meagre in his eyes so that abandoning it becomes easy. Since your portion is less than the trifling whole, why would He quarrel with you over the details of what is permitted, the taḥlīl, so long as your pact remains secure from alteration? If the value of this world is trifling, then the cheapest of the cheap are those content with the cheap in lieu of the precious. The believer is torn from the worlds of being in degrees. First He seizes him from the present world by what comes next. Then He robs him of both worlds with the words of the verse: what is with Allah is better and more lasting (42:36).

### In short

- In Makkah the command was to hold back, pray, give zakāh and endure. Fighting waited for a town, strength and support, and came with the Migration.
- When the order finally arrived, some feared men as they should fear Allah and wished for delay. The wish was weakness rather than defiance, and a thought in the heart is no sin.
- The answer points to worth, not length: the world's enjoyment is little, the Hereafter far better for the mindful, and no deed is wronged by the width of a faṭīl.
- Prayer and charity correct the self before jihād corrects the world, and the trifling world is left behind degree by degree.

<<<VERSE 4:78>>>
Death stands behind the command: "Wherever you may be, death will overcome you" (4:78). The verse closes the escape route the deserters from jihād were quietly counting on, for some imagined that by dodging the battlefield they might also dodge death. The occasion was raw. When Muslims were martyred at Uḥud, the hypocrites who had failed to join the fighting said that if their killed brothers had remained with them, they would not have died or been killed. Ibn ʿAbbās, in a report through Abū Ṣāliḥ, narrates this setting.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Wherever you may be, death will overcome you"** addresses the sincere believers, on land or on the sea, in settlement or travelling. Death keeps its appointment exactly where you are, on the day fixed for it, and none of you shall ever escape it. Every soul will taste death, whether it performed jihād or not, for everyone has an appointed time and a limited term of life: "Whatsoever is on it will perish" (55:26).

**"even if you were in fortified towers"** spells out the futility. The towers are entrenched, fortified, high and towering, and no caution or fortification can ever avert death. So there is no sense in backing out of fighting for fear of it.

**"When something good befalls them, they say, 'This is from Allah'"** turns to the hypocrites and the Jews of Madinah. The good is fertile years, produce, children and the like, in the gloss of Ibn ʿAbbās, Abū al-ʿĀliyah and as-Suddī, along with rainfall, lower prices and a good year. They credited all of it to Allah, adding that He knew they were good.

**"but when something evil befalls them, they say, 'This is from you.'"** names the other half of their arithmetic. The evil is drought, famine, shortage of fruits, the death of children and animals, hardship and high prices, as Abū al-ʿĀliyah and as-Suddī stated. "From you" means because of you, and because we followed you and embraced your religion. It was the ill omen they pinned on Muḥammad ﷺ and his Companions, on the grumbling of the Jews of Madinah that since he arrived, their harvests and fields kept ever decreasing. The habit is ancient. Whenever good came to Firʿawn's people they said it was theirs, and whenever evil afflicted them they ascribed it to evil omens connected with Mūsā (Moses) and those with him (7:131). The Qur'ān also sketches "the man who worships Allah as it were upon the edge", standing in doubt (22:11), and the hypocrites were of that make. Outwardly they had embraced Islam, inwardly they disliked it, so when a calamity befell them they attributed it to following the Prophet ﷺ. After the setback at Uḥud the opportunists went further, blaming the debacle on his poor strategy and trying to turn people against his ability to lead, for they found the new instructions toward sacrifice disturbing to the pattern of their lives.

**"Say, 'Both have been destined by Allah.'"** corrects the whole account. Everything, hardship and blessing alike, occurs by the decision and decree of Allah, and His decision comes to pass for the righteous and the wicked, the faithful and the disbelievers. The people of the heart add that since death is inevitable, submitting to His decree willingly is better than tolerating it unwillingly, and that death is joy to the believer, the news of its drawing near good news, because it is a means of reaching his Lord. Whoever loves to meet Allah, Allah loves to meet him. The fault in these people runs deeper than a slip of logic. Because of the weakness of their faculties of insight and the infirmity of their beliefs, they are joyful and grateful when a good thing befalls them, but when an evil thing befalls them they are not guided back to Allah. A Magian disposition flows in them, one that attributes the evil thing to something created. The reply names the true order: everything is from Allah, who creates and originates, produces and invents, decrees and makes easy.

**"So what is the matter with these people? They can hardly comprehend anything"** ends in wonder. The question is an interrogative of amazement at their extreme ignorance. To say they do not even come near to comprehending any word delivered to them is rhetorically more intense than saying they cannot understand it, and that is the force of the closing line.

### The Sword of Allah on his bed

Khālid bin al-Walīd, the famous soldier and general of Islam, carried the oft-repeated title Saifullah, the Sword of Allah. He had a burning desire to die a martyr in the way of Allah, so he fought all his life, engaging in one jihād after another and longing to fall in the heat of battle. He killed thousands of disbelievers on the field, lived daringly through many a trial, and kept praying to be spared the fate of dying in bed and favoured with a fearless soldier's death. As decreed by Allah, he died nowhere else but on his own bed in the house. In the illness that preceded his death he spoke from his bed:

> I participated in battle after battle, and every part of my body sustained an injury from a stab or a shot. Yet here I am, I die in my bed. Let not the eyes of the cowards ever taste sleep.

### The woman and the spider

A story Mujāhid told, as Ibn Jarīr and Ibn Abī Ḥātim relate it, carries the same lesson at length. A woman of an earlier community gave birth to a girl and sent her servant out to fetch fire. At the door a man appeared suddenly and asked what the woman had borne. Told it was a baby girl, he said to remember this: the girl would sleep with a hundred men and finally die through a spider. The servant went back in with a knife, slit the infant's abdomen open, and fled believing her dead. Her mother stitched the skin closed, and the girl grew up so beautiful that she was called the jewel of the city. The servant escaped overseas, stayed long and assembled a fortune. When he planned to marry, he returned to his old city and told an old woman he wanted the most beautiful bride in town. She matched him with that very girl, and the rich returnee married his own unrecognised victim. As they came to know each other, the stories met. She learned who he was, showed him her abdomen, and the cut mark was still there. He then passed on the two prophecies. She confessed the first, saying she could not remember the number, and he told her the number was a hundred. For the second he had a grand palace built for her, absolutely free of spider webs. On a day of rest a spider appeared on the wall of their room. She asked whether this was the spider he frightened her with, sprang up, downed it on the floor and trampled it dead. The poison from it infected her feet and nails, and the message of death became all too clear. Here was a woman in a new and spotless palace who died suddenly through a spider. Compare her with the many who spent a lifetime in battles and met no death there. The arrangement of life and death stays in the hands of the Creator, whose decisions are final. If He wills, He gives death on a luxury bed at the hands of a spider, and if He elects to save, He keeps a person alive amid swinging swords and roaring fires.

### In short

- Death meets every soul at its appointed term, in a fortress or on a battlefield. Staying behind the walls saves no one.
- The hypocrites and some Jews of Madinah credited the good to Allah and hung the evil on the Prophet ﷺ, as Firʿawn's people once hung it on Mūsā.
- Both good and evil come by Allah's decree, and the closing question marvels at people who come nowhere near understanding a word.
- Khālid the Sword of Allah died in bed after a body of wounds, and the bride of the spider-proof palace died by a spider. The term arrives regardless.

<<<VERSE 4:79>>>
The rule of attribution: "Whatever good befalls you is from Allah and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself" (4:79). The verse takes the two half-truths of the previous one and sorts them into a single account of where blessing and calamity come from, then widens into the mission of the one who brought it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Whatever good befalls you is from Allah"** speaks of blessings, for the word of the verse means blessing in al-Mazharī's vocabulary. No blessing that reaches a person comes to him as a matter of right. It is nothing but the grace of Allah, His faḍl in the common terminology of Islam. No matter how much a person devotes himself to the acts of worship laid upon him, he cannot claim to deserve blessing on the strength of his deeds alone, for the simple reason that the very ability to engage in worship comes from none but Allah Himself, His tawfīq. Then the blessings of Allah are countless. How could they be procured by limited acts of worship, least of all worship that hardly befits the supreme mastery and power of the Lord of this universe and of the universes beyond? The Prophet ﷺ said it in a hadith:

> No one shall enter Paradise unless it be with the mercy of Allah. The narrator asked: not you either? He said: yes, not me either.

**"and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself"** turns to the word that means muṣībah, calamity and misfortune, again in al-Mazharī's vocabulary. Although calamities are created by Allah, their cause lies nowhere else but in the misdeeds of man himself. as-Suddī, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Zayd read it as because of your errors. Qatādah read it as punishment for you, O son of Adam, because of your sins. What it means depends on who is struck. If the person is a disbeliever, a kāfir, the evil that hits him in this mortal world is only an ordinary sample of the ultimate punishment, for the punishment of the world to come is much more than the worldly one. If the person is a believer, all his misfortunes become an expiation, a kaffārah, for his sins, and thereby the very cause of his salvation in the Hereafter. Two sayings of the Prophet ﷺ carry it:

> No evil ever touches a Muslim, but it becomes a source of forgiveness for him, even the pinch of a thorn in his feet.
>
> No hardship befalls a servant of Allah, be it light or tough, but it is always due to his sin, and the sins Allah forgives without afflicting with a calamity are much more.

Al-Mazharī relates both from at-Tirmidhī, the second reported by Abū Mūsā. The address, though spoken to the Prophet ﷺ, refers to mankind in general, for Allah addressed him while meaning his people. The readings then multiply. It is because of the purity of yourself, in that He purifies you by it. Or the good means conquest and booty, an honour from Allah, and the bad means killing and defeat, such as befell at Uḥud, which came of the transgression of the Companions when they left their positions. Or the good is whatever you do by Allah's given success and help, and the evil whatever you commit by the transgression of your soul and His forsaking you. The inner teaching agrees in its own idiom: good is a bounty from Allah, and evil is from yourself through your own acquisition, your kasb, while both of them are created by Allah.

**"We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a messenger to ˹all˺ people"** states the reach of the mission. The Holy Prophet ﷺ was not simply a messenger for the Arabs alone. His prophethood is universal, open to all human beings of this entire universe, to jinn and men, whether present at this point of time or to come right through the Qiyāmah, the fateful Last Day, as al-Mazharī reads it. His task is to convey Allah's commandments to them, what He likes and is pleased with, and what He dislikes and refuses.

**"And Allah is sufficient as a Witness"** closes the account. Allah is sufficient as witness that He sent him, and He is witness over the messenger and over the people alike, with full knowledge of what is conveyed to them and of the disbelief and rebellion with which they respond to the truth. To any who demand a witness to testify that he is a messenger from Allah, Allah Himself is witness enough.

### Towers and trust

The towers of the previous verse leave a ruling behind them. Building a good and strong house to live in, with ensured security of person and things, is not contrary to the dictates of tawakkul, the trust in Allah, and it is not against the rules of the Sharīʿah either, as al-Qurṭubī judges. Trust in Allah does not demand open doors and paper walls. It demands a heart that knows the wall saves nothing unless He wills, which is exactly what the verse of the towers said.

### In short

- Blessing is never a wage. It arrives by grace, and even the strength to worship and so to earn is itself given.
- Calamity is created by Allah but caused by sin, a small foretaste for the kāfir and an expiation for the believer.
- The Prophet ﷺ was sent to all peoples, jinn and men, down to the Qiyāmah, and Allah is sufficient as witness.
- A strong and secure house contradicts neither trust nor law, for the term of death walks through walls.
