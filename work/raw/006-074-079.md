<<<VERSE 6:74>>>
And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said to his father, Ȃzar, “Do you take idols as gods? It is clear to me that you and your people are entirely misguided.”

The passage about ridicule and company now gives an example, and the example is a son speaking to his father.

### Phrase by phrase

**"And when Abraham said to his father, Azar"** The address is respectful and the content is not. Azar is the name commonly given as his father's name, while most historians give the name as Tarikh and treat Azar as a title. Imam al-Razi and a group of early scholars hold instead that Tarikh was the father and that Azar was the name of his uncle, who became a polytheist after entering the service of Nimrud. Since calling an uncle father is ordinary in Arab usage, Azar is named here as his father. Al-Zarqani, in his Sharh al-Mawahib, collects several proofs for that reading.

**"Do you take idols as gods?"** Do you worship an idol in place of Allah? The question is a rebuke, not a request. The interrogation is meant to make him say out loud what he does: he takes, as objects of worship, things his own hands have made, many of them, small and large, male and female.

**"It is clear to me that you and your people are entirely misguided"** You who follow your way, and the people who go along with you, are confused about the path of truth, and you are in an error plain to anyone with sound reason, wandering in confusion and unaware of where to go. Ibrahim saw that the idols of earth were being made in the shape of heavenly angels, so that those angels would intercede for them with the Creator. His people thought they were too insignificant to worship Allah directly. That is why they turned to the worship of angels as a way of getting provision, victory and the satisfaction of their needs. Allah is the only being who deserves to be worshipped, and nothing else has any right to be worshipped besides him.

**"Your people"** The phrase does more work than it appears to. Ibrahim did not attach himself to his father's people here, and said "your people" instead of "my people". The choice was a sacrifice, since it cut the bond of blood brotherhood in the way of Allah, and it showed that the nationality of believers is founded on the bonds of Islam. Wherever nationality built on race or homeland clashes with that, the verse says by its wording, the first has to be left. The Qur'an raises the whole community to the same standard by pointing at him: it was said that certainly the best example, worthy of being followed and emulated, is the way and conduct of Ibrahim and of those with him, who told their brothers in blood, race and geography frankly that they were wary of them and of their false objects of worship, and that the wall between them stands until they believe and submit to none but Allah. On the journey of his last hajj, the Prophet met a caravan on the road and asked them which nationality they came from, and they answered: we are a nation of Muslims (al-Bukhari). In the old Arab way of naming themselves they gave no tribe and no family line, and by calling themselves Muslims they named the nationality that holds in every time, right through the trials of the Hereafter. At the place where he had to declare his principled disassociation, he did address them as his own, saying: "O my people, I am free of what you associate with Allah" (6:78). The hint runs: you are my people in race and homeland, and your disbelief has compelled me to give up your brotherhood.

### The same story in the surahs about the cave and Mary

The Qur'an tells this advice in other places at greater length. There he is a man of truth and a prophet, and he says: "O my father, why do you worship what does not hear, does not see, and cannot avail you in anything? O my father, there has come to me knowledge that has not come to you, so follow me, and I will guide you to a straight path. O my father, do not worship Satan, for Satan has been a rebel against the Most Merciful. O my father, I fear that a torment from the Most Merciful will overtake you, so that you become a companion of Satan in the Fire." The father answers: do you reject my gods, O Ibrahim? If you do not stop, I will stone you, so leave me in safety before I punish you. Ibrahim says: peace be upon you. I will ask my Lord's forgiveness for you, for he has been most gracious to me. And I will turn away from you and from those you call on besides Allah, and I will call on my Lord, and I hope I will not be unanswered in my supplication to him (19:41-48).

He kept asking forgiveness for his father as long as his father lived. When his father died an idolater and he learned it, he stopped, and he disassociated himself from him. That is the sense of the verse about his father's forgiveness: it came only from a promise Ibrahim had made the man, and when the man turned out to be an enemy of Allah, Ibrahim drew away from him. He was patient in supplication and forbearing (9:114).

A report in the authentic collection describes the meeting of the two on the Day of Rising. Ibrahim will meet his father Azar, and Azar will say to him: my son, I am not going to disobey you today. Ibrahim will say: Lord, you promised me you would not disgrace me on the day they are raised, and what could be more shameful than being disgraced by my father? Then Allah will tell him: look behind you, Ibrahim. He will look and see that his father has been turned into a male hyena covered in dung. The creature will be seized by the legs and thrown into the fire.

### Reform begins at home

The verses about Ibrahim are placed here to support the call of the Prophet, and the placement is not accidental. Arabia's people held Ibrahim as their patriarch, and the whole peninsula paid him homage, so the argument runs through the man they could not dismiss. He is being presented as the one who taught them what a true belief in Allah's oneness looks like.

The first thing the verse shows is where the call began: with a respectable elder of the family. This is the same command the Prophet received: "warn your near relatives of the punishment" (26:214), and he obeyed it by gathering his own family when he stood on the hill of Safa to deliver his call. Tafsir al-Bahr al-Muhit draws the lesson that inviting a respected elder of one's family to the right path is not a breach of reverence, and that wishing well for him is exactly what is happening. It adds that beginning the work of daʿwah, and of the reform that follows it, from one's home, family and immediate circle is a sunnah of the prophets. The ummah that came after him was given this as its order of battle: the call starts with the people who know you best.

### The two-fold error he argued against

Ibrahim and the people of his father were caught in a shirk with two faces, and he debated both of them. They worshipped idols, and they worshipped stars. Against idolatry he spoke plainly, because the error was in common sight, and he told his father flatly that he and his people had gone astray. Against star-worship he took a different method, stage by stage, and that debate is what the next verses set out. The reason for the difference is in the material itself: the falsehood of a statue you have carved is obvious, while the falsehood of a bright object in the sky is not.

And the universe itself testifies against both kinds. Heaven and earth, with the systems running through them, deny that any being has power except the one Supreme Being. A person who makes worldly glory his purpose, and tramples truth and justice to get it, is behaving as though this world were a showplace for selfish men. But the perfect order of the universe shows resplendent glimpses of a perfect Maker, and it cannot be imagined that such a being would set up a showplace with no meaning. What a person sees now is temporary. Allah may issue a new command at any moment and break the present system down, and then human freedom of action will end and his authority will run over people the way it already runs over everything else in creation. At that point only those who surrendered during the test will succeed, those who feared him before any pressure was put on them to bow.

### In short

- Ibrahim's first address is to a family elder, and the verse makes plain that daʿwah starts at home.
- The name in the verse is read either as his father's name or as his uncle's, and the entry gives both readings with their reasons.
- "Your people" and then "my people" marks a deliberate line: the bond of faith outranks the bond of race and homeland.
- His long prayer for his father, and his withdrawal after the man died an idolater, are the background of the rule that a promise does not outlive enmity to Allah.
- The error he argued against had two faces, idols and stars, and each needed a different method.

<<<VERSE 6:75>>>
We also showed Abraham the wonders of the heavens and the earth, so he would be sure in faith

A verse of one line, placed between the quarrel with his father and the argument with the star-worshippers, and it explains where the argument came from.

### Phrase by phrase

**"We also showed Abraham"** Just as Allah had shown him the misguidance of his father and his people, so he showed him what is in the heavens and the earth. The showing is described as a gift given before the debate. So the debate speaks from what he had been shown, and not from speculation.

**"the wonders of the heavens and the earth"** That is, the kingdom of both, and everything between them: the sun, the moon and the stars. The sense of the showing is that Ibrahim saw what Allah has created in a way that unravelled the reality of these created things, manifestly and one after another. He was then able to infer from them the truth of Allah's oneness, and that nothing besides him deserves worship.

**"so he would be sure in faith"** So he might be among those who know with certainty, and whose certainty is firm. They acknowledge that Allah is one, the maker of the heavens and the earth and of everything in them. The verse pairs with two invitations to look: "Say, behold all that is in the heavens and the earth" (10:101), and "do they not see what is before them and what is behind them of the heaven and the earth? If he wills, Allah may sink the earth with them, or cause a piece of the sky to fall upon them. In that is a sign for every servant who turns to him" (34:9).

### What the showing did for him

One account of the same moment has Ibrahim shown all this when he came out of the hole into which he had been thrown. Another has Allah showing it to him on the night he was made to ascend. He saw as far as the seventh heaven and the seventh earth, and his certainty became the certainty of inspiration rather than of report. In either account the sequence in the verse matters: the seeing comes first, and the argument afterwards. Allah describes him elsewhere by saying that he had already been given his portion of guidance, and that he was well acquainted with him, and that he said to his father and his people: "what are these images to which you are devoted?" (21:51-52).

Verse 75 is also a preface to a wondrous dialogue, and it is worth noticing that the Qur'an introduces the debate by naming the qualification of the debater. Stage by stage, what follows shows a quality of argument that survives even in translation.

### In short

- Certainty is given as the fruit of looking at creation, and it is placed before the argument rather than after it.
- The showing is a favour, and the verse's grammar ties it to the misguidance Ibrahim had already been shown.
- Two other verses are cited as the same invitation to observe, and they carry the same threat inside the invitation.
- The quarrel with the stars that follows is spoken from what he had been shown, which is why it is so short and so plain.

<<<VERSE 6:76>>>
When the night grew dark upon him, he saw a star and said, “This is my Lord!” But when it set, he said, “I do not love things that set.”

The argument begins in the way his people were already thinking, and ends by dismantling that way of thinking.

### Phrase by phrase

**"When the night grew dark upon him"** The night covered him with its darkness. His people were astrologers, and the heavens were where they looked for lordship.

**"he saw a star"** The star is named in the reports as the planet Venus, the brightest of the objects they honoured after the sun and the moon. He said: "this is my Lord", and the sense in which he said it is not a confession but a trap. As you think and believe, is this not my Lord and your Lord? In a little while, you will see how much that claim is worth. One reading takes the words as a question: could this be my Lord? Another takes them as spoken in mockery of his people, since they worshipped the sun, the moon and the stars, and his point is: can the like of these be a Lord? The sentence can also be filled out as: this is evidence of my Lord, for my Lord has no beginning and no end, and this has set.

**"But when it set, he said, 'I do not love things that set'"** The word for setting gives the whole point, since it comes from a root for vanishing and disappearing. Whatever sets, whatever changes colour and fades out of sight, cannot be given the status of an object of worship, because what is worshipped has to be most worthy of love and reverence. A true Lord cannot be transformed or moved from place to place, since those are the marks of created things. So the sentence means: I do not love a lord who does not last. Qatadah commented on this that Ibrahim knew his Lord to be eternal, never ceasing. The argument is drawn from observation, and it testifies to the belief he already held. The way he conducts it has a name in the books of dialectic, since he repeats the words of the addressee and then convinces him, and the same method appears elsewhere in the Qur'an, at verse 97 of the chapter numbered 20. The signs of creation spread through the universe are not only a source of increase in faith. They also provide strong arguments for the call to the truth.

### Why he chose the setting out of all the states a star has

A star could have been argued against at several moments. It could have been shown rising, and held to be untrustworthy while it climbed. Its middle stage, before it sets, could have been used, since it moves in a pre-specified orbit at another's command and swerves neither to the right nor to the left, and has no say at all in its own affairs. Ibrahim chose the moment of its setting, because a setting spells decline in the sight of ordinary people. That is the usual method of the prophets. They do not chase philosophical truisms. They address people at the level of common comprehension, and they prove the helplessness of what is worshipped with the fact that everyone can already see.

### In short

- The opening words are not a statement of belief but a device, and the entry records the three ways the sentence has been read.
- The star named is Venus, and its brightness is the reason it was chosen first for the argument.
- Setting, vanishing and changing place are treated as marks of a created thing, and therefore as disqualifications for lordship.
- The moment picked for the argument is the one the crowd can see, which is the point about the prophets' method.

<<<VERSE 6:77>>>
Then when he saw the moon rising, he said, “This one is my Lord!” But when it disappeared, he said, “If my Lord does not guide me, I will certainly be one of the misguided people.”

The second round of the argument uses the same move on a bigger object, and adds a sentence that is easy to miss.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Then when he saw the moon rising, he said, 'This one is my Lord'"** He followed the earlier method with the moon, on another night, with the moon glowing in front of him. According to your view, this is my Lord, and this is bigger than the first. Its reality you will find out in a little while. The purpose was to carry his people through the same steps and convince them by what they watched.

**"But when it disappeared, he said, 'If my Lord does not guide me, I will certainly be one of the misguided people'"** This is the sentence that is not about the moon. Unless my Lord establishes him within true guidance, he will be one of those who have strayed, like the rest of them, and the moon would have been what he took as his Lord and his object of worship. He is saying that guidance keeps a man out of the abyss, and that argument alone does not. It also tells them that Allah's oneness is not reached by cleverness but by being made firm on it. And there is a hint in the wording that the Lord of Ibrahim is a different order of being entirely, from whom guidance keeps arriving to him.

The sentence also tells the hearers something about the debater. A man who says "unless my Lord guides me" is not claiming to be immune. He is telling them that a person can be wrong without noticing, and that the only safety is being made firm. It is an intimation to his people that they are astray, delivered as a confession about himself, and it is recorded that, like the first round, this had no effect on them.

### In short

- The moon round repeats the star round's structure, and the escalation is the point: bigger does not mean greater.
- The words about being guided are Ibrahim speaking of himself, and they place certainty in Allah's hand rather than in the argument.
- The verse hints that the Lord who gives guidance is not of the same order as the objects being watched.
- Persistence in the argument produced no effect, which is why the next round is the last.

<<<VERSE 6:78>>>
"Then when he saw the sun shining, he said, “This must be my Lord—it is the greatest!” But again when it set, he declared, “O my people! I totally reject whatever you associate ˹with Allah in worship˺”"

The third round ends the argument, and the sentence that follows is not about the sun any more.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Then when he saw the sun shining, he said, 'This must be my Lord, it is the greatest'"** This is bigger than the first and the second, and more radiant. The people's reckoning was that the sun was the brightest and most honoured of the objects they worshipped, and Ibrahim worked through their list in the order of their reverence. The seven planets they named were the moon, Mercury, Venus, the sun, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, and he began with Venus, then the moon, then the sun. When he had proved that these three were not gods, although they are the brightest objects an eye can see, the rest of the list went by the same argument.

**"But again when it set, he declared, 'O my people! I totally reject whatever you associate with Allah'"** Now the reality had become too obvious to be argued about, and the words change from "I do not love" to "I am free of". He is free of worshipping those objects and of taking them as protectors. If they are gods, as you claim, then bring your plot against me all of you, and give me no respite. One account of his life has him speak this at seventeen years old, when he emerged from the hole he had been thrown into, went to his people, looked at the sky and the earth, and said that his Lord is the one who created this. Then he came upon a group of them devoted to their idols, and said what the verse reports.

### The three tests the objects fail

The case Ibrahim builds against star-worship rests on three marks, and each of them is visible without instrumentation. A thing that is subject to change cannot be anyone's Lord. A thing that alternates in its conditions cannot be anyone's Lord. A thing that moves in obedience to another power cannot be anyone's Lord. Nothing in the sky is independent in its existence. The heavenly bodies are hemmed in on every side by the changes of rising and setting. So no created body, however bright, can be the Lord of life who nurtures, cherishes and sustains it.

### Two lessons for whoever carries the call

The method here gives preachers their guidance. First, in carrying the call to people and seeking reform among them, being universally rigid or universally lenient is not appropriate. Each has its occasion and its limit. Ibrahim used strong words about idolatry, because its error is in common sight, and he used a graduated method about the stars, because the helplessness of the planets is not obvious to the people who consult them. So when ordinary people are caught in an error of judgment or conduct that they do not recognise as such, the scholar or the preacher should avoid a hard, dogmatic line and find a better way to remove the doubt.

Second, the manner in which the truth is presented belongs to the argument. Ibrahim did not order his people to do what had to be done. He told them how matters stood for him: he could not declare as objects of worship things that are caught in a cycle of rising and setting, and that is why he had turned to the being who created, nurtured and cares for them. The purpose was to bring them round to the same place, and he avoided a direct command lest they refuse simply for the sake of refusing. The job of a reformer is not to state what is true in any manner that comes to hand. It is to say it in the way that will actually land.

### In short

- The sun is the largest and the last, and the argument stops where the people's reverence was strongest.
- "I do not love" becomes "I am free of", which is the move from a personal preference to a public break.
- Three tests are given for anything that claims to be a lord: change, alternation, and dependence on another's command.
- The graduated method against the stars and the plain method against idols are both kept as instruction for preachers, each with its occasion.

<<<VERSE 6:79>>>
"I have turned my face towards the One Who has originated the heavens and the earth—being upright—and I am not one of the polytheists.”"

The declaration that the three rounds were built to reach, and it is written as an about-turn.

### Phrase by phrase

**"I have turned my face towards the One Who has originated the heavens and the earth"** To turn one's face to someone is to direct one's religion and one's deeds to him sincerely. He originated them, made them without any precedent, and shaped them. Having shown that nothing in the sky can be a Lord, he names as his Lord the one who made the sky, the one who decrees and governs its affairs and holds it in obedience, in whose hand is the dominion of everything. He is the creator, the Lord, the king and the God of all that exists. The wording here matches the verse in the heights: "Your Lord is Allah, who created the heavens and the earth in six days and then rose over the Throne. He brings the night as a cover over the day, seeking it rapidly, and he made the sun, the moon and the stars subject to his command. Surely his is the creation and the command. Blessed is Allah, the Lord of the worlds" (7:54).

**"being upright"** The word translated as upright means one by nature upright, inclining to the straight religion, avoiding shirk and embracing tawhid. The upright person is not the one who has found a position and is defending it. He is the one whose orientation has been corrected.

**"and I am not one of the polytheists"** He states the exclusion as plainly as the commitment, and the word used is that he is not a mushrik, a disbeliever or a polytheist like them. The line is drawn as a permanent one, after a whole debate that never once asked them to admit they were wrong.

### The inner reading of the three rounds

A Sufi treatment of these verses takes the sequence as the movement of a heart rather than as a lesson in astronomy. First Allah showed Ibrahim the dominion of the heavens and the earth, so that he would come to know the maker by way of inference. He looked at the star and said "this is my Lord", meaning that it was evidence pointing to his Lord. His Lord has no beginning and no end, and this thing sets. At each piece of evidence he clung to it, until he passed beyond the degrees of evidence, and then the beauty of the real showed its face to him. By inference and proof he returned to witnessing and to face-to-face vision. He turned away from everything, and said, as another verse puts it on his lips: "Surely they are an enemy to me, and what you worship besides Allah will not benefit me" (26:77). One report has him say to Gabriel, when Gabriel came at that point: as for turning towards you, no. He was at first like one who knows, and then he became like one who recognises.

Al-Wasiti put the distinction in a sentence of his own: the creatures are going to Allah, and the recognizers are coming from him. And he said: if a man tells you that he came to know Allah through evidence, ask him how he came to know the evidence. The entry of evidence is unavoidable at the beginning, and this was the beginning of Ibrahim's path. When he reached each piece of evidence he said: this is my Lord. When he passed beyond all of it, he saw the beauty of tawhid with the eye of direct vision, and he said: "O my people, I am free of what you associate", that is, I am free of inferring the Creator from the creatures, since there is no evidence for him but himself. It is the same stand taken by a great name of this religion, who said that he came to know Allah through Allah, and came to know what is beneath Allah through Allah's light.

### In short

- The declaration is an orientation, not a creed recited from a distance, and the wording puts the face, not the mouth, into the sentence.
- The one he turns to is named as the originator of the heavens and the earth, which answers the sky-worshippers on their own ground.
- Being upright is defined as a turning away from shirk and a turning to tawhid at the same time.
- The inner reading treats the three rounds as a path from evidence to witnessing, in which the evidence is honoured and then left behind.
