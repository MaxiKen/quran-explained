<<<VERSE 5:3>>>
Allah specifies the foods and practices excluded from the earlier permission to eat livestock. The verse begins, "Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine", then distinguishes unlawful ways of killing an animal, sacrifices directed to others besides Allah, and decisions made through divining arrows. It does not treat every kind of meat or every means of obtaining it as equivalent. Both the manner of death and the religious purpose of a sacrifice matter.

In the middle of these practical rulings comes a declaration of the greatest favour: "Today I have perfected your faith for you, completed My favour upon you, and chosen Islam as your way." The believers are told not to fear the opponents who had hoped to turn them away from their religion. They must fear Allah instead. The verse ends by allowing a person in extreme hunger to eat otherwise forbidden food without intending disobedience. The law is complete, and mercy toward genuine necessity belongs within it.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine"** forbids eating these things. Al-maytah, carrion, is an animal that dies without proper slaughter or lawful hunting. Blood here means blood that flows out, as the more specific wording elsewhere explains (6:145). The prohibition of swine includes the whole animal, not only lean meat. Fat, connective tissue and other parts are not excluded by the word "flesh".

**"what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah"** excludes an animal dedicated to something besides Him. Calling on an idol or another supposed object of worship while slaughtering makes the meat unlawful. The prohibition also covers a sacrifice whose real purpose is devotion to another being, even if Allah's name is spoken over it. Pronouncing the right words does not make a contrary dedication lawful.

**"what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death"** names particular forms of death without proper slaughter. An animal may choke in a rope, die from blows, fall from a height or be fatally struck by another animal's horns. These are included within carrion, but are singled out because people before Islam had treated such meat as permissible.

**"what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it"** excludes prey that dies from the attack of a wild animal. The exception concerns an animal reached while still alive and then properly slaughtered. It also applies to the preceding cases of injury, not only to the last one. It does not mean that an already dead animal can be made lawful by cutting it afterward.

**"and what is sacrificed on altars."** An-nusub were stones used for pagan sacrifices, including those placed around the Ka'bah. The meat is forbidden because the sacrifice belongs to worship directed away from Allah. The prohibition still applies if Allah's name is mentioned while the animal is being offered for that idolatrous purpose.

**"You are also forbidden to draw lots for decisions."** Al-azlam were small arrows used to seek a verdict about what to do or to allot shares. The practice claimed knowledge or direction through a drawn object rather than through Allah's guidance. It also became a way of distributing meat by chance, depriving some participants and giving others more than their due.

**"This is all evil."** Fisq means departing from obedience. It describes these prohibited practices as wrongdoing, not harmless inherited customs. The explanation particularly stresses the misguidance involved in divining arrows. It also distinguishes committing a forbidden act from declaring lawful what Allah has forbidden, describing the latter as disbelief.

**"Today the disbelievers have given up all hope of ˹undermining˺ your faith."** The opponents had hoped the believers would abandon Islam and return to their former religion. Now they saw its strength and lost that hope. The declaration is associated with the Farewell Pilgrimage, when the believers' position was no longer what it had been in their earlier weakness.

**"So do not fear them; fear Me!"** directs fear away from those opponents and toward Allah. Believers must not abandon the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his religion to satisfy others. They are to remain firm in obedience and leave the outcome to Allah, who promises to uphold them against their enemies.

**"Today I have perfected your faith for you"** announces the completion of the religious instructions being given to this community. Its obligations, boundaries and rules of conduct have been made clear. This does not imply that Allah previously sent people a defective religion. Each earlier dispensation was complete for the people and period to which it was addressed.

**"completed My favour upon you"** includes the gift of the completed religion and the believers' establishment in security and authority. Its explanations mention entry into Makkah, the defeat of hostile power and the removal of pagan customs from the pilgrimage. Allah's gifts, outward and inward, have reached their completion.

**"and chosen Islam as your way."** Allah has selected submission to Him as the religion of this community. The believers should willingly accept what He has chosen. It is not one interchangeable preference among many, but the way He approves and through which salvation is sought.

**"But whoever is compelled by extreme hunger"** returns to the prohibited food. A person facing a genuine threat from starvation may eat what would otherwise be forbidden. This is a concession for necessity, not a cancellation of the ordinary prohibition.

**"not intending to sin"** limits that concession. The purpose must be relief from the emergency, not enjoyment of prohibited food or deliberate disregard for the law. The explanation requires eating enough to remove the danger rather than using the emergency as an excuse for indulgence.

**"then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** assures the person who uses this permission of Allah's pardon and compassion. He knows the hardship of His servants. The concession itself is an expression of mercy, and accepting it within its limits is not rebellion.

### Why distinguish carrion from properly slaughtered meat?

Carrion includes an animal that dies naturally or through sickness, as well as one killed in a manner that does not count as lawful slaughter or hunting. The explanation connects its prohibition with bodily and spiritual harm. It describes blood remaining in the dead animal instead of being released through slaughter, and sees that as part of the reason for excluding the meat.

Dhabh is the prescribed manner of slaughter. Its purpose here is explained through the draining of blood, in contrast with methods that leave blood within the flesh. The discussion treats what is wholesome or harmful as affecting character as well as the body. These are the reasons offered for the religious classification, not a separate medical assessment of an individual meal.

Not every creature that dies without slaughter falls under the same prohibition. Fish and locusts are excepted in the reports. Abu Hurayrah relates that the Messenger ﷺ was asked about seawater. He answered that the water is pure and that the sea's dead creatures are permitted.

Malik records that report in the Muwatta. It is also recorded in the Sahih collections of Ibn Hibban and Ibn Khuzaymah, and in the Sunan of An-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah, Abu Dawud and At-Tirmidhi. The ruling permits fish without requiring slaughter in the manner used for livestock.

A report from Ibn 'Umar names two permitted dead creatures and two permitted forms of blood. The creatures are fish and locusts. The forms of blood are the spleen and liver. Muhammad bin Idris Ash-Shafi'i records it, as do Al-Bayhaqi, Ad-Daraqutni, Ibn Majah and Ahmad bin Hanbal. The transmission mentioned passes through 'Abdur-Rahman bin Zayd bin Aslam, who is described as a weak narrator. That reservation must remain attached to this report.

### Does the prohibition include the liver and spleen?

Ibn 'Abbas and Sa'id bin Jubayr explain the prohibited blood as flowing blood. The specific wording in the other verse supplies the distinction (6:145). Organs that are called blood in this discussion do not become forbidden merely on that description.

Ibn Abi Hatim records that Ibn 'Abbas was asked about eating the spleen. He allowed it. When someone objected that it was blood, he answered that the prohibition concerns blood poured out. The report naming the liver and spleen makes the same practical distinction.

This matters because a broad word must be read with its supplied qualification. The verse does not require the reader to ignore the difference between spilled blood and the organs excepted in the explanation. Both the prohibition and the exception belong to the ruling.

### What does the prohibition of swine cover?

The word lahm, flesh, includes the body of the animal as a whole in this use. Domestic pigs and wild swine are both included. A person cannot avoid the prohibition simply by calling a part fat rather than meat.

Muslim records a report from Buraydah bin Al-Husayb Al-Aslami comparing the player of Nardshir, a dice game involving gambling, to someone putting a hand into swine flesh and blood. The discussion uses the severity of that image to underline the prohibition of consuming it. If contact is used as such a warning, feeding on it is not a minor matter.

The Two Sahihs record the Prophet's ﷺ prohibition of trade in pigs, idols, carrion and intoxicating drink. People asked about the fat of dead animals because it was used on boats and hides and burned in lamps. He did not approve the proposed exception, but declared it unlawful.

Al-Bukhari also records Abu Sufyan telling Heraclius, the Roman emperor, that Muhammad ﷺ prohibited blood and the meat of dead animals. The food restrictions were therefore among the teachings publicly identified with his message.

### Why does the intention of sacrifice matter?

Animals belong to Allah, who created them. His name is to be mentioned when they are slaughtered. Substituting the name of an idol, monument or false deity makes the meat unlawful by the agreement described here. The act is not merely a different style of preparation. It gives a religious claim to something besides Allah.

The same warning is applied to slaughter offered in the name of a saint or revered person. Honour for a person cannot turn that person into the recipient of sacrifice. Shirk means assigning a share in Allah's unique divinity to another, and the discussion identifies this dedication as falling within that danger.

There are two related cases. In one, another name is expressly invoked at slaughter. In the other, Allah's name is pronounced, but the sacrifice is actually offered to please or approach another being. The latter is also declared unlawful by the juristic agreement cited. The purpose of the offering cannot be concealed by its spoken formula.

This explains why the stone altars receive a separate mention. Mujahid and Ibn Jurayj identify them as the stones set around the Ka'bah. Ibn Jurayj describes 360 such altars. Animals were sacrificed before them, blood was applied in the pagan rites, and pieces of meat were placed upon the stones. Sacrificing there for that purpose was an act of idolatry, even where Allah's name was also said.

Nusub is explained as the plural of nusab. Whether called altars or standing stones, their role in worship is what gives the practice its significance. The verse does not treat a sacrifice as lawful merely because its animal belongs to a lawful species.

### Which injuries make an animal unlawful?

The verse names several ways an animal might die before proper slaughter. Al-munkhaniqah is one killed by strangling. This can happen deliberately or accidentally, as when a restrained animal struggles and tightens the rope around its neck. An accident does not make the resulting carrion lawful.

Al-mawqudhah is an animal beaten to death. Ibn 'Abbas explains it through a blow from a staff. A rod, rock or other heavy object may kill by impact rather than by the wound required in lawful hunting. Qatadah recalls that people before Islam struck animals with sticks and ate them after they died. The verse forbids that practice.

Al-mutaraddiyah is one that dies from falling. Ibn 'Abbas mentions a mountain, Qatadah a well, and As-Suddi gives both examples. The explanation also includes falls from a mound or a high building. What matters is death from the fall rather than proper slaughter.

An-natihah is an animal killed by another animal's goring. The legal discussion also applies the category of fatal collision to impact with a vehicle or train. Even when a horn opens a wound and blood flows from the neck, that accidental injury does not itself count as the required slaughter.

The final injury described is an attack by a predator. Examples include wolves and dogs, as well as lions, tigers and leopards. If the predator eats part of the animal and the animal dies from the attack, its meat is forbidden. This remains so even if the neck is wounded and blood flows out. The people before Islam had eaten livestock left partly consumed by predators, but that practice is excluded here.

### What is the distinction between a cutting wound and a fatal blow?

'Adi bin Hatim asked the Prophet ﷺ about hunting with a mi'rad, a hunting implement that could strike with a sharp part or with its broad surface. The answer distinguished the two. If its sharp edge pierced the animal, the meat could be eaten. If its broad side killed by force, it was an animal beaten to death and could not be eaten. The report is recorded in the Sahih, and the distinction is described as agreed upon by the jurists.

Al-Jassas also records the question in explaining the ruling. The discussion adds that Allah's name, bismillah, must be said when releasing the hunting implement. A piercing wound is not a substitute for the other conditions of lawful hunting.

The legal passage extends the category of a fatal blunt blow to what it calls a gun bullet. It attributes a corresponding ruling to 'Abdullah ibn 'Umar through Al-Jassas, and names Malik, Abu Hanifah and Ash-Shafi'i as agreeing in the discussion cited through al-Qurtubi. This is the ruling and terminology as transmitted in that passage. It is not an independently established modern ruling about every firearm or projectile. The controlling distinction explained here remains killing by a cutting wound rather than by a crushing impact.

There is also a question of uncertainty about the cause of death. A report from 'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud describes game shot on a mountain. Even if Allah's name was said when the arrow was released, the animal is not to be eaten if the shot causes it to fall and die. It may have died from the fall rather than the arrow's wound.

Similarly, a bird struck by an arrow may fall into water. If drowning could have caused its death, the meat is not treated as lawful. 'Adi ibn Hatim also reports this ruling from the Prophet ﷺ, and Al-Jassas cites it. A successful shot alone does not establish that the animal died by a lawful means when another fatal cause intervened.

### When can an injured animal still be eaten?

The words "unless you slaughter it" allow an injured animal to be made lawful if it is reached alive and properly slaughtered. This exception applies to the strangled, beaten, fallen, gored or predator-attacked animal. It does not bring carrion back to life, make spilled blood into meat, or change a forbidden species into a lawful one.

Ibn 'Abbas explains the condition through finding the animal alive and slaughtering it before death. Sa'id bin Jubayr, As-Suddi and Al-Hasan Al-Basri give the same explanation. The discussion also names 'Ali and Qatadah among those who restrict the exception to these later injury categories rather than the first four prohibitions.

Ibn Jarir records 'Ali saying that movement of a foot or leg can show that the animal is still alive. Similar explanations are reported from Tawus, Ad-Dahhak, 'Ubayd bin 'Umayr, Qatadah and Al-Hasan. The signs of life must be present when slaughter takes place. Cutting an animal after it has already died does not satisfy that condition.

Rafi' bin Khadij asked the Prophet ﷺ about slaughtering without knives when they feared meeting an enemy the next day. Could reeds be used? The reply allowed an implement that made the blood flow, with Allah's name mentioned, but excluded teeth and claws. Teeth were identified as bones, while claws were associated with the slaughtering practice of Ethiopians. The Two Sahihs record the report.

The instruction ties the act to its effective means and its religious condition. It does not insist on a knife as the only possible instrument, but neither does it allow any way of killing merely because a person calls it slaughter.

### How did divining arrows work?

Al-azlam is the plural of zalam or zulam. The objects are described as small arrows without heads or feathers. They were used to obtain supposed directions, settle disputes or determine shares. This practice put a claim of authority into the result of a draw.

One account describes three arrows. One commanded action, another forbade it, and a third was blank. The inscriptions might say that the person's lord commanded or forbade the proposed act. If the blank arrow came out, the process was repeated until a definite answer appeared. The person then acted according to that result.

Another account describes seven arrows kept by the custodian of the Ka'bah. A person seeking to know whether an undertaking would be beneficial presented a payment, and the custodian drew arrows to produce an answer. Muhammad bin Ishaq also describes seven arrows associated with Hubal, the major idol of Quraysh. It stood above a well inside the Ka'bah where gifts and treasure were kept. Disputants accepted the answer supplied by the drawn arrow.

Ibn 'Abbas explains the arrows as instruments for seeking decisions. Al-Bukhari records that, on entering the Ka'bah after the conquest of Makkah, the Prophet ﷺ saw pictures of Ibrahim (Abraham) and Isma'il (Ishmael) holding such arrows. He condemned the idolaters for depicting them that way, saying they knew those prophets had never used the practice.

The prohibition reaches attempts to uncover the unseen through numbers, palm-reading or omens. Divination by numbers is named as 'ilm al-jafr. The discussion groups these practices together because they seek knowledge of hidden outcomes through devices that have not been given that authority.

### How is this connected with gambling?

Groups could jointly slaughter a camel or another animal and then use arrows to divide its meat. Instead of receiving shares according to their contributions, some received too much, some too little and some nothing. This explains why the subject appears alongside food prohibitions. The animal might be of a lawful kind while the way its meat was allotted involved wrongdoing.

The term can therefore also be used for qimar, gambling, or maysir. Chance is made the means of transferring or determining rights. Mujahid connects the arrows with the dice used by Persians and Romans. Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Al-Sha'bi also mention gaming pieces, including pieces used in chess and backgammon, when employed to determine shares in that way.

There is a qualification to Mujahid's explanation. Treating the arrows as nothing but gambling equipment is questioned, because their use in divination is also clearly described. The two accounts can be held together if the arrows were sometimes used for gambling and sometimes for other decisions. The distinction should not be erased.

Later in the surah, intoxicants, gambling, al-ansab or sacrificial stones, and al-azlam are named together (5:90-91). They are described as means by which Shaytan stirs hatred, creates enmity and diverts people from prayer and remembrance of Allah. Their prohibition concerns the life they produce, not only the objects themselves.

### What should replace divination?

Believers are taught to ask Allah for a good outcome through worship and supplication, rather than seeking it through arrows. Jabir bin 'Abdullah reports that the Prophet ﷺ taught istikharah, asking Allah to guide a choice, with the care with which he taught passages of the Qur'an. Ahmad, Al-Bukhari and the compilers of the Sunan record the report. At-Tirmidhi describes it as hasan sahih gharib, good, authentic and singly transmitted.

A person considering an undertaking is to pray two rak'ahs, units of prayer, apart from the obligatory prayer. He then asks Allah to guide the matter through His knowledge and power and to grant from His immense bounty. The prayer acknowledges that Allah knows and can accomplish what the servant neither knows nor can accomplish independently. Allah knows the unseen.

The person names the matter. If it is good for religion, livelihood and the eventual outcome, he asks Allah to decree it, make it easy and bless it. The report also expresses the contrast as present and later needs. If it is harmful, he asks that it be kept away and that he be turned away from it. He finally asks for the good wherever it may be and for contentment with it.

This is the opposite of pretending to possess hidden knowledge. The servant acts through worship, admits his limits and entrusts the unknown result to Allah. The request is for what is genuinely good, not merely for confirmation of an existing desire.

### What hope had the opponents lost?

Ibn 'Abbas explains their despair as the loss of hope that Muslims would return to their former religion. 'Ata' bin Abi Rabah, Muqatil bin Hayyan and As-Suddi give a similar explanation. The strength of Islam had become visible. The earlier calculation that a small and vulnerable community could be eliminated no longer held.

A report in the Sahih says that Shaytan has lost hope of being worshipped by those who pray in the Arabian Peninsula, though he will continue stirring trouble among them. This preserves an important distinction. The end of one hope does not mean the end of every danger or temptation.

Another possible explanation emphasises how the believers' qualities set them apart from idolatry and its adherents. Their opponents and Shaytan cannot simply be made equivalent to them. The command is therefore to remain patient and firm in that distinction, fearing Allah rather than pressure from outside.

The instruction includes reassurance of victory and honour in this life and the next. It also directs the community's attention inward. With the position Allah had given it, its deepest danger would come from its own weakness and disobedience. Fear of Allah within its members is presented as the best safeguard against that decline.

### When was the declaration of perfection revealed?

It came on the day of 'Arafah during the Farewell Hajj in the tenth year after the Hijrah. That day, the ninth of Dhu al-Hijjah, was a Friday. The setting is described as the plain of 'Arafat near Jabal ar-Rahmah, the Mount of Mercy, after the afternoon prayer. The day, place and hour brought together several occasions of blessing and answered prayer.

The Prophet ﷺ was making wuquf, the standing at 'Arafat that is a central rite of Hajj. He was on his she-camel, Adba', with the Companions gathered around him. The account describes about 150,000 Companions participating. When revelation came, its weight bore down on the camel until it crouched and sat.

Ibn 'Abbas describes the verse as among the last revelations, with no new ruling of lawful and unlawful after it. Later verses are explained as exhortation or repetition of rulings already established. The account states that the Prophet ﷺ lived another eighty-one days. It gives his departure from this life as the twelfth of Rabi' al-Awwal in the eleventh year after the Hijrah. These are the dates and interval supplied in that account, not a new calculation imposed on it.

A report from Tariq bin Shihab tells of a Jewish man speaking to 'Umar bin Al-Khattab. Had this verse been revealed to his own community, he said, they would have made its day a festival. 'Umar answered that he knew exactly where and when it had come to the Messenger ﷺ: on Friday evening at 'Arafah.

Ahmad records the report, as do An-Nasa'i, At-Tirmidhi, Muslim and Al-Bukhari. Another wording in Al-Bukhari speaks of Jews addressing 'Umar and records his emphasis that he himself was at 'Arafah. In that transmission Sufyan was uncertain whether Friday had been mentioned.

The uncertainty is explained as doubt about whether his teacher included that detail in the wording, not a reason to deny the date. The Friday setting is described as agreed upon by specialists in the Prophet's ﷺ life and Islamic law. Numerous authentic reports support it, with transmission described as mutawatir, widely conveyed through multiple routes. The discussion names Sufyan Ath-Thawri while explaining this point.

### What exactly was perfected?

The declaration brings three gifts together: the perfection of the religion, the completion of Allah's favour and His choice of Islam for the community. The first concerns the body of guidance. Ibn 'Abbas and others explain it as the completion of obligations, limits, commands, prohibitions and the refinements of personal and social behaviour.

There is no need for another religion or another prophet after Muhammad ﷺ. He is the final Prophet, sent to humanity and the jinn. What he conveys is true, and the law he brings is just. Its reports do not contain falsehood or contradiction. The cited statement about the Lord's word being fulfilled in truth and justice expresses these two qualities together.

Another explanation stresses the wording "your faith for you". What has reached completion is the process of communicating the guidance destined for this community. It is not a declaration that religion itself had previously been imperfect in every age. The successive gifts beginning with Adam were suited to their recipients and circumstances.

Al-Qaffal al-Marwazi explains the distinction through the periods of earlier laws. A dispensation could be complete for its own time and people while Allah knew it would later be replaced. The final Shari'ah, the revealed law of Islam, is not confined in that way to a particular region, people or period. Its completion is for the time remaining until the Last Day.

The discussion therefore treats finality as a distinction of Muhammad ﷺ and his ummah, his community. The divine gift reaches its final form through him. Accepting that gift means adhering to it, not looking for another religion to correct a supposed deficiency in it.

### Does perfection leave room for legal reasoning?

Yes. Ijtihad is the disciplined effort of qualified jurists to understand the application of revealed law to new circumstances. Its principles belong to the guidance already given. Using those principles is not adding an independent revelation to a defective religion.

The rulings derived through sound ijtihad are subordinate to the Qur'an's authority. They explain and apply its instructions. New situations may multiply, and their detailed treatment may multiply with them, without the foundation becoming incomplete.

This also differs from abrogation. Removing a revealed ruling requires revelation. With the Prophet's ﷺ departure and the ending of revelation, no later jurist can cancel the Qur'an by claiming that his own judgment supersedes it. Elaboration under its principles is one thing. Replacing its authority is another.

### How is the favour different from the religion?

The explanation of completed favour includes the believers' rise and the defeat of their opponents. Makkah had been conquered, pagan practices had been displaced, and idolaters were absent from the pilgrimage that year. One gloss specifies 'Arafat, Mina, circling the Ka'bah and moving between As-Safa and Al-Marwah as rites no longer shared with pagan pilgrims.

The wording associates ikmal, perfection, with religion and itmam, completion, with favour. Raghib al-Isfahani distinguishes the terms. Perfection means that the intended purpose has been achieved. Completion means that nothing further is needed to make the thing whole. The words can overlap, but the difference helps explain their placement here.

Thus the purpose of the religious instructions has been accomplished, while the community has also received the means to practise them without dependence on hostile power. Allah has given security, strength and authority through which His commands can be carried out.

Ibn al-Qayyim also notes the attributions. Religion is called "your" religion because it appears in the community's faith and conduct. Favour is called "My" favour because its granting comes directly from Allah. Human obedience is a duty, while the gift that makes the community's establishment possible is His generosity.

### Why did 'Umar weep at such good news?

Ibn Jarir records, through Harun bin 'Antarah from his father, that 'Umar cried when the declaration came on the great day of Hajj. Asked why, he said that what reaches completion is then exposed to decline. The Prophet ﷺ affirmed his observation.

The explanation connects this with the authentic report that Islam began as something unfamiliar and will again become unfamiliar, with blessedness promised to those who remain faithful in that condition. The religion's completion is not a promise that every later generation will live by it perfectly.

Another account of 'Umar's tears emphasises the Prophet's ﷺ approaching departure. If the work of conveying the religion was complete, his remaining time in the world might be short. The Prophet ﷺ agreed with that understanding too. The reported eighty-one days before his death gave the concern its painful confirmation.

These responses do not reject the joy of the gift. They recognise the responsibility and loss that can accompany completion. The guidance has been given, but the community must preserve obedience after the Messenger ﷺ is no longer physically among it.

### Why did 'Umar point to Friday and 'Arafah rather than create another festival?

His answer identifies two existing occasions of rejoicing: Friday and the day of 'Arafah. The explanation draws from this a principle about commemoration. The focus should be religious purposes and acts of obedience, rather than creating festivals around the personal fame of individuals or every memorable historical event.

Ibrahim was honoured as Allah's friend and succeeded in the trials set before him (2:124). Yet the argument does not point to an annual birthday or death anniversary as the way that legacy was preserved. Instead, particular acts connected with submission were retained: sacrifice, circumcision, the movement between Safa and Marwah, and the throwing of pebbles at the three places in Mina.

The same distinction is applied to Isma'il and his mother. Their remembered acts teach the surrender of personal desire and even the dearest attachments for Allah's pleasure. What is preserved is a living practice with a religious purpose, not simply an occasion for praising a name.

The discussion mentions Laylat al-Bara'ah, the Night of Deliverance, Ramadan, Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, and the days of 'Arafah and 'Ashura. These are presented as occasions connected with worship. The two regular festivals of rejoicing are 'Id al-Fitr, following Ramadan and preceding the Hajj months, and 'Id al-Adha after the pilgrimage rites.

A restrictive view is then argued against adding a festival for the Prophet's ﷺ birth by imitating Christian birthday observances. It objects to treating street processions, night-time illumination and similar displays as worship without a basis in the practice of the Companions, their Successors or the early righteous community. This is the position being advanced here, rather than a survey of every later scholarly view about such observances.

Its argument is not that the Prophet ﷺ lacks events worth remembering. Quite the opposite. His character from childhood, his trustworthiness, the Qur'an's revelation, the Hijrah and the conquest of Makkah all deserve attention. So do the campaigns of Badr, Uhud, Khandaq, Hunayn and Tabuk, together with his miracles and the rest of his life. Every hour could furnish something worth recalling.

The argument extends this difficulty to more than 120,000 prophets, then to the roughly 120,000 Companions mentioned in the discussion, and then to countless later scholars and righteous people. Choosing only a few anniversaries would not capture all their merit. Attempting to mark them all would fill every day, even every hour, with competing celebrations.

The proposed alternative is to preserve guidance through obedience and the rites already appointed. 'Umar's answer is read as consistent with that choice. It recognises the extraordinary revelation without making the occurrence itself a new annual festival.

### How much hardship permits otherwise forbidden food?

The closing concession concerns real necessity, especially hunger severe enough to threaten life. The person may eat enough of the prohibited food to remove that danger. He is not instructed to choose death rather than use the mercy Allah has granted.

Ibn 'Umar reports that the Messenger ﷺ said Allah loves His allowances to be used, just as He dislikes disobedience. The report appears in the Musnad and the Sahih of Ibn Hibban. A rukhsah is such a legal allowance in a condition of hardship. Using it properly is not a failure to take religion seriously.

There is no requirement to wait three days before eating. That belief is explicitly rejected. The relevant question is whether necessity has arisen, not whether an arbitrary period has passed.

Ahmad alone records here a report from Abu Waqid Al-Laythi. People who often faced famine asked when carrion became permissible. The Prophet ﷺ described the situation in which they could find neither their meals nor produce to eat, and allowed it then. The chain is described as meeting the standards of the Two Sahihs.

The permission does not make the food ordinarily lawful. It relieves the particular person in the emergency. Nor should the person intend to enjoy prohibited food, eat his fill merely for pleasure, or extend the concession beyond what removes the necessity. The condition about having no inclination to sin governs its use.

Some scholars also draw a restriction on concessions during a journey undertaken for wrongdoing. A person cannot earn a dispensation through the very sin he has set out to commit. A gloss gives the highway robber or active criminal as an example. This is presented as the legal view derived from the condition, not as an excuse to erase the general mercy toward the genuinely compelled person.

### What inward warning is drawn from carrion?

The allusive reading contrasts permitted necessity in physical food with the image of eating another person's flesh through backbiting. The Qur'an compares backbiting to eating the flesh of a dead brother (49:12). The admonition treats this as worse than ordinary carrion: hunger can permit a measured amount of the latter, but it gives no permission for this attack on a fellow Muslim's honour.

A second image concerns the soul. An animal properly slaughtered under the law is pure and lawful to eat, whereas one that dies by itself is carrion. The soul subdued through struggle, discipline and obedience is compared to the first. Its selfishness has been brought under the revealed law. Its company brings rest to the heart and happiness to the spirit.

By contrast, a soul sunk in heedlessness is pictured as spiritually dead. It no longer attends to religion or makes an effort to keep its limits. The image of carrion warns against seeking companionship in that state. This is an analogy about moral discipline and heedlessness, not a command to injure oneself or another person.

The available allusive passage ends by repeating the categories of injured animals without completing another explanation. No further meaning needs to be invented beyond the contrast it actually gives: a self trained in obedience and a self abandoned to forgetfulness are not the same.

### In short

- Lawful meat depends on the species, the way the animal dies and the dedication of the sacrifice. An injured lawful animal can still be eaten if it is reached alive and properly slaughtered.
- Flowing blood is prohibited, while fish, locusts, liver and spleen have the stated exceptions. Swine and sacrifices dedicated to others besides Allah are not made lawful by ordinary slaughter.
- Divination and gambling are forbidden ways of deciding affairs or distributing shares. Istikharah turns instead to Allah through prayer, trust and acceptance of His knowledge.
- The declaration at 'Arafah announces completed guidance and favour. It affirms the finality of the religion without excluding legal reasoning conducted under its own principles.
- Genuine starvation permits enough otherwise forbidden food to relieve the danger, without an intention to disobey. Allah's mercy is part of the completed law.
