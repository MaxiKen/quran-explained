<<<VERSE 5:3>>>
This verse is the list of what is forbidden to eat, and it ends with a message that the material calls one of the most important in the whole Qur'an. It opens: "Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine". It then names the animal that was killed in the name of someone other than Allah, the animal killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or a goring, and the animal half eaten by a predator. It forbids the animal sacrificed on stone altars and the practice of drawing arrows to decide matters. Then the verse turns to the believers and tells them that the disbelievers have given up hope of destroying their faith, that Allah has perfected their religion and completed His favour on them, and that Islam is their way. It ends with a permission: the person who is driven by severe hunger, without any intention to sin, may eat what is otherwise forbidden, because Allah forgives and shows mercy.

The verse sits inside a longer passage about food, oaths, the Hady, and the duties of the believers. The previous verse set out the limits of the sacred months and the pilgrim's obligations, and this verse goes on to the rules for what is lawful to eat. The reports say the verse came down during the Farewell Pilgrimage, on the day of 'Arafah in the tenth year after the Hijrah, which fell on a Friday. The material treats that date as a sign of the completion of the religion, and it is discussed at length in the entry below.

### Phrase by phrase

**"Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine"** names the three basic prohibitions. Carrion, in Arabic maytah, is an animal that died without being slaughtered, whether from sickness or from natural death. The material says it is harmful to the body and also to character. Blood means blood that flows. The verse refers to flowing blood, as Ibn 'Abbas and Sa'id bin Jubayr explained, and the material notes that the Qur'an says the same in 6:145. Liver and spleen are therefore not included, since they are not blood that flows out. Swine means the whole animal, including the fat, since the Arabic word lahm covers the whole flesh of the animal. A report collected by Muslim makes the point clear, through Buraydah bin al-Husayb al-Aslami, who said that the Prophet ﷺ compared a person who plays the dice game with someone who puts his hand into the flesh and blood of swine. If touching is that serious, eating is much worse.

**"what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah"** is the ban on sacrifice to anyone but Allah. The material says that when a name other than Allah is spoken over an animal at slaughter, whether an idol, a false god, or a shrine, the meat becomes unlawful, and that the scholars agree on this. Muslim jurists also treat as forbidden an animal that is offered to someone other than Allah, even if the name of Allah is spoken, because the act is still a form of associating partners with Him. Abu Sufyan is reported to have told the Emperor Heraclius that the Prophet ﷺ forbade eating dead animals and blood, which the material cites as a sign of how widely the prohibitions were known.

**"what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death"** lists four ways an animal can die without the proper slaughter. Strangling, whether by mistake or on purpose, makes the animal unlawful. A beating with a staff, rod or stone makes the animal unlawful, and the material notes that the people of the time of ignorance used to beat animals and then eat them. A hunter who strikes game with the broad side of an arrow, so that it is killed by the blow and not by the cut, may not eat it. The report of 'Adi bin Hatim says that if the sharp edge hits and wounds the game, it may be eaten. A fall from a height or into a well, or a death from being gored by another animal's horns, also makes the animal unlawful, even if blood is seen to flow.

**"what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it"** forbids an animal that a lion, leopard, tiger, wolf or dog has attacked and partly eaten, if it then dies. The material says this too was the custom of the pre-Islamic period, which the Qur'an forbade. The exception is found in the phrase "unless you slaughter it". The material explains that an animal in this state that is still alive and moving may be properly slaughtered and then eaten. Ibn 'Abbas, Sa'id bin Jubayr, al-Hasan al-Basri and as-Suddi all gave this reading, and 'Ali is reported to have said that if the animal still moves a foot or a leg it may be slaughtered and eaten. Rafi' bin Khadij, in a report in the two Sahih collections, asked the Prophet ﷺ whether they could use reeds to slaughter an animal when they had no knives. He said that anything that makes the blood flow may be used, with the name of Allah spoken, but not teeth or claws.

**"and what is sacrificed on altars"** forbids the animal offered on the stone altars, an-nusub, which stood around the Ka'bah. The material notes that there were about three hundred and sixty of them, and that the Arabs of the time of ignorance would slaughter animals before them, sprinkle the blood on the stones and cut up the meat and place it on the altars. The Qur'an forbids this, and it also forbids eating the meat of animals slaughtered near the altars, even where the name of Allah was spoken, because the practice is itself a form of associating partners with Allah.

**"You are also forbidden to draw lots for decisions"** deals with the arrows, al-azlam. The material describes a method in which three arrows were used: one marked "do", one marked "do not", and one blank. The blank arrow meant the draw had to be repeated until one of the others came out. Some arrows bore the words "my Lord commanded me" and "my Lord forbade me". Muhammad bin Ishaq and others say that the chief idol of Quraysh, Hubal, stood inside the Ka'bah, and that seven arrows were kept near it and drawn to settle disputes. Al-Bukhari recorded that when the Prophet ﷺ entered the Ka'bah after the conquest of Makkah, he saw pictures of Ibrahim and Isma'il holding the arrows, and he said: may Allah fight them, they never used the arrows for decisions. Mujahid described the arrows as the ones the Arabs used, and the dice the Persians and Romans used for gambling. The material notes this is doubtful unless the arrows were used both for gambling and for decisions at different times. It also notes that the same practice appears in 5:90 and 5:91, where the arrows are named together with intoxicants and gambling as an abomination of Shaytan's work. The verse says this practice is a serious departure from obedience and a form of associating partners with Allah. The believer seeks guidance from Allah instead, and the material cites the report of Jabir bin 'Abdullah, which Ahmad, al-Bukhari and the collectors of the Sunan recorded. The Prophet ﷺ taught his Companions the prayer of istikharah, a voluntary two rak'ahs and a prayer asking Allah to choose the good for them, in all their affairs, just as he taught them a surah of the Qur'an. The material notes that Ahmad and At-Tirmidhi, who graded it Hasan Sahih Gharib, record its wording.

**"This is all evil"** is the verse's summary. The material says that the word for it refers to disobedience, sin, misguidance, ignorance and, above all, the association of partners with Allah. Since drawing lots is a form of that association, the Qur'an calls the whole list an abomination. Ibn 'Abbas and others said that Allah wants the believers to seek the decision from Him, after first worshipping Him.

**"Today the disbelievers have given up all hope of ˹undermining˺ your faith"** speaks of a day. The material gives this as the day of 'Arafah in the tenth year after the Hijrah, when the Muslims stood at the Plain of 'Arafah with the Prophet ﷺ. The disbelievers had hoped to bring the believers back to their old religion, and by this point they had no hope of it. Ibn 'Abbas explained that they gave up hope that the Muslims would return to their religion. The material links this to a saying in the two Sahih collections, that Shaytan has despaired of being worshipped in the Arabian Peninsula, though he will still stir trouble among its people.

**"So do not fear them; fear Me!"** is the instruction to the believers, not to be afraid of the disbelievers, because they now have no power to harm them, but to fear Allah alone. The material says that the believers were to stand firm, be patient, oppose the disbelievers in what they do, and fear none but Allah, who would grant them victory, raise them above their enemies in this life and in the next, and please their hearts.

**"Today I have perfected your faith for you, completed My favour upon you, and chosen Islam as your way"** is the most discussed sentence in the passage. The material gives three rewards in it. The first is the perfection of the religion. Ibn 'Abbas explained that this means all the limits, obligations, rules and refinements of personal and social conduct have been completed, so that nothing needs to be added and nothing is missing. The material says that no new rule was revealed after this, apart from a few verses of encouragement and reminder. The jurists who work out new rulings by careful reasoning, called ijtihad, do not add anything to this perfection, because their rulings rest on principles that the Qur'an itself laid down. The second reward is the completion of the favour. The material explains this as the rise of the Muslims and the defeat of their enemies, shown in the conquest of Makkah, the end of the customs of ignorance, and the absence of disbelievers from the Hajj that year. The third reward is that Allah has chosen Islam as the religion of this community, the last and most complete religion, after which no other will come. The material notes a difference in the wording. "Perfection" is the purpose of the law being completed, and "completion" is that nothing more is needed. The religion is attributed to the believers, because it is shown in what they do, and the favour is attributed to Allah, because it comes from Him.

The material also says that the perfection does not mean the earlier prophets had an imperfect faith. Each prophet's religion was complete for its own time and people. Islam is different because it was sent last and is meant for every time, place and people until the Last Day. The material adds that this completion was the climax of a faith that began with Adam and continued through the prophets, and that this final form was given to the Last of the Prophets ﷺ and to his community.

Some reports describe the reaction of the Companions. A Jewish man said to 'Umar bin al-Khattab that if this verse had been revealed to them, they would have made the day of its revelation a festival. 'Umar replied that he knew exactly when and where it was revealed, on the evening of 'Arafah on a Friday. In another report, 'Umar wept when the verse came down, and the Prophet ﷺ asked why. 'Umar said the verse seemed to mean that the time of the Prophet ﷺ in this world was nearing its end, because the religion had now been perfected. The Prophet ﷺ agreed with him. The material notes that the Prophet ﷺ died eighty-one days after the revelation of the verse.

The material draws a lesson from 'Umar's answer. Islam does not make festivals of the births, deaths or anniversaries of people, and it does not turn historical events into celebrations as other peoples did. Instead, it marks acts of worship and principles. The two festivals of Islam are 'Id al-Fitr after Ramadan and 'Id al-Adha after the Hajj. The material notes that the Night of Deliverance, Laylat al-Bara'ah, the Night of Power, the Day of 'Arafah and the Day of 'Ashura are honoured because of the acts of worship linked to them. It criticises later invented celebrations, including the birthday of the Prophet ﷺ, as having no basis in the words or deeds of the Companions or the early righteous generations.

**"But whoever is compelled by extreme hunger—not intending to sin—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** is the exception. The material explains that a person who faces death from hunger may eat a little of what is forbidden, just enough to remove the danger, and not to satisfy appetite. His heart must not be inclined to sin. The verse is an exception to the general rule, and it does not make the forbidden things lawful in themselves. The material says that the phrase about forgiveness shows the prohibition still stands and that only the person in this state is granted leave. The reports add that the permission does not require a waiting period of three days, as some unlearned people believed. Ahmad recorded that Abu Waqid al-Laythi asked the Prophet ﷺ when they might eat carrion in a land where famine was common, and he said: when you find no food for your meal and no produce, then eat from it. The material says the chain of this report meets the standard of the two Sahih collections. The Prophet ﷺ also said, as recorded by Ibn Hibban and in the Musnad, that Allah likes His permissions to be taken, just as He dislikes to be disobeyed. A verse of Surat al-Baqarah gives the same exception with the condition that one neither wilfully disobeys nor goes beyond the necessary limit. The material notes that some scholars took this to mean a traveller who sets out to sin does not receive the concessions of travel.

### The fish and the locust

The reports make two exceptions to the rule of carrion. Ibn 'Umar, in a report collected by Ibn Majah, ad-Daraqutni, al-Bayhaqi and Ahmad through 'Abdur-Rahman bin Zayd bin Aslam, says the Prophet ﷺ allowed two dead things and two kinds of blood. The dead things are fish and locust. The bloods are the liver and the spleen. The material notes that 'Abdur-Rahman is a weak narrator. The material also records that Abu Hurayrah reported that the Prophet ﷺ, asked about the sea, said its water is pure and its dead are lawful, and this report was collected by Malik in the Muwatta, by Abu Dawud, at-Tirmidhi, an-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah in their Sunan, and by Ibn Khuzaymah and Ibn Hibban in their Sahih collections. The material notes that the same rule covers locusts, as a later report shows.

### Why the forbidden things are harmful

The material explains the reasons. Some animals are harmful to the body, because disease can pass from them, or to morals and character. These the Qur'an declares evil and unlawful. Carrion is unlawful partly because its blood stays in its flesh and is not drawn out, as it is when the animal is slaughtered properly. Blood is forbidden as it is poured out, and the slaughter of an animal properly is the way it is drained. The material adds that other forms of killing do not drain the blood in the same way. This is the reason for the careful method of slaughter, dhabh, which makes sure the blood flows out. The material also notes that many other things in the animal's body besides the flesh are unfit to eat.

Some scholars explained the prohibition of swine and of invoked animals as a matter of purity: both are associated with unbelief and with things that corrupt the soul. The material says that for this reason the meat of an animal slaughtered in the name of a false god is unlawful in itself, whatever the method of slaughter.

### The inner meaning

The material also gives a reading that uses the verse to describe the soul. It says that an animal's flesh has two conditions. When it is killed in the proper way under the law, it is clean and may be eaten. When it dies by itself, it is defiled. In the same way, when the human self is slain in the struggle for discipline, subdued by religion, made a servant of the law and humbled by the burden of obedience, the soul becomes clean and close. But when a soul dies in heedlessness, no longer aware of the religion and no longer working within the limits of the law, it becomes like carrion, which must be avoided. The same reading takes the Qur'anic phrase about the strangled, the beaten and the fallen as images of the states of the soul.

The material also cites a report, given by Ibn Hibban, that a Muslim's backbiting is a form of carrion worse than the rest. The Qur'an, in 49:12, forbids backbiting. The material says that the prohibition is stronger here, and that it is never allowed under compulsion or free choice.

### In short

- The verse forbids carrion, blood, swine, the animal slaughtered for a name other than Allah, and the animals killed by strangling, beating, a fall, a goring or a predator, unless they can be properly slaughtered.
- It forbids the animals sacrificed on stone altars and the use of arrows to decide matters, which the material calls an abomination and a form of associating partners with Allah.
- It names the day of 'Arafah in the tenth year as the time when the religion was perfected, the favour completed and Islam chosen as the way of the community.
- It allows a person in extreme hunger, without any intention to sin, to eat what is otherwise forbidden, only as much as needed to save him.
- The material also links the verse to lessons about festivals, the nature of the soul, and the prohibition of backbiting.
