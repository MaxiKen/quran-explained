<<<VERSE 4:23>>>
The verse of the muḥarramāt, the women prohibited in marriage: "˹Also˺ forbidden to you for marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother's daughters, your sister's daughters, your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters, your mothers-in-law, your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers—but if you have not, then you can marry them—nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together at the same time—except what was done previously. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" (4:23). One verse closes a whole map of relationships, by blood, by suckling and by marriage, and it ends with forgiveness for what the past had already allowed.

### Phrase by phrase

**"your mothers, your daughters, your sisters"** opens with the closest degrees. Mothers includes every grandmother, paternal or maternal. Daughters includes the daughter's daughter and the son's daughter, down the line. Sisters covers the full sister, the ʿallātī sister of the same father, and the akhyaḳī sister of the same mother. A daughter born of zinā follows the rule of the daughter, and marriage with her is not correct, just as a stepdaughter from a different husband has her own rule further in the verse. Adopted children, by contrast, are not blood, and it is permissible to marry them or their offspring, provided nothing else prohibits it. Even where a father has fallen into illicit relations with a woman, his son may still not marry her.

**"your paternal and maternal aunts"** closes the aunts in full. The father's sister is prohibited, whether she is his full sister or a half-sister from either side, and the same holds for the mother's sister. The readings extend both to great-aunts, the sisters of grandfathers and grandmothers.

**"your brother's daughters, your sister's daughters"** takes in the nieces, again whether the brother or sister is full, ʿallātī or akhyaḳī, and the children of these daughters follow them into prohibition.

**"your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters"** builds a second family out of milk, with the same force as the first, as the sections below unfold.

**"your mothers-in-law"** prohibits the wife's mother from the moment the marriage contract exists, consummated or not, and the word mothers there includes all the wife's grandmothers, maternal, paternal, lineal or foster.

**"your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers"** gives the rule of the rabība, the wife's daughter by another husband: prohibited once the husband has consummated the marriage with her mother, with her granddaughters included in the prohibition. **"but if you have not, then you can marry them"** is the mercy at its centre: where the marriage was contracted but never consummated, the stepdaughter remains lawful.

**"nor the wives of your own sons"** prohibits the daughter-in-law, and the word sons reaches to grandsons, paternal or maternal, with a decisive restriction examined below: from your own loins, which excludes the adopted son.

**"nor two sisters together at the same time"** bars the rival sisters, real or half, by lineage or by suckling.

**"except what was done previously"** forgives the marriages of the Jāhiliyyah, and **"Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"** promises that what people did erroneously before Islam will be overlooked once they have embraced it.

### Seven by blood, seven by marriage

Ibn ʿAbbās counted the verse's work in two sevens. Allah has prohibited seven types of relatives by blood and seven by marriage, he said, and then recited this verse. At-Ṭabarī recorded the saying, and the count itself is a summary of the map: mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal aunts, maternal aunts, brother's daughters, sister's daughters, then the second seven built by milk and by contract.

### What suckling establishes

The milk-relationship is not a metaphor. ʿĀʾishah, the Mother of the Faithful, is the narrator, in the records of al-Bukhārī and Muslim alike, that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "Suckling prohibits what birth prohibits." In another narration reported by Muslim: "Suckling establishes prohibited degrees just as blood does." The woman who suckles a child becomes his foster-mother, her husband his foster-father, her children his brothers and sisters, her sisters his maternal aunts, her husband's brothers and sisters his foster-uncles and paternal aunts, and marriage is prohibited across the whole map exactly as it is by kinship.

How much suckling establishes it was itself once recited. In his Ṣaḥīḥ, Muslim recorded that ʿĀʾishah said that among the parts of the Qur'an that were revealed was the statement: "Ten incidents of suckling establishes the prohibition concerning marriage." Five later replaced the ten, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ passed away with the statement still recited as part of the Qur'an. Sahlah bint Suhayl narrates the application: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ told her to give Sālim, the freed slave of Abū Hudhayfah, five sucklings. Less than five incidents of suckling will not establish the prohibition.

The timing matters. The suckling must occur within the usual days of suckling, before the age of two, as the Qur'an sets the term: "The mothers should suckle their children for two whole years" (2:233), the rule explained in Sūrat al-Baqarah. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Fosterage is only from hunger," meaning it counts where the child has no option but to suckle and grow through it, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim. According to Imam Abū Ḥanīfah the period runs from birth to two and a half years. According to other jurists, among them his special disciples Imam Abū Yūsuf and Imam Muḥammad, the period is two years only, and suckling after the age of two establishes no prohibition.

The jurists drew out the working rules. Two children suckled by one woman cannot marry each other, and the daughter of a foster-brother or foster-sister is equally closed. The lineal mother of a foster-brother remains lawful, as does the foster-mother of the lineal sister of a foster-sister. The feed counts if received through the mouth or the nose in the days of suckling, but not through any other inlet, and milk of animals or of a man establishes nothing. Where a woman's feed is mixed into medicine or animal milk, the prohibition stands only if her milk is the greater part or at least equal. If a man's glands were ever to lactate, it proves nothing. Mere mouthing at the breast without certainty of a feed establishes no prohibition, for the law is not built on a doubt. Where a woman claims after a marriage that she suckled both spouses, the claim confirmed by both dissolves the marriage, while a claim rejected by both leaves it standing, though the God-fearing pair would do well to separate. Proof needs two practicing Muslim witnesses, one man and two women equalling two men, and given that a whole life turns ḥalāl or ḥarām on it, the prudent course is to hold back from the marriage even on a single upright witness's word.

### The mother-in-law and the stepdaughter

The mother of the wife is prohibited by the contract alone. Even if the husband never slept with his wife, her mother is unlawful for him to marry, and the rule runs wider. The mother of a woman with whom a man slept in the belief she was his wife, though she was not, and the mother of a woman with whom he committed zinā, or whom he touched lustfully, is equally unlawful to him.

The stepdaughter's rule turns on consummation. When a man marries a woman and sleeps with her after the nikāh, the woman's daughter from another husband becomes unlawful for him, and with her the daughter's granddaughters on both sides. If he has not slept with the wife and then divorces her after the contract, her daughter and granddaughter remain lawful for him. And consummation is read broadly: a lustful touch after the nikāḥ, or a look at her private part with desire, is taken as having had relations with her, and closes the daughter.

The words "your women" are general. It is not the legally wedded wife only whose daughters become unlawful, but any woman with whom the man has had intercourse, in marriage, in the mistaken belief she was his wife, or in zinā. The daughter and granddaughter of such a woman are closed to him.

### The stepdaughter hadith

Whether the stepdaughter was under the stepfather's guardianship makes no difference. The majority of scholars state that the stepdaughter is prohibited for the stepfather who consummated the marriage with her mother, guardianship or no guardianship, and the Prophet's ﷺ own case settles it. The Two Ṣaḥīḥs record that Umm Ḥabībah said: "O Messenger of Allah, marry my sister, the daughter of Abū Sufyān," named in one narration as ʿAzzah bint Abū Sufyān. He asked: "Do you like that I do that?" She said she would not give him up for anything, but the best person she liked to share with her in righteous good was her sister. He said: "That is not allowed for me." She said they had been told that he wanted to marry the daughter of Abū Salamah. He asked: "The daughter of Umm Salamah?" She said yes. He said: "Even if she was not my stepdaughter and under my guardianship, she would still not be allowed for me." The reason lay in milk: she was his niece through suckling, for Thuwaybah had nursed him and Abū Salamah both. He closed the matter with the words: "Therefore, do not offer me to marry your daughters or sisters." In another narration: "Had I not married Umm Salamah, her daughter would not have been allowed for me anyway." His marriage to Umm Salamah was the real reason behind the prohibition, and the foster-tie reinforced it.

### Sons' wives, and the adopted

The wife of one's own son is prohibited, and the universality of "sons" includes grandsons, paternal or maternal. The restriction "from your own loins" excludes the adopted son, for adoption was a common practice of the Jāhiliyyah, and the wife of an adopted son is lawful to marry once he has released her. Allah said: "So when Zayd had accomplished his desire from her, We gave her to you in marriage, so that there may be no difficulty to the believers in respect of the wives of their adopted sons, when the latter have no desire to keep them" (33:37).

Ibn Jurayj said he asked ʿAṭāʾ about this part of the verse, and was told that the Prophet's ﷺ marriage to the former wife of Zayd, his adopted son before Islam ended the practice, drew criticism from the idolators of Makkah. Allah then sent down the verse on wives of sons born of one's own loins, together with "nor has He made your adopted sons your real sons" (33:4), and "Muhammad is not the father of any of your men" (33:40). Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded that al-Ḥasan ibn Muḥammad said these verses are encompassing, covering the wives of the sons and the wives' mothers whether the marriage was consummated or not, and there is consensus on that ruling. The same explanation is given by Ṭāwūs, Ibrāhīm, az-Zuhrī and Makhūl.

One question remained. Why is the wife of a son by suckling prohibited, when there is no blood? The answer is the Prophet's ﷺ word already cited: suckling prohibits what birth prohibits.

### Two sisters together

No one may take or keep two sisters as rival wives, or as rival female-servants, according to the consensus of the Companions' scholars, their followers and the Imams of old and present. The sisters may be real, ʿallātī or akhyaḳī, by lineage or by suckling. Where one sister has been divorced, marrying the other becomes permissible only after her ʿiddah, the waiting period, has expired. The sunnah adds the aunts: it is not lawful to combine a woman with her paternal aunt, nor with her maternal aunt, as reported in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, where the Prophet ﷺ said: "Do not combine a woman with her paternal aunt, nor a woman with her maternal aunt." The jurists state the general principle: any two women who, were one of them a male, could not lawfully marry each other, cannot be combined in marriage with one man. Each of them may be married separately, and handmaidens may be owned together, but the bed is shared with only one at a time.

### The exception of the past

Whatever happened in the Jāhiliyyah is not called to account. The words appeared in the previous verse and appear again here: what came to pass then has passed, and the one who embraces Islam is not judged by it, though he must leave the practice now. That included separation. Whoever held the wife of his father, or two sisters, in marriage at the coming of Islam had to part from them, one sister necessarily being divorced. A man who had married his father's wife was, as al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib narrated, executed on the Prophet's ﷺ order, the mission going to Abū Burdah ibn Niyār. And the converts came with their own cases. Ad-Daḥḥāk bin Fayruz, in a report Imam Aḥmad carries, said that his father told him: "I embraced Islam while married to two sisters at the same time, and the Prophet ﷺ commanded me to divorce one of them." Ibn Firoz ad-Dailamī narrates from his father the same ruling in his own words: "When I embraced Islam, I had two sisters married to me. I went to the Prophet ﷺ with the problem. He said: 'Separate by divorcing one and keeping the other.'" The same reaching back is the verse's own closing: Allah is Forgiving of what people did in the pre-Islamic era, and Merciful regarding what ensues from them in Islam, provided they repent.

### Why the limits at all

The verse answers a human fact. Desires make themselves felt in people, sexuality among them, and the Islamic law, which fixes a limit for every emotion, fixes the limits of sexual alliance too. Only the alliance established through a standard, solemn social agreement, which is marriage, is valid, and the family life it builds must carry an environment of chastity. For that purpose, the closest relations are declared prohibited, kept beyond and above sexual desire, so that a zone of the household remains forever free of it, where trust needs no guard.

### The inner reading

Read inwardly, the verse teaches how the believer stands before the law at all. The sharīʿah rests on servanthood, not on self-exertion. Its canon comes by transmission, not by rational derivation, and the sunnah stands on surrender, not on the hunting for reasons. Surrender is an easy road, a flourishing dwelling, and its goal is the approval of the All-Merciful. Self-exertion and self-determination make a hard road through ruins, ending in what is unwholesome. So leap clear of self-exertion's road and cling to surrender, and quit the search for causes. Whatever the sharīʿah has declared forbidden, consider forbidden, a consignment by the Desire built on the Will. Had the same thing been permitted instead, the surrender would be the same, for He is the Real, majestic in His majesty, who "does whatsoever He wills" (3:40) and "decrees whatsoever He desires" (5:1). To whom He wills He forbids, and to whom He wills He allows. His craftsmanship is without cause, His decree without protester.

### In short

- Seven degrees of blood and seven of marriage are closed forever: the direct line up and down, the sisters, the aunts on both sides, the nieces, and, by milk and by contract, their counterparts.
- Suckling prohibits what birth prohibits, once it passes the threshold of five feeds within the suckling age, two years by the dominant reckoning, and its whole family map follows.
- The mother-in-law is prohibited by the contract alone. The stepdaughter, by consummation with her mother, broadly read to include the lustful touch. The daughter-in-law, by the marriage of one's own son, adopted sons excluded by the Qur'an's own ruling.
- Two sisters, and a woman with her aunt, may not be combined in one marriage, and the converts of the Jāhiliyyah were ordered to release what they held.
- The believer receives the list in surrender, for the law is transmitted, not bargained over, and its Maker decrees what He desires.
