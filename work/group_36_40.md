## Verse 36

Verse 36 says: *When she delivered, she said, “My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—“the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.”*

In simple terms, the verse tells the outcome of the vow: when the wife of 'Imran gave birth, she discovered it was a girl — and Allah knew exactly what she had delivered. She said the male is not like the female, named the girl Mary, and sought Allah's protection for her and her offspring from Satan the accursed.

"When she delivered, she said, "My Lord! I have given birth to a girl"" — the verse's drama is the difference between the vow and the outcome. The mother had pledged a *muharrar* — a dedicated servant — expecting (as Al-Jalalayn and Ibn Kathir note) a male, for in the law of the earlier people only males served in the Temple in that manner. She was old, she had waited, she had vowed — and the child was a girl. Ibn Kathir: "She did not know then what she would give birth to, a male or a female." The verse records the moment of discovery: the mother's own words — "My Lord! I have given birth to a girl."

"—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered" — the parenthetical is the Qur'an's most important comment in the verse: *wa Allah a'lamu bima wada'at* — and Allah knew better what she bore. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings treat the parenthetical as addressed to the reader: the mother's first word was disappointment at the gender; the Qur'an corrects the reader's assumption — Allah knew what she bore, and what she bore was the woman who would carry the Messiah. The "girl" was the greater gift. The surah's hierarchy of the sexes is here stated against the background of the era's valuation: the birth of a girl was to the Arabs of the pre-Islamic era a shame; the Qur'an's story of the chosen family begins with the birth of the girl, and the birth of the girl is the birth of the mother of the Messiah.

"the male is not like the female" — *laysa al-dhakar ka al-untha* — the male is not like the female. Ibn Kathir: "in strength and the commitment to worship Allah and serve the Masjid in Jerusalem." The mother's statement is the acceptance of the difference: the girl who was born was not the son she had envisioned, and the service she had pledged would be served by a girl. The verse's context makes the statement rich: the male is not like the female — in the world's valuation of 3:14 the sons and the desire for children are the world's ornaments; the Qur'an's chosen line is carried by the daughter.

"I have named her Mary" — *innami sammaytuha Maryam* — I have named her Maryam. Ibn Kathir draws a legal and prophetic point: "thus testifying to the fact that it is allowed to give a name to the newly born the day it is born, as is apparent from the Ayah, which is also a part of the law of those who were before us." He cites the Sunnah: the Prophet said, "This night a son was born for me and I called him by my father's name, Ibrahim" (al-Bukhari and Muslim), and the narration of Anas that the Prophet chewed a date, put it in the newborn's mouth, and called him 'Abdullah. The naming is the mother's act of identification: the nameless girl is given her name — Maryam.

"and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed" — the mother's second prayer after the birth: *u'idhuha bika wa dhurriyyataha min al-Shaytan al-rajim* — I seek refuge for her and her offspring from Satan. Ibn Kathir: "she sought refuge with Allah from the evil of Shaytan, for her and her offspring, i.e., 'Isa, peace be upon him. Allah accepted her supplication." He cites the hadith recorded by 'Abd ar-Razzaq from Abu Hurayrah: the Prophet said — there is no newborn child who is not touched by Satan at birth, except Maryam and her son; and Abu Hurayrah invited: "Recite if you wish: 'I seek refuge with You for her and her offspring from Satan the accursed.'" The prayer of the mother was accepted: the protection from the accursed was granted to the mother of the Messiah and to her son.

For the reader, the verse is the turning of the story: the mother's "disappointment" at the birth of a girl is the Qur'an's demonstration that the valuation of the world is not the valuation of Allah. A girl was born to a woman who had vowed a son; Allah knew what she bore — and the girl became the mother whose story the angels will witness, chosen above the women of the worlds (3:42). The verse also contains the mother's wisdom: she did not withdraw the vow with the change of the child; she committed the girl, named her, and placed her under the protection of Allah. The lesson of the parent: the outcome of the dedication may not match the plan; the dedication continues — and the protection is sought before the child begins the journey.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 37

Verse 37 says: *So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” She replied, “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.”*

In simple terms, the verse records the acceptance: the Lord accepted Mary with gracious acceptance, caused her to grow in a good manner, placed her under the care of Zachariah; every time he entered the sanctuary he found provisions with her; he asked from where, and she answered: from Allah — for Allah provides for whom He wills without limit.

"So her Lord accepted her graciously" — *fa-taqabbalaha rabbuha bi qabulin hasan* — accepted her with goodly acceptance. The request of 3:35 ("accept it from me") is answered: the dedication was accepted, and the acceptance was *hasan* — good, beautiful, complete. Al-Jalalayn: "Her Lord accepted the child — He received Mary from her mother with gracious acceptance." The mother's vow, the mother's naming, the mother's refuge — all were received; the girl born to the woman who had longed for a son was accepted with the goodly acceptance.

"and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing" — *wa ambataha nabatan hasanan* — grew her, caused her to grow, with a good growing. Al-Jalalayn: "made her grow up with excellent character. She would grow in a day by as much as a new-born grew during a year." Ibn Kathir: "made her conduct becoming, her mannerism delightful and He made her well liked among people. He also made her accompany the righteous people, so that she learned righteousness, knowledge and religion." The growing is total — character, conduct, popularity with people, the association of the righteous: the upbringing of Maryam was the work of the One who accepted her.

"entrusting her to the care of Zachariah" — *wa kaffalaha Zakariyya* — and He gave her in care to Zakariyya. Al-Jalalayn's narrative: her mother took her to the priests, the keepers of the Holy House of Jerusalem, and said, "This here before you is the dedication I offered." They competed for guardianship of her, because she was the daughter of their religious leader; Zachariah said, "I am most worthy of her for her maternal aunt lives with me." The others said, "Not until we have cast lots." All twenty-nine of them departed to the River Jordan, where they cast their quills, agreeing that the one whose quill remained fast and floated to the surface would be most worthy to be guardian. Zachariah's quill remained fast and surfaced. He took charge of her and built for her a gallery-room with a ladder in the Temple, and none apart from him went up to her; he used to bring her food, drink, and oil. Ibn Kathir also notes the tradition that Zakariyya was the husband of her maternal aunt — quoting the hadith of the night journey, "I saw John and 'Isa, who are maternal cousins" — and the hadith from the Two Sahihs that the Prophet decided 'Amarah, the daughter of Hamzah, be raised by her maternal aunt, the wife of Ja'far bin Abi Talib, saying: "The maternal aunt is just like the mother." The guardianship of Maryam was the sign of her honor: the chosen girl was placed in the care of a prophet.

"Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions" — *kullama dakhala 'alayha Zakariyya al-mihrab* — every time he entered upon her the mihrab, the prayer chamber, the most noble seat of the Temple. The mihrab is her private place of worship, and in it he found *rizqan* — provision, sustenance. Ibn Kathir records the classical readings: Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Abu al-Sha'tha, Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, Ad-Dahhak, Qatadah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas, 'Atiyah al-'Awfi, and as-Suddi said — "He would find with her the fruits of the summer during winter, and the fruits of the winter during summer." Al-Jalalayn: "He would find her with summer fruits in winter and winter fruits in summer." The provision was out of season — the ordinary order of provision reversed, the sign of the One who provides without the means.

"He exclaimed, "O Mary! Where did this come from?"" — the question of the prophet to the girl: *anna laki hadha* — where is this from for you? The question is the natural response to the sign: the guardian prophet's own supplies were ordinary; the girl's supplies were from a source he could not see.

"She replied, "It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit."" — the answer is the verse's center: *huwa min 'indi Allah* — it is from Allah — *inna Allah yarzuqu man yasha' bi ghayr hisab* — Allah provides for whom He wills without reckoning. The girl's answer is the creed in one line: the provision that has no visible source has the Source; the provision that has no calculation has the One who does not need the calculation. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn (on the parallel 3:27) tie the *bi ghayr hisab* to the same phrase in 3:27 — the provision without limit. The "little" girl at the Temple answered the prophet with the doctrine the whole surah teaches: the provision, like the sovereignty and the honor, is in the hand of the One who gives without measure.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's demonstration of the relationship between dedication and provision: the mother dedicated the child, the Lord accepted the dedication, and the provision followed the accepted one. It is also the verse of the child's faith: the only speech the Qur'an records directly from the young Maryam before the story of 'Isa is the statement of tawhid — "It is from Allah." The girl raised in the sanctuary under the care of a prophet, supplied with out-of-season fruits, answered the prophet's question with the doctrine that the whole passage teaches: the provider without limit. The believer who reads the verse and wonders about his own provision — where it comes from, how long it will last, how it will reach him — is being taught that the provision has one Source and no arithmetic.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 38

Verse 38 says: *Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the prophet's response to the sign he witnessed: immediately, Zachariah prayed to his Lord — grant me from Your grace pure offspring; You are truly the Hearer of supplication.

"Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord" — *hunalika da'a Zakariyya rabbahu* — at that point, there, at that place and moment, Zachariah called upon his Lord. Al-Jalalayn: "Then when Zachariah had seen this and realized that the One with power to bring something about in other than its natural time is able to bring about a child in old age and with those of his family line all deceased, he prayed to his Lord." The trigger was the sign of the out-of-season provisions: the One who gives summer fruits in winter is the One who can give a child to an old man — the same logic by which the whole surah's argument has run. The prophet was not amazed into silence; he was moved to supplication.

"when he entered the sanctuary to pray in the middle of the night" — Al-Jalalayn places the prayer: when he entered the sanctuary to pray in the middle of the night. Ibn Kathir: "When Zakariyya saw that Allah provided sustenance for Maryam by giving her the fruits of winter in summer and the fruits of summer in winter, he was eager to have a child of his own. By then, Zakariyya had become an old man, his bones feeble and his head full of gray hair. His wife was an old woman who was barren. Yet, he still supplicated to Allah and called Him in secret." The one who saw the provision of the girl in the sanctuary prayed for a child in the same sanctuary, in the middle of the night, in secret.

"Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring" — *hab li min ladunka dhurriyyatan tayyibah* — grant me from You, from Your very self, good offspring. The *min ladunka* — from Yourself — is the key phrase: not from my effort, not from my capacity, not from the natural order — from You. And *tayyibah* — good, pure, excellent: not any offspring, but the good one. Ibn Kathir: "A good offspring — meaning a righteous offspring." The request came from the man whose wife was barren and whose age was past — the request was for exactly what the natural order could not give, from the One who had just overridden the natural order in the sanctuary.

"You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers" — *innaka Sami' al-du'a'* — You are the Hearer of supplication, the One who hears the call. The name is the same as 3:34's *Sami'* and 3:35's *al-Sami' al-'Alim* — the chain of hearing: the All-Hearing heard the barren woman in the story before; the same All-Hearing is addressed by the old prophet in the story now. The two prayers of the house of 'Imran's story are paired: the woman's dedication-prayer in 3:35 and the prophet's offspring-prayer in 3:38; both end in the hearing.

For the reader, the verse is the model of the believer's response to the signs of Allah: to see the provision and to ask; to witness the power over the natural order and to turn the witness to the need; to pray in secret at the middle of the night; and to ask from Allah, not from the means. The verse also answers the believer who has concluded that his situation is beyond hope: the man in the verse was old, his bones feeble, his wife barren, his family line deceased — the verse's point is precisely that this was the man who prayed, and the prayer was answered. The natural order is the thing the Qur'an repeatedly shows overridden by the One who set it. The believer's part — as the middle of the night, the secret prayer, and the "from You" show — is to make the request of the One who orders the natural world.

## Verse 39

Verse 39 says: *So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary, “Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John who will confirm the Word of Allah and will be a great leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the answer to the prayer: while Zachariah was standing in prayer in the sanctuary, the angels called to him — Allah gives you good news of John, who will confirm a word from Allah, and he will be a leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.

"So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary" — *fa-nadathu al-mala'ikah* — so the angels called him — *wa huwa qa'imun yusalli fi al-mihrab* — while he was standing in prayer in the mihrab. Ibn Kathir: "the angels spoke to him directly while he was secluded, standing in prayer at his place of worship." The multiplicity of the address ("the angels called him") emphasizes the moment: the prayer in the sanctuary was answered in the sanctuary — the standing man received the good news while still standing in the prayer. The answer to the prayer of the middle of the night came in the moment of the prayer itself.

"Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John" — *anna Allah yubashshiruka bi Yahya* — Allah gives you glad tidings of Yahya. The name itself: Ibn Kathir records Qatadah and others: "he was called Yahya (literally, 'he lives') because Allah filled his life with faith." The good news names the child before the child is born — the same structure as the good news of 'Isa — and the name carries the meaning: the one who lives, filled with faith.

"who will confirm the Word of Allah" — *musaddiqan bi kalimatin min Allah* — believing in, confirming, a Word from Allah. Ibn Kathir records the classical identification: Al-'Awfi reported that Ibn 'Abbas said, and Al-Hasan, Qatadah, 'Ikrimah, Mujahid, Abu al-Sha'tha, as-Suddi, ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Ad-Dahhak and several others said — the "Word from Allah" means 'Isa, son of Maryam. Yahya would confirm, believe in, and testify to the truth of the Messiah: the man was sent before 'Isa to prepare and to testify — the same role in relation to 'Isa that the Qur'an assigns Zachariah's son and the Messiah with whom he is a maternal cousin (Ibn Kathir cites the hadith: "I saw John and 'Isa, who are maternal cousins"). The confirmation is the prophecy of the coming Word: Yahya was the sign of the One who would come after him.

"and will be a great leader" — *wa sayyidan* — and a master, a noble, a leader. Ibn Kathir collects the readings: Abu al-'Aliyah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Qatadah and Sa'id bin Jubayr said "a wise man"; Ibn 'Abbas, Ath-Thawri and Ad-Dahhak said "the noble, wise and pious man"; Sa'id bin al-Musayyib said "the scholar and faqih"; 'Atiyah said "the man noble in behavior and piety"; 'Ikrimah said "a person who is not overcome by anger"; Ibn Zayd said "the noble man"; Mujahid said "honored by Allah." The title *sayyid* encompasses the whole: the leading, the noble, the wise, the pious, the learned — a man whose station among his people was the station of the master.

"chaste" — *wa hasuran* — self-restrained, chaste. Ibn Kathir is explicit about the meaning: "does not mean he refrains from sexual relations with women, but that he is immune from illegal sexual relations. This does not mean that he does not marry women and have legal sexual relations with them, for Zakariyya said in his supplication for the benefit of Yahya, 'Grant me from You a good offspring' — meaning, grant me a son who will have offspring." The chastity is the purity from the unlawful — the control of the desire, not the abolition of the natural order.

"and a prophet among the righteous" — *wa nabiyyan min al-salihin* — and a prophet from among the righteous. Ibn Kathir: "delivers more good news of sending Yahya as Prophet after the good news that he will be born. This good news was even better than the news of Yahya's birth." The structure of the good news is the double glad tidings: the child will be born, and the child will be a prophet. Ibn Kathir compares it to the good news given to the mother of Musa: "We shall bring him back to you, and shall make him one of the Messengers" (28:7).

For the reader, the verse is the answer to the prayer of 3:38: the man who prayed in the sanctuary at the middle of the night received the answer in the sanctuary. And the verse's description of Yahya is the portrait the Qur'an returns to in 19:12–15 — the child given wisdom in childhood, the one who was told "O Yahya, take hold of the Book with might," the one given tenderness from Allah in his youth. The verse is also the surah's first announcement of the chain that leads to the Messiah: the good news of John, who will confirm the Word of Allah — the Word who is connected in the same surah to the dispute with the Christians. The good news of Yahya ascends the surah's story and points to the good news of 'Isa: both are signs of the One who does what He wills.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 40

Verse 40 says: *Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the human question and the divine answer: Zachariah asked how he could have a son when he was very old and his wife was barren — and the reply was: so will it be; Allah does what He wills.

"Zachariah exclaimed, "My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?"" — the verse records the prophet's response to the good news. Ibn Kathir: "When Zakariyya heard the good news, he started contemplating about having children at his age. He said, 'O my Lord! How can I have a son when I have reached old age and my wife is barren?'" Al-Jalalayn reads the same: the request for the sign is the natural human response to the impossible — the man who had prayed "grant me from You" now asks from the position of the natural order: the age is past, the wife is barren, the human means are absent.

The question is the question of the whole surah's argument: Zachariah is placed in the position of the questioner who must be taught the answer. The man who saw the summer fruit in winter, who saw the provision without source, who had himself prayed "from You" — he knows in one sense and must be moved in another: the knowledge of Allah's power over the natural order and the natural order's fixed limits must be reconciled. The question is the Qur'an's own rhetorical device: the same human situation as the question of Abraham (11:72 — "How can I have a child when I am old and my wife is barren?"), the same answer; and the same situation as the question the surah is answering about the Messiah: how can a child be born without a father? The answer to both is the same.

"He replied, "So will it be. Allah does what He wills."" — *kadhalika Allah yaf'alu ma yasha'* — thus it will be — Allah does what He wills. Al-Jalalayn and the classical readings: the reply is the doctrine — the One who has power to bring something about other than in its natural time (as the prophet had just witnessed in the out-of-season provisions) is the One who does what He wills. The verse's "*yasha'*" — wills — is the hinge of the surah's theology of signs: the natural order is the regular expression of His will; the sign is the direct expression of the same will; both are the actions of the One who does what He will. The barrenness of the wife, the age of the man — these are the laws of the ordinary; the child, the virgin birth, the resurrection — these are the exceptions spoken by the same will that set the laws.

For the reader, the verse is the answer to every human "how can it be?" in the surah — the birth of John to the barren, the birth of 'Isa to the virgin, the day of gathering, the victory of the small army at Badr, the transfer of the kingdom from one people to another. "So will it be. Allah does what He wills" settles the matter of the means: the believer asks the human question, and the answer is the divine will. The verse also resolves the tension of the surah's argument: Zachariah's "how" is the human reason's last word, and Allah's reply is the revelation's first — the same "kun fa-yakun" (Be! and it is) that the surah will use three verses about 'Isa (3:47, 3:59): the One who does what He wills says "Be" and the impossibility exists. The reader who has a "how" is being taught the answer before the answer arrives: the impossible is possible to the One whose will is the cause of all things; the "how" belongs to the order of means, and the means are His.
