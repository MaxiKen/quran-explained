## Verse 6

Verse 6 says: *Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the instruction of the previous verse: guardians must test orphans' competence until they reach the age of marriage, return their property when the orphans show sound judgment, avoid consuming it carelessly or in haste before then, and regulate the guardian's own compensation — the wealthy guardian takes nothing, the poor guardian may take a reasonable amount. The hand-over must be witnessed, and Allah stands as the final Reckoner.

The testing described is not a single examination but a period of observation. Al-Jalalayn explains that the guardians test the orphans in the duties of religion and before they can legally manage their own affairs, until the age of marrying — which al-Shafi'i fixed at the completion of fifteen years, while other jurists used the signs of puberty and maturity. The test is practical: the orphan is given small responsibilities and observed to see whether he keeps accounts, keeps promises, controls spending, and acts with sense in religious and worldly matters. Ibn Kathir frames it as the point where the guardian perceives "right judgement in matters of religion and their property."

The return of the property is conditional on *rushd* — rendered here "capable of sound judgment." *Rushd* is more than physical adulthood; it is the capacity to manage one's affairs without ruining oneself. This is why the Qur'an does not fix a single age in absolute terms: the criterion is competence, and competence is assessed. The jurists built an elaborate law on this verse — the *ḥajr* (legal restriction on disposition of property) of minors, the mentally ill, and spendthrifts, and the test of sound judgment before the restriction is lifted. The underlying principle is that guardianship exists for the ward's benefit, and the moment the ward is able to manage his own property, the guardianship must end.

The verse then forbids two destructive attitudes. First, *wa-lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa-bidāran* — "do not consume it wastefully and hastily." Ibn Kathir explains this as spending beyond what is needed and eating it in haste, "fearing they grow up and you will be obliged to hand it over." The verses came down precisely because some guardians were consuming orphans' property while waiting for the orphans to mature, treating the delay as a window for spending. The Qur'an names both the wastefulness (*isrāf*) and the haste (*bidār*), because the guardian's motive is often not greed for the property itself but greed for the time in which it is held. The warning is that the property is not a salary for a job the guardian never finished.

The treatment of the guardian's compensation is one of Islam's most careful social rulings: the rich guardian abstains entirely; the poor guardian may consume *bi-al-maʿrūf* — honourably, in line with the wage for his work. Al-Jalalayn explains: "Rich guardian abstains; poor guardian consumes honourably — in line with the wage for his work." The rule means the guardian must not use custody as a source of income when he does not need it; and where he is poor, his service is real work and the law permits him to take what is reasonable — not a commission or a cut, but provision in proportion to the work actually done for the ward. This is the Quranic model of trusteeship: service without exploitation, and reward without extortion.

The command to call witnesses at the hand-over — *wa-ashhidū ʿalayhim* — is presented by Al-Jalalayn as "so you are absolved of the obligation and if any dispute occurs you are able to refer to a clear proof." Ibn Kathir notes the command is intended for guidance and protection. The witnesses do not make the transaction legal; they make it demonstrable. A hand-over that leaves no trace invites false accusations and denied claims. In the same way, guardians who keep written records of property in their care are following the spirit of the verse, and the closing clause — "sufficient is Allah as a vigilant Reckoner" — reminds both sides that even where witnesses are absent or records lost, the accounting is not lost.

For modern practice the verse has clear applications. Guardians, executors, and trustees should set testing criteria and a defined age at which the ward's competency is assessed rather than clinging to control indefinitely; they should keep records precisely so that the hand-over can be witnessed and verified; they should pay themselves from their own means where they can, and only where genuinely poor take a modest, reasonable fee commensurate with work done; and they should never spend the ward's capital merely because the ward cannot yet ask for it. The whole verse is a system of checks — the ward is protected from guardians, and guardians are protected from suspicion — and at the head of the system is the Reckoner who misses nothing.

## Verse 7

Verse 7 says: *For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares.*

In simple terms, the verse establishes that both men and women are entitled to inherit from parents and close relatives — no matter whether the estate is small or large — and that these shares are fixed and obligatory. It overturns the pre-Islamic practice in which men took everything and women and children received nothing.

The verse responds to a real historical practice. Ibn Kathir reports that the idolators used to give adult men a share of the inheritance and deprive women and children of it; Sa'id bin Jubayr and Qatadah are cited for the same point. A man's inheritance went to his male heirs — often his adult sons or brothers — while daughters, wives, mothers, and young children got nothing, on the reasoning that only men who could fight, earn, and carry the family name had a claim to property. The verse's structure answers this directly: *li-al-rijāl naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūn, wa-li-al-nisāʾ naṣībun...* — for men there is a share, and for women there is a share. The repetition is not accidental: it places women absolutely on the same plane of entitlement as men. Ibn Kathir explains that "everyone is equal in Allah's decision to inherit, though shares vary by degree of relationship."

The phrase "whether it is little or much" is also deliberate. *Mimmā qalla minhu aw kathur* — from what is little of it or much. The pre-Islamic Arabs and some later customs tried to distinguish: a daughter might receive a nominal share when the estate was small, or nothing when a man's male heirs were numerous. The Qur'an closes that loophole: the *naṣīb* (share) applies to every estate, regardless of size, and no estate is too small to fall under the rule. This has practical legal consequence — even a small inheritance must be divided according to the prescribed limits, and heirs cannot agree to bypass the shares on the ground that the estate is trivial.

The occasion of revelation clarifies the verse's social force. Ibn Marduwyah records from Jabir that Umm Kujjah came to the Prophet and said, "I have two daughters whose father died, and they do not own anything," and Allah revealed this verse. Maududi, following Al-Sabuni's Safwat al-Tafasir, adds the well-known tradition of Sa'd bin Ar-Rabi' after the Battle of Uhud: Sa'd was killed leaving two daughters, and his brother took the entire property, telling Sa'd's widow, "I will look after them" — meaning he kept the inheritance for himself. The two girls were left with nothing, and the situation was brought to the Prophet, who sent word that Sa'd's daughters be given two-thirds and the widow one-eighth — the ruling that later appears in 4:11 and 4:12.

The verse's final words, "obligatory shares," render *naṣīban mafrūḍan* — a share apportioned and made obligatory. The term *farḍ* is the same root as *farāʾiḍ*, the science of Islamic inheritance law, and it signals that inheritance is not a matter of the deceased's preference or the family's custom: it is a divine allocation. This is why the classical scholars, following 4:11–12, treated the fixed shares as belonging to the heirs by right and not subject to the deceased's will except within the permitted third.

The modern significance of the verse is immediate and serious. In countries where inheritance law discriminates against women, the Quranic principle stands as the criterion; in Muslim communities where daughters are pressured to sign away their shares "to keep the property in the family," the verse declares such pressure a violation of an obligatory right; and in families where the sisters' shares are treated as optional charity, the verse returns the matter to law. The verse does not say women may be given shares; it says they have shares. And the fact that the share is *mafrūḍ* — fixed — means the entitlement can be claimed, not merely hoped for.

For the reader, the verse also teaches a deeper point about the Islamic law of inheritance: the system is not a single arithmetic formula but a set of shares that vary by relationship, built on the twin principles of entitlement and responsibility. The equality announced here is equality in the right to inherit, and the different amounts set in the coming verses are a matter of that same divine allocation, balancing nearness, dependency, and responsibility in a way the reader will see as the surah unfolds.

## Verse 8

Verse 8 says: *If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly.*

In simple terms, the verse commands that when an estate is being divided, relatives who are not entitled to inherit, as well as orphans and the needy who are present, should be given something from the estate and addressed with kindness. The inheritance feast is not to be an occasion that deepens the pain of those who have no share.

The occasion is vivid. Ibn Kathir reports Al-'Awfi from Ibn 'Abbas: when the poor relatives who are ineligible to inherit, and orphans and the poor, attend the division of an estate — sometimes it is substantial — their hearts "will feel eager to have a share, seeing each eligible person assuming his share; while they are desperate, yet are not given anything." The pre-Islamic system had deprived women and children entirely, and even after the Quranic shares were assigned, some categories of kin — such as a poor uncle or a married daughter's children, or distant relations — remained outside the fixed shares. When these kin watched the heirs divide the estate, the injustice of their own position was laid bare. The verse commands: give them something, and give them kind words.

The classical scholars disagreed about whether the provision is obligatory or recommended, and Ibn Kathir sets the dispute out honestly. Al-Bukhari records Ibn 'Abbas as saying the verse "was not abrogated" — meaning its instruction remains in force — and Ibn Jarir records the same Ibn 'Abbas saying it still applies and should be implemented. Mujahid, through Ibn Abi Najih via Ath-Thawri, says the share is required from those who have anything to inherit, paid from whatever portions their hearts are satisfied giving away; similar statements are reported from Ibn Mas'ud, Abu Musa, 'Abdur-Rahman bin Abi Bakr, Abu Al-'Aliyah, Ash-Sha'bi, and Al-Hasan. Ibn Sirin, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Makhul, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i, 'Ata' bin Abi Rabah, Az-Zuhri, and Yahya bin Ya'mar also said it is obligatory. Other authorities held that the verse refers to bequeathal at death, and still others considered it abrogated by 4:7's fixed shares.

The strength of the majority position lies in the verse's own language: the command *irzuqūhum minhā* — "provide for them from it" — is an imperative, and the phrase "and speak to them kind words" is added in the same imperatival form. Ibn Kathir summarizes: the poor relatives who are ineligible, the orphans, and the poor who attend the division should be given "as an act of kindness, charity, compassion and mercy." The amount is not fixed — the translation's "small provision" reflects that the giver gives according to his means and the size of the estate — but the giving itself is a requirement of the occasion. The jurists who held the verse obligatory typically fixed the amount as whatever the heirs find suitable, with no upper limit beyond the estate's thirds and no lower limit beyond a meaningful gift.

There is a social philosophy here that deserves attention. The division of an inheritance is not a private family transaction; it is a public social moment. People gather, property is displayed, and the gap between those who receive and those who do not becomes visible. In such moments the community's character is tested. The verse requires the heirs to convert the moment of division into a moment of provision and honour rather than exclusion — giving the poor and the grieving a portion, however small, and addressing them not as intruders but as people whose presence is recognized with *qawlan maʿrūfan*, words that are known to be good.

Applied today, the verse covers the distribution of estates, the division of property among children, the settlement of family businesses, and even the reading of wills in front of relatives who were left out. The heirs who divide an inheritance while a widowed mother, a poor uncle, or a disabled relative watches are being addressed by this verse. The command's spirit also reaches beyond inheritance: any occasion on which resources are distributed — zakat collection, endowments, community funds, corporate bonuses in family firms — carries the same obligation to remember those present without a share, and to speak to them with kindness rather than embarrassment.

The verse is also a guard against a wrong reading of the fixed inheritance system. Islam's shares are precise, but they were never intended to produce an atmosphere of calculation and coldness in which the poor relation, the orphan, and the needy are treated as obstacles. The Qur'an wraps the precision of law inside the warmth of mercy: give the shares, give the fixed portions, and then — because people are watching and hearts are present — give as well. Justice and generosity are not rivals in the Quranic ethic; they are two faces of the same command.

## Verse 9

Verse 9 says: *Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably.*

In simple terms, the verse addresses all who supervise the affairs of orphans and vulnerable dependants: let your care for them be the care you would want for your own young children if you were the one who died and left them behind. Therefore fear Allah and speak justly — especially in advising about wills and inheritances.

The verse's force is the psychology of a dying man. Ibn Kathir records 'Ali bin Abi Talhah from Ibn 'Abbas: the verse refers to a man near death who dictates a will that harms the rightful inheritors — for example, he leaves everything or most of it to strangers or to one child, or he makes bequests within the estate in a way that strips his children. Whoever hears such a will must fear Allah and direct the dying man to what is right and fair, "being as eager to protect the inheritors of the dying man as he would be with his own." The command is addressed to the guardians and witnesses: the one who stands beside the dying man has power in that moment — the power to encourage, to redirect, to object, to witness. The verse tells him to use that power as if his own children were the ones about to be harmed.

The instruction is tied to a famous prophetic scene. The Two Sahihs record that when Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas was ill in Mecca, the Prophet visited him. Sa'd asked whether he might give two-thirds of his wealth in charity; the Prophet said no. Half? No. One-third? The Prophet said, "One-third, and even one-third is too much," and added: "You'd better leave your inheritors wealthy rather than leaving them poor, begging from others." Sa'd said he was wealthy and had only one daughter, and the Prophet's answer was clear: the primary duty of a man's property is to his own inheritors, and a bequest that leaves them destitute is not charity — it is a failure of duty. The verse before us generalizes the same principle to every guardian and every witness of a will.

The verse's language is built on reciprocity: *li-yakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim* — "let those fear who, if they left behind weak offspring, would fear for them." The word *ḍiʿāf* means weak, helpless, vulnerable. A dying man's imagination of his own children's future is the measure against which the guardian is asked to measure every orphan he is responsible for. This is the Quranic version of the golden rule: what you would want for your own flesh and blood, do for those whom Allah has placed in your care.

Then follows the double command: *fa-li-yattaqū Allāha wa-li-yaqūlū qawlan sadīdan* — "so let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably." The word *sadīd* means straight, correct, well-aimed. The demand is not only for truthful words but for *useful* truth — words that serve the situation, that protect the vulnerable, and that are spoken at the right moment. Mutatis mutandis, the modern application reaches the lawyer drafting a will, the witness present at a deathbed declaration, the executor, the judge deciding custody, the doctor, and the family member with the ear of an elderly parent. Each of them is asked: speak what is straight, and let the imaginary future of your own children govern your advice.

The verse also warns against a subtle selfishness — the guardian who ensures the orphan is comfortable while the will is read, but then quietly lets his own advice strip the orphan's rights; or the executor who accepts fees, watches the family divide, and says nothing. Quranic ethics here is active: the guardian is not merely forbidden from harm, he is commanded to act. "Let them be as concerned as they would if they were to die and leave their own helpless children behind" turns passivity into an offence.

Finally, the verse prepares the reader for 4:10, which follows immediately: the threat of fire for those who consume orphans' wealth. The sequence is a complete psychology of guardianship — first, in 4:2–6, the rules of property; then, in this verse, the inner state required of every witness and guardian; then the verdict for those who violate the trust. The believer is being trained at every level: the rule, the intention, and the consequence. The guardian who feels his own children's fear in the orphan's face is being formed into the kind of trustee the Qur'an can safely entrust with another's life.

## Verse 10

Verse 10 says: *Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!*

In simple terms, the verse delivers the verdict on the conduct forbidden in the previous verses: those who take the wealth of orphans unjustly are, in reality, swallowing fire into their stomachs, and they will enter a blazing fire that consumes them.

The verse's striking image is *innamā yaʾkulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran* — "they are only eating fire into their bellies." The Qur'an chooses the language of eating deliberately, because the sin is an eating — consumption of another's property. The one who devours the orphan's wealth does not simply commit a crime against property; he fills his body with the thing that will burn him from within. Ibn Kathir quotes Abu Hurayrah's report in the Two Sahihs: the Prophet said, "Avoid the seven great destructive sins" — associating partners with Allah, magic, killing the life Allah has forbidden except for just cause, consuming interest, consuming an orphan's property, turning the back and fleeing the battlefield, and accusing chaste believing women who never think of anything harmful to their chastity. The list places the consumption of an orphan's property alongside shirk, murder, and usury — among the seven that destroy the one who commits them.

The word "unjustly" is the verse's legal hinge. Not every consumption of property is forbidden: guardians may take what is owed to them; lawful debts may be collected; wages may be paid; and 4:6 allowed the poor guardian a reasonable provision from the orphan's estate. What is punished here is *ẓulm* — consuming the orphan's wealth without right, by false claim, by exchange of bad for good, by mixing and devouring, or by failing to hand it over at maturity. The verse's *ẓulm* carries the sense of placing a thing where it does not belong — the wealth that belonged to the orphan has been placed in the guardian's stomach.

Two readings of the following clause are recorded by Al-Jalalayn: *yāṣlawna* (active — they will enter) and *yuṣlawna* (passive — they will be made to enter); both lead to the same meaning, that they are delivered into the blaze and will burn. The word *saʿīr* — translated "blazing Hell" — is one of the Qur'an's words for the Fire and carries the sense of intense, sustained heat. The picture is doubled: what was swallowed in this life becomes the fire that consumes in the next, and the "eating" is matched by a "burning." In Ibn Kathir's framing, the verse warns that the consumed property "will kindle in your stomach on the Day of Resurrection," and the classical commentators expanded this from the hadiths that describe the punished person's food rising up and crying out.

The wider web of warnings in the Qur'an reinforces the severity. Zakat and inheritance law, honesty in trade, the prohibition of usury, and the repeated command to treat orphans with kindness all belong to a single system, because property in the Quranic view is an amanah laid upon human beings by the One who owns everything. The orphan, meanwhile, is singled out by the Qur'an more than any other vulnerable group — the surahs constantly join faith with the care of orphans (2:220, 4:6, 6:152, 17:34, 89:17–18, 93:9, 107:1–3), and the Prophet was himself an orphan, raised by his grandfather and uncle. The Qur'an's concern for the orphan is not sentimental; it is the test of whether a society's law protects those who have no one to protect them.

The verse also implies the rights of the estate beyond the literal orphan. The principle of "consuming what is not yours unjustly" extends to every form of wrongful appropriation: the trustee who dips into a trust, the administrator who charges what the law forbids, the relative who pressures a widow or a minor to surrender shares, the state that seizes property without right, the business partner who keeps silent while another's share is absorbed. In each case the Quranic category is the same: eating the property of another with no right, and the outcome — however delayed — is the fire.

Modern readers should feel the verse's force in two directions. For the one who holds another's property, it is a deterrent with a terrifying image. For the one whose property is held, it is assurance: the system is under divine supervision, and the unjust trustee does not escape. This is also why the verse closes the sequence that began at 4:2 — the commands to give, not to exchange, not to mix, not to consume, and to witness the hand-over, followed now by the verdict. Property law in the Qur'an is never abstract; it is a frame around the face of an orphan, and the fire that punishes the consumer is the measure of how seriously Allah takes that face.
