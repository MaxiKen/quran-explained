## Verse 66

Verse 66 says: *"So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing."*

In simple terms, the verse states the purpose of the Sabbath-breakers' punishment: Allah made their fate an example — a visible warning — for those who were present at the time, those who came after them, and ultimately for all who are God-fearing. The transformation was not merely a punishment of the culprits; it was a sign, a *nakal*, a public lesson set in history.

The word *nakāl* in the verse is the key term. It means an exemplary punishment, a deterrent, a lesson taught by the fate of the punished. The Qur'an uses the same family of words for the punishment of Pharaoh ("And Allah seized him as an example of both the Hereafter and this life," 79:25) and for the revelations given to the nations. The point is that the punishment of a single community carries a message beyond its own borders: the fate of the Sabbath-breakers was written into the history of the Children of Israel as a warning for everyone who would hear the story.

The phrase "to present and future generations" — *limā bayna yadayhā wa mā khalfahā* — literally "for what was before it and what was behind it." The punishment was an example for the generations that came before (the other nations who had been punished, who had already seen the pattern) and for the generations that came after (the later Children of Israel, the contemporaries of the Prophet ﷺ, and every reader since). The construction is a way of saying: this is not a private event; it is a public sign in the river of history, pointing in both directions.

The final phrase narrows the audience: "a lesson to the God-fearing." The punishment is an example for everyone, but it is a *lesson* only for those with taqwa. This is one of the Qur'an's constant principles: the same event, the same history, is received differently by different hearts. The rebellious read the fate of the Sabbath-breakers and think only of the past; the God-fearing read it and learn: this is what happens when the commands of Allah are treated as flexible. The verse teaches that the value of history is not merely its factual content but its moral application: history is an instructor, and the only students who learn are those who fear Allah.

Why is a "lesson" tied specifically to the God-fearing? Because it is the God-fearing who are waiting to learn — who are humble enough to receive correction, who take the warnings seriously, and who are prepared to change as a result. The person who is not God-fearing reads history and finds entertainment, or indignation, or curiosity; the person who is God-fearing reads the same history and finds himself measured against it. The lesson is not read by the eyes; it is received by the heart.

The verse also carries an important message about the purpose of punishment in the Qur'anic worldview: it is educational as well as retributive. The punishment of the Sabbath-breakers was not merely the settling of an account; it was a demonstration, designed to prevent the same failure in others. In the same way, the difficulties and failures a person experiences are not merely consequences but lessons. The believer who reads this verse is invited to ask a question about his own life: what has Allah taught me through what I have suffered, and am I learning the lesson? The person who suffers without learning repeats the lesson; the person who learns from history is among the God-fearing who receive it.

The verse is also a critique of an attitude common in the modern world: the treatment of history as mere data. If the punishment of the Sabbath-breakers is read as an anthropological curiosity — a strange ancient story with no bearing on the present — then the very purpose of the narrative has been missed. The Qur'an's history is for "those in front and behind," and its primary value is not academic but spiritual: it is example, warning, and lesson.

## Verse 67

Verse 67 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, "Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow." They replied, "Are you mocking us?" Moses responded, "I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now tells the story of the cow — the story after which the entire surah is named. The event began when a man of the Children of Israel was murdered and the identity of the killer was unknown. The people asked Moses to pray for Allah to reveal the murderer. Allah's command was unexpected: sacrifice a cow. The people responded with disbelief and mockery: "Are you making fun of us?" Moses answered with the dignity of a prophet: "I seek refuge in Allah from being among the ignorant."

The occasion of the story is given in the classical tafsir. Ibn Abi Hatim recorded from 'Ubaydah al-Salmani that there was a wealthy man among the Children of Israel who had no children and whose only heir was his nephew. The nephew killed him in order to inherit his wealth, and then disposed of the body and denied any knowledge of the murder. The community was in confusion: the killer was not known, the family was divided, and justice seemed impossible. They came to Moses and asked him to discover the truth. Allah, who knows the hidden and the revealed, gave the answer — but in a form that was itself a test: sacrifice a cow.

The people's response — "Do you make fun of us?" — reveals their state: they had been given a command of enormous spiritual significance, and all they could see was an absurdity. They had seen the sea split, the manna descend, the dead revived — and they still treated the command of Allah as if it were a joke. Their question was a question of pride, not of seeking: they could not imagine that the answer to a murder might lie in the slaughter of a cow. This is the exact psychology of the person who demands that the divine wisdom match his own categories: if it doesn't fit, he assumes it is foolish. Moses' answer is a prophet's rebuke: "I seek refuge in Allah from being among the *jāhilīn*" — from being among the ignorant. The word *jāhilīn* is more than "fools"; it refers to those who act without knowledge, without respect for the limits, and without the moral seriousness required for divine commands. Moses is saying: if I mocked Allah's command, I would be one of them; my dignity is in taking the command seriously, and yours is not.

The deeper lesson of the verse is about the relationship between divine command and human categories. Human beings often receive the command of Allah as if they were entitled to understand its complete wisdom before obeying. The cow story is a training in the opposite: obedience comes first, and understanding follows. The command to sacrifice a cow was given for a specific, hidden purpose — the revelation of the murderer — but the people were not told the purpose at first. They were asked to trust. Their mockery showed that they did not. The same principle runs through the whole of Islamic law and life: many commands are obeyed before their wisdom is fully seen; the believer trusts the One who knows.

In modern life, this verse speaks to the place of trust in religion and in authority. The skeptic often enters a relationship with revelation on the condition that every command be justified to him in advance. The Qur'anic model is the reverse: the believer enters with trust, receives the command, and discovers the wisdom through obedience. The story of the cow is the story of every believer who has obeyed a command before understanding its benefit — fasting before knowing its effect, charity before knowing its reward, restraint before knowing its blessing. And it is also a warning against the pride that assumes divine wisdom is a matter of human taste.

## Verse 68

Verse 68 says: *"They said, "Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!" He replied, "Allah says, 'The cow should neither be old nor young but in between. So do as you are commanded!'""*

In simple terms, the people were not satisfied with the first command. They asked Moses to ask Allah for more specification: what type of cow? The answer came: a cow neither old nor young, but in between. And then, with a note of impatience, the command was repeated: "So do as you are commanded!"

The exchange is the beginning of a pattern that runs through the whole story: each time the people were given a simple command, they asked for more details, and each time the details increased the difficulty. Ibn Kathir's commentary, following the early authorities, makes the point explicitly: "Had they not disputed, it would have been sufficient for them to slaughter any cow. However, they disputed, and the matter was made more difficult for them, until they ended up looking for the specific cow that they were later ordered to slaughter." The same principle appears in the famous hadith of the Prophet ﷺ, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah, in which he said: "Avoid that which I forbid you to do and do that which I command you to do to the best of your capacity. Verily the people before you were destroyed only because of their excessive questioning and their disagreement with their Prophets." The people had been told: sacrifice a cow. The command was clear, and any cow would have fulfilled it. But they did not obey; they negotiated.

The answer — "neither old nor young but in between" — is a description of a mature cow, in the prime of its life. It was a fitting symbol for a community that was itself supposed to be in the middle: neither the unthinking obedience of youth nor the hardened refusal of age, but the mature response of a people who had been given the covenant. The word *'awān* in the Arabic carries the sense of a cow in its prime, fit for sacrifice. The description was not a riddle; it was an answer to their question. And the phrase "So do as you are commanded" — *fa af'alū mā tu'marūn* — is a gentle rebuke: you asked, I answered, now do what you are told.

The lesson is deep and universal: the believer who delays obedience with unnecessary questions may be doing so because the real obstacle is not a lack of information but a lack of submission. The people did not ask because they genuinely needed the details; they asked because they were resisting. Their questions created a sequence of escalating difficulty, and the cow story becomes a parable of the person who negotiates with God rather than obeys. The same pattern is visible in religious life whenever a person searches for the "specific ruling" to avoid the general one, or asks for the "clarification" in order to postpone the act.

In modern life, this verse is a challenge to the culture of infinite questioning. It is possible to seek knowledge sincerely, and it is possible to use questions as a shield against action. The Qur'anic diagnosis of the Children of Israel is that their questions were not a search for truth but a defense against it. The believer is called to the opposite: seek knowledge, but let the knowledge lead to action. The first command in the story — sacrifice a cow — was enough. Every added specification was a consequence of the resistance, and the same is true in the believer's life: obedience, when it is sincere, is simple; resistance, when it is hidden, creates complexity.

## Verse 69

Verse 69 says: *"They said, "Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour." He replied, "Allah says, 'It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.'""*

In simple terms, the people asked again: what is the colour? The answer came: a bright yellow cow, pleasant to look upon. The description is precise and vivid — a radiant yellow, a colour that pleases the eyes. The verse continues the story's pattern — the escalating questioning and the ever-finer specifications.

The word *ṣafrā'* — yellow — is the colour named. The description *fāqi'* means "bright," "clear," "deeply saturated." The scholars note that the phrase "pleasant to see" (*yasurru al-nāẓirīn*) describes a colour that gladdens the eye — perhaps a deep golden yellow, or a colour that reflects light in a way that is pleasing. The Qur'an's use of this description serves a purpose: the cow that was commanded was not merely any cow but a cow of exceptional beauty, distinguished in its colour and appearance. In the classical accounts, this specification made the cow even more difficult to find, and the people's questioning had the same effect in the area of colour as it had in the area of age: it made the command harder, not easier.

The verses also show a pattern that is important for understanding the Qur'an's method of revelation. The command was given first in general: "sacrifice a cow." The people asked, and the answer became more specific. This is not a case of Allah withholding information in order to test — it is a case of the people creating their own difficulty by refusing the first, simplest command. The Qur'an is teaching that the wisdom of obedience is lost when obedience is postponed. The general command was easy; the specifications were the result of resistance, and each specification narrowed the field until the task became almost impossible.

The phrase "pleasant to see" has a deeper spiritual meaning. The cow that was commanded was a beauty, a pleasure to the eyes — and yet the people's hearts were so hardened that the pleasure of the sight was not enough to move them. They had seen the sea split; they had seen manna descend; they had seen the dead be revived; and now they were being shown a cow of such colour that it would gladden the viewer. And they were still negotiating. The Qur'an's lesson is that the visual beauty of a sign does not by itself produce faith; the heart decides what the eyes will show. The same people who were given the most beautiful objects in the world were the people who had made their hearts as hard as stone (a condition the verses will describe in 2:74).

In modern life, the verse speaks to the psychology of endless specification. In many areas — religious, professional, and personal — people use the language of "clarification" to postpone commitment. The person who says "I will do it when I understand every detail" may actually be saying "I do not want to do it." The Qur'anic answer is the pattern of the story: the command was clear, the details were requested, and the details were given; the problem was never information, and the solution was never more data. The person who has received a clear command is told — as Moses told the people — "Do as you are commanded." The beauty of the command and the clarity of the answer are not substitutes for the obedience they require.

## Verse 70

Verse 70 says: *"Again they said, "Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow, for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.""*

In simple terms, the people asked a fourth time: make it clear to us which cow, because the cows are all alike to us. And here, for the first time, they add a phrase that changes the tone: "Then, Allah willing, we will be guided." The verse marks the point where the story moves from resistance toward reluctant compliance — but the reluctance is the point.

The phrase "all cows look the same to us" is not a statement of ignorance but a statement of refusal disguised as confusion. The cow that had been described — neither old nor young, in its prime, bright yellow, pleasing to the eyes — was not "the same as all cows." The description had already distinguished it. The people's claim that the cows were alike shows that they were using their own perception as the standard, and that they had not bothered to look for what was described. The same psychology runs through religious life: the person who says "all religions are the same" or "all paths are equal" is not making a claim of knowledge but a claim of refusal — he has not looked at the distinctions because looking would obligate him.

The phrase "Allah willing, we will be guided" is the one hopeful element in the verse. The words *in shā' Allāh* are the words of a person who is beginning to submit — who is acknowledging that guidance is not in his control but in Allah's. Up to this point, the people had spoken with the certainty of resistance: "Are you mocking us?" Now they speak with the humility of reaching: "If Allah wills, we will be guided." The change is subtle but real. The scholars note that this is the point where the story turns: the people had resisted, and the resistance was exposed; and they were beginning to concede that the path of guidance required submission.

Yet even the concession is imperfect: they said "we will be guided" *after* asking for the fourth specification, which means they still wanted one more detail. The pattern is meticulous: they were not ready to act on what they had already been told. The command to sacrifice a cow is now repeated a fourth time, and with each repetition the cow becomes more particular — age, colour, and now the work history and qualities of verse 71. The narrative is one of the most precise portraits in the Qur'an of how resistance to a simple command compounds itself.

In modern life, the verse teaches about the difference between the language of submission and the act of submission. A person can say "If Allah wills" and still be resisting, if the phrase is used as a cover for postponement. The Qur'an's answer is the story itself: the people were given a command, they negotiated through four stages, and they only acted when the command became practically impossible to refuse. The believer is being taught to recognize the pattern in himself — the habit of asking for more clarity when the answer is already clear, the tendency to accept obligation in words while postponing it in deeds. And when the resistance is finally broken, the verse's lesson is that the one who genuinely submits says, "If Allah wills, we will be guided" — and then acts on the guidance that has already come.
