## Verse 41

Verse 41 says: *Zachariah said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” He said, “Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the answer to Zachariah's request for a sign: his sign will be that he cannot speak to people for three days except by gestures — and in those three days he is to remember his Lord often and glorify Him morning and evening.

"My Lord! Grant me a sign" — Zachariah's request follows the good news of Yahya in 3:39–40. Ibn Kathir records the sequence: when Zakariyya heard the good news, he started contemplating having children at his age, asked "How can I have a son?", and the answer "Thus Allah does what He wills" was given. Then he asked for a sign. Al-Jalalayn's treatment of the parallel passages notes the pattern of the prophets' requests: they ask for a sign because the good news is about an event that both exceeds the natural order and is deeply personal. The request is not a doubt of the news; it is a settled believer's request for the confirmation that will anchor his heart and be a living reminder of the divine power at work in his own household.

"Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures" — the sign is the temporary loss of ordinary speech: *alla tukallima al-nas thalathata ayyam illa ramzan* — you will not speak to people for three days except by gesture, by sign. The sign is parallel to what the Qur'an says of Maryam at the birth of 'Isa (19:26: "say: I have vowed a fast to the Most Merciful, so I will not speak to anyone today"), and it functions as a test of the heart: the one who receives the good news is asked to hold the news in silence, and the silence is itself the proof. The classical exegetes note that the silence was not a prohibition of the remembrance of Allah but of speech with people: the man was cut off from the human audience so that he would be turned to the divine one.

"Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening" — the command that fills the silence: *udhkur Rabbaka kathiran wa sabbih bi al-'ashiyyi wa al-ibkar* — remember your Lord much, and glorify Him in the evening and the morning. The instruction pairs the remembrance that is inward and constant with the glorification that is timed and public — the *tasbih* of the morning and evening, the *adhyar* and *adkhar* that the Qur'an commands in several places (e.g., 33:41–42, 20:130, 50:39–40). Ibn Kathir notes the sign's design: the cessation of speech becomes the occasion of the increase of the divine remembrance; the one who cannot speak to people speaks to his Lord. Al-Jalalayn's reading of the passage (on 3:38–41 as a unit) emphasizes that the silence was the answer to the prophet's request for the sign, and the remembrance was the content of the silence.

There is a deep point in the order of the verse. The sign is not a miracle performed on the world; it is a modification of the prophet himself. Zachariah asked for a sign; Allah gave him a condition — the man's own tongue is suspended, and the suspension is the proof that the One who gave him the good news controls even the organs of speech. The believer who requests certainty often looks for a sign in the world; the Qur'an's answer is that the clearest sign is a change in the self — the tongue that stops speaking to people and turns to Allah. And the verse's final instruction is the believer's daily discipline: the remembrance of the Lord often, and the glorification at the two ends of the day. The prophet who is cut off from speech is the model of the believer who is not cut off from remembrance.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 42

Verse 42 says: *And ˹remember˺ when the angels said, “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world.*

In simple terms, the verse records the address of the angels to Mary: Allah has chosen you, purified you, and selected you above all the women of the worlds.

The verse is the first of the announcements to Maryam that begin the surah's account of the birth of 'Isa. Ibn Kathir frames the section as "The Virtue of Maryam Over the Women of Her Time": "Allah states that the angels spoke to Maryam by His command and told her that He chose her because of her service to Him, because of her modesty, honor, innocence, and conviction. Allah also chose her because of her virtue over the women of the world."

"when the angels said" — the address is by the angels, and Al-Jalalayn identifies the speaker: "namely Gabriel." The plural is the Qur'an's standard usage for the angelic address that conveys the divine word — the same form used in 3:39 for the call of Zachariah and 3:45 for the good news to Maryam. The address to Maryam places her in the company of the recipients of the divine message: a woman being directly spoken to by the angels is a woman in the line of the prophets, not in their shadow.

"Allah has selected you" — the verb is *istafa* — the same verb used of the chosen families in 3:33: *istafa Adam wa Nuh* — Allah chose Adam and Noah. The selection of Maryam is the selection of the house of 'Imran: the word that was used for the lineages is now used for the daughter of the lineage. Al-Jalalayn: "God has preferred you, He has elected you."

"purified you" — *tahharaki* — cleansed, purified. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn explain the purification in the context: "made you pure of the touch of men" — the purity that will be the condition of the birth of a child without a father; and more broadly the purity of faith, conduct, and character. The purification is the divine preparation: before the good news of 'Isa, Maryam was made clean — the vessel was prepared for the word.

"and chosen you over all women of the world" — the second *istafa* of the verse, now with the object *'ala nisa' al-'alamin* — above the women of the worlds. Al-Jalalayn's gloss: "the inhabitants of your time" — the preference is relative to her time and the worlds of the narration, as the hadiths specify. Ibn Kathir collects the two famous hadiths of the Prophet: "The best woman in her time was Maryam, daughter of 'Imran, and the best woman of the Prophet's time is Khadijah, daughter of Khuwaylid" (at-Tirmidhi; the Two Sahihs); and "Many men achieved perfection, but among women, only Maryam the daughter of 'Imran and Asiyah, the wife of Fir'awn, achieved perfection" (Ibn Jarir from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari; the Six with the exception of Abu Dawud; al-Bukhari's wording adds the superiority of 'A'ishah to other women like the superiority of tharid to other food).

For the reader, the verse is decisive against every reading of the Qur'an that treats women as secondary to the message. The verse's structure is the same as the verse of the chosen lineages (3:33): the same word *istafa*, the same elevation, the same divine selection. The chosen woman is addressed by the angels, purified by Allah, selected above the women of the worlds — and the surah's whole account of the birth of 'Isa flows from her. The verse also contains the theology of preparation: the one who is to be the vessel of a divine miracle is first made pure; the selection, the purification, and the elevation belong together as the divine preparation of the one who will carry the Word.

## Verse 43

Verse 43 says: *O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives Mary's instruction after her selection: be devoutly obedient to your Lord, prostrate and bow with those who bow.

The verse is the command that follows the selection of 3:42. Ibn Kathir explains the sequence: "Allah states that the angels commanded Maryam to increase acts of worship, humbleness, submission, prostration, bowing, and so forth, so that she would acquire what Allah had decreed for her, as a test for her. Yet, this test also earned her a higher grade in this life and the Hereafter, for Allah demonstrated His might by creating a son inside her without male intervention." The selection is not the end of the story; it is the beginning of the worship — and the worship is the response to the selection.

"Be devout to your Lord" — *iqnuti li Rabbiki* — be obedient, be submissive, be devout: the *qunut*, the standing obedience. Ibn Kathir: "As for Qunut (Aqnuti in the Ayah), it means to submit with humbleness," and he cites 2:116: "all surrender with obedience (Qanitun) to Him." The command is not a ritual formula but the posture of the whole person: the obedience of the one who has been selected.

"prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down" — *usjudi wa irka'i ma'a al-rak'iyin* — prostrate and bow with the bowing ones. The verse names the two postures of the prayer — the *sujud* and the *ruku'* — and its phrasing places Maryam in a congregation: "along with those who bow down." The instruction is both individual ("prostrate yourself") and communal ("with those who bow"): the worship of the one selected is the worship of the community, and the *salat* of Maryam is the *salat* of the believers: the same prostration, the same bowing, the same qiblah.

There are two points of lasting significance in the verse. First, the Qur'an makes the prayer of Maryam a model: the woman chosen above the women of the worlds is commanded to *sujud* and *ruku'* — the same prayer the Muslim performs daily — and the verse is one of the foundational proof-texts for the presence of prayer, prostration, and bowing in the religion of the earlier prophets (the *salat* of the people of the Book, including Maryam and 'Isa, is attested in several verses — cf. 19:30-31, where 'Isa says "He has enjoined prayer and zakat upon me as long as I live"). Second, the verse answers the Christian tradition's tendency to separate Maryam from the ordinary worship: the Qur'an places her in the congregation of the bowers. Her greatness is not the fabrication of a new religion but the perfection of the old worship: obedience, prostration, and the congregation.

For the reader, the verse is the model of the response to the divine selection: if the angels say "Allah has chosen you," the commanded response is not pride but prostration. The believer who receives a gift, a station, or a testimony of the divine favor is being shown the pattern: the greater the selection, the deeper the bow. The *qunut*, the *sujud*, the *ruku'* — the worship that is individual and communal — is the form that the acceptance of the divine favor takes; and the verse's "along with those who bow down" removes the worship from the private tower of the saint and places it in the shared congregation of the believers.

There is also a point of detail in the way the command is addressed. The verse does not command the general worship of the Lord but the *qunut* of the Lord, the *sujud* of the Lord, and the *ruku'* with the bowers — the three are the components of the *salat* as the Qur'an knows it, and the verse is one of the places where the Qur'an testifies that the prayer of the earlier communities had the same form as the prayer of the Muslims. The *qunut* is the standing obedience, the *sujud* and the *ruku'* are the postures of the prayer, and the *ma'a al-rak'iyin* — "along with those who bow" — is the congregation. Maryam is commanded to do all three: the personal obedience, the personal prostration, and the communal bowing. The classical picture of Maryam — the woman secluded in the mihrab, supplied with out-of-season fruit, addressed by the angels — is the picture of the woman of prayer. Her seclusion was not the seclusion of the hermits of later traditions; it was the seclusion of the one who prostrated, bowed, and stood in the congregation of the worship.

The verse also has a quiet polemical edge in the surah's context. The Christians of Najran had come to argue about the Messiah and about the place of his mother in their religion. The Qur'an's answer in 3:43 is not an argument but the picture: this is what Maryam was commanded to do — be devout to your Lord, prostrate, and bow with the bowers. The mother of the Messiah is not the object of the worship; she is the model of the worship. The one who is chosen above the women of the worlds is the one who is commanded to prostrate and to bow with the bowers — and the same command that is given in this verse is the command that the Prophet and his community fulfill in the *salat*: the same qunut, the same sujud, the same ruku', the same congregation of the believers.

The verse therefore works as the surah's demonstration of the continuity of the religion: the religion of Maryam and the Messiah is the religion of the prostration and the bowing, the same religion of the worship of the One — and the one who is chosen is the one who bows. The believers are not asked to build a new theology of Maryam; they are asked to see her in the posture that the Qur'an gives: the devout one, the prostrating one, the one bowing with the bowers — the model of the worship of the Lord for every generation of the believers, and the proof that the religion of the earlier prophets and the religion of the later are one: the worship of the One, in the same postures, in the same congregation.

## Verse 44

Verse 44 says: *This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺.*

In simple terms, the verse points to the proof of the revelation: this is news of the unseen sent to Muhammad; he was not present when they cast their pens to decide who would take charge of Mary, nor when they disputed about it.

"This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺" — *min anba' al-ghayb* — from the reports, the tidings of the unseen. Ibn Kathir: "This is a part of the news of the Ghayb which We reveal — and narrate to you, O Muhammad." The verse's claim is the argument for the prophethood itself: Muhammad, an unlettered man in Makkah and Madinah, in a land far from Jerusalem, could have no source for the story of the guardianship dispute of Maryam except the One who witnessed it. The *ghayb* — the unseen — is the domain of the revelation: the news of the unseen is the sign that the news-bearer is from the One who sees the unseen.

"You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary's guardian" — *idh yulquna aqlamahum* — when they threw their pens, their quills. The image is the divination of the priests of the Temple: the casting of the writing instruments upon the water, the lot falling to determine the guardian. Al-Jalalayn's account (on 3:37) records the full narrative: the mother of Maryam took her to the priests, the keepers of the Holy House of Jerusalem, and said, "This here before you is the dedication I offered." They competed for guardianship of her, because she was the daughter of their religious leader. Zachariah said, "I am most worthy of her, for her maternal aunt lives with me." The others said, "Not until we have cast lots." All twenty-nine of them departed to the River Jordan, where they cast their quills, agreeing that the one whose quill remained fast and floated to the surface of the water would be most worthy to be guardian over her. Zachariah's quill remained fast and surfaced. He took charge of her. Ibn Kathir also records the version of Ibn Jarir from 'Ikrimah: the mother took the infant to the rabbis from the offspring of Aaron, responsible for the care of Bayt al-Maqdis; they were the ones who cast lots. The verse's point is not the mechanism but the unseen-ness: whatever the details narrated by the traditions, no one in Madinah was there.

"nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺" — *idh yakhtasimun* — when they disputed, contended. Two events are cited: the casting and the disputing. Ibn Kathir: "You were not present, O Muhammad, when this occurred, so you cannot narrate what happened to the people as an eyewitness. Rather, Allah disclosed these facts to you as if you were a witness, when they conducted a lottery to choose the custodian of Maryam, seeking the reward of this good deed." The narrations of the casting and the dispute are the first of the surah's *ghayb* proofs. The second is to come in 3:44's parallel at 3:52–54 — the plot against 'Isa; and the same argument recurs throughout the surah — the Qur'an's reports of the unseen are the sign of the unseen's revealer.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's method in miniature: the story is told, and the story's impossibility for the teller is noted. Muhammad could not have witnessed it; the People of the Book could have, but they were not the ones narrating it; the only narrator is the One who was there. The verse is also a lesson in the discipline of the prophetic narration: the Prophet is not narrating an eyewitness account or a travelers' tale; he is delivering the news of the unseen, and the authority of the account is the authority of the One who saw. The believer who reads the surah is being taught not merely the story of Maryam but the epistemology of the revelation: the unseen is reported by the Revealer; the report is the sign; and the report's truth is the proof of the messenger.

## Verse 45

Verse 45 says: *˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the second good news to Mary: Allah gives her glad tidings of a Word from Him whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary — honored in this world and the Hereafter and among those nearest to Allah.

"the angels proclaimed" — the same angelic address of 3:42, now bringing the good news that is the whole surah's pivot. Ibn Kathir frames the section: "Delivering the Good News to Maryam of 'Isa's Birth": "This Ayah contains the glad tidings the angels brought to Maryam that she would give birth to a mighty son who will have a great future."

"Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him" — *bi kalimatin minhu* — of a Word from Him. The phrase is the Qur'an's own name for the Messiah: a Word from Allah. Ibn Kathir: "a son who will come into existence with a word from Allah, 'Be,' and he was. This is the meaning of Allah's statement (about Yahya) — 'Believing in the Word from Allah' (3:39) — according to the majority of the scholars." The Word from Him is 'Isa himself — the created one who came into being by the word "Be" — and the phrase is the Qur'an's answer to the Christian doctrines of the incarnation and of the Logos: Jesus is a Word from Allah in the sense that he was brought into being by Allah's command, not a word of Allah in the sense of a part of the divine essence. Al-Jalalayn: "a boy whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary. He addresses her, attributing him to her, in order to point out that she will give birth to him without a father — for the custom is to attribute the child to its father."

"his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary" — *al-Masih 'Isa ibn Maryam* — the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary. Ibn Kathir explains the name al-Masih: "because when he touched (Mash) those afflicted with an illness, they would be healed by Allah's leave." The name is both title and description: he is the anointed one, the one sent, the one through whom healing would come by Allah's permission. And he is called "son of Mary" — the genealogy that is itself the sign and the argument: "relates 'Isa to his mother, because he did not have a father" (Ibn Kathir). The surah's insistence on "son of Mary" is deliberate: the one the Christians would call son of God is called son of Mary by the Qur'an — and the sonship of the mother is the refutation of the sonship of the Lord, for the one born of a mother is not the Lord of the mother.

"honoured in this world and the Hereafter" — *wajihan fi al-dunya wa al-akhirah* — held in honor, of high standing, in this world and the Hereafter. Ibn Kathir: "he will be a leader and honored by Allah in this life, because of the Law that Allah will reveal to him, sending down the Scripture to him, along with the other bounties that Allah will grant him with. 'Isa will be honored in the Hereafter and will intercede with Allah, by His leave, on behalf of some people, just as is the case with his brethren the mighty Messengers of Allah." Al-Jalalayn: "honoured in this world through prophethood, and in the Hereafter through his intercession and the high stations." The honor is real and it is granted: the Messiah is honored in both realms — but the honor is the honor of the servant, granted by the One who honors him, not the property of the one honored.

"and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺" — *min al-muqarrabin* — among those brought near, drawn close. The phrase is the Qur'an's highest rank of the believers (cf. 56:88, 56:11 — the *muqarrabun* of the worthy). Jesus is among the nearest: the servant at the highest station of service, brought near by his obedience and his prophethood.

For the reader, the verse is the fulcrum of the surah's whole argument with the Christians. The Qur'an acknowledges everything the Christians claim about the Messiah's greatness — the Word from Him, the honor in this world and the Hereafter, the rank among the nearest — and then the next verses will show the boundary of the claim: the Word from Him is a created one; the honor is the grant; the nearness is the rank of the servant. The Christians of Najran came disputing the divinity of 'Isa; the Qur'an's answer is not the denial of his greatness but the completion of it: he is the highest of the created, the Word brought into being by the command "Be," son of Mary, honored, brought near — and precisely because he is a Word from Him, a creation of His command, he is not the Lord who speaks the Word.
