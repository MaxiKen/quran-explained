## Verse 261

Verse 261 says: *"The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the parable of the spending in Allah's cause: a grain that grows into seven ears, each with a hundred grains — the reward multiplied by Allah's will — and He is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.

"The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains" — the verse's parable: the grain and the seven hundred. Ibn Kathir: this is a parable Allah made of the multiplication of rewards for those who spend in His cause, seeking His pleasure — Allah multiplies the good deed ten to seven hundred times. The verse's frame: the spending scaled as the seed: the small seed becomes the big harvest — the charity is the seed of the Hereafter. The verse's *fi sabil Allah* — in the cause of Allah — the seeking of His pleasure, not the praise of people: the cause is the field.

"And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills" — the verse's multiplication: the will beyond the seven hundred. Ibn Kathir: Allah gives manifold increase to whom He wills — the seven hundred is the measure's example; the multiplication is the grace's measure: beyond the countable, by the will.

"For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing" — the verse's close: the All-Bountiful (Wasi') — the vast bounty — the All-Knowing ('Alim) — the knowledge of the spender and the deed. Ibn Kathir: Allah is All-Sufficient for His creatures' needs, All-Knower.

The verse's place: after the command to lend to Allah (2:245) — the parable of the interest's counterpart: the charitable spending multiplies where the greed of the hoarding does not. The verse's frame: the believer's economics: the expenditure is the investment; the seven-hundred-fold is the divine yield.

The verse's lesson: the charity is the seed; the reward is the harvest; the multiplication is Allah's will. And the reader's part: give — for the giving is the planting, and the crop is with the All-Bountiful, the All-Knowing.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 262

Verse 262 says: *"Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words—they will get their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, the verse rules on the charity's purity: the spenders who do not follow their giving with reminders of the favor or hurtful words — their reward is with their Lord; no fear for them, nor grief.

"Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words" — the verse's two prohibitions: the mann — the reminder of the favor — and the adha — the injury, the hurtful word. Ibn Kathir: Allah praises those who spend from their money in His cause and then refrain from reminding those who received the charity of that fact — whether these hints take the form of words or actions; "or with injury" indicates that they do not cause harm to those to whom they gave the charity — "for this harm will only annul the charity." The verse's frame: the charity is not the deed only; the charity is the deed plus the silence — the giving without the branding.

"they will get their reward from their Lord" — the verse's reward: from their Lord — the reward of the righteousness. Ibn Kathir: their reward is with their Lord — indicating that Allah Himself will reward them for these righteous actions. The verse's frame: the reward with the Lord — not the people's praise, not the recipient's gratitude: the Lord's account.

"and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve" — the verse's peace: the no-fear of the Day of Resurrection's horrors, the no-grief for what they leave behind. Ibn Kathir: no fear regarding the horrors of the Day of Resurrection, nor shall they grieve regarding the offspring they leave behind and the adornment and delights of this world — they will not feel sorry, because they will acquire what is far better for them.

The verse's companion hadith (Muslim, from Abu Dharr): the Prophet said — three persons whom Allah shall neither speak to on the Day of Resurrection, nor look at, nor purify — and they shall receive a painful torment: he who reminds people of what he gives away, he who lengthens his clothes below the ankles, and he who swears an oath while lying to sell his merchandise (recorded by Ibn Kathir in the commentary of 2:262–264). The verse's frame: the mann is the belittling of the gift — the reminder erases the deed's face.

The verse's lesson: give — and then let it go: the reward is with the Lord, and the reminder and the injury are the charity's annulment. And the reader's part: the pure charity is the generous hand and the silent tongue.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 263

Verse 263 says: *"Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing."*

In simple terms, the verse compares the two: a kind word and forgiveness are better than a charity followed by injury — and Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing.

"Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury" — the verse's comparison: the qawl ma'ruf — the kind word, the compassionate address — and the maghfirah — the forgiveness — better than the sadaqah followed by the adha. Ibn Kathir: "Kind words" — compassionate words and a supplication for Muslims — "and forgiving" — forgiving an injustice that took the form of actions or words — are better than charity followed by injury. The verse's frame: the word is a deed; the forgiveness is a charity — the kind refusal is better than the hurtful gift.

"And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing" — the verse's close: the Ghani — the Rich, free of all needs — the Halim — the Most Forbearing, who forgives, releases, and pardons. Ibn Kathir: Allah is Rich — not needing His creation — Most Forbearing — forgives, releases and pardons them. The verse's frame: the Self-Sufficient does not need your charity's interest — and the Most Forbearing's forgiveness is the model of your forgiveness: He forgives the injury, so forgive the injury of the asker.

Maududi (Tafhim): it is a thousand times better to offer some reasonable and appropriate excuse to the person who asks, if the giver feels unable to give — and if the asker's impolite behavior angers the giver, it is also a thousand times better to forgive than to give charity followed by pain. The verse's frame: the dignity of the asker is the charity's part — the refusal with the kind word preserves the honor that the hurtful gift destroys.

The verse's lesson: the kindness of the word and the forgiveness of the fault are the charity's greater part. And the reader's part: when you cannot give, give the kind word — and when you are wronged, forgive — the Self-Sufficient's bounty and the Most Forbearing's pardon are the model.

## Verse 264

Verse 264 says: *"O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone. Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity. Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people."*

In simple terms, the verse warns against nullifying charity by reminders and injury — like the show-offs who donate to be seen and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day; their example is the barren rock: the rain washes away the thin soil, leaving it bare — no reward preserved — and Allah does not guide the disbelieving.

"O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words" — the verse's command: the la tubtilu — do not nullify, do not waste — the sadaqah with the mann and the adha. Ibn Kathir: "Do not render in vain your sadaqah by reminders of your generosity or by injury" — the charity will be rendered in vain if it is followed by harm or reminders — in this case, the reward of giving away charity is not sufficient enough to nullify the harm and reminders. The verse's frame: the annulment: the given gift is erased by the reminder — the repeated "I gave you" is the charity's cancellation.

"like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day" — the verse's likeness: the riya' — the showing off — the one who spends to be seen of men. Ibn Kathir: "like him who spends his wealth to be seen of men" — do not nullify your acts of charity by following them with reminders and harm, just like the charity of those who give it to show off to people — the boasting person pretends to give away charity for Allah's sake, but in reality seeks people's praise and the reputation of being kind or generous, or other material gains of this life — all the while he does not think about Allah or gaining His pleasure and generous rewards. Ad-Dahhak: the example fits one who follows his acts of charity with reminders or harm. The verse's frame: the show-off's charity is the display, not the deed — the appearance of the giving, the absence of the belief.

"Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone" — the verse's image: the safwan — the smooth rock — with a little dust, the heavy rain leaving it bare. Ibn Kathir: heavy rain left the rock completely barren of dust — such is the case with Allah's action regarding the work of those who show off — their deeds are bound to vanish and disappear, even though people think these deeds are as plentiful as specks of dust. The verse's frame: the thin soil over the stone: the appearance of the garden in the dust — the rain (the exposure, the testing) reveals the stone — the earth's layer was never the root.

"Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity" — the verse's loss: la yaqdirun — they are not able, they can do nothing with what they have earned. The verse's frame: the reward's preservation: the earned reward scattered by the rain of the exposure — the show-off's harvest is the bare stone.

"Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people" — the verse's close: the guidance of the disbelieving — withheld. Ibn Kathir: Allah does not guide the disbelieving people.

The verse's lesson: the reminder and the injury are the charity's ruin; the showing off is the deed's emptiness; the reward is preserved by the sincerity. And the reader's part: give in secret from the eye — the soil that the rain washes is the appearance; the root that the rain feeds is the sincerity.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 265

Verse 265 says: *"And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah's pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the counterpart example: the donors who seek Allah's pleasure and are certain of the reward — their spending is like a garden on a fertile hill: the heavy rain doubles the produce; the drizzle alone suffices — and Allah is All-Seeing.

"And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah's pleasure and believing the reward is certain" — the verse's subject: the ibtigha' mardat Allah — the seeking of Allah's pleasure — and the tathbit — the certainty, the assurance of the reward. Ibn Kathir (with 2:265): the garden on the height — the believer's spending with sincerity and certainty. The verse's frame: the two conditions the show-off lacks: the seeking of the pleasure (not the praise) and the certainty of the reward (not the doubt): the garden of the sincere.

"is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce" — the verse's image: the rabwah — the elevated, fertile ground — struck by the wabil — the heavy rain — yielding the double produce. Ibn Kathir: "Wabil falls on it" — heavy rain — so it produces its yield of harvest — fruits, produce — doubled as compared to other gardens. The verse's frame: the elevation and the rain: the secure ground (the faith's certainty) and the abundant watering (the reward's rain): the double yield of the sincere.

"If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient" — the verse's second measure: the tall — the drizzle, the light rain. Ibn Kathir: ad-Dahhak said the tall is light rain — the ayah indicates that the garden on the rabwah is always fertile — for if heavy rain does not fall on it, light rain will suffice for it. The verse's frame: the little giving and the great giving both suffice — the believer's spending, whether small or great, is multiplied (Tanwir, Ibn 'Abbas): the sincere little is the garden's drizzle; the sincere great is the garden's downpour.

"And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do" — the verse's close: the All-Seer — the seeing of the sincerity and the deed. The verse's frame: the seen by the Seer: the hidden intention, the private giving — the All-Seeing knows the certainty of the heart that the people cannot see.

The verse's lesson: the two parables side by side (2:264 and 2:265): the bare rock of the show-off and the fertile garden of the sincere — the same rain, the two yields. Maududi (Tafhim): the contrast of the two conditions — the show-off's deeds wash away; the sincere's garden bears twice. And the reader's part: plant on the high ground of the certainty — the reward's rain will come — and the drizzle of the small giving will suffice.

===VERSE-END===
