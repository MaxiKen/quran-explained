## Verse 156

Verse 156 says: *"who, when faced with a disaster, say, "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.""*

In simple terms, the verse defines the patient ones: those who, when faced with a disaster, say, "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will all return."

The verse's definition of the patience is the content of the good news: the patient ones are not the ones who do not feel the disaster — they are the ones who respond to the disaster with the statement of the return. The verse gives the believer's response to the calamity: *inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un* — "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will return."

The statement's first half is the declaration of the ownership: "to Allah we belong." The disaster is the loss of something that was never the believer's own. The verse says: the property, the life, the crop, the loved one — all of them belonged to Allah; He gave them, and He took them; the believer's statement is the recognition of the ownership. The statement is the confession that the person has no absolute property over anything: the person is the servant of the Owner, the things are the trust of the Owner, and the taking of the trust is the decree of the Owner.

The statement's second half is the declaration of the return: "to Him we will return." The disaster is not the end: the believer is returning to the One who gave; and the return is the destination of all. The verse's statement is the answer to the grief: the one who has lost has returned to the One to whom the lost one belonged — and the return of the death is not the annihilation but the return to the Lord.

The commentators note the doctrine in the statement: the patient person is the one who, at the moment of the disaster, remembers that the disaster belongs to the decree of Allah and responds with the acceptance. Ibn Kathir links the verse with the hadith of Umm Salamah, recorded in the collections: the Prophet said that when a calamity strikes, the one who says "Inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un, Allahumma ajirni fi musibati wa akhlif li khayran minha" ("O Allah, reward me in my calamity and give me something better than it") — Allah will reward him and give what is better. Umm Salamah's reading of the verse is the famous example: her husband died, she made the supplication, and Allah gave her what was better — the Prophet himself.

In modern life, the verse is the believer's grammar of the grief. The modern world describes the disaster as the loss and the grief as the end; the verse describes the disaster as the ownership and the return. The one who says the verse's words at the disaster has named the truth: nothing was the person's own; the return is the destination; and the grief is borne by the One who owns whatever was lost — and by the promise that He replaces the loss with what is better.

## Verse 157

Verse 157 says: *"They are the ones who will receive Allah's blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the reward of the patient ones: they are the ones who will receive Allah's blessings and mercy. And it is they who are rightly guided.

The verse's first gift — *salawat* (rendered "blessings") — is the special mention of the Lord: the blessings of Allah upon the patient ones. The word is the same root used for the divine mention in the hadith qudsi of the remembrance; the commentators explain that the *salawat* from Allah are the praise, the mercy, the mention in the higher assembly: the Lord's own eulogy of the patient believer. The verse's blessing is not merely the reward of the Hereafter; it is the present favor: the One who is with the patient (2:153) now declares the blessings upon them.

The verse's second gift — "mercy" — is the *rahmah*: the compassion that covers the grief, the mercy that follows the acceptance. The pair — blessings and mercy — is the verse's summary of the reward of the patient: the mercy answers the loss; the blessings answer the test.

"And it is they who are rightly guided" — the verse's final clause is the third element: the guidance. The patient ones are guided: the guidance in the disaster — the understanding that the loss belongs to Allah, that the return is to Allah; the guidance in the path — the right, the straight path that the qibla passage has been describing; the guidance in the reward — the increase of the faith through the trial. The verse's "they" is emphatic: the ones who receive the blessings and mercy are exactly the ones who are guided; the patience is the mark and the guidance is the fruit.

The verse also closes the teaching that began at 2:153: "Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient" — and the verse here gives the conclusion: the patient ones receive the blessings and the mercy and are the guided ones. The test was the instrument; the patience was the path; the reward and the guidance are the end.

In modern life, the verse is the completion of the believer's hope. The one who has borne the disaster with the statement of the return is told: you have not lost — you have received the blessings of Allah and His mercy, and you are among the rightly guided. The verse does not say the loss is restored in this world; it says the reward is given: the mention of the Lord, the mercy, and the guidance — the rewards that the modern world cannot see and cannot measure, and that the patient believer holds as the true result of the test.

## Verse 158

Verse 158 says: *"Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse establishes the rite of the walking between Safa and Marwah: indeed, the hills of Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or the minor pilgrimage, let them walk between the two hills. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing.

The verse was revealed in the context of the hesitation of the early Muslims. The commentators record (Ibn Kathir, from the hadith of 'Urwah from Aisha, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim): at the time of Jahiliyyah, the Arabs had placed idols on the two hills — Isaf on Safa and Na'ilah on Marwah — and the people used to touch or kiss them. When Islam came, the Muslims hesitated to walk between the two hills, thinking the rite was the worship of the idols. Aisha's narration: "During the time of Jahiliyyah we used to hesitate to perform Tawaf between Safa and Marwah. Allah then revealed: 'Verily, Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah...'" And Aisha said: "Allah's Messenger has made it the Sunnah to perform Tawaf between them, and thus no one should abandon performing it."

The verse answers the hesitation with the declaration: Safa and Marwah are among the *sha'a'ir Allah* — the symbols, the rites, the marks of Allah. The worship of the idols had been there, the idols had been placed there — but the hills themselves were not the idols; they were the symbols of the religion of Abraham, the places of the miracle of Hagar and Ishmael, the sites of the search for the water that became Zamzam. Ibn Kathir: the Tawaf between Safa and Marwah is among the acts that Allah legislated during the Hajj for Prophet Ibrahim; the origin is the Tawaf of Hajar between the two hills seeking water for her son, and Allah answered her prayer.

"So whoever performs the pilgrimage or the minor pilgrimage, let them walk between the two hills" — the verse commands the *sa'i*: the walking between the two hills is an integral rite of the Hajj and the Umrah. The Prophet, in the narration, began the rites by reciting the verse and then said: "I start with what Allah has commanded me to start with" — beginning the sa'i from Safa (the narrations of the sa'i and the wording recorded by the collectors).

"And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing" — the verse's closing clause is the larger principle: the voluntary good — beyond the obligatory rite — the one who walks the sa'i *with a willing heart*, or who adds to the rite the devotion, is met by Allah: the "Appreciative" — *Shakir* — who repays the good, and the "All-Knowing" — *'Alim* — who knows the intention behind the act. The verse's final words answer the whole question of the ritual: the rite is the command; the willingness is the heart; Allah appreciates and knows.

In modern life, the verse is the lesson about the rites and the hesitation. The believer is often hesitant about the acts that have been associated with the ignorance or the corruption of the past: the places that were defiled, the customs that were abused, the traditions that were misused. The verse says: the symbol belongs to Allah, and the rite belongs to the religion of Abraham — the defilement of the past does not cancel the symbol of the Lord; the command of Allah repossesses the place for the worship of the One. And the verse's closing: the good done willingly — with the heart, not only the body — is the good that Allah appreciates and knows.

## Verse 159

Verse 159 says: *"Those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed— after We made it clear for humanity in the Book—will be condemned by Allah and ˹all˺ those who condemn."*

In simple terms, the verse states the condemnation of the concealment: those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed — after We made it clear for humanity in the Book — will be condemned by Allah and all those who condemn.

The verse identifies the crime: the concealment of the clear proofs and the guidance — the signs and the evidences and the guidance — that Allah revealed, after He made it clear for humanity in the Book. The revelation was made clear: the proofs were given, the guidance was set, the Book was sent, the description of the Messenger was in the Scriptures of the People of the Book. The concealment is the act of the ones who had the clear proofs and the guidance and hid it. Abu al-'Aliyah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas and Qatadah said that the ayat were revealed about the People of the Scripture who hid the description of Muhammad (as recorded by Ibn Kathir); Al-Jalalayn notes the context: the Jews concealed the stoning verse and the description of Muhammad.

"Will be condemned by Allah and all those who condemn" — the verse's penalty is the *la'n*: the divine curse, the removal from the mercy. The verse names the cursers: Allah — the first curse; and "all those who curse" — the angels, the believers, and (as the commentators add, in the various statements of the tafsir) everything that curses, each in its own way. Ibn Kathir records the hadith: "Whoever was asked about knowledge that he has, but he hid it, then a bridle made of fire will be tied around his mouth on the Day of Resurrection" (recorded in the collections, with the warning against the concealment of knowledge); and the hadith of Abu Hurayrah: "If it were not for this verse in the Book of Allah, I would not have narrated a hadith for anyone" — the hadith of the verse's warning becoming the shield of the transmission of the knowledge. The commentators note the contrast: everything, including the fish in the sea, asks forgiveness for the scholars; and the ones who hide the knowledge are cursed by Allah and the cursers.

The verse's position in the surah is exact: the passage opened with the "fools" mocking the qibla change and the People of the Book who knew and hid; the passage closes with the concealment of the proofs and the guidance — the same people, the same crime: the knowledge and the suppression.

In modern life, the verse is the standing warning to the keepers of the knowledge. The scholar, the teacher, the editor, the authority — the one who holds the clear proofs and the guidance — is warned: the concealment after the clarity is the condemned act. The person who knows the truth and suppresses it for the fear, for the profit, for the favor, for the authority — the verse's curse is the measure of the crime. And the verse's counterpart: the transmission of the knowledge — the narration, the publication, the clarification — is the opposite of the concealment, and the ones who spread the knowledge with honesty have the forgiveness of the creation and the reward of the Lord.

## Verse 160

Verse 160 says: *"As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse states the exception to the condemnation: as for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known — they are the ones to whom I will turn in forgiveness, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

The verse's three conditions are the way out of the condemnation: (1) *tabu* — they repent: the return to Allah from the concealment, the regret of the act, the turning in repentance; (2) *aslahu* — they mend their ways: the reform of the conduct, the correction of the behavior, the change of the practice; (3) *bayyanu* — and let the truth be known: the declaration, the publication of the truth they have hidden. The three are the complete repentance: the return of the heart, the reform of the deed, and the disclosure of the truth.

The third condition is the specific one — the truth that was concealed must be disclosed. Ibn Kathir: the ayat refers to those who regret what they have been doing and correct their behavior, and thus explain to the people what they have been hiding. The concealment of the truth is not repented by the private regret alone; the one who hid the truth is commanded to let the truth be known — the public disclosure is part of the repentance. The verse is the corrective of the concealment: the act of the hiding is undone by the act of the telling.

"They are the ones to whom I will turn in forgiveness" — the verse's response is the divine acceptance: the One who condemned the concealment now declares the acceptance of the repentance. The verb *atubu* — "I turn" — is the divine side of the repentance (tawbah): the servant turns to Allah and Allah turns to the servant; the acceptance is the divine turning. And the verse's final words give the ground: "for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful" — *al-Tawwab* — the One who accepts the repentance again and again — and *al-Rahim* — the Most Merciful.

The verse's lesson: the condemnation of the concealment (159) was not the closing of the door; the next verse opens the door: the repentance is accepted, the mend is required, the disclosure is commanded — and the One who condemns is the One who accepts: Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

In modern life, the verse is the structure of the honest correction. The person who has hidden the truth, suppressed the evidence, spoken against the clarity — the verse's way: repent, mend, and let the truth be known. The verse is the model of the correction for the institutions and authorities that have hidden what should have been public: the correction is the public disclosure, not only the private regret; and the verse's promise: the One who is the Accepter of Repentance and the Most Merciful turns to the one who turns, and discloses.
