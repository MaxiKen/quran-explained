## Verse 131

Verse 131 says: *"When his Lord ordered him, "Submit ˹to My Will˺," he responded, "I submit to the Lord of all worlds."*"

In simple terms, the verse gives the moment of the religion in its purest form: when his Lord ordered him, "Submit," Abraham responded, "I submit to the Lord of all worlds." The whole of the religion, the whole of Islam, the whole of the dispute between the communities, is in these two sentences: the command and the response.

"Submit" — *aslim* — is the imperative of the religion. The verse does not say "believe," or "understand," or "reason"; it says submit. The command is the first and the final form of the relation between the creature and the Creator: the surrender of the will. Abraham was not commanded to debate, to investigate, or to negotiate the terms; he was commanded to submit, and the verse is the record of the response that the command received.

The response is the definition of the believer: "I submit to the Lord of all worlds." The verse uses two nouns: *aslamtu* — I submit — and *rabb al-'ālamīn* — the Lord of all worlds. Abraham submits to the Lord of the worlds — not to the god of a tribe, not to the god of a nation, not to the god of his ancestors alone, but to the Lord of everything that exists. The religion of Abraham is not provincial; it is universal. The one who submits to the Lord of all worlds has submitted to the Lord of every person, every nation, and every age.

The verse also sets the standard for the whole Islamic call: the message of the Prophet Muhammad was, in its essence, the same command that the Qur'an records here — "submit to the Lord of all worlds" — the same command that was given to Abraham. This is the reason the verse sits at the centre of the surah's dispute with the People of the Book: the Jews and the Christians were arguing over the religion of Abraham, and the verse says the religion of Abraham is the religion of the submission — a religion that neither the Jews nor the Christians of that time could claim as their exclusive possession, because it was the religion that preceded them both and that Abraham received directly from his Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the point at which every person's religion is tested. The test is not whether one can defend the creeds, or argue the doctrines, or claim the inheritance; the test is the response to the command of submission. The person who has the arguments without the submission has the shell of the religion; the person who has the submission — the surrender of the will, the acceptance of the command, the trust in the Lord of all worlds — has the religion of Abraham, whatever his community and whatever his age.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 132

Verse 132 says: *"This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children, ˹saying˺, "Indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.""*

In simple terms, the verse records the testament of the fathers: this was the advice of Abraham, as well as Jacob, to his children: indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in a state of full submission. The verse gives the inheritance that the prophets left, and it is not land, not wealth, not leadership — it is the faith and the command to die in it.

The verse's content is the greatest of the testaments. Abraham and Jacob, the fathers of the two lines, both gave their children the same instruction. The instruction has two parts. First: "Allah has chosen for you this faith" — the faith is not the people's invention; it is the divine choice. The religion is not a cultural preference; it is the selection of Allah, and the children of the prophets are told that the selection is their heritage. Second: "do not die except in a state of full submission" — the command that determines the whole life. The way the person lives determines the way he dies, and the way he dies determines what follows. The prophets teach their children that the final act of the life is the completion of the religion: the one who has lived in submission dies in submission.

The classical commentators (Ibn Kathir among them) explain the advice as the instruction to live the path so that death comes upon the path: the person is usually resurrected upon what he died, and he dies upon what he lived. The verse is thus the Islamic doctrine of the final moment: the believer is commanded to live the faith so completely that the death finds him in it. And the commentators note the divine promise in this: the One who helps the seeker of good remains on the path of the good — the death in submission is not the believer's achievement alone; it is the grace of the Lord of the path.

The verse is also the answer to the dispute over the inheritance. The Jews and the Christians each claimed Abraham through their line, and the verse says: the heirs of Abraham are not the claimants; they are the ones who received the testament — the faith chosen by Allah, and the life and death in the submission. The inheritance of the prophets is not the name; it is the instruction, and the heirs of the instruction are the ones who follow it.

In modern life, the verse is the criterion of the spiritual inheritance. The parent, the teacher, the founder — the inheritance they leave is not the property; it is the faith and the manner of the life. The verse's command is addressed to every child of every believer: the religion is the chosen thing, and the life is to be lived so that death finds one in it — not in the middle of a negotiation, not in the middle of a refusal, not in the state that the person has not prepared.

## Verse 133

Verse 133 says: *"Or did you witness when death came to Jacob? He asked his children, "Who will you worship after my passing?" They replied, "We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.""*

In simple terms, the verse addresses the claimants of Jacob with the question: did you witness when death came to Jacob? The question is the exposure: the Jews who claimed the inheritance of Jacob were not present at his death — the Qur'an records what happened, and the record contradicts the claim. At the moment of the death, Jacob asked his children: who will you worship after my passing? And they answered: the God of your forefathers — Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac — the One God; and to Him we all submit.

The question "did you witness" is the Qur'an's rhetorical method: the claimants had been asserting that Jacob was a Jew or a Christian — an impossible claim, since both came after him — and the verse says: you were not there; and the record of what was said at the deathbed is the answer. The record is the testament: the father's last question was not about property, not about lineage, not about the tribe — it was about worship. The religion of the deathbed is the religion of the life: the father asks, and the children answer with the Oneness of God.

The children's answer is the most important statement: "We will continue to worship your God, the God of your forefathers — Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac — the One God. And to Him we all submit." The answer includes the three forefathers — and it is worth noting that it includes Ishmael, the ancestor of the Arabs, alongside Abraham and Isaac. The Qur'an's record of the deathbed is a statement of the unity of the two lines: the religion of the children of Jacob is the religion of Abraham and Ishmael and Isaac — the One God, and to Him submission. The verse is the refutation of the claim that the heritage was exclusive to any one line.

The answer's final clause — "And to Him we all submit" — is the *islām* of the family: the submission of the children of Jacob to the One God. The Qur'an's portrait of the deathbed is the portrait of the true religion passing from the father to the children: not by blood alone, but by the testimony that the children give — the God of the fathers, the One God, the submission.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the family religion and the deathbed testament. The parent who wants the faith to continue asks the question: who will you worship after my passing? And the children's answer is the inheritance: not the name of the father, but the God of the father, the One God, and the submission to Him. The verse's lesson is that the true legacy is the one that the children can testify to at the moment of the passing — the God they will continue to worship, and the submission they will continue to live.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 134

Verse 134 says: *"That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done."*

In simple terms, the verse closes the discussion of the ancestors with the principle of individual accountability: that was a community that had already gone before; for them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned; and you will not be accountable for what they have done. The verse is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements of the doctrine that the deed is personal.

"That was a community that had already gone before" — the verse points to the fathers: Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes. They are gone; their communities are in the past. The verse's point is the separation: the ancestors are not present, and their relationship with Allah is their own. The claim of the descendants — that the standing of the fathers is transferable to the children — is destroyed by the simple fact of the passing.

"For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned" — the arithmetic of the hereafter: every community is credited with its own work. The Jews and the Christians who claimed the standing of Abraham were drawing on a credit that was not theirs; the verse says the account of Abraham is closed — for him is what he earned. The account of the claimants is open — for them is what they have earned. The standing of the ancestor cannot be inherited because the account of the ancestor is not a fund; it is a record.

"And you will not be accountable for what they have done" — the principle of the personal responsibility: no soul bears the burden of another. The Qur'an's statement here is the foundation of its whole ethics: the person who is a descendant of Abraham is not saved by the faith of Abraham; he is saved by his own faith. And the person who is not a descendant of Abraham is not denied the faith of Abraham; he can earn the same.

In modern life, the verse destroys the two errors of the inherited religion: the pride of the descendants ("I am saved by the standing of my fathers") and the despair of the outsiders ("I am not of the family"). Both are errors against the same principle: the account is personal. The believer who looks at the ancestors and claims their standing has misunderstood the record; the one who looks at the ancestors and despairs has misunderstood the record equally. The faith is not an inheritance; it is an earnings — and the earning is the person's own.

## Verse 135

Verse 135 says: *"The Jews and Christians each say, "Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided." Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.""*"

In simple terms, the verse records the invitation of the two communities — the Jews and the Christians each say: follow our faith to be rightly guided — and the answer: no! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not a polytheist. The verse is the Qur'an's refusal of the two invitations and its return to the source.

The verse's context is the debate with the People of the Book who had come to the Prophet with their claims. Each of them offered their own religion as the path of guidance: follow us, and you will be guided. And the two were mutually exclusive — the Jews required the following of the Torah of their time, the Christians required the following of the gospel of their time, and both rejected the message the Prophet brought. The Qur'an's answer is not a third religion in the sense of a new line; it is the return to the original: the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not a polytheist.

"The upright" — *ḥanīf* — is the key word of the verse. The hanif is the one who turns away from every false direction and faces the truth: the monotheist of the original religion, the one who is not a polytheist. The Qur'an uses the word for Abraham repeatedly and describes him exactly as the verse does: not a Jew, not a Christian, but upright and submissive (3:67); not of the polytheists. The answer to the two invitations is therefore not a rejection of the scriptures but a return to the source from which both descended: the faith of the one who was already a Muslim — a submitter — before either "Jew" or "Christian" existed.

The verse is also a statement of the method of the Qur'an's argument: the dispute is not settled by the claims of the later communities but by the record of the ancestor. The Jews said: follow us; the Christians said: follow us; the Qur'an says: we follow Abraham — and the one who follows Abraham is the one who follows the way of the upright, not the way of the polytheists of any age.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to every exclusive invitation: follow our path — the path of our community, our school, our nation — and you will be guided. The Qur'an's answer is the criterion: the path that leads to guidance is the path of Abraham — the upright, the non-polytheist, the submitter — and the person who follows any path that is not the path of the upright has followed the path of the exclusion. The believer's loyalty is not to the invitation of a community but to the faith of the one who was the friend of Allah, the upright, the one who was not of the polytheists.

===VERSE-END===
