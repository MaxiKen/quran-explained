## Verse 121

Verse 121 says: *"Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it. As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers."*

In simple terms, the verse introduces the positive counterpart to the whole preceding section. After the long indictment of the People of the Book who cast the Scripture behind their backs, the Qur'an now says: those to whom the Book has been given and who follow it as it should be followed — it is they who truly believe in it. The verse recognizes the existence of the faithful among the People of the Book, and it defines the standard by which genuine belief is measured.

"Follow it as it should be followed" — the Arabic is *yatlūnahu ḥaqqa tilāwatihi*, literally "they recite it with true recitation." The classical scholars explain that this "recitation" is not merely the sound of the words; it is the complete reception of the Book. Ibn Abbas explained it as making lawful what the Book makes lawful, prohibiting what it makes prohibited, reciting it as it was revealed, not changing the words from their places, and not interpreting it with other than its real meaning. In other words, the true recitation is the living of the Book: the reciter is the one who obeys, follows, and does not distort. Ibn Kathir notes that the verse's standard applies generally to the community of the Prophet as well: the one who reads the Qur'an with true recitation is the one who receives it with faith and practice.

The verse also answers a question implicit in the whole section: is the People of the Book as a whole condemned? No. The Qur'an has been indicting the majority who rejected the messenger, distorted the Scripture, and broke the covenant. But it now names the exception — and, importantly, it identifies the exception by the standard of the Book itself. The faithful among them are not the ones with the identity; they are the ones whose recitation of the Book is true. And the reverse is stated with equal force: "As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers." It is not the rejection of the new messenger that is the test in this verse; it is the rejection of the Book they already hold. The People of the Book who kept their Scripture properly could not reject the messenger who confirmed it — the two rejections are one.

In modern life, the verse is a criterion for every reader of revelation. The question is not whether one owns the Book — every believer owns it — but whether one follows it as it should be followed: making lawful what it makes lawful, prohibiting what it prohibits, reading it as it was revealed, and refusing to twist its meaning. The verse's verdict is that the one who rejects the Book after this is the loser; the one who follows it truly believes, whatever his community.

## Verse 122

Verse 122 says: *"O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others."*

In simple terms, the verse is a direct address to the Children of Israel: remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others. The command of remembrance is the recurring refrain of the surah (2:40, 2:47), and it is the method of the message: the people whose history is a history of favours and failures are called to remember the favours, so that the failures can be measured.

The verse has two objects of remembrance: the favours (*ni'mah*) and the honour (*faddala*). The favours are the concrete acts: the deliverance from Pharaoh, the parting of the sea, the manna and the quails, the Book, the prophets, the covenant. The honour is the status: "how I honoured you above the others." As the surah stated in 2:47, this honour is to be understood in its historical context — the Children of Israel were honoured among the nations of their era, chosen for the prophetic line and the covenant of the age before the final messenger. It is not a claim of perpetual or unconditional superiority; it is the record of the divine choice in history.

Why does the Qur'an call this to remembrance after the long indictment? Because the privilege is the measure of the obligation. The people who were given more are asked for more. The address is therefore not a compliment but a summons: the favours are evidence, and the evidence stands against those who have responded to the favours with the rejection of the messengers. The same command appears in the surah's opening address to the Children of Israel (2:40) and returns here as the surah moves from the history of the covenant to the memory of Abraham — the ancestor whose religion both the Jews and the Christians were claiming.

In modern life, the verse is addressed to every community that has been given more — the community of every prophet, every nation that has received the Book, every person who has been given faith, health, security, and knowledge. The command is the same: remember. The person who forgets the favours becomes ungrateful in the most subtle way: not by denying that they happened, but by no longer being changed by them. Remembrance — in prayer, in gratitude, in the daily turning to the Giver — is the Qur'an's medicine for the amnesia that makes privilege a possession rather than an obligation.

## Verse 123

Verse 123 says: *"And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given."*

In simple terms, the verse now completes the address to the Children of Israel with the warning: guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. The verse then makes the terror of that Day precise in three negations: no ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given.

The verse is the second appearance of this description in the surah (2:48 used nearly the same words), and it is placed here deliberately, after the citation of the favours and before the story of Abraham. The logic is the warning of accountability: the people who were given the favours and the honour, and who are now being addressed about their response, are being told that the Day of the reckoning cannot be paid off. The negations close every exit: the ransom (the payment), the intercession (the mediator), the help (the ally) — the three forms of rescue that human beings trust in.

The three negations require careful understanding, because the Qur'an elsewhere affirms intercession — the Qur'an itself states that intercession with Him can only take place with His permission (2:255) and that those whom He permits will intercede. The verse before us is not denying that intercession will happen; it is denying the assumption that it is available to everyone without qualification. The intercession of the verse's context is the automatic rescue that the claimants of privilege expected: the belief that their ancestors, their prophets, their lineage would stand for them on the Day regardless of their own deeds. That intercession — the purchased, inherited, assumed intercession — does not exist. Intercession, where the Qur'an affirms it, is a grace granted by Allah to whom He wills, and it is granted to those who have believed and done good — not to those who demanded it as a right.

"Guard yourselves" — *ittaqū* — the command is the whole message: the protection of the Day is not bought or inherited; it is built in this life by the awareness of Allah. The people of the verse had been assuming the protection of their history; the verse says the protection is personal. The Day arrives with no helpers and no payment, and the only thing that stands is what the person has done.

In modern life, the verse remains the most direct answer to the religion of safety. Every community and every person has a version of the assumed rescue: the membership, the ancestry, the connections, the reputation, the good standing that will stand in for the deed. The verse removes all of them at once. The Day is the most personal event in existence — no soul will be of any help to another — and the only currency that survives it is the one the person has sent ahead.

## Verse 124

Verse 124 says: *"˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, "I will certainly make you into a role model for the people." Abraham asked, "What about my offspring?" Allah replied, "My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.""*

In simple terms, the verse turns from the Children of Israel to Abraham — the ancestor whom both the Jews and the Christians claimed as the founder of their religion. The verse records the divine test: Abraham was tested with certain commandments, and he fulfilled them. The reward: "I will certainly make you into a role model for the people." Abraham asked about his offspring, and the reply cut through every claim of inheritance: "My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers."

The "commandments" — *kalimāt* — are a famous subject of discussion among the commentators. They are the divine commands and trials through which Abraham was tested: the exegetes differ in enumerating them — the rites he was commanded to establish, the sacrifice of his son, the settling of his family in the barren valley, the commands of the hajj — but the point the verse makes is the outcome, not the list: "which he fulfilled." The verse's emphasis is on the completion: the trials were not met halfway, not negotiated, not explained away. Abraham fulfilled them. The word *atammahunna* — he completed them — is the standard: not merely obedience but completion.

The reward is the appointment: "I will certainly make you into a role model for the people" — the word *imām*: a leader, a model, one who is followed. The appointment is the point of the whole passage: Abraham did not receive his standing by inheritance; he earned it through the fulfilment of the trials. The Qur'an's history is the exact opposite of the assumption the People of the Book had built: their claim was that the prophetic and religious standing belonged to their line by birth; the Qur'an's answer is that the standing belongs to those who are tested and fulfil, and that the line only shares in it through the fulfilment.

"And from my offspring?" — Abraham's question is the natural one of a father and a prophet: the man who has been made a leader wants the leadership to continue in his descendants. The answer is the most important sentence for the entire dispute: "My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers." The covenant is not a blood possession; it is a covenant with righteousness. The children of Abraham are those who follow Abraham's way — the path of submission and fulfilment — not those who merely descend from him. As the commentators note, the principle severs every claim of entitlement based on lineage: the divine honour and the divine covenant belong to those who are upright, not to those who simply inherit the name.

In modern life, the verse is the definitive correction of inherited religion on every level: the religious identity that is assumed from the family, the leadership that is claimed from the name, the security that is drawn from the ancestry. The verse says the covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers — and the one who wants the covenant must ask the same question Abraham was tested with: what are the commandments, and have I fulfilled them?

## Verse 125

Verse 125 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, "˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer." And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺."*

In simple terms, the verse now moves from the test of Abraham to the House: the Sacred House was made a centre and a sanctuary for the people. The verse then gives the two commands that belong to the House: take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer; and the covenant entrusted to Abraham and Ishmael: purify the House for those who circle it, meditate in it, and bow and prostrate in prayer.

The "centre" — *mathābah* — carries the sense of a place of return, a place to which people come back repeatedly. Ibn Kathir explains that Allah made the House a place of resort for people and a place of safety: the hearts are eager to visit it and never tire of it, and those who enter it are safe even from the conflicts that surround it. The House is not a building that belongs to one community; it is the place of return for the people, and the verse's "sanctuary" — *amnan* — is the security that belongs to it: the place where the warring and the feuding come under the peace of Allah.

The "standing-place of Abraham" is the stone on which he stood while raising the walls of the House, and the Qur'an commands it to be taken as a site of prayer. The narration recorded in Sahih Muslim relates that Umar suggested to the Prophet that the Maqam be taken as a place of prayer, and the verse was then revealed — and the Prophet himself, in his farewell hajj, recited the verse and prayed two units after the tawaf with the Maqam between him and the House. The verse's command is thus a piece of the worship and a sign of the continuity: the prayer of the believers is the prayer of Abraham, and the place of his standing is the place of their worship.

"Purify My House" — the command entrusted to Abraham and Ishmael is the purification of the House from all that does not belong to it: the idols, the filth, the corruption, and, in every age, the turning of the sacred into the commercial. The verse describes the worshippers in four states — those who circle it (the tawaf), who meditate in it (the staying, the contemplation), and who bow and prostrate (the prayer) — the whole range of the worship of the House. The House is purified for them, and the purification is the covenant that the builders and the worshippers inherit together.

In modern life, the verse is the Qur'an's teaching on sacred space: the place of worship is not an end in itself; it is a centre to which the hearts return and a sanctuary in which the conflicts cease. And the verse's command of purification applies to every "House of Allah" — the masjid of the neighbourhood, the heart of the believer, the religion itself: each is to be kept clear of what does not belong to it, for those who circle, who meditate, and who bow and prostrate.
