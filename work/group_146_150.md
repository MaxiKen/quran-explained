## Verse 146

Verse 146 says: *"Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children. Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly."*

In simple terms, the verse returns to the knowledge of the People of the Book: those We have given the Scripture recognize this Prophet as they recognize their own children. Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly.

The recognition "as they recognize their own children" is one of the Qur'an's most powerful descriptions of certainty. The scholars of the People of the Scripture knew the truth about what the Messenger was sent with — they knew his description, his coming, the qibla change, the whole matter — the way a parent knows a child: immediately, unmistakably, without needing to be told. Ibn Kathir notes that this is a parable the Arabs use to describe what is very apparent: the recognition of the child is the recognition of the most evident, most intimate, most undeniable fact of a person's life. The scholars of the People of the Book had that level of certainty about the Messenger — it was written in their Books, read by them, known by them.

The verse's structure is the pairing of the knowledge with the hiding: "they recognize this Prophet, yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly." The verse is the same pattern established in the whole surah — the knowledge and the concealment; the recognition and the suppression; the certainty of the scholars and the failure to act on it. The word *fariq* ("a group") identifies that not all of them hid it: the Believers among the People of the Book believed (2:121); the group that hid it — the scholars who knew and suppressed — is distinguished from the ones who accepted.

The commentators note the many references to the fact that their Books contained the description of the Messenger: his name, his lineage, his resting place, the qibla, the religion of Abraham (cf. 7:157). The recognition was not the recognition of the Prophet personally; it was the recognition of the description in the Book. The verse says the group did it "knowingly" — *wa hum ya'lamun* — hiding the truth, while they know it.

In modern life, the verse is the distinction between the knowledge and the confession. The people who know are not the same as the people who acknowledge: the person can recognize the truth with the certainty of the father recognizing the child — and still suppress it, still hide it, still act as if he did not know. The verse's lesson is the one the whole surah has been teaching: the knowledge is not the criterion; the criterion is what the one who knows does.

## Verse 147

Verse 147 says: *"˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord, so do not ever be one of those who doubt."*

In simple terms, the verse is the confirmation of the whole matter: this is the truth from your Lord — so do not ever be one of those who doubt. The verse is addressed to the Prophet in direct speech, and it is the command of certainty in the face of the opposition.

The commentators read the verse as the fortification of the Prophet and the believers: the Jews knew the truth and hid it; the opposition of the ones who know is one of the hardest forms of the test — the believer begins to wonder: if they know and they reject, perhaps the truth is not what I think? The verse says: the matter is the truth, from your Lord; the opposition's knowledge and concealment do not change the truth; do not be among the doubters.

Al-Jalalayn notes the eloquence: "be not among the doubters" — the verse says be not of such a disposition, which is more eloquent than merely saying "do not waver." The prohibition is of the character of doubt, not merely the act of doubting. The verse set the standard: the believer is not the person who never has questions, but the person whose character is not the character of the doubter — the one who does not live in the hesitation, the one who has the truth from the Lord and stands on it.

The passage also answers the practical questions: after the qibla change, the Muslims who had died facing Jerusalem, the prayers performed toward the old direction, the standing of the community before the command of the change — the verse says: this is the truth from your Lord, do not doubt. The verse's "truth" covers the whole passage: the qibla change was true; the acceptance of the previous prayers was true; the knowledge of the People of the Book was true; the standing of the believers was true.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to the doubt that comes from the opposition of the knowledgeable. The person who knows the truth and sees the educated, the learned, the powerful reject it, is tempted to doubt. The verse says: the truth does not depend on the number of its opponents; the truth is from the Lord; and the believer is commanded to not live in the doubt — to keep the character that stands on the given truth.

## Verse 148

Verse 148 says: *"Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺. So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything."*

In simple terms, the verse states the plurality of the directions and the unity of the destination: everyone turns to their own direction of prayer. So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together for judgment — for He is Most Capable of everything.

The commentators note that the verse is connected to the statement in 5:48: "To each among you We have prescribed a law and a clear way" (the commentators quote this in the tafsir of 2:148 — the communities had different laws and directions, each prescribed by Allah). The verse's first clause is the recognition of the plurality: every community had its own direction — the Jews theirs, the Christians theirs, the Muslims theirs. The plurality is not the chaos; it is the divine decree: the directions were commanded, each community was given its own law and its own way.

"So compete with one another in doing good" — the verse turns the plurality into the competition. The religious communities were not competing over directions — the verse says the directions were each their own; the real competition is the *khayrat* — the good deeds. The verse's shift is the whole point: the believers were told to compete in the good, not to dispute the directions. The direction is the obedience; the competition is the good by which the obediences are measured.

The verse's relation to the Muslims is also the relation within the Muslim community: the differences of the communities — the Schools, the communities, the ways — apply; the competition that matters is the competition in the good. The differences are the matters of the command; the good is the matter of the account.

"Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together for judgment" — the verse states the destination of all the directions. The directions diverge in the world; the gathering is one: the Day of Judgment. The verse states the fact — *Allah will bring you all together* — and the ground: He is Most Capable of everything. The gathering of the scattered on the Day is the proof of the power: the one who can gather all the directions and all the people is the One who is Most Capable.

In modern life, the verse is the instruction for the community in the face of the divisions. When people differ over directions — over the details, the schools, the ways — the verse says: everyone has a direction; the direction is the command; the competition is the good. The person who turns the difference of the direction into a competition of the direction has missed the verse's point: the competition is in the good, and the gathering is with Allah, the Most Capable.

## Verse 149

Verse 149 says: *"Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. This is certainly the truth from your Lord. And Allah is never unaware of what you ˹all˺ do."*

In simple terms, the verse repeats the command of the direction for the Prophet in the strongest form: wherever you are, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque — this is certainly the truth from your Lord; and Allah is never unaware of what you all do.

The verse's repetition is the emphasis. The qibla command was given twice, three times in the passage (2:144, 2:149, 2:150), and the commentators note the repetition is deliberate: Al-Jalalayn notes that the repetition is intended to point out that the stipulation applies equally to being on a journey or otherwise — the command of the direction is not limited to Mecca, not limited to home, not limited to one location. Wherever you are — *min haythu kharajta* — from wherever you set out — the face is turned toward the Sacred Mosque. Ibn Kathir records the earlier discussion: "and wheresoever you are, turn your faces in that direction is a command from Allah to face the Kaaba from wherever one is on the earth: the east, west, north, or south."

"This is certainly the truth from your Lord" — the verse attaches the truth to the command. The qibla change was not a political adjustment, not a concession to the Arabs, not a strategic change — it was the truth from the Lord. The verse says it to the Prophet in the face of the objectors: the change is true, the direction is true, the command is true.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you all do" — the verse's closing clause returns to the watch. The People of the Book were doing — hiding, opposing, mocking — and the believers were doing — obeying, changing, turning. The verse says: the One who is never unaware sees all: He sees the opposition and the obedience, the concealment and the command-following.

In modern life, the verse is the teaching about the command's scope. The believer's religion is not confined to the times and places where the worship is convenient: the command of the direction follows the believer — wherever he is, the face is turned. The verse also teaches the source of the command: the direction is the truth, not the preference; the Muslims were not turning to the Kaaba because it pleased them, but because it was the truth from their Lord — and the same standard governs every command the believer obeys.

## Verse 150

Verse 150 says: *"Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. And wherever you ˹believers˺ are, face towards it, so that people will have no argument against you, except the wrongdoers among them. Do not fear them; fear Me, so that I may ˹continue to˺ perfect My favour upon you and so you may be ˹rightly˺ guided."*

In simple terms, the verse now extends the command to the believers: wherever you are, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque — so that people will have no argument against you, except the wrongdoers among them. Do not fear them; fear Me — so that I may perfect My favour upon you and so you may be rightly guided.

The verse's address is to the whole community: the command of the Prophet is repeated, and then the command of the believers. The direction is not a private matter of the Messenger; it is the communal command. The whole ummah faced the Sacred Mosque — the direction of Abraham, the House to which the pilgrims turn.

"So that people will have no argument against you" — the verse states the positive purpose of the command: the change to the Kaaba closed the argument of the People of the Book. Ibn Kathir explains: the Jews had disliked the command and asked what had turned the Muslims away from the direction they used to face (the objection of 2:142); the command to face the Kaaba now met the matter directly — the Kaaba was the qibla of Ibrahim, more respected and honored than the qibla of the Jews, and the People of the Book knew from the description of the Muslim Ummah that they would be ordered to face it — so the change closed their argument. The Arabs — the polytheists of Quraysh — had the opposite objection: "This man claims that he follows the religion of Ibrahim; if his facing Bayt al-Maqdis was a part of the religion of Ibrahim, why did he change it?" The verse answers: the direction that pleased the Lord — the direction of Abraham — was the command, and the objection was answered.

The "wrongdoers among them" — the ones who would still argue: Ibn Kathir identifies them as the Mushrikin of Quraysh, whose unsound statement was the one quoted above. The verse anticipates: even the perfect command and the perfect answer will not stop the wrongdoers from arguing; the argument of the wrongdoers is the argument of the ones who do not want the truth.

"Do not fear them; fear Me" — the verse's command is the heart of the matter. The believers were facing not only the theological argument but the social pressure: the Jews were a powerful community, the Quraysh were the masters of the Kaaba, and the change of the direction was met with the social opposition. The verse says: fear them not — the opposition's ridicule, the opposition's argument, the opposition's power — fear Me. The fear of Allah is the command that replaces the fear of the people.

"So that I may perfect My favour upon you and so you may be rightly guided" — the verse states the purpose of the command: the perfecting of the favor and the guidance. The qibla change was not a burden; it was the completion of the favor: the religion of Abraham, the direction of the Kaaba, the full measure of the nation's guidance. The command of the direction is the completion of the religion; the obedience to it is the guidance.

In modern life, the verse is the two-fold teaching: the command and the fear. The command is the direction of the community — the direction that the believer obeys wherever he is, whatever the situation, whatever the cost. The fear is the standard: the one who fears the people more than Allah will turn the command to please the people; the one who fears Allah will obey the command and let the argument of the wrongdoers stand unanswered. The favor is completed by the obedience; the guidance is the fruit of the fear.
