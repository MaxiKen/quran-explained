## Verse 136

Verse 136 says: *"Say, ˹O believers,˺ "We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.""*

In simple terms, the verse gives the creed of the believers in its fullest form: we believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us, and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. The creed then adds the decisive clause: "We make no distinction between any of them." And the conclusion: to Allah we all submit.

The creed is the Qur'an's answer to the partial belief of the communities. The Jews believed in the prophets of their line and rejected Jesus and Muhammad; the Christians believed in their prophets and rejected the final messenger. The believers' creed is the complete belief: every prophet, every revelation, from the Lord. The verse names the sequence: Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants — the line of the fathers; Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets — the line of the law and the gospel. And then one phrase covers the whole: "We make no distinction between any of them."

"We make no distinction between any of them" — this is one of the most important doctrines of Islam. The religion of the believers does not select its prophets; it receives them all — his own too. The Qur'an elsewhere states the same creed (3:84), and the doctrine is the refutation of the very error the surah has been exposing: believing in part of the Book and rejecting the rest, accepting some messengers and killing others (2:87, 2:91). The believer who makes a distinction between the prophets has made a distinction between the revelations, and the distinction is the beginning of the rejection.

The creed is also the answer to the Muslim's own temptation: the temptation to believe in the Qur'an and to neglect the prophets, to accept the revelation of one age and to reject the revelation of the previous. The verse says the full belief is the belief in all: what has been revealed to us, and what was revealed to them — all from the same Lord, all to be received.

"To Allah we all submit" — the conclusion of the creed is the religion: the belief in all the prophets is not a catalogue; it is the submission to the One who sent them all. The one who believes in the prophets without the submission has accepted the history and refused the religion.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the religious community that has become partial. The community that honors its own prophets and dismisses the others, that reveres its own book and despises the ones before it — makes the distinction the verse forbids. The believer's faith is the whole line: the prophets all, the revelations all, and the Lord to whom they all called the same.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 137

Verse 137 says: *"So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺. But Allah will spare you their evil. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse states the consequence of the creed: if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be rightly guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed to the truth. And the verse closes with the assurance: Allah will spare you their evil, for He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

"If they believe in what you believe" — the condition is the same: the creed the believers just declared is the standard. The People of the Book who accept the whole line of the prophets — the line that includes the final messenger — are guided. The verse does not say they become Muslims by the change of the name; it says they believe in what the believers believe: the complete creed, the same submission. The guidance is not a label; it is the content of the belief.

"But if they turn away, they are simply opposed to the truth" — the verse's diagnosis is one of the Qur'an's sharpest words: the turning away is not a neutral disagreement; it is opposition. The person who has heard the creed, the whole line of the prophets, and the call to the submission — and who turns away — is not a seeker who has missed the truth; he is opposed to it. The Qur'an uses the same word for the opposition that the surah has exposed from the beginning: the people of the Book who recognized the messenger and rejected him (2:89), who cast the Book behind their backs (2:101), who are in dispute over the truth they know (2:139).

"But Allah will spare you their evil" — the assurance is for the believers: the opposition of the others will not harm them. The verse was revealed in a period of hostility, and the Prophet and the believers were being told: their hostility is not your concern; the One who hears their words and knows their plots will spare you their evil. The verse is not a promise of immunity from injury; it is a promise of the divine protection in the outcome: the evil they intend will not prevail against the people of the truth.

In modern life, the verse is the assurance for the community that has been opposed. The believer who has delivered the truth and been rejected is told: the rejection is not your failure; the one who turns away is opposed to the truth, and the One who is All-Hearing — who hears their objections — and All-Knowing — who knows their intentions — will spare the believers their evil. The community's task is the creed and the delivery; the outcome is with the One who hears and knows.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 138

Verse 138 says: *"This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the name of the believers' religion: this is the natural Way of Allah. The Arabic is *ṣibghat Allāh* — the dye, the baptism, the coloring of Allah. The verse asks: who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And declares: and we worship none but Him.

The classical scholars explain the word *ṣibghah* in several related ways. Ibn Abbas explained it as the religion of Allah — the pure faith of the submission that the prophets taught. Some commentators note the reference to the Christian rite of baptism, by which the converts of that community were initiated — and the Qur'an's answer: the believers' "baptism" is the baptism of Allah: not the water, but the religion; not the rite of the community, but the coloring of the faith itself. The word is also used for the dying of cloth — the coloring that gives the fabric its identity — and the metaphor is the person who has been dyed by the religion: the faith is not a garment that can be removed; it is the color that has penetrated the whole person. The believer is the one who has been "dyed" with the color of Allah — the monotheism, the submission, the uprightness.

"Who is better than Allah in ordaining a way?" — the question answers itself. The Christians claimed that their baptism was the way into the faith; the Jews claimed their law was the path; the verse says the way belongs to Allah, and no one is better than Allah in ordaining the way. The ordainer is the One who knows what the way requires: the One who has made the religion, chosen the prophets, sent the books, and given the rites. The way of Allah is the most perfect way because the ordainer is the One who knows the human being and the purpose of the human being.

"And we worship none but Him" — the conclusion is the tawhid: the way of Allah is the worship of Allah alone. The religion is not a set of claims about the prophets; it is the worship of the One who sent them all. The verse brings the whole discussion to the point: the Muslims' creed is not a position in the debate between the communities; it is the worship of the one God, without the partner of the Christians' trinity and without the polytheism of the pagans, and without the partiality of the Jews and the Christians toward their own lines.

In modern life, the verse is the definition of the identity of the believer. The identity is not in the name of the community; it is in the "color" of the faith — the depth to which the religion has penetrated the person: the monotheism that colors his worship, the submission that colors his will, and the uprightness that colors his life. The verse's question — who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? — is the believer's answer to every offer of a better religion: the way has been ordained, and the ordainer is the best of the ordainers.

## Verse 139

Verse 139 says: *"Say, "Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺.""*

In simple terms, the verse now turns the debate to its true subject: would you dispute with us about Allah — while He is our Lord and your Lord? The verse states the ground of the dispute and the boundary of the dispute: the same Lord, the separate accounts, and the devotion of the believers to Him alone.

"Would you dispute with us about Allah" — the question exposes the whole controversy. The Jews and the Christians had been disputing with the believers about religion — about Abraham, about the prophets, about the scriptures — but the dispute was really about Allah: about His choice, His grace, His messenger, His way. The believers' answer: the One who is our Lord is your Lord. There is no rival Lord; there cannot be; the quarrel about the religion of Allah is a quarrel about the One who is the Lord of both parties. The person who claims to serve the same Allah while disputing the way Allah has sent is disputing with his own Lord.

"We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours" — the verse states the principle of the separate accounts, the same principle of 2:134: the deeds are individual, and the accountability is individual. The believers do not answer for the deeds of the People of the Book, and the People of the Book do not answer for the believers. The statement is the removal of the weapon of the dispute: the believers are not responsible for the deeds of the others; the others will not be judged by the believers' claim. The accountability is the boundary, and the boundary is the judgment of Allah.

"And we are devoted to Him alone" — the final clause is the believers' position in the dispute: the devotion is not divided. The word *mukhliṣūn* — rendered "devoted" — carries the sense of the purified and the sincere: the worship that has no partner, no mixed motive, no divided loyalty. The believers are devoted to Allah alone, and that devotion is the content of their creed, the same creed that the whole section has been building: the submission to the One, the belief in all His prophets, the worship of none but Him.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the theological dispute conducted with the boundaries of the faith. The believer disputes — the verse does not forbid the dispute — but the dispute is grounded: the same Lord is the Lord of both; the accounts are individual; and the believers' devotion is to Allah alone. The verse forbids the dispute that has forgotten the common Lord, and the dispute that has forgotten the separate accounts — the dispute that judges the other's deeds as though the judgment were the believer's. The believer disputes about the religion and leaves the judgment to Allah.

## Verse 140

Verse 140 says: *"Or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians?" Say, "Who is more knowledgeable: you or Allah?" Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah? And Allah is never unaware of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse now confronts the claim directly: do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians? The question is impossible on its face — the patriarchs lived generations before either community existed — and the Qur'an answers it with two questions: who is more knowledgeable, you or Allah? And then the condemnation: who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah?

The first question exposes the absurdity of the claim. The people of the Book were claiming that the patriarchs of the ancient world were members of the religions that arose after them — a claim that even their own histories contradicted. The Qur'an's answer is: who knows better than you, or does Allah? The title of the knowledge belongs to Allah: He knows who the patriarchs were and what they worshipped, and His testimony — in the Scripture of the very claimants — states the truth. The claim of the people of the Book was not merely false; it was made against their own knowledge.

The second question is the condemnation: "Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah?" The verse names the crime: the concealment. The People of the Book had the testimony in their hands: their Scripture stated the true religion is the submission, and it stated that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes were neither Jews nor Christians — they had testified to these facts. Al-Hasan al-Basri, as recorded by the commentators, explained that they used to recite the Book which stated these things and then hid them from the people. The crime is not ignorance; it is the concealment of the known. The wrongdoer par excellence is the one who has received the testimony and hides it.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you do" — the verse closes with the warning: the concealment has been observed. The people who thought the testimony could be hidden from the public — and from the Prophet and the believers — had forgotten the One who sees the concealment. The verse's point is the whole of the section's argument: the true religion of the patriarchs is known to Allah, and the ones who have claimed them for their own religions have done so by the suppression of their own testimony.

In modern life, the verse is the standard of the religious knowledge that has been received and suppressed. The person who knows the testimony and hides it has done a greater wrong than the one who does not know it: the truth is not merely unknown in his case — it is suppressed. And the verse's question — who knows better than you, or Allah? — is the ground of the believer's confidence in the truth over the claims of the authorities: the One who knows the testimony is the One whose knowledge does not change under the press of the interests and the claims of the communities.
