## Verse 31

Verse 31 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the test and the reward of love: if you truly love Allah, then follow me — and Allah will love you and forgive your sins; Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

The verse is one of the most important verses in the Qur'an for the religion's structure of authority, and Ibn Kathir frames the whole surah from here. "This honorable Ayah judges against those who claim to love Allah, yet do not follow the way of Muhammad. Such people are not true in their claim until they follow the Shari'ah (Law) of Muhammad and his religion in all his statements, actions and conditions." The claim to love Allah is a claim that the Qur'an itself tests: the condition of the love of Allah is the following of the Messenger.

Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: "When they said 'We only worship idols out of our love for God, that they might bring us close to Him,' the following was revealed." The verse's first audience was the polytheists of the era and the people who claimed devotion while keeping invented intermediaries. Al-Hasan al-Basri and several scholars among the Salaf commented: "Some people claimed that they love Allah. So Allah tested them with this ayah." The claim of love is not accepted at face value; it is tested by the evidence of following.

"If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me" — the conditional is the test: *in kuntum tuhibbuna Allah* — if you love Allah — *fattabi'uni* — then follow me. The word *ittiba'* is the following that is submission and imitation: following in practice, in method, in obedience. Ibn Kathir: the following of his Shari'ah and religion "in all his statements, actions and conditions." The claim of love without the following is a self-description that contradicts its own evidence; the following is the proof of the love, and it is the way the love becomes real.

"Allah will love you" — the reward is greater than the claim. Ibn Kathir: "what you will earn is much more than what you sought in loving Him, for Allah will love you." The structure of the exchange is the Qur'an's deepest spiritual teaching: the human love of Allah is not the possession of an object but the entry into a relationship; the one who loves Allah enters into the divine response — and the divine love is the return of the love of the believer. The believer seeks His love; Allah's love is given to the one who follows the Messenger. Al-Jalalayn and the classical reading tie the "Allah will love you" to the reward of the following and the forgiveness of sins.

"and forgive your sins" — *wa yaghfir lakum dhunubakum* — He will forgive you your sins. The reward of the following is double: the love of Allah and the removal of the burden. Ibn Kathir: "by your following the Messenger, you will earn all this with the blessing of his mission." The forgiveness is the mercy's removal of what stands between the believer and the relationship: the sins that made him unworthy of the love are removed, and the relationship is restored.

"For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" — the two names close the verse as the guarantee: the One who commands the following is the One who forgives — *Ghafur* (All-Forgiving, the One who covers the faults) and *Rahim* (Most Merciful, the One who wishes the good for the servant). The command of the verse is not a legalistic test but the setting of the mercy's path: follow, and the mercy will meet you.

The verse's significance in the surah is polemical and structural. Polemically: the People of the Book claimed the love of Allah and a special relationship with Him while rejecting the messenger. The Qur'an's answer: the claim is tested by the following — if the love is real, it follows the one whom Allah sent; if it does not, the claim is not the love. Structurally: the verse is the foundation of the religion's authority — the sunnah of the Prophet is not a supplement to the Qur'an or an optional expression of culture; it is the concrete form of the love of Allah and the visible evidence of the following. The Muslim reader of the verse understands why the Prophet's example carries the centrality it does: the love of Allah is not an abstraction; it is embodied in the following of His Messenger.

## Verse 32

Verse 32 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Obey Allah and His Messenger.” If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers.*

In simple terms, the verse advances from following to obedience: say — obey Allah and His Messenger; if they turn away, then Allah does not like the disbelievers.

The verse is the command form of 3:31's condition. The previous verse said "follow me"; this verse says "obey Allah and His Messenger." Ibn Kathir connects them: "Allah next commands everyone: 'Say: Obey Allah and the Messenger.'" The two verbs — *ittiba'* (follow) and *ta'a* (obey) — are the two sides of the relationship: the following of the example and the obedience of the command. The verse keeps them together: obedience is to Allah and to the Messenger; the two coordinates of the believer's action are in a single line.

"Obey Allah and His Messenger" — the phrase is the Qur'an's standard pairing: *ati'u Allah wa ati'u al-Rasul* — obey Allah and obey the Messenger; the repetition-form makes the command emphatic. The classical doctrine that this verse anchors is the inseparable authority of the two: obedience to the Messenger is the form of obedience to Allah, and there is no obedience to Allah that bypasses the Messenger. Ibn Kathir's treatment states it in the strongest terms: "testifying that defiance of the Messenger's way constitutes Kufr. Indeed, Allah does not like whoever does this, even if he claims that he loves Allah and seeks a means of approach to Him, unless, and until, he follows the unlettered Prophet, the Final Messenger from Allah to the two creations: mankind and the Jinn."

"If they still turn away" — *fa in tawallaw* — if they turn away, they withdraw, avert. The verse does not postulate a category of people who reject Allah but obey the Messenger, or who obey Allah but reject the Messenger; the turning away is the turning from the command as a whole. Ibn Kathir: the condition is "by defying the Prophet."

"then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers" — the sentence is the negative of the love of 3:31. The verse does not say Allah will not forgive them, that He will punish them, or that their deeds are void — it says He does not like them: *fa inna Allah la yuhibb al-kafirin*. The *kafirin* — the deniers, the disbelievers — are the ones who turn away from the command after the call. The "not liking" is the Qur'an's way of stating the edge of the relationship: the one who turns away from the love-test of 3:31 and the obedience-command of 3:32 places himself outside the divine approval — however much he claims the love.

For the reader, the verse completes the pair: 3:31–32 is the two-step statement of the believer's relation to the Messenger — follow his example, obey his command. The verse's strength is in the name of the turning-away: the person who "claims that he loves Allah and seeks a means of approach to Him" (Ibn Kathir's wording for the claim without the following) is counted among the disbelievers in the sense of the verse's verdict: the denial of the Messenger's authority is the denial of the relationship the verse defines. The surah's polemic with the People of the Book is here at its sharpest: they claimed the love of Allah and His covenant; the Qur'an says the claim is tested by the obedience, and the turning away from the messenger is the evidence against the claim.

The verse is also one of the Qur'an's anchor texts for the doctrine of *ta'a* — obedience in Islam — and the classical scholars read it as the command that encompasses the whole religion: the obedience to Allah in His commands and prohibitions, and the obedience to the Messenger in the transmission and explanation of the commands. The believer who accepts the Qur'an as the book of Allah necessarily accepts the Prophet as the explainer of the book; the two acceptances are one acceptance.

## Verse 33

Verse 33 says: *Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺.*

In simple terms, the verse begins the second discourse of the surah with the statement of divine election: Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of 'Imran above the worlds — the people of their time.

"Indeed, Allah chose" — *istafa* — chose, selected, preferred, purified, singled out. The verb is from *safw* — purity, the picking of the best. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that He has chosen these households over the people of the earth." Al-Jalalayn: "He preferred their selves, Abraham and 'Imran, above the worlds by making prophethood reside in them and their progeny." The election is the Qur'an's answer to the People of the Book's claim of a special relationship: the election is real, and it is Allah's — and the elected houses are listed precisely.

"Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of 'Imrân" — the four chosen: Adam, the father of mankind; Noah, the first messenger to the earth; the family of Abraham, the father of the prophets; and the family of 'Imran, the house of Maryam and 'Isa. Ibn Kathir expands each: Allah chose Adam — created him with His Hand, blew life into him, commanded the angels to prostrate before him, taught him the names of everything, allowed him to dwell in Paradise, but then sent him down out of His wisdom. Allah chose Nuh and made him the first Messenger to the people of the earth, when the people worshipped idols and associated others with Allah — he called his people day and night, in public and in secret, for a very long time; his calling only made them shun him more; he supplicated against them, and Allah caused them to drown, none saved except those who followed the religion Allah sent with him. Allah chose the household of Ibrahim, including the master of all mankind and the Final Prophet, Muhammad. And Allah chose the household of 'Imran — the father of Maryam bint 'Imran, the mother of 'Isa. And 'Isa is from the offspring of Ibrahim.

"above all people ˹of their time˺" — *'ala al-'alamin* — above the worlds, above the peoples of their respective eras. The phrase moderates the claim: each house was elevated above the peoples of its time, not all peoples of all times in a single unqualified ranking. The election is the elevation of the bearers of the message: the prophets among them and the continuous line of guidance.

Maududi provides the historical frame: the verse marks the beginning of the second discourse of the surah, revealed about 9 A.H., when a delegation from the Christian republic of Najran visited the Prophet. Najran lay between the Hijaz and Yaman, and comprised seventy-three towns and villages; an estimated one hundred and twenty thousand men could bear arms; the entire population was Christian, under three chiefs — the 'aqib (head of the community), the sayyid (collective and political affairs), and the usquf (bishop, religious leader). When the Prophet annexed Makka, the whole of Arabia became convinced the future of the area was bound up with him, and deputations began to visit him. The three chiefs of Najran came to Madina with sixty people — not prepared to go to war, the alternatives were to embrace Islam or to live as dhimmis (protected non-Muslim subjects of the Islamic state). It was on this occasion that Allah revealed this discourse — an invitation to the people of Najran to accept Islam. And Maududi notes the identification: 'Imran was the father of Musa and Harun, mentioned in the Bible as Amram — though the 'Imran of this surah's story is the father of Maryam, and the exegetes note the two 'Imrans or the family line connecting them (Ibn Kathir's discussion; cf. the note in most editions of the tafsir).

For the reader, the verse is the surah's pivot from the argument against the People of the Book to the story of the chosen family — and the two are connected. The People of the Book claimed the election; the Qur'an grants the election and names it — Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, the family of 'Imran. The election is not a blanket property of a people; it is the elevation of particular houses for the carrying of the message, and the message is the same message the surah has been preaching: Tawhid, the following of the messenger, and the submission. The story that follows (3:35–64) is the story of the house of 'Imran — the mother of Maryam, Maryam herself, Zachariah, Yahya, and 'Isa — and the surah will use the story to show the People of the Book that the message of their own chosen families is the message of the Messenger, and that the disputes over 'Isa are disputes over the very sign of the chosen lineage.

## Verse 34

Verse 34 says: *They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.*

In simple terms, the verse links the chosen families: they are a single line of descent — one from the other — and Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

"They are descendants of one another" — *dhurriyyatan ba'duha min ba'd* — offspring, one from another, a seed-line in which each is the descendant of the previous. Ibn Kathir: "Offspring, one of the other." The chosen families are not four disconnected names; they are one continuous chain: the message is carried by descent of spirit and blood from Adam to Noah to Abraham to 'Imran — and the chain continues through the families to the prophets within them and ultimately to the Messenger from the offspring of Abraham. Al-Jalalayn: the election was making prophethood reside in them and their progeny — the continuity is the point. The chosen are a lineage, not a list.

The meaning is double: biological and spiritual. Biologically, the families are one line — the prophets are blood-related, and 'Isa is from the offspring of Ibrahim (Ibn Kathir). Spiritually, the line is the continuity of the message: the same Tawhid preached by Adam (in his repentance and recovery), by Noah (in the call against idolatry), by Abraham (in the call of the pure creed), and by the family of 'Imran (in the story of Maryam and 'Isa). The descent is the descent of the *din* — the way — as much as of the blood.

"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — *Sami' 'Alim* — All-Hearing, All-Knowing. The verse's attribute-closing does the work of the whole passage: the One who chose the chain is the One who heard the prayers of the chosen — the prayer of the wife of 'Imran in 3:35, the prayer of Zachariah in 3:38 — and the One who knows the hearts, the intentions, the circumstances of each link. Ibn Kathir and the classical readings place the names at the point where they carry the narrative: the All-Hearing hears the du'a of the barren woman and the aging prophet; the All-Knowing knows the child in the womb, the name, the future, the refuges, and the provisions. The verse is the bridge from the doctrine of election to the story of the family of 'Imran — and the two names are the guarantee that the story is heard and known by the One who tells it.

For the reader, the verse teaches the continuity of revelation and the continuity of the chosen. The religion is not a series of disconnected events; it is one line — the line of the prophets — and the believer who reads the surah is being given the family tree of the message: Adam, Noah, Abraham, 'Imran — and within the family of 'Imran, Maryam and 'Isa — and beyond 'Imran, through Abraham's line, to Muhammad. The "one from another" also answers the People of the Book's separatism: the chosen families are not separate communities with separate religions; they are one line of the same din, and the message of the final Messenger is the continuation of the family's message. The verse's last two names remind the reader that the One who chose the line is the One who heard every prayer in it and knows every heart in it — the story that follows is a story of prayers heard and knowledge revealed.

There is a further point in the connection between the two halves of the verse. The "descendants of one another" is not only a statement about the past — Adam to Noah to Abraham to 'Imran — but a structure of the surah's whole argument. The People of the Book grounded their claims in their descent: they were the children of Abraham, the heirs of the chosen. The Qur'an grants the descent and immediately turns it into a criterion rather than a privilege: the descent is a line of transmission — the one message, the one din, the one submission — and the value of a descendant is measured by the carrying forward of what the line carried. Al-Jalalayn's reading makes the criterion explicit: the election was "making prophethood reside in them and their progeny" — the chosen are chosen to transmit, and the transmission is the test. The Qur'an is therefore not disputing the People of the Book's genealogy; it is showing them what the genealogy was for. And the attribute that closes the verse — the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing — is the attribute of the One who can distinguish the true transmission from the false claim: the descent that carries the message is honored; the descent that rejects the messenger has the genealogy but not the election.

There is also a balance worth seeing in the four names. Adam is the individual chosen and tested and sent down; Noah is the chosen one of the era of the flood, the first messenger after the idolatry of the peoples; the family of Abraham is the chosen household of the father of the prophets, the one who purified the creed and the one from whose line the final Prophet comes; and the family of 'Imran is the chosen household of the mother of Maryam and the grandfather of 'Isa — the household whose story the surah is about to tell. The four names span the whole history of the guidance and each one leads to the next: Adam's fall and recovery, Noah's call and deliverance, Abraham's creed and lineage, 'Imran's family and the Messiah — and then, in the verses that follow, the Messiah's story itself. The reader who tracks the line is following the surah's own movement from the first chapter of revelation to the story of Maryam, 'Isa, and the dispute with the Christians — which is precisely the argument the surah is building: the chosen lineage ends in the family the Christians dispute about, and the dispute is about what the lineage means.

## Verse 35

Verse 35 says: *˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”*

In simple terms, the verse begins the story of the family of 'Imran: the wife of 'Imran, when she conceived, dedicated the child in her womb entirely to Allah's service and asked her Lord to accept it — for He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

The verse introduces the story that the surah uses to answer the Christians of Najran: the story of Maryam and 'Isa — the house of 'Imran — as the sign of the chosen lineage and the truth about the Messiah. The woman is the mother of Maryam, and Ibn Kathir gives her name: Hannah bint Faqudh. Al-Jalalayn: Hanna said this after she had reached old age and longed for a child, supplicated to Allah, and sensed she was carrying a child.

"when the wife of 'Imrân said" — the verse opens with the remembered moment: *idh qalat* — when she said. The narrative's frame is the surah's own address: remember, O Prophet, when she said. The story is told not as an ancient anecdote but as a sign for the present dispute. Ibn Kathir's account: "Hannah could not have children, and one day, she saw a bird feeding its chick. She wished she could have children and supplicated to Allah to grant her offspring. Allah accepted her supplication, and when her husband slept with her, she became pregnant." The longing of a barren woman who watches a bird feed its young, the supplication, the acceptance, and the vow — the story opens at the level of the ordinary human desire for children and moves to the extraordinary.

"I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service" — *nadhartu laka ma fi batni muharraran* — I have vowed to You what is in my womb, as *muharrar* — dedicated, consecrated, set free from the distractions of the world for the service of Your Holy House in Jerusalem. The *nadhr* — the vow — is a serious religious act: the mother pledged the child before it was born to the exclusive service of the Temple. Ibn Kathir: "She vowed to make her child concentrate on worship and serving Bayt al-Maqdis, when she became aware that she was pregnant." Al-Jalalayn: "one liberated and delivered from the distractions of this world, for the service of Your Holy House in Jerusalem." The child was dedicated to the service of Allah before it drew its first breath — the story begins with a parent's total dedication of the unborn child to Allah.

"so accept it from me" — *fa-taqabbal minni* — so accept it from me. The request of acceptance follows the vow: the dedication is the human act; the acceptance is the divine. Al-Jalalayn notes: 'Imran died while she was still pregnant — the mother of Maryam faced the dedication alone, as a widow, carrying the child she had pledged.

"You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — *innaka anta al-Sami' al-'Alim* — the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing: the two names from 3:34 now perform their narrative function. Ibn Kathir: "meaning, You hear my supplication and You know my intention." The All-Hearing hears the prayer of the barren woman; the All-Knowing knows the intention behind the vow. The verse's ending is the assurance that the prayer's two halves — the hearing and the knowing — are both with Allah.

For the reader, the verse is the model of dedication: the parent who offers the child to Allah before the child's birth, with the request of acceptance. The Qur'an does not present this as a ritual to be copied literally — the dedication of a child to lifelong temple service belongs to the law of the earlier people — but as the spirit of the commitment: the parent's life is not the parent's own; the child is a trust; and the deepest word a parent can speak over the unborn is the word of dedication. And the verse's last line is the reader's own frame: the All-Hearing hears; the All-Knowing knows — and what is heard and known can be accepted. The mother asked the accept; the next verse answers.
