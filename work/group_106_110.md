## Verse 106

Verse 106 says: *"If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?"*

In simple terms, the verse addresses the objection that the Jews raised against the changing of divine rulings: how can Allah command one thing and then command another? The Qur'an's answer is the verse of abrogation: if We abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. And the question at the end carries the whole argument: do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?

The background is recorded in the classical tafsir. The Jews — as Ibn Kathir explains, citing Ibn Jarir (al-Tabari) — denied that the rulings of the Torah could be abrogated, and they used that denial as the basis for rejecting Jesus and Muhammad, because both came with rulings that changed some provisions of the Torah. The Qur'an's answer is that the authority belongs to the One who gave the command: He who owns the heavens and the earth has the right to legislate, to change, to postpone, and to replace as He wills. Ibn Abbas explained the verse's first word as "whatever verse We abrogate"; Mujahid (via Ibn Jurayj) explained it as "whatever ruling We repeal, making the lawful unlawful and the unlawful lawful"; and the commentators note that abrogation in the classical framework concerns commandments, prohibitions, and permissions — the engaged law of the community, not the truth of the divine message itself.

The verse's second clause — "or cause it to be forgotten" — was read in two ways by the early scholars. One reading holds that it refers to verses whose recitation was lifted and whose rulings were replaced; another holds that it refers to postponement — *nunsi'ha* — the delaying of a ruling to a later time. Some early authorities (including a report from Ibn Abbas) understood "cause it to be forgotten" in the sense of leaving the verse in place without change. The difference between the readings is less important than the point they share: the divine law is not a frozen artifact; it is revealed in the world of human capacity and circumstance, and the One who revealed it knows what is better for His servants at every stage.

"We replace it with a better or similar one" — "better," the commentators explain, is better for the people addressed: more beneficial, easier to bear, more suited to their state. Qatadah said the replacement is an ayah that is "more facilitating, permitting, commanding, or prohibiting." The verse is thus not a statement of arbitrariness; it is a statement of wisdom at work in history: the legislation is progressive, and the progression is for the servants, not for the pleasure of the Lawgiver — though both are true, since whatever Allah does for His servants is what He wills.

In modern life, the verse is often the subject of misunderstanding, so it is worth stating clearly what it does not say. It does not say that what Allah once commanded was evil and was replaced by good; everything Allah commands is good — the abrogated command was good for its time, and the replacement is good for its time. And it does not say that the truth of the revelation changes: the doctrine of abrogation in the classical exegetical tradition concerns the legal rulings that were addressed to communities in different circumstances. The verse's deeper lesson is the humility of the creature before the authority of the Creator: the objection "how can Allah change His command?" is answered with "do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?"

## Verse 107

Verse 107 says: *"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?"*

In simple terms, the verse continues the argument of the previous one with two statements: the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs only to Allah; and you — the people of the Book, the objectors, and every creature — have no guardian or helper besides Allah. The two statements are the ground of the whole dispute: the One who owns everything may decree everything, and no one has a protector other than Him.

The first statement establishes the authority. The word "kingdom" — *mulk* — is not a metaphor; it is the actual ownership of everything that exists. The heavens and the earth are not a property of nature that operates on its own; they are a kingdom administered by its Owner. Ibn Jarir (al-Tabari), as quoted by Ibn Kathir, explains the verse precisely in this way: the One who owns the heavens and the earth has the authority to command whatever He wills, to forbid whatever He wills, to abrogate whatever He wills, and to uphold whatever He wills. The Jews' objection — that the Torah does not change — was an objection to the Owner's right over His own property.

The second statement addresses the objectors' sense of security: "you have no guardian or helper besides Allah." This is one of the Qur'an's most repeated warnings, and it is directed at a specific illusion. The People of the Book believed that their relationship with Allah was a kind of protection — that their ancestry, their scripture, their covenant made them independent of the demands of the present messenger. The verse says: you have no guardian besides Allah. Neither the covenant nor the ancestry nor the scripture protects anyone against the commands of the Owner; there is no second authority to appeal to over the authority of the One who owns the kingdom.

The two statements together are also the Qur'an's answer to the sense of autonomy that characterizes every human community. The person who believes that he can negotiate the terms of his relation to God — that he can keep the convenient parts and dismiss the rest, as the surah has already described (2:85) — is living in the illusion that he has some power over the Owner. The verse removes the illusion: the kingdom is not shared; and the creature has no protector other than the One he has been refusing.

In modern life, the verse is a radical statement of the dependence of everything. The person who believes that his position, his community, or his achievements secure him — as the Jews believed their covenant secured them — is building on the ground the verse levels. The believer's security is not in his standing but in the standing of the One he stands with: the Owner of the kingdom, the only guardian, the only helper.

## Verse 108

Verse 108 says: *"Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way."*

In simple terms, the verse now turns to the believers with a warning: do you intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? The reference is to the demands of the Children of Israel — the demand to see Allah plainly, the demand for signs, the demand that the prophet prove himself. The Qur'an warns the believers against repeating the pattern, and closes with the law: whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way.

The "as Moses was asked before" refers to the history the surah has already narrated: the Children of Israel asked to see Allah, and they were taken by the thunderbolt (2:55); the Children of Israel asked Moses for all manner of specifics in the story of the cow (2:67-71). The verse is not condemning questions that seek understanding; it is condemning the questions that test the prophet, that demand proof instead of submission, that put the prophecy on trial. The classical commentators discuss in this context the Jews' earlier request of Moses: "Show us Allah plainly" (4:153) — a demand the Qur'an elsewhere says the People of the Book had made of Moses. The pattern the verse warns against is the demand for a sign that would replace faith, the asking that carries the spirit of refusal.

The verse's warning to the believers is remarkable in that it is addressed to them directly, not to the hostile outsiders. The believers could fall into the same trap: they could imitate the People of the Book in their interrogations, in their testing of the prophet, in their demands — and in doing so, they would have traded belief for disbelief. The "trade" is the Qur'an's word for every abandonment of faith, and the verse says the one who makes it has strayed from the Right Way — he has left the path, not merely stumbled.

The verse also establishes a principle of the prophetic relationship: the prophet is not a performer who must constantly demonstrate his credentials. The believer receives the message and asks about its meaning; the disbeliever demands the prophet prove the message. The two questions look similar; the spirit behind them is opposite. The Qur'an's command to the believers is to ask with the intention of understanding (the verse's "Unzurna" from the previous passage), not with the intention of testing.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the culture of evidence-seeking as a replacement for commitment. In every field—religion, science, public life — there are questions that seek knowledge and questions that postpone the decision, and the person who always demands more proof may be using the demand to avoid the step. The verse's warning is gentle but firm: do not let the demand for signs become the replacement for faith; the person who turns the prophetic relationship into an interrogation has already left the path he claims to be investigating.

## Verse 109

Verse 109 says: *"Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything."*

In simple terms, the verse names the motive and sets the response. The motive: many among the People of the Book wish they could turn the believers back to disbelief, out of envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. The response: pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. The verse is one of the most important statements of the Islamic approach to hostility in the period before the command of armed struggle.

The motive is named with precision. It is not ignorance: "after the truth has been made clear to them." It is not a difference of opinion: it is envy — the anger at the good that has been given to someone else. The commentators explain that the envy is "from their own selves" — it was not provoked by anything the believers did but arose spontaneously within them, as the previous verses described with the resentment at the gift of prophecy being given to an Arab. The object of their wish is the most extreme: they wish to turn the believers from belief to disbelief. The verse is not describing an argument they had; it is describing the deepest desire of their hearts, and the Qur'an names it so that the believers are not naive about the people they deal with.

The response is the instruction. "Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision." The believers are commanded to pardon — to let the offense go — and to bear with — to be patient with the hostility and the provocation. The "decision" that Allah would deliver is understood by the classical commentators as the command concerning fighting; Ibn Kathir records that Abu al-'Aliyah, al-Rabi' bin Anas, Qatadah, and al-Suddi said this verse was later abrogated by the "verse of the sword" (9:5 and 9:29). In the period of the verse, the command was patience: the Muslims were to absorb the provocation without retaliation, to forgive, and to wait — not because the hostility was insignificant, but because the time had not come, and the believers were being trained in the strength that forgives rather than the anger that retaliates.

The verse is thus a historical document of the Islamic approach to a hostile religious minority in the period of weakness, and it is also a permanent moral teaching: the believer's first response to the provocation of the envious is not rage but self-control. The "Most Capable" at the end is the assurance: the One who will deliver the decision is the One who sees the hate and the envy, and He is fully capable of everything — including the protection of those who are being provoked while they are still commanded to be patient.

In modern life, the verse teaches the response to those who wish ill upon the community — the envious, the hostile, the provocateurs. It does not teach the believer to be naive; it names the motive of the others accurately. It teaches that the response to envy is not the surrender of the truth but the forgiveness of the persons — and the knowledge that the decision belongs to Allah, who sees the envy as clearly as the believers see it, and who will deliver it at the time He has chosen.

## Verse 110

Verse 110 says: *"Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the command of patience is the command of action: establish prayer, pay alms-tax. And then the guarantee: whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will certainly find its reward with Allah. The believer who is commanded to be patient in the face of envy is also commanded to be active in the face of the world.

The verse is the answer to a subtle danger. The believers had just been told of the envy of the People of the Book and their wish to turn them from faith. The temptation in such a situation is to become preoccupied with the enemies — to build the religion as a reaction to their hostility, to let the provocation set the agenda. The verse redirects: the believer's life is not defined by the envious; it is defined by the prayer, the alms, and the good that he sends ahead. The response to those who wish you harm is not to orbit their wishes but to build the life that Allah has commanded.

The two commands are the two pillars of the individual and social religion: prayer is the personal connection to the Lord, established at its times and in its form; alms are the social connection to the people, the portion of the wealth that the poor have a right to. The Qur'an joins them constantly — prayer without alms is a religion that stops at the individual; alms without prayer is an ethic without a foundation (the surah has already commanded both together in 2:43 and 2:83). The verse before us belongs to the crisis period of the community, and it is the Qur'an's instruction for the crisis: the believer is not to neglect the prayer and the poor while watching the enemies.

"Whatever good you send forth for yourselves" — the phrase is the Qur'an's image of the afterlife as a banking: what the person sends ahead is what he will find. The good is "for yourselves" — the charity is not a loss but a deposit; the prayer is not a cost but a provision. The verse's guarantee is absolute: "you will certainly find its reward with Allah." Not with the people, not in the reputation, not in the accounting of this world — with Allah, in the place where nothing is lost and nothing is forgotten. The closing "Allah is All-Seeing of what you do" is the ground of the guarantee: the One who sees the deeds knows the deeds, and nothing is omitted from the account.

In modern life, the verse is the cure for the person whose life is organized by the hostility of others. The believer who has been provoked, mocked, envied, and tempted to build his identity in reaction is commanded instead to pray and give, and to send ahead. The enemies may see the prayer and the alms and despise them; the guarantee is not with the enemies. The verse's advice is the same for every person in every conflict: do not let the unjust define your agenda; let the good you send ahead define your life.
