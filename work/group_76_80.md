## Verse 76

Verse 76 says: *"When they meet the believers they say, "We believe." But in private they say ˹to each other˺, "Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand?""*

In simple terms, the verse describes the duplicity of a group among the People of the Book. When they meet the believers, they declare belief. But in private, they ask one another with alarm: will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand? The Qur'an exposes the lifestyle of hypocrisy: one face in public, another in private.

The verses can be read as describing a specific situation in Madinah. Some Jewish scholars had accepted Islam, or were close to it, and they related to the Muslims the things they knew from their own scripture — including the descriptions of the Prophet ﷺ and the signs of his coming. Their fellows then rebuked them: why are you disclosing to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you? Now that they know it, they will use it as argument against you on the Day of Judgment. The question "Do you not understand?" is a rebuke of the group's failure to see the consequence of what they were sharing.

The irony is pointed. The very thing the scholars had — knowledge of the Book and the prophecy of the coming Messenger — was the thing they were being told to hide. The Qur'an describes the same concealment elsewhere (2:146, 2:159-160, 3:71): a people who know the truth and suppress it. Now, in this verse, they are shown in their private consultations, debating whether to continue sharing. The verse is a portrait of a community that lives in two worlds: the public world where they claim belief, and the private world where they plan concealment.

The phrase "so that they may use it against you before your Lord" reveals what the concealers feared. They were not afraid of the believers' arguments in this world; they were afraid of the evidence in the next. The knowledge that the Muslims possessed — the prophecies, the descriptions, the signs in their own scripture — was evidence that would testify against them on the Day of Judgment. The concealment was a strategy to avoid the evidence.

This is one of the Qur'an's most penetrating diagnoses of hypocrisy: the hypocrite manages appearances in the public realm while his true intent operates in the private. The believer, by contrast, is told by the Prophet ﷺ to be the same inside and outside. The Qur'an will later name the same pattern in the hypocrites of Madinah, whose outward appearance and speech please the onlooker but who regard every cry of alarm as aimed against them (63:4). The verse before us is an early portrait of that same spiritual condition.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the danger of the double life — the person who claims belief publicly while concealing the evidence of its obligations privately; the person who behaves differently before the believers and before his own company. The Qur'an's answer is integrity: the believer is to be one person, the same in public and private, and the test of faith is not the public appearance but the private consultation. The verse also teaches the believer to be aware of the difference between profession and truth: "We believe" can be a statement of fact, an expression of hope, or a performance — and only Allah sees which.

## Verse 77

Verse 77 says: *"Do they not know that Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal?"*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the description of the private consultation is the answer: Allah knows the concealment and the revelation. The people thought their hypocrisy was safe in the privacy of the meeting; the Qur'an says the One who knows both is Allah. The concealment has no chamber that Allah cannot see.

The verse is a single, devastating sentence. The structure is deliberate: "Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal." The pair — concealment and revelation — covers the whole of human activity: what is hidden and what is shown. The Qur'an is saying there is no third category. Whatever a person does, whether in the public assembly or the private consultation, it falls into one of these two — and Allah knows both. The People of the Book could hide their scheming from the believers; they could not hide it from Allah.

The theological principle is one of the Qur'an's foundational teachings. Allah's knowledge is not partial or general; it is particular and absolute. He knows what is in the heavens and the earth, the secret and the more secret: "Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, Allah knows it" (3:29); and He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal (40:19). The knowledge of Allah is not deduced from the evidence; it is immediate, without intermediaries, complete. The verse's question, "Do they not know?", is itself the judgment: the only explanation for their conduct is that they have forgotten, or never understood, the One who sees them.

This is the same spiritual failure that the Qur'an identifies throughout the story of the Children of Israel: they knew the truth and their knowledge did not change them. Here, the pattern is even more direct: they know that Allah sees their secret. And they still behave as though the knowledge does not matter. The verse is a description of the state in which knowledge of Allah is present in the mind but absent in the conduct — the state the Qur'an calls heedlessness (*ghaflah*) and the condition of a heart that is hardened.

The lesson for the believer is about the reality of being seen. The person who is tempted to do wrong in secret — the wrong that no one will see — is told that the One who sees everything is watching, and that the "secret" was never secret. The beauty of the same truth is the other side: for the believer who fears that Allah has abandoned him in an unseen struggle, the verse is a comfort — Allah sees the struggle, the intention, the inner state that no one else can see, and the reward for that hidden obedience is His knowledge of it. The same divine knowledge that makes concealment impossible makes sincerity visible.

In modern life, the verse is a challenge to the split between private and public morality, the idea that what is done in the private realm is no one's business. The Qur'an says the private is observed: the hidden conversation, the concealed motive, the secret transaction. And the believer is called to live so that the private and the public are one — because they are both observed by the One who knows.

## Verse 78

Verse 78 says: *"And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate."*

In simple terms, the verse now distinguishes between the learned and the unlettered among the People of the Book. There is a group among them that does not know the Scripture at all — the description is stark: they know nothing about it except lies. What they have are *amānī* — fantasies, wishes, and assumptions — and nothing but speculation.

The word *ummīyūn* is critical. It refers to those who are unlettered, without knowledge of writing and scripture. The classical commentators explain it in the context: these were the common people among the Jews and Christians who did not know the actual contents of the Torah or the Gospels. They had no direct knowledge of the Book; their religion was inherited, superficial, and unexamined. What they had was *amānī* — a term that carries the double sense of "desires" and "claims." Ibn Abbas (as reported through al-Dahhak) explained the word as "false statements they utter" — things they say without grounding. They were not necessarily the intentional distorters of verse 79; they simply did not know the Scripture, and what they carried instead was a mixture of wishful claims and inherited speculation.

The phrase "they ˹wishfully˺ speculate" — *wa hum yakhruṣūn* — is the final and most important word. *Yakhruṣūn* describes the act of guessing, of making estimates without foundation — which is why the supplied translation renders it "wishfully speculate." The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere, describing those who follow nothing but conjecture and what their own souls desire — conjecture that avails nothing against the truth (53:23, 53:28). The unlettered people were not indifferent to religion; they were *confident* about religion — certain in their identity and their destiny — but their certainty was built on wishes and assumptions, not knowledge.

The Qur'an thus draws a double portrait of the People of the Book: the learned corrupters, who know the truth and hide or distort it (verses 75-77), and the unlearned who do not know the Scripture at all and are confident on the basis of their wishes (this verse). Between the two, the people had built a religion that was either falsified at the top or fantasized at the bottom. The same structure appears in every community that has drifted from its scripture: elites who distort, masses who inherit, and a religion that in both cases has lost its authority.

In modern life, the verse is a powerful critique of inherited religion without knowledge. Millions of people hold to a religious identity without knowing the content of their revealed Book — without reading it, asking about it, or examining their claims. They believe what they are told, desire what they are promised, and are confident without evidence. The difference between the illiterate of the verse and the modern believer is the accessibility of the Book: the Torah is everywhere, the Gospels are everywhere, the Qur'an is everywhere — and no one can say today that he "knows nothing about the Scripture," because the ignorance is chosen rather than inevitable. The verse is an invitation to the believer: do not be among those whose religion is wishful thinking; be among those who read, understand, and live the revelation.

## Verse 79

Verse 79 says: *"So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, "This is from Allah"—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned."*

In simple terms, the verse announces the most severe condemnation of religious corruption: those who distort the Scripture with their own hands, then attribute it to Allah, seeking a fleeting gain. The verse is a double woe: woe for what they have written, and woe for what they have earned. The scholars describe this as a reference to those among the Jews who fabricated portions of scripture in order to profit from them — who wrote a "book" themselves, attributed it to Allah, and traded it for worldly gain.

The verse's target must be understood precisely. The Qur'an was not describing a wholesale replacement of the divine scripture; the classical commentators identify the reference as a specific group among the Jews who, in their time, manufactured material and sold it, or who altered the meanings and claimed the authority of revelation. Ibn Abbas and al-Dahhak, as recorded in the tafsir, explain the verse as referring to the scholars of the Children of Israel who took worldly gain by writing books with their hands and saying "this is from Allah." The crime is the combination of three acts: distortion or forgery, false attribution, and trade. The forgery is the use of writing to create a false document; the false attribution is the claim that the document is divine; and the trade is the sale of the document for the gain of the world. Any one of these is serious; all three together are the corruption of the very source of truth.

The gain is called "fleeting" — and in the Qur'an's parallel passages it is called "a paltry price" (2:41, 3:187, 5:44, 9:9). The gain is small not because the profit is small but because the gain in the next world is infinitely greater, and the traders have lost the eternal to obtain the momentary. A few coins, a little authority, a small comfort — this is what the verse says they have sold the truth for. And the woe is doubled: for the falsehood they wrote, and for the profit they earned. Both will testify against them.

The deeper horror of the crime is that it attacks the revelation itself. The people who read the corrupted text believe they are reading the word of God. The forgery occupies the place of the truth, the false attribution gives it authority, and the trade gives it a motive. The result is that generations are misled, and the corruption of one text corrupts the religion of thousands. This is why the Qur'an reserves its harshest language for those who lead others astray while claiming divine authority.

In modern life, the verse is astonishingly contemporary. When religious authorities forge texts, fabricate sayings, invent traditions, or manufacture "scripture" for their own profit — and then sell the forgery through books, courses, and platforms for worldly gain — they are doing exactly what the verse describes. The believer is called to a fierce loyalty to the original; to verify, to examine, to refuse the manufactured authority; and to be vigilant against the tendency to create a religion of our own design. And the religious leader is warned with the verse's double woe: the falsehood will be written against him, and the profit will be collected from him.

## Verse 80

Verse 80 says: *"˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, "The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days." Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?""*

In simple terms, the verse now addresses the claim of some Jews that the Fire will not touch them except for a number of days. The claim was the basis of their confidence: they believed that whatever punishment they faced would be temporary and limited, and that their status as "the chosen people" would finally save them. The Qur'an's answer is the question — have you taken a pledge from Allah? — and the accusation: you are saying about Allah what you do not know.

The claim "the Fire will not touch us except for a number of days" was a theological position among the Jews of Madinah and their predecessors. In its historical form, the idea may have been connected to the golden calf: the report that the punishment for the calf-worshippers would last a fixed term, such as the forty days associated with that episode, or a belief that the punishment of the Children of Israel would be limited to a bounded period of the covenant's violation. Some commentators connect the claim to the idea of a limited term of atonement for the sin of the calf. The essential point of the verse is not the precise shape of the claim but its logic: the people had arrived at a comfortable conclusion — the punishment is limited and we will be saved — without any divine warrant for it.

The Qur'an's response is a masterclass in the argument against presumption. The question "Have you taken a pledge from Allah?" is devastating: if you have a pledge, produce it; if you do not, then your confidence is invention. The word behind "pledge" is *'ahd* — a covenant, a binding promise. The question is designed to distinguish between a claim based on revelation and a claim based on hope. In the Qur'anic framework, no one may make a claim about Allah — about who enters Paradise, who escapes the Fire, what is permissible and what is forbidden — without the authority of revelation. The claim about the limited duration of the punishment was not revealed; it was invented. And the verse names the sin precisely: "or are you saying about Allah what you do not know?"

This is the same human pattern the whole section has shown: building religious confidence on wishes instead of knowledge. The learned among them had corrupted the Scripture; the illiterate had only wishful speculation; and now, in this verse, a group of them claims a pledge from Allah that He never gave them. The root of the claim is the same in all cases: the desire to be saved without the conditions of salvation, to have the benefit of religion without the burden of it, to know the outcome without the submission.

The theological principle is universal: anyone — Muslim, Jew, Christian, or other — who claims a guarantee of salvation without the conditions of faith and deed is making the same error. The Qur'an's own response to a similar claim among the groups was: "The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true'" (2:111). The proof is required.

In modern life, the verse is a challenge to the religion of entitlement. The person who says "God is merciful, so I will be fine" without the mercy being reflected in faith and obedience has taken these claimants' position; the person who says "I am a Muslim, so I am saved" while rejecting the obligations of Islam is making the same presumption. The verse is not a denial of the mercy of Allah — the Qur'an is full of it — but a correction of the abuse of that mercy. History is full of communities that believed "the Fire will not touch us" and were still held to account. And Allah never breaks His word — which means if the claim of the pledge is not based on His promise, the claim is not His; it is the speaker's own invention.
