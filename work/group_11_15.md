## Verse 11

Verse 11 says: *Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them —they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the historical pattern: the fate of those who reject the signs is the fate of the people of Pharaoh and of the nations before them — they rejected the signs, Allah seized them for their sins, and Allah is severe in punishment.

The Arabic word at the opening is *ka-da'b* — "like the custom, the practice, the behavior, the habit." Ibn Kathir collects the classical readings: Ad-Dahhak reported that Ibn 'Abbas said the meaning is "like the behavior of the people of Fir'awn"; the same interpretation came from 'Ikrimah, Mujahid, Abu Malik, and Ad-Dahhak. Other scholars read it as "like the practice, conduct, likeness of the people of Fir'awn." All the readings agree that *da'b* is practice, behavior, tradition, and habit. The verse is therefore not merely a comparison of punishment; it is a comparison of behavior — the disbelievers addressed in this surah behave the way Pharaoh's people behaved, and therefore they will end the way Pharaoh's people ended.

"they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins" — the causal chain is stated with perfect clarity: *kadhdhabu bi ayatina* — they denied, called lies, rejected Our signs — *fa-akhadhahum Allah bi dhunubihim* — so Allah seized them, took them, for their sins. Al-Jalalayn: "They denied Our signs; God seized them — He destroyed them — for their sins; this statement explains the previous one." The sequence is the law of divine dealing with nations: the signs are sent, the signs are clear, the denial is chosen, and the seizure follows. The word "seized" (*akhadha*) is exactly the word of power: a hand that takes, that grasps, that does not miss. And what is seized is for "their sins" — *bi dhunubihim* — not for accidents of history, not for tribal bad luck, but for the sins they committed, the rejection itself being the conclusive sin.

The people of Pharaoh are the standard example because their story is the most complete in the Qur'an: the signs of Musa before them — the staff, the hand, the plagues, the sea — were witnessed by Pharaoh and his court, and they refused. The nations before them — 'Ad, Thamud, and others, as Al-Jalalayn enumerates — rejected their prophets. The point of the verse is that this is not a one-off judgment but a system: "the same end the people of Fir'awn and the previous nations met, those who rejected the Messengers, the ayat, and proofs of Allah that they were sent with" (Ibn Kathir). The disbelievers, says Ibn Kathir, will not benefit from their wealth or offspring (3:10); rather, they will perish and be punished.

"And Allah is severe in punishment" — *shadid al-'iqab* — severe, intense, extreme in punishment. Ibn Kathir: "His punishment is severe and His torment is painful. None can escape Allah's grasp, nor does anything escape His knowledge. Allah does what He wills and prevails over all things; it is He to Whom everything is humbled, and there is no deity worthy of worship, nor any Lord except Him." The name is a balance: the same surah opens with the mercy of the revealer (3:3–4) and the mercy of the Giver (3:8); the severity of punishment stands beside the mercy as the guarantee that the evil is not indifferent — that the One who gives the signs also enforces them.

For the reader, the verse establishes a habit of reading history. The Qur'an does not read the past as a collection of curiosities; it reads the past as a collection of signs — patterns of cause and effect in the moral order of the world. The people of Pharaoh are not merely an ancient story; they are the archetype of the powerful who receive the clear signs and still refuse. Every passage of the surah — the Jews of Madinah who disputed the messenger, the Quraysh who met the Muslims at Badr, the Christians of Najran who argued about Jesus — is being read against this template. The lesson: no amount of ancient precedent (Pharaoh's splendor, his armies, his magicians, his claims to divinity) protected anyone; and no amount of current wealth will protect anyone either (3:10). The wages of the rejection of clear signs are the seizure of the one who rejects them.

## Verse 12

Verse 12 says: *˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the disbelievers, “Soon you will be overpowered and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!”*

In simple terms, the verse commands the announcement of the coming defeat: tell the disbelievers that soon they will be overcome and then gathered to Hell — and what an evil place to rest!

The verse is the first of the surah's direct prophecies of events that actually happened. *Satughlabun* — "you will be overcome, you will be defeated" — is a statement of the future, and the surah itself records the fulfillment: the battle of Badr (3:13) and later the expulsion of the Jews of Madinah. *Tuhsharun* — "you will be gathered, mustered" — the same gathering of 3:9, now applied to the disbelievers: gathered to Hell. The two clauses give the double fate: the defeat in this world and the gathering to Hell in the Hereafter.

Ibn Kathir gives the historical setting. Muhammad bin Ishaq recorded that 'Asim bin 'Umar bin Qatadah said that when the Messenger of Allah gained victory at the battle of Badr and went back to Madinah, he gathered the Jews in the marketplace of Bani Qaynuqa' and made the call to them — and it was about them that this ayah was revealed. Al-Jalalayn records the occasion: when the Prophet enjoined the Jews to enter Islam after his return from Badr, they said, "Do not fool yourself just because you killed a few men of Quraysh, inexperienced and knowing nothing about fighting," whereupon this was revealed. The verse is thus a reply to the arrogance of a people who had been watching the newly established Muslim community, who had the scriptures describing the coming prophet, and who still counted the Muslims as a temporary novelty. The reply is the prophecy: you will be defeated in this world (through being killed, taken captive, or made to pay the jizya, as actually happened) and gathered to Hell in the Hereafter.

"—what an evil place to rest!" — *bi'sa al-mihad* — "what an evil resting place, what a bad bed, what a miserable cradle." The word *mihad* (bed, resting place, cradle) is chosen with terrible irony. The disbelievers in 3:10 had wealth and children, the goods of this world as their bed of comfort; here they are told that their final bed, the place where they will rest, is Hell — and it is an evil bed. Al-Jalalayn: "an evil cradling! an evil resting place." The same phrase returns at the end of the surah in 3:197 ("what an evil place to rest"), closing the surah's treatment of the prosperity of the disbelievers: the brief enjoyment, then the evil bed.

The verse is also an image of the surah's method. The Qur'an does not merely argue against disbelief; it announces the outcomes of the argument in history. The announcement to the disbelievers — "soon you will be overpowered" — is a prophetic test: if the announcement was true, it was a sign; if it had failed, it would have destroyed the Prophet. It did not fail. Badr came within months, and the Jews of Bani Qaynuqa' were expelled. The reader is being shown that the Qur'an's news is verifiable: the victory promised in the surah to the cause of truth is the same victory that the surah's own narrative of Badr records.

For the believer, the verse is a lesson in the relationship between truth and power. The disbelievers' confidence rested on counts — the numbers of the Quraysh at Badr, the experience of the Jews in war, the wealth (3:10) and the armies (3:11). The verse announces the reversal: the power that counts is the power that decides the outcome, and it is not the count. And the believer draws the comfort: the same Allah who told the Prophet to announce the defeat of the deniers is the One who will accomplish it. The word "soon" is the word of the believer's hope as much as the disbeliever's warning — not long, not far, sooner than the deniers suppose.

## Verse 13

Verse 13 says: *Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle— one fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial. The believers saw their enemy twice their number. But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills. Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight.*

In simple terms, the verse points to the battle of Badr as a sign: two armies met — one fighting for Allah's cause, the other in denial; the believers could see the enemy was twice their number; yet Allah supported with His victory whom He wills — and this is a lesson for people of insight.

The verse is the historical demonstration of the prophecy of 3:12. Ibn Kathir: the two armies are the two camps that met in combat at Badr, one fighting in the cause of Allah — the Muslims — and the other in disbelief — the idolaters of Quraysh at Badr. The occasion is also framed: "There has already been a sign for you, O Jews, who said what you said — you have an ayat, meaning proof, that Allah will make His religion prevail, award victory to His Messenger, make His Word apparent and His religion the highest" (Ibn Kathir). The verse is addressed to the Jews who mocked the Muslims' victory and to anyone who doubts that Allah's religion will prevail; the proof is the battle itself.

"The believers saw their enemy twice their number" — the Arabic *yarawnahum mithlayhim ra'y al-'ayn* — "they saw them twice their number, with the sight of the eye." There are two classical explanations, both reported by Ibn Kathir. The first: the idolaters thought that the Muslims were twice as many as they themselves were, for Allah made this illusion a factor in the victory that Islam had over them. This is the explanation of Ibn 'Abbas as given in Tanwir al-Miqbas: the believers were 313 men, and Abu Sufyan's army was 950 — and the disbelievers saw the believers as twice their own number. Al-Jalalayn gives the numbers likewise: the disbelievers numbering almost a thousand saw the Muslims as twice their number, more numerous than themselves, as the eye sees. The second explanation: the Muslims saw twice as many idolaters as themselves, and still Allah gave them victory. Ibn Mas'ud said: "When we looked at the disbelievers' forces, we found that they were twice as many as we were. When we looked at them again, we thought they did not have one man more than we had" — and he cited 8:44, where Allah says He showed them to the Muslims as few in their own eyes and made the Muslims appear few in the eyes of the enemy.

Both explanations belong to the same divine purpose, and Ibn Kathir states it precisely: "When the two camps saw each other, the Muslims thought that the idolators were twice as many as they were, so that they would trust in Allah and seek His help. The idolators thought that the believers were twice as many as they were, so that they would feel fear, horror, fright, and despair. When the two camps stood in lines and met in battle, Allah made each camp look smaller in the eyes of the other camp, so that they would be encouraged to fight each other — so that Allah might accomplish a matter already ordained (8:42): so that the truth and falsehood are distinguishable, and thus the word of faith prevails over disbelief and deviation, so that the believers prevail and the disbelievers are humiliated." The numbers were managed by Allah, both for the encouragement of the believers and the terror of the enemy. The sign is the visible working of the invisible decree.

"But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills" — *yu'ayyid bi nasrihi man yasha'* — He strengthens, supports, reinforces with His victory whom He wills. The victory is not the property of the larger army nor of the more experienced soldiers; it is the grant of Allah, and He grants it to whom He wills. The verse thus contains both the doctrine and the election: the power is Allah's, the choice is Allah's, and the believers are the recipients of the grant. The same point will be made at the end of the surah (3:160): "If Allah supports you, none can defeat you."

"Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight" — *la 'ibrah li uli al-absar* — a lesson, a clear instruction, for the possessors of vision, the people of insight, the ones who see through the event to the sign. The eye saw the numbers; the insight sees the decree. Ibn Kathir: "This should be an example for those who have intelligence and sound comprehension. They should contemplate about Allah's wisdom, decision..." The difference between the observer and the person of insight is precisely the reading of the battle: the observer saw a coincidence, a surprise, a lucky day; the person of insight saw the assistance of Allah and the wisdom of the management.

For the reader, Badr is the surah's central historical sign, and the verse is its summary. The lessons: (1) the cause of Allah is not measured by numbers — the believers were a third of the enemy's size and still won; (2) the victory belongs to Allah — the believers who attributed the result to their own strength were corrected later at Uhud (3:146–152), where the defeat taught the same lesson in reverse; (3) the sign is for the people of insight — the same event that hardened the deniers in doubt is the evidence of faith for those who see; (4) the wisdom is in the management — Allah arranged even the appearance of the armies so that faith would be tested, hearts purified, and the truth distinguished from falsehood. The reader who faces his own "Badr" — his own confrontation of a cause that is outnumbered, outfunded, and outmatched — is told: the support is from Allah, and the sign is in the outcome.

## Verse 14

Verse 14 says: *The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination.*

In simple terms, the verse names the attractions of the world that are made appealing to people — women, children, hoards of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land — and then sets them against the better: these are the pleasures of the worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination.

The verse opens with *zuyyina li al-nas* — "made attractive, beautified, adorned for people." The passive form is deliberate: the appeal is not entirely the people's own invention; it has been placed in the attractions themselves. Ibn Kathir explains: "Allah mentions the delights that He put in this life for people, such as women and children, and He started with women, because the test with them is more tempting." The list is not random; it is the catalogue of what the human heart, across all times, has been drawn to, and it is arranged deliberately: women, then children, then heaped-up treasures of gold and silver, then fine branded horses, then cattle, then tilled land. It is the whole grammar of worldly ambition — family, wealth, livestock, property — the things that men fight for, build dynasties over, and measure success by.

Ibn Kathir gives the classical treatment of each item, showing that the Qur'an's list is not a condemnation of the things but a diagnosis of the attachment. The desire for women and children is not evil in itself: "When one enjoys women for the purpose of having children and preserving his chastity, then he is encouraged to do so." He records the hadiths of the encouragement of marriage — "Marry the loving, fertile woman, for I will compare your numbers to the rest of the nations on the Day of Resurrection"; "This life is a delight, and the best of its delight is a righteous wife"; "I was made to like women and perfume, and the comfort of my eye is the prayer" — and the saying of 'A'ishah about the Prophet's love of women and horses. But: "The desire to have children is sometimes for the purpose of pride and boasting, and as such, is a temptation. When the purpose for having children is to reproduce and increase the Ummah of Muhammad with those who worship Allah alone without partners, then it is encouraged and praised." Likewise wealth: "The desire of wealth sometimes results out of arrogance, and the desire to dominate the weak and control the poor, and this conduct is prohibited. Sometimes, the want for more money is for the purpose of spending it on acts of worship, being kind to the family, the relatives, and spending on various acts of righteousness and obedience; this behavior is praised and encouraged." The famous hadith of the Prophet — "I did not leave behind me a test more tempting to men than women" — is cited as the reason women are mentioned first. The interest is in the heart's relation to the attraction, not in the attraction itself.

There is also a fine point in the word *qanatir al-muqantarah* — "heaped-up," "piled-up" treasures, the word *qintar* being a large unit of wealth. Ibn Kathir reports that the scholars differ on the amount of a qintar, all agreeing that it is a large amount; Abu Hurayrah said the qintar is twelve thousand uqiyah, each uqiyah better than all between the heavens and the earth (recorded by Ibn Jarir). The "heaped-up" repetition conveys the excess: not a coin or two, not a modest savings, but piles — the object of greed.

"These are the pleasures of this worldly life" — *mata' al-hayat al-dunya* — the enjoyment, the provision, the temporary goods of the present life. The word *mata'* is used in the Qur'an for provisions and enjoyments that are consumed and pass.

"but with Allah is the finest destination" — *wa Allah 'indahu husn al-ma'ab* — and with Allah is the beautiful return, the good final abode. The comparison is the point of the verse. The world's attractions are real and attractive; the verse does not pretend otherwise. The claim is that they are not the best, and the best is with Allah. The next verse (3:15) will name the better: Gardens with rivers, eternal life, purified spouses, and the pleasure of Allah. The structure is the Qur'an's characteristic method: it does not shame desire; it redirects it. The same appetite that runs after the world is not annihilated but reoriented toward the higher good, so that the love of woman, children, wealth, horses, cattle, and land becomes the love of the Garden, the eternal, the purified, and the pleasure of the Lord.

For the reader, the verse is one of the most practical passages in the surah. It diagnoses the two errors of the human relationship with the world: the error of asceticism that denies the goods are good (the verse begins by affirming they have been made appealing — the attraction is real and the goods are gifts), and the error of idolatry that treats them as the point of life (the verse ends by locating the finest destination elsewhere). The believer is invited to hold the goods as goods and not as God — to enjoy the wife and the child, to earn and spend, to ride and cultivate, and to do it all with the destination in view: the finest return is with Allah, and the proper use of the world's attractions is to travel toward it.

## Verse 15

Verse 15 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall I inform you of what is better than ˹all of˺ this? Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and pure spouses, along with Allah’s pleasure.” And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants,*

In simple terms, the verse proposes a comparison: shall I tell you of something better than all of this? For the mindful — the people of taqwa — there are Gardens with their Lord, rivers flowing beneath them, eternity, purified spouses, and — above all — Allah's pleasure. And Allah is All-Seeing of His servants.

The verse is the direct answer to 3:14. The catalogue of the world — women, children, hoards, horses, cattle, land — is met with a question: *qul a'unabbi'ukum bi khayrin min dhalikum* — "Say: shall I inform you of something better than that?" The interrogative, Al-Jalalayn notes, is affirmative in intent: it is a statement dressed as a question, and the answer is yes. Ibn Kathir frames the whole passage as "The True Value of This Earthly Life": after listing the delights of this life, Allah presents the better of the two, and the better is the reward of the mindful.

"For those mindful ˹of Allah˺" — the reward belongs to the *muttaqin*, the people of taqwa — the same word used in 2:2 for the audience of the Book, and the same word at the end of this surah (3:186, 200). Taqwa is the guarding of the self against what displeases Allah, the God-conscious awareness that orders the heart.

"Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever" — the description is the Qur'an's recurring image of Paradise: *jannat tajri min tahtiha al-anhar* — Gardens beneath which rivers flow. The qualifier "with their Lord" (*'inda rabbihim*) is the Qur'an's way of placing the reward in the divine presence itself, not merely in a location. And the eternity follows: *khalidin fiha* — abiding forever. The contrast with the world of 3:14: the goods there are passing, the enjoyment is brief; the reward here is eternal and unending. The greater destroys the lesser — an eternity in the divine presence against a lifetime's possessions.

"and pure spouses" — *azwaj mutahharah* — purified spouses, purified mates. Al-Jalalayn: "purified of menstruation and other impurities." Ibn Kathir and the classical notes: the spouses of Paradise are free of the impurity, the defect, the friction that belongs to the spouses of this world. It is the completion of the world's best attraction: the verse of 3:14 listed the love of women at the head; the reward names the purified spouses at the heart. The same desire is not abolished; it is perfected.

"along with Allah's pleasure" — *wa ridwan min Allah* — and the pleasure, the good pleasure, of Allah. This is the crown of the reward, the item that the classical commentators single out as the greatest of the promised gifts. The phrase is the Qur'an's fullest statement of the goal: the Garden, the rivers, the eternity, the purified spouses — and above all of it, the pleasure of the Lord. Ibn Kathir and the commentators note this as the highest — the believer's aim is not merely escaping punishment or receiving gifts but drawing near and finding the Lord pleased.

"And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants" — *basir bi al-'ibad* — He sees the servants well, He is the Seer of the servants. The verse ends by connecting the promise to the evidence: the reward is for the mindful, and the One who judges who is mindful is the One who sees. Al-Jalalayn: "God is Seer, Knower of His servants, requiting each of them according to his deeds." The point is that the promise is not a reward for appearance. The mindfulness that earns it is known by the One who sees what is hidden.

There is a deep structure to the pair 3:14–15. The same components — women, children, wealth, land — are exactly the components of the reward in the language of the Qur'an's own imagery of Paradise. The believer who is tested by the beauty of the world and the desire for the family and the wealth is not asked to annihilate the desire; he is asked to receive its fulfillment from the right hand. The world gives the goods briefly; the Lord gives them purified, with eternity, with the presence, with His pleasure. The "better than that" is not a different good; it is the same goods, healed of their passing, their impurity, and their separation from Allah, and crowned by His pleasure. This is what makes the verse a reorientation rather than a rejection: the test of the world is whether the heart will take the promise and wait, or take the appearance and consume it now.
