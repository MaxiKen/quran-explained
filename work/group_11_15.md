## Verse 11

Verse 11 says: *Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female. If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate. But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half. Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring. But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third. But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse lays down the law of inheritance for children and parents: a son receives twice the share of a daughter; two or more daughters together receive two-thirds of the estate; one daughter receives one-half; each parent receives one-sixth when there are children; if the deceased leaves no children and the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives one-third; and if the deceased leaves siblings, the mother receives one-sixth. All these shares apply after bequests and debts are fulfilled. The verse closes by reminding the heirs that they cannot fully judge who benefits them most, and that this allocation is an obligation from the All-Knowing, All-Wise Allah.

The verse is the foundation of the Islamic science of inheritance — the *farāʾiḍ* — and the classical scholars gave it enormous attention. Ibn Kathir notes that the fixed shares are derived from 4:11, 4:12, 4:176, and the hadiths that explain them, and he quotes Sufyan bin 'Uyaynah: "Knowledge of Al-Fara'id was called half of knowledge, because it affects all people." Every human being will die, and every family will face an estate; the law of inheritance is the rare legal science to which every household is a party.

The occasion of revelation is instructive. Ibn Kathir records two hadiths. The first, reported by Al-Bukhari through Jabir, concerns the death of Jabir's father: when Jabir was unconscious and the Prophet visited him with Abu Bakr and poured water on him, Jabir asked, "What do you command me to do with my money, O Allah's Messenger?" — though Ibn Kathir notes this report relates to 4:176, since Jabir had only sisters and no children or parents. The second, reported by Imam Ahmad through Jabir, is the famous case of Sa'd bin Ar-Rabi': his wife came with their two daughters after Sa'd was killed as a martyr at Uhud, complaining that their uncle had taken all the money and left nothing — "They will not be married unless they have money." The Prophet said, "Allah will decide," and after the inheritance verse was revealed he sent word: "Give two-thirds to Sa'd's two daughters and one-eighth for their mother, and whatever is left is yours" — recorded by Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah. The case shows that the Quranic shares were applied directly to a real estate in the Prophet's presence.

The rule of the male receiving twice the female's share is often misunderstood. Ibn Kathir explains the underlying logic: men need money for dependants, commercial transactions, work, and obligations — the Quranic system allocates wealth according to responsibility, not dignity. The verse does not value a female at half a male; it assigns to the male twice the burden and twice the portion. Al-Jalalayn notes the practical arithmetic: if two women inherit with a male, half the property is his and half is theirs; if one female inherits with him, she has one-third and he two-thirds; if only one heir, he takes it all.

The verse also resolves the Jahiliyyah practice. Ibn Kathir records Al-Bukhari from Ibn 'Abbas outlining the old custom: property went to the offspring, and parents inherited only if named in the will — so a man's father and mother might receive nothing while a distant male cousin took everything. Allah ordained new limits: the male's double share, a sixth for each parent, an eighth or fourth for a wife, a half or fourth for a husband. Ibn 'Abbas notes that where daughters number two or more they receive two-thirds. Some early readers thought the phrase "two females" meant exactly two, but Ibn Kathir rejects this: the Sa'd hadith proves the two-thirds applies to two, and "more" extends it by the rule that what applies to two in this context also applies to more.

The parents' shares have detailed sub-rules. With children present, each parent receives one-sixth — but with a single daughter, she takes half, the parents take a sixth each, and (per Ibn Kathir's explanation of the standard ruling) the father takes an additional sixth, receiving the surplus. Where there are no children and only the parents, the mother takes one-third and the father two-thirds — twice the mother's share. Where a spouse is also present, the spouse takes half (husband) or a quarter (wife), and the mother receives one-third of the remainder; Ibn Kathir attributes this refined rule to the majority of the Companions. Where the deceased leaves siblings (brothers or sisters) at the mother's side, the mother is reduced to one-sixth, though Qatadah notes via Ibn Abi Hatim that the presence of the siblings reduces the mother while the siblings themselves do not inherit — the reduction is attributed to the father, who carries the financial responsibility of marrying them off from his own resources. Al-Jalalayn adds that *walad* includes a grandchild and *abb* a grandparent in the general reading.

The order of distribution is fixed: debts come before bequests, and bequests come before the fixed shares. Ibn Kathir states this sequence and says it is agreed upon by the predecessors and successors — the estate is first cleared of its debts, then the valid bequests are paid (within the one-third limit), then the appointed shares are distributed. The closing verse's words — "you do not fully know who is more beneficial to you" — are a rebuke to those who thought they could choose the allocation better than Allah, and a consolation to those who cannot see why a daughter receives less than a son: the wisdom is with the All-Knowing, All-Wise.

For modern readers, the verse establishes the central principle that inheritance is a divinely fixed right, not a family preference; that wealth follows responsibility rather than gender alone; that no heir may be disinherited by the deceased's will beyond the permitted third; and that the system, with its multiple rules for multiple family configurations, preserves both justice and family cohesion across generations.

## Verse 12

Verse 12 says: *You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother’s side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺. ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the spouse shares and adds the rule for a person who dies without children or parents — the *kalālah* case: a single maternal brother or sister takes one-sixth; more than one share one-third equally. All shares again apply after bequests and debts, without any harm to the heirs, and the whole arrangement is declared a commandment from the All-Knowing, Most Forbearing Allah.

The spouse shares are simple but precise. A husband inherits half his wife's estate when she dies childless and one-fourth when she leaves a child. A wife (or wives, who divide the share together) inherits one-fourth of her husband's estate when he is childless and one-eighth when he leaves a child. Ibn Kathir notes the classical agreement that these rules apply to grandchildren as well as children — indeed, to descendants at every level — so *walad* in this verse is read in its comprehensive sense, a point the later jurists applied consistently to the whole inheritance system. Where there are multiple wives, the fourth or eighth is divided equally among them.

The *kalālah* case — the person who "leaves neither parents nor children" — is a genuinely technical term in Islamic inheritance law, and the verse handles it expressly. A man or woman who dies without parents or offspring has few close heirs; the verse specifies the maternal sibling shares: a single maternal brother or sister takes one-sixth; if there are more than one, they share one-third equally — and Ibn Kathir notes this concerns siblings from the same mother, corresponding to Ibn Mas'ud's reading of the verse. The maternal siblings' share — one-sixth each, one-third collective — is distinct from the shares of full and paternal siblings, which are treated separately in 4:176, where full or paternal siblings receive larger settled portions.

The phrase "after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm to the heirs" is one of the most important protective clauses in the entire inheritance passage. Ibn Kathir states flatly that "there is a consensus on this matter among the scholars": the debt is paid before the will, and the will is carried out before the fixed inheritance shares are distributed. The clause "without harm" (*ghayra mudārrin*) matters because the pre-Islamic and early juristic concern was that a dying man might issue a bequest that strips the heirs — or that a debt might be used as a device to defeat them. Al-Jalalayn explains "without causing any prejudice to the inheritors by bequeathing more than the third." A bequest to an heir, or a bequest exceeding one-third of the estate, is precisely the "harm" the verse excludes, and the Prophet's instruction to Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas — "one-third, and even one-third is too much" — was built on this principle. The clause is therefore both a procedural rule and a substantive limit.

The verse ends with *waṣiyyatan min Allah* — "a commandment from Allah" — emphasising that these entitlements are not judicial inventions but divine ordinance, and with two divine names: *ʿAlīm* (All-Knowing), because the shares are based on knowledge no family could assemble on its own — nearness, dependency, obligation, and need; and *Ḥalīm* (Most Forbearing), interpreted by Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn as deferring punishment while the people act. The Forbearance of Allah in the verse is connected to the fact that the law of inheritance is one the heirs might be tempted to manipulate, and Allah — who knows their motives and delays their reckoning — is nonetheless watching.

Ibn Kathir adds a crucial juristic note: the Sunna specifies that the individuals mentioned inherit provided they are not barred from inheritance by a legal impediment — murder, difference of religion, or slavery. These exclusions do not come from this verse; they come from the prophetic rulings and the consensus of the scholars, and they belong to the same system: inheritance is a right of relationship unless the relationship of the heir to the deceased has been broken by a prohibitively severe act.

For the modern reader, the verse teaches three things. First, the rights of spouses in inheritance are real and explicit — a wife is not left dependent on the goodwill of her husband's family, and a husband does not automatically take his wife's entire estate. Second, the family structure the verse contemplates is broad — it includes the childless, whose wealth must not be swallowed by relatives with no claim, and the sibling-only household, whose members receive defined rather than arbitrary shares. Third, the orderly processing of an estate matters more than its size: debts first, bequests within limits, then shares, with no harm to anyone. The All-Knowing, Most Forbearing Allah has arranged it all, and the believer's task is to carry out the arrangement rather than engineer around it.

## Verse 13

Verse 13 says: *These ˹entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph!*

In simple terms, the verse declares that the inheritance rules just given — and, in the broader reading, the whole set of divine limits — are boundaries fixed by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger by respecting these limits is promised Gardens beneath which rivers flow, to remain there forever; that is the great success.

The word translated "limits" is *ḥudūd* — the plural of *ḥadd*, a boundary, limit, or demarcation. Ibn Kathir explains that the *farāʾiḍ* (the fixed inheritance shares) are Allah's set limits, allotted to heirs by degree of relation and dependency. The word *ḥudūd* is used across the Qur'an for the laws Allah has drawn around human conduct — marriage, divorce, inheritance, punishment — and the image is precise: law is a fence around what is permitted. The believer does not stand on one side of the fence and the other side open; every allocation is bounded, and every deviation — giving an heir more than his share by trickery, moving money to defeat another heir, inventing allocations to please one relative — is a transgression of the limit itself.

The verse then defines the path of obedience: *man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa-rasūlahu* — whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger. Ibn Kathir frames the obedience "regarding the inheritance" as not adding or decreasing the fixed shares by tricks and plots, but giving each heir his appointed share. The pairing of Allah and the Messenger is significant throughout the Qur'an: the Messenger is the one who explains, applies, and embodies the limits, so obeying the command is impossible in practice without the prophetic application — including the rules the revelations themselves either established or confirmed, such as the one-third bequest limit, the exclusion of murderers and disbelievers from inheritance, and the system of witness and arbitration that the Sunnah elaborated. Obedience is not a matter of preference; it is the only door the verse names.

The reward is described with the Qur'an's standard and richest imagery: *jannāt tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — Gardens under which rivers flow. Ibn Kathir and the classical commentators read the rivers flowing beneath the Gardens, so that their banks and orchards are irrigated continually; the image combines rest, water, fruit, and permanence. The reward is qualified with *khālidīna fīhā* — "to stay there forever" — which is the point: the shares of this world are temporary and small, and the one who refuses to cheat an heir of a few dirhams is being offered a reward with no end. The verse calls the outcome *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* — "the ultimate triumph" or "the great success" — the word the Qur'an uses for the final, decisive victory, the one that cannot be reversed or contested.

The placement of the reward clause is not accidental. The inheritance verses are the most temptation-prone legislation a society has: every family estate is a miniature contest between greed and fairness, and the dying man, the heirs, the witnesses, and the executor each face an opportunity. The Qur'an counters the temptation by moving the reader's gaze from the estate to the eternal Gardens — the one who obeys here is admitted there, and the few dirhams at stake in a will become small beside rivers flowing under Paradise.

For the modern reader, the verse also clarifies the status of these rulings in the believer's framework. Inheritance law is not one of the "spiritual" teachings that may be followed in spirit while its details are negotiated away; it is *ḥudūd* — a limit with the same standing as the other divine boundaries. The family that quietly "fixes" the daughter's share downward, or that uses a debt claim to defeat an heir, is not merely being ungenerous; it is crossing a limit, and the verse's promise and threat both hang on that crossing. The believer who embraces the inheritance rules as divine limits has already chosen, in effect, between the estate and the Gardens.

## Verse 14

Verse 14 says: *But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and exceeds their limits will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever. And they will suffer a humiliating punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the counterpart of the previous promise: whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and goes beyond the limits He fixed — including by manipulating, suppressing, or violating the inheritance shares — is cast into Hell forever and suffers a humiliating punishment.

The pairing with 4:13 is exact, and the structure of the two verses is a standard Quranic pattern: promise and warning, obedience and disobedience, Paradise and Hell. The verse names two failures: *yaʿṣi Allāha wa-rasūlahu* — disobedience of Allah and the Messenger — and *yataʿaddā ḥudūdahu* — exceeding His limits. Ibn Kathir notes that the one who instead changes what Allah ordained, and disputes His judgment, is punished with humiliation in the eternal, painful torment. The two clauses correspond to two possible failures: outright disobedience to the command itself, and the more subtle transgression of the boundary — giving an unapproved extra, forcing an unauthorized deduction, circumventing the shares through legal devices while outwardly performing the text.

The punishment is stated plainly: *yudkhilhu nāran khālidan fīhā* — He will admit him into a Fire, to remain there forever. Al-Jalalayn records the variant reading *nudkhilhu* ("We shall admit him") along with the standard *yudkhilhu* ("He shall admit him"), and in both cases the meaning is the same: the admittance is certain. The word *khālidan* — "eternal" — deserves care. The classical scholars understood the eternity of the Fire for those who die upon unrepented kufr and major sin and are judged accordingly; the verse's language is unconditional, and the believer's response to it is neither complacency nor despair but awe, obedience, and hope in Allah's mercy.

The verse's characterisation of the punishment as *ʿadhābun muhīn* — "a humiliating punishment" — is precise. *Muhīn* comes from the same root as *hāna* (to be lowly, contemptible); it is the punishment of being brought low, exposed, and disgraced. There is a subtle appropriateness in this for the inheritance context: the punisher of the one who humiliated the weak — the orphan whose wealth was consumed, the daughter whose share was suppressed, the widow whose rightful portion was seized — is himself humiliated. The Quranic principle of recompense is not merely retributive; it is descriptive — the one who was unjustly high in this life is brought low in the next.

The hadith of Abu Hurayrah, recorded by Imam Ahmad and cited by Ibn Kathir, gives the verse its most vivid application. The Prophet said: "A man might perform the actions of righteous people for seventy years, but when it is time to compile his will, he commits injustice. So his final work will be his worst, and he thus enters the Fire. A man might perform the deeds of evil people for seventy years, yet he is fair in his will. So his final work will be his best, and he thus enters Paradise" — and then he recited: "These are the limits (set by) Allah... a disgraceful torment." Abu Dawud records the same from Abu Hurayrah with "sixty years," and the recitation of 4:13–14 follows. At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah also record it; At-Tirmidhi graded it Hasan Gharib. The hadith is a profound warning: the last act of a long life can undo it, and one's final decisions about a will are decisions about one's own destiny. This is exactly the verse's point — the inheritance is not merely a financial document; it is a moral event, and the hand that writes the will writes the record.

For the modern reader, the verse corrects a common slide in how inheritance is discussed. It is easy to treat family estate planning as a private matter of preference — who "deserves" more, who "needs" it, whose feelings must be managed. The Qur'an categorically refuses that framing: the shares are limits fixed by Allah, and the person who engineers an estate to defeat those limits is not managing a family but crossing a boundary. The verse also warns against the specialist's rationalization — the lawyer, accountant, or family elder who says "the law is one thing, but in practice we can arrange it differently." In the Quranic frame, there is no "in practice" beside the limit: the practice is the limit, and the outcome is the eternal Fire or the eternal Gardens.

## Verse 15

Verse 15 says: *˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves. If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them.*

In simple terms, the verse prescribes an early Islamic response to illicit intercourse by women: four witnesses from the community must testify, and if they do, the offenders are confined at home until death or until Allah provides a different ruling. That "different way" arrived with Surat An-Nur, which replaced house confinement with the prescribed punishments.

Understanding the verse requires seeing it historically. Ibn Kathir explains that at the beginning of Islam, if a woman committed adultery with sufficient proof, she was confined to her home until she died. Ibn 'Abbas states plainly: "The early ruling was confinement, until Allah sent down Surat An-Nur which abrogated that ruling with the ruling of flogging (for fornication) or stoning to death (for adultery)" — and Ibn Kathir lists similar statements from 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Abu Salih, Qatadah, Zayd bin Aslam, and Ad-Dahhak, noting the matter is agreed upon among the commentators. The verses that follow (4:16) also belong to this interim period; both were superseded by the detailed rulings of Surat An-Nur.

The requirement of four witnesses is the verse's most enduring element, and it survived into the final law. The Qur'an repeatedly demands four witnesses for a charge of *zinā* (24:4 — "those who accuse chaste women then do not bring four witnesses, flog them eighty lashes"), and the point is the protection of the accused: a charge that can destroy a woman's life and a family's honour cannot rest on rumour, suspicion, or two idle observers. The witnesses must be from among the community — "from among yourselves" — meaning persons known, of upright character, and accountable in the social order, not strangers passing through. Al-Jalalayn notes the four must testify to the act itself, and the substantive law required the testimony to be direct, consistent, and unrefuted. The threshold was set deliberately high, both because of the gravity of the accusation and because the punishment, once stipulated, was itself severe.

The remedy of confinement is the interim regulation: the women who committed the offence were kept under house arrest, *ḥattā yatawaffāhunna al-mawt* — "until death overtakes them" — or *yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlan* — "until Allah makes a way for them." The "way" the Qur'an leaves open is the promise of a new ruling, and the commentators identify it with the legislation of Surat An-Nur: the unmarried offender receives one hundred lashes and, in the Sunna's established practice, a year of banishment; the married offender receives one hundred lashes and stoning. Ibn Kathir records Imam Ahmad from 'Ubadah bin As-Samit, who said the Prophet declared: "Take from me: Allah has made some other way for them. The married with the married, the unmarried with the unmarried. The married gets a hundred lashes and stoning to death, while the unmarried gets a hundred lashes then banishment for a year" — recorded by Muslim and the collectors of the Sunan, with At-Tirmidhi grading it Hasan Sahih. Al-Jalalayn frames the same hadith: "Come listen to me! Come listen to me! God has now made a way out for them" (Muslim). The verse's phrase — "or Allah ordains a different way for them" — was thus fulfilled.

The verse also says "the offenders" — the plural in the translation reflecting that the confinement applied to the woman and, in the context, those involved with her; 4:16 develops the case of the two who commit the sin. The protection of the accused is reinforced by the fact that the offence described is one against both the individual and the social moral order — the Qur'an addresses it as a community matter, requiring public evidence and a public response rather than private revenge or vigilantism. The family that "handles it quietly" with harm is not the Quranic framework; the framework requires witnesses, process, and the appointed consequence.

For the modern reader, the verse carries three lasting lessons. First, the charge of immorality is a legal matter with evidentiary rules, not a social weapon — four upright witnesses, not suspicion, are the test. Second, the Qur'an's law is revealed progressively: rulings were given as the community matured, and the final law of Surat An-Nur supersedes the earlier confinement, a point the classical scholars followed in their understanding of abrogation. Third, the moral order is protected through due process: punishment follows proof, the accused is not destroyed by rumour, and the community's morality is maintained by public institutions rather than private vendettas. The verse's lasting command — four witnesses — remains the eternal standard of the law of slander that follows in Surat An-Nur.
