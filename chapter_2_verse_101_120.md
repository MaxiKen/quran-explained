## Verse 101

Verse 101 says: *"Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them— confirming their own Scriptures—some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs as if they did not know."*

In simple terms, the verse continues the accusation of the previous section. A messenger had come to them from Allah, confirming their own Scriptures — and some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs, as if they did not know it. The image is of a person who takes a priceless document and throws it away; the addition "as if they did not know" is the verdict on the performance.

The word "cast" is the key. The Qur'an does not say they rejected the Book, or neglected it, or misunderstood it. It says they *nabadhū* — they flung it behind their backs. This is the gesture of a person who turns away from something deliberately, with contempt, and it is the same word used in the previous verse for casting the covenant aside. The covenant, the Scripture, and the messenger are one sequence: the people who throw the covenant aside are the same people who throw the Book behind them, and the same people who throw the messenger aside.

"As if they did not know" — the phrase is the completion of the irony. They did know. The previous verses said so explicitly: they recognized the Book (2:89); they recognized the messenger as they recognized their own children (2:146). The "as if" describes the performance: they behaved as though they were ignorant, though they were not. The Qur'an is not describing ignorance; it is describing the appearance of ignorance chosen after knowledge. This is the most serious form of rejection, because it was not a failure to find the truth but a refusal to acknowledge it.

The classical commentators note in this context that the Jews had, in their own scripture, the descriptions and the predictions of the coming prophet; and when the Prophet arrived, the scholars among them — as the previous verses showed — had two responses: some denied him openly, and some turned to the books of their tradition, particularly the lore of magic, which the next verse describes. The verse before us is the moment of the casting: the truth was present, and it was thrown away.

In modern life, the verse describes the most dangerous form of rejection of knowledge: the person who has the evidence before him and acts as if it were not there. This is not the person who has not encountered the truth; it is the person who encountered it and turned his back — who reads the message and tosses it behind him. The verse's phrase "as if they did not know" is the mirror in which such a person may recognize himself: the performance of ignorance that is actually the avoidance of acknowledgment.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 102

Verse 102 says: *"They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, "We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith." Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!"*

In simple terms, the verse now exposes what the People of the Book had turned to instead of the Book: they followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. The Qur'an then sets the record straight on three points: Solomon did not disbelieve — the devils disbelieved; the devils taught magic; and the account of the two angels in Babylon, Harut and Marut, with their warning — "We are only a test for you, so do not abandon your faith." The verse ends with the verdict on the buyers of magic: miserable was the price they sold their souls for.

The occasion is recorded in the classical tafsir. As-Suddi's account, transmitted by Ibn Kathir, describes how the devils had spread the claim that Solomon's power over the jinn and the wind was magic — that he had control over them by a book of sorcery buried and then found. The claim was repeated until people said Solomon was a sorcerer, and the books were adopted. When the Prophet came, some of the People of the Book argued against him with these books, as though the Qur'an were the same kind of thing. The verse clears the prophet Solomon: he was a prophet, not a sorcerer; the disbelievers were the devils, who taught the people magic.

The account of Harut and Marut is one of the most discussed passages in the tafsir, and it is important to present the differences of opinion honestly. The mainstream reading is that these were two angels sent to Babylon as a test, and whatever "was revealed" to them was accompanied by the warning: "We are only a test, so do not abandon your faith" — meaning that the knowledge they made available was a trial, not a license. Some early authorities, including a view reported from al-Tabari, understood the passage differently — arguing that Allah did not send magic down at all and that the devils fabricated the teaching; al-Tabari's specific explanation (that Harut and Marut were two men, with Gabriel and Michael being the "two angels" of the verse) is recorded by Ibn Kathir, who states plainly that he finds it implausible. Other authorities, such as al-Qurtubi and al-Rabi' bin Anas, held that nothing magical was sent down; the verse's phrase is understood as the devils' claim. The point that all the authorities share is the verse's central warning: the knowledge was a test, learning magic is disbelief, and the teachers warned before teaching.

The verse gives the social consequence of magic in one clause: it causes a rift between husband and wife. Magic is not a neutral curiosity; it is a weapon of alienation, and its deepest victims are the closest bonds. The Qur'an then makes the theological point that contains the whole matter: "their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will." The magician acts within the world Allah created; no power is independent of Allah. The people learned what harmed them and did not benefit them — the Qur'an's exact description of every transaction with the occult, and indeed of every trade of the soul for a power that turns against the one who sought it.

In modern life, the verse is a warning against the fascination with the occult in every age: the astrology, the charm, the curse, the manipulation of unseen forces — all sold as power and bought at the price of the soul. The Qur'an's verdict is the same in every generation: the person who buys into it has no share in the Hereafter; the price is miserable; and the buyer knew it all along.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 103

Verse 103 says: *"If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew!"*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the account of magic is the counterfactual: if only they were faithful and mindful of Allah, there would have been a better reward from Allah. The "if only" is the Qur'an's lament over a choice: the people who sold their souls for magic could have had the reward of Allah — had they believed and been mindful.

The verse is a single sentence with two "if only"s — *law annahum āmanū* and *law kānū ya'lamūn* — and the repetition carries the whole of the human tragedy. The first "if only" names the condition they did not meet: faith and mindfulness. The second names the failure behind it: "if only they knew" — they did not know what they were trading away. In the previous verse, the buyers of magic "already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter" — they knew the doctrine; what they did not know was the value of what they were losing. The second "if only" is not about information; it is about appreciation. A person can know the value of a thing with his mind and not know it with his heart — and the person who trades the heart's treasure for the hand's amusement has not "known" in the sense the verse means.

The verse also completes the evaluation of the trade. The previous verse said "miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls." This verse shows the alternative: faith and mindfulness would have brought "a better reward from Allah." The two verses are the two sides of the account: what they lost, and what they could have had. The "better reward" is not better than magic — it is better than everything, because it is with Allah: the reward that is not lost, not corrupted, and not taken away.

The word "mindful" here is *taqwā* — the God-consciousness that the surah has been teaching from its earliest verses. Faith without mindfulness is incomplete; mindfulness without faith is impossible. The verse names the two together because the believer is the person who believes and therefore lives in the awareness of Allah — the awareness that changes what he buys, what he trades, and what he values.

In modern life, the verse is the voice of regret before it happens. The person who spends his life on what passes while neglecting what remains will one day use this verse's words: if only I had been faithful and mindful, there would have been a better reward. The verse's purpose is to place that sentence before the reader now, while the "if only" can still be turned into "yes." The reward of Allah is available; the only question is whether the person will know its value at the time of the choice.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 104

Verse 104 says: *"O believers! Do not say, "Râ'ina." [Herd us!] But say, "Unẓurna," [Tend to us!] and listen ˹attentively˺. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment."*

In simple terms, the verse now addresses the believers directly with a command about speech: do not say "Ra'ina" — but say "Unzurna" — and listen attentively. The background is one of the most remarkable episodes of the early Madinan period, and it is a lesson in the etiquette of words.

The background is recorded in the tafsir. The word *ra'ina* in Arabic means "attend to us" or "hear us" — a polite request for attention. But in Hebrew, the same sound is an insult — a curse — and the Jews used the Arabic word deliberately, pronouncing it in a way that sounded identical while intending the Hebrew insult, as a device for mocking the Prophet without the Muslims realizing it. When they said "Ra'ina" they were saying something else; and they were delighted when some Muslims, hearing the Arabic meaning, began to use the same word themselves — because now the Muslims had been tricked into participating in the mockery of the Prophet. Ibn Abbas, as recorded by Ibn Kathir, said they used to say to the Prophet words that were insults; al-Suddi mentions a Jewish man from Banu Qaynuqa' who would come to the Prophet and say "hear, let you hear nothing" — words the Muslims took as greetings, not realizing the abuse they contained. The believers were therefore commanded to use a different word — "Unzurna," "tend to us" — a word with no double meaning, so that the mockery could not be performed under cover of piety.

The verse's command is thus a lesson in the ethics of language: not every word that is permissible in one's own language is safe from being used against the truth; and the believer is commanded to use words that are unambiguous, so that his speech cannot be turned against the message or the messenger. The broader command ends with "and listen attentively" — the verse is not only about the word used but also about the manner of receiving the message: the believer hears and gives attention, whereas the mockers pronounced words of sarcasm while refusing to listen.

The verse's final clause — "And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment" — connects the etiquette of speech with the verdict of the hereafter. Mockery of the prophet is not a small fault; it is a form of disbelief, and the Qur'an names it as such. The person who uses words as instruments of contempt, who twists language to insult the truth while appearing to speak respectfully, has done something that the verse does not classify as a minor sin.

In modern life, the verse is the foundation of the etiquette of speech in a world of double meanings, irony, and coded insults. It teaches that words are not neutral: the same sound can mean one thing to one hearer and another to another, and the person who speaks without attention to what his words may carry has abdicated the moral responsibility of his tongue. It also teaches the believers to choose words that cannot be twisted, and to refuse the game in which the truth is mocked under the cover of politeness.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 105

Verse 105 says: *"The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord, but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty."*

In simple terms, the verse states the psychological background of the entire section: the disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want the believers to receive any blessing from their Lord. The statement is in the negative form — "they would not want" — and the verse then gives the answer: but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy, for Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

The verse identifies the common ground of the two groups. The People of the Book and the polytheists had nothing else in common — one group had a scripture and the other worshipped idols — but they shared this: neither wanted the blessing to descend on the believers. The blessing in question is the revelation, the prophethood, and the guidance sent to the Prophet and his community. The previous verses have shown the envy of the People of the Book (2:89-90); this verse names the same attitude at the level of desire: they do not want the good to come to you.

"But Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy" — the verse locates the matter in the choice of Allah. The objection of the People of the Book was not finally an objection about who should receive revelation; it was an objection about the freedom of Allah. In their view, the prophecy belonged to them by right of lineage; the Qur'an's answer is that prophecy is not an inheritance but a choice, and the choice belongs to the One who gives the blessing. "And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty" — the final clause is the refutation at the level of the divine: the bounty is not scarce; it is not competed for; it flows from the One whose treasury is not diminished by the giving.

The verse also teaches the believers the correct response to the hostility of others: not to build the religion in reaction to the wishes of the people, not to believe that the truth depends on their approval — but to receive the blessing with gratitude and to leave the choice where it belongs, with Allah. The believers' task is not to convince the envious but to receive the gift.

In modern life, the verse speaks to every community that has received a blessing — an opportunity, a message, a mission — and finds itself surrounded by those who do not want it to succeed. The attitude of such people is real and can be measured, but the verse says it is not decisive: the blessing comes by the choice of Allah, not by the consent of the onlookers. The believer's posture is gratitude and perseverance, and the believer's security is the infinite bounty of the One who has already chosen to give.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 106

Verse 106 says: *"If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?"*

In simple terms, the verse addresses the objection that the Jews raised against the changing of divine rulings: how can Allah command one thing and then command another? The Qur'an's answer is the verse of abrogation: if We abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. And the question at the end carries the whole argument: do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?

The background is recorded in the classical tafsir. The Jews — as Ibn Kathir explains, citing Ibn Jarir (al-Tabari) — denied that the rulings of the Torah could be abrogated, and they used that denial as the basis for rejecting Jesus and Muhammad, because both came with rulings that changed some provisions of the Torah. The Qur'an's answer is that the authority belongs to the One who gave the command: He who owns the heavens and the earth has the right to legislate, to change, to postpone, and to replace as He wills. Ibn Abbas explained the verse's first word as "whatever verse We abrogate"; Mujahid (via Ibn Jurayj) explained it as "whatever ruling We repeal, making the lawful unlawful and the unlawful lawful"; and the commentators note that abrogation in the classical framework concerns commandments, prohibitions, and permissions — the engaged law of the community, not the truth of the divine message itself.

The verse's second clause — "or cause it to be forgotten" — was read in two ways by the early scholars. One reading holds that it refers to verses whose recitation was lifted and whose rulings were replaced; another holds that it refers to postponement — *nunsi'ha* — the delaying of a ruling to a later time. Some early authorities (including a report from Ibn Abbas) understood "cause it to be forgotten" in the sense of leaving the verse in place without change. The difference between the readings is less important than the point they share: the divine law is not a frozen artifact; it is revealed in the world of human capacity and circumstance, and the One who revealed it knows what is better for His servants at every stage.

"We replace it with a better or similar one" — "better," the commentators explain, is better for the people addressed: more beneficial, easier to bear, more suited to their state. Qatadah said the replacement is an ayah that is "more facilitating, permitting, commanding, or prohibiting." The verse is thus not a statement of arbitrariness; it is a statement of wisdom at work in history: the legislation is progressive, and the progression is for the servants, not for the pleasure of the Lawgiver — though both are true, since whatever Allah does for His servants is what He wills.

In modern life, the verse is often the subject of misunderstanding, so it is worth stating clearly what it does not say. It does not say that what Allah once commanded was evil and was replaced by good; everything Allah commands is good — the abrogated command was good for its time, and the replacement is good for its time. And it does not say that the truth of the revelation changes: the doctrine of abrogation in the classical exegetical tradition concerns the legal rulings that were addressed to communities in different circumstances. The verse's deeper lesson is the humility of the creature before the authority of the Creator: the objection "how can Allah change His command?" is answered with "do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?"

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 107

Verse 107 says: *"Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?"*

In simple terms, the verse continues the argument of the previous one with two statements: the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs only to Allah; and you — the people of the Book, the objectors, and every creature — have no guardian or helper besides Allah. The two statements are the ground of the whole dispute: the One who owns everything may decree everything, and no one has a protector other than Him.

The first statement establishes the authority. The word "kingdom" — *mulk* — is not a metaphor; it is the actual ownership of everything that exists. The heavens and the earth are not a property of nature that operates on its own; they are a kingdom administered by its Owner. Ibn Jarir (al-Tabari), as quoted by Ibn Kathir, explains the verse precisely in this way: the One who owns the heavens and the earth has the authority to command whatever He wills, to forbid whatever He wills, to abrogate whatever He wills, and to uphold whatever He wills. The Jews' objection — that the Torah does not change — was an objection to the Owner's right over His own property.

The second statement addresses the objectors' sense of security: "you have no guardian or helper besides Allah." This is one of the Qur'an's most repeated warnings, and it is directed at a specific illusion. The People of the Book believed that their relationship with Allah was a kind of protection — that their ancestry, their scripture, their covenant made them independent of the demands of the present messenger. The verse says: you have no guardian besides Allah. Neither the covenant nor the ancestry nor the scripture protects anyone against the commands of the Owner; there is no second authority to appeal to over the authority of the One who owns the kingdom.

The two statements together are also the Qur'an's answer to the sense of autonomy that characterizes every human community. The person who believes that he can negotiate the terms of his relation to God — that he can keep the convenient parts and dismiss the rest, as the surah has already described (2:85) — is living in the illusion that he has some power over the Owner. The verse removes the illusion: the kingdom is not shared; and the creature has no protector other than the One he has been refusing.

In modern life, the verse is a radical statement of the dependence of everything. The person who believes that his position, his community, or his achievements secure him — as the Jews believed their covenant secured them — is building on the ground the verse levels. The believer's security is not in his standing but in the standing of the One he stands with: the Owner of the kingdom, the only guardian, the only helper.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 108

Verse 108 says: *"Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way."*

In simple terms, the verse now turns to the believers with a warning: do you intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? The reference is to the demands of the Children of Israel — the demand to see Allah plainly, the demand for signs, the demand that the prophet prove himself. The Qur'an warns the believers against repeating the pattern, and closes with the law: whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way.

The "as Moses was asked before" refers to the history the surah has already narrated: the Children of Israel asked to see Allah, and they were taken by the thunderbolt (2:55); the Children of Israel asked Moses for all manner of specifics in the story of the cow (2:67-71). The verse is not condemning questions that seek understanding; it is condemning the questions that test the prophet, that demand proof instead of submission, that put the prophecy on trial. The classical commentators discuss in this context the Jews' earlier request of Moses: "Show us Allah plainly" (4:153) — a demand the Qur'an elsewhere says the People of the Book had made of Moses. The pattern the verse warns against is the demand for a sign that would replace faith, the asking that carries the spirit of refusal.

The verse's warning to the believers is remarkable in that it is addressed to them directly, not to the hostile outsiders. The believers could fall into the same trap: they could imitate the People of the Book in their interrogations, in their testing of the prophet, in their demands — and in doing so, they would have traded belief for disbelief. The "trade" is the Qur'an's word for every abandonment of faith, and the verse says the one who makes it has strayed from the Right Way — he has left the path, not merely stumbled.

The verse also establishes a principle of the prophetic relationship: the prophet is not a performer who must constantly demonstrate his credentials. The believer receives the message and asks about its meaning; the disbeliever demands the prophet prove the message. The two questions look similar; the spirit behind them is opposite. The Qur'an's command to the believers is to ask with the intention of understanding (the verse's "Unzurna" from the previous passage), not with the intention of testing.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the culture of evidence-seeking as a replacement for commitment. In every field—religion, science, public life — there are questions that seek knowledge and questions that postpone the decision, and the person who always demands more proof may be using the demand to avoid the step. The verse's warning is gentle but firm: do not let the demand for signs become the replacement for faith; the person who turns the prophetic relationship into an interrogation has already left the path he claims to be investigating.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 109

Verse 109 says: *"Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything."*

In simple terms, the verse names the motive and sets the response. The motive: many among the People of the Book wish they could turn the believers back to disbelief, out of envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. The response: pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. The verse is one of the most important statements of the Islamic approach to hostility in the period before the command of armed struggle.

The motive is named with precision. It is not ignorance: "after the truth has been made clear to them." It is not a difference of opinion: it is envy — the anger at the good that has been given to someone else. The commentators explain that the envy is "from their own selves" — it was not provoked by anything the believers did but arose spontaneously within them, as the previous verses described with the resentment at the gift of prophecy being given to an Arab. The object of their wish is the most extreme: they wish to turn the believers from belief to disbelief. The verse is not describing an argument they had; it is describing the deepest desire of their hearts, and the Qur'an names it so that the believers are not naive about the people they deal with.

The response is the instruction. "Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision." The believers are commanded to pardon — to let the offense go — and to bear with — to be patient with the hostility and the provocation. The "decision" that Allah would deliver is understood by the classical commentators as the command concerning fighting; Ibn Kathir records that Abu al-'Aliyah, al-Rabi' bin Anas, Qatadah, and al-Suddi said this verse was later abrogated by the "verse of the sword" (9:5 and 9:29). In the period of the verse, the command was patience: the Muslims were to absorb the provocation without retaliation, to forgive, and to wait — not because the hostility was insignificant, but because the time had not come, and the believers were being trained in the strength that forgives rather than the anger that retaliates.

The verse is thus a historical document of the Islamic approach to a hostile religious minority in the period of weakness, and it is also a permanent moral teaching: the believer's first response to the provocation of the envious is not rage but self-control. The "Most Capable" at the end is the assurance: the One who will deliver the decision is the One who sees the hate and the envy, and He is fully capable of everything — including the protection of those who are being provoked while they are still commanded to be patient.

In modern life, the verse teaches the response to those who wish ill upon the community — the envious, the hostile, the provocateurs. It does not teach the believer to be naive; it names the motive of the others accurately. It teaches that the response to envy is not the surrender of the truth but the forgiveness of the persons — and the knowledge that the decision belongs to Allah, who sees the envy as clearly as the believers see it, and who will deliver it at the time He has chosen.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 110

Verse 110 says: *"Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the command of patience is the command of action: establish prayer, pay alms-tax. And then the guarantee: whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will certainly find its reward with Allah. The believer who is commanded to be patient in the face of envy is also commanded to be active in the face of the world.

The verse is the answer to a subtle danger. The believers had just been told of the envy of the People of the Book and their wish to turn them from faith. The temptation in such a situation is to become preoccupied with the enemies — to build the religion as a reaction to their hostility, to let the provocation set the agenda. The verse redirects: the believer's life is not defined by the envious; it is defined by the prayer, the alms, and the good that he sends ahead. The response to those who wish you harm is not to orbit their wishes but to build the life that Allah has commanded.

The two commands are the two pillars of the individual and social religion: prayer is the personal connection to the Lord, established at its times and in its form; alms are the social connection to the people, the portion of the wealth that the poor have a right to. The Qur'an joins them constantly — prayer without alms is a religion that stops at the individual; alms without prayer is an ethic without a foundation (the surah has already commanded both together in 2:43 and 2:83). The verse before us belongs to the crisis period of the community, and it is the Qur'an's instruction for the crisis: the believer is not to neglect the prayer and the poor while watching the enemies.

"Whatever good you send forth for yourselves" — the phrase is the Qur'an's image of the afterlife as a banking: what the person sends ahead is what he will find. The good is "for yourselves" — the charity is not a loss but a deposit; the prayer is not a cost but a provision. The verse's guarantee is absolute: "you will certainly find its reward with Allah." Not with the people, not in the reputation, not in the accounting of this world — with Allah, in the place where nothing is lost and nothing is forgotten. The closing "Allah is All-Seeing of what you do" is the ground of the guarantee: the One who sees the deeds knows the deeds, and nothing is omitted from the account.

In modern life, the verse is the cure for the person whose life is organized by the hostility of others. The believer who has been provoked, mocked, envied, and tempted to build his identity in reaction is commanded instead to pray and give, and to send ahead. The enemies may see the prayer and the alms and despise them; the guarantee is not with the enemies. The verse's advice is the same for every person in every conflict: do not let the unjust define your agenda; let the good you send ahead define your life.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 111

Verse 111 says: *"The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.""*

In simple terms, the verse now turns to the mutual exclusivity claims of the People of the Book: the Jews claim that none will enter Paradise except Jews, and the Christians claim that none will enter Paradise except Christians. The Qur'an's answer is in two words: "These are their desires." And then the demand: show your proof if what you say is true.

The verse's first statement is the description. The two communities, each of whom had received a scripture, each of whom claimed the exclusive path to Paradise, were saying — in effect — the same thing about one another. The claim was mutual, and its only basis was the claim itself. The Qur'an's term for the claim is *amānī* — "wishes," "imaginings," "desires" — and it is the same word used in 2:78 for the wishful speculation of the unlettered. The claim of exclusive salvation is not knowledge; it is the wish of the claimant to be saved by the group to which he belongs.

"Show your proof if what you say is true" — the demand is the refutation. A claim about the destination of the soul, the exclusion of others, and the guarantee of one's own group is a claim of the highest seriousness, and the Qur'an demands evidence. The evidence would have to be divine: a scripture, a covenant, a prophecy that establishes the exclusivity. The verse says: if you have it, produce it. The fact that they could not — and the verse knows they cannot — is the point: the claim of exclusive salvation is not something that can be demonstrated; it is something that can only be wished.

The verse also contains a profound correction of the theology of privilege. The Qur'an's own framework has already stated the criterion in 2:62 and restated it in 2:82: whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good — from among the Jews, the Christians, the Sabians, or the believers — will have his reward with his Lord. The verse before us attacks the opposite: the claim that salvation is a matter of group membership rather than of faith and deed. The Qur'an is not saying the groups are equal; it is saying the claims are not evidence.

In modern life, the verse is the diagnosis of group-based certainty. Every community — religious, national, ideological — has its version of the claim: the good are us, and the others are not. The verse names the mechanism by which such claims survive: they are desires, not proofs. The person who has been told that his salvation is guaranteed by his membership, and who never asks for the proof, is living off a wish. The verse's demand — show your proof — is addressed to every group that makes the claim, and to every person who has accepted a claim about himself that he has never examined.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 112

Verse 112 says: *"But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."*

In simple terms, the verse is the positive statement that follows the refutation: the criterion of salvation is not group membership but submission to Allah combined with good deeds. Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good — that person will have his reward with his Lord; there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

The phrase "submits themselves to Allah" — in Arabic *aslama wajhahu lillāh*, literally "submits his face to Allah" — is the origin of the word *islām* and its precise meaning: the person who turns his face, his whole orientation, his entire direction, toward Allah in surrender. The commentators bring out the meaning of sincerity: Abu al-'Aliyah and al-Rabi' bin Anas explained it as "whoever is sincere with Allah"; Sa'id ibn Jubayr explained the "face" as the whole of the religion — the person who is sincere in his submission. The "face" is the noblest part of the person and the part that shows his direction; the Qur'an uses it to mean the whole self turned in one direction.

"And does good" — *wa huwa muḥsin* — the second condition. Submission alone is not enough; the submission must be accompanied by the good that submission produces. The verse joins the two as the Qur'an always joins them: the outward submission (Islam) and the inward goodness (ihsan) — the two are the two halves of the religion, and the person who has one without the other has not the reward the verse describes.

The reward is stated in the exact words that the surah used for the believers of every community in 2:62: "will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve." The repetition is deliberate: the criterion of 2:62 and the criterion of this verse are the same — believe in Allah and the Last Day and do good (2:62); submit to Allah and do good (2:112). The verse before us is the universal principle restated in a way that answers the exclusivity claims: no one is excluded by his label, and no one is saved by his label; the saved are those who have submitted and done good, whether they were born Jews, Christians, Muslims, or pagans.

In modern life, the verse is the definition of religion in one sentence, and it is a warning to every community that has reduced religion to an identity. The person who is a member of the right group but has never submitted — never turned his face to Allah, never surrendered the will, never done the good — has not the reward. And the person who has submitted and done good has the reward regardless of the group that failed to recognize him. The verse's comfort is the same one it gives to the seeker of truth: the door is not locked by the keepers of the claims; it is opened by the submission and the deeds.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 113

Verse 113 says: *"The Jews say, "The Christians have nothing to stand on" and the Christians say, "The Jews have nothing to stand on," although both recite the Scriptures. And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺. Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute."*

In simple terms, the verse now describes the mutual rejection between the Jews and the Christians: the Jews say the Christians have nothing to stand on; the Christians say the Jews have nothing to stand on — although both recite the Scriptures. And the verse adds a third voice: those with no knowledge say the same about people of faith. The verse ends with the verdict: Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute.

The first irony is the mutual recitation. Each community had a scripture; each community claimed the other was without foundation; and each community's scripture contained — as the commentators point out — the testimony that would undermine its claim. Al-Jalalayn's commentary states it precisely: the Jews rejected Jesus, though the Torah they recite contains the covenant to believe in him; the Christians rejected Moses and the Torah's confirmation, though they recite the Gospel. Each party was reading the affirmation of what they denied. The Qur'an's description — "although both recite the Scriptures" — is the exposure: the two groups were not ignorant of each other's foundations; they were reading the evidence and rejecting it.

The second irony is the third voice. The pagans — those who have no knowledge, no scripture — said "the same about people of faith." The Arab polytheists said the same kind of thing about both groups: that they have nothing to stand on. The verse's arrangement is the refutation of the whole pattern: the Jews say the Christians have nothing; the Christians say the Jews have nothing; the pagans say they both have nothing. Everyone rejects everyone on the basis of belonging, and no one has produceable proof.

"Surely Allah will judge between them" — the verse's answer to the dispute is not another community's verdict but the Judge. The Qur'an does not attempt to end the argument by siding in this verse; it locates the argument where it belongs: with Allah, on the Day of Judgment. The verse is a statement of epistemic humility: the justice of the dispute — who was right, who was wrong, what each group genuinely believed and genuinely did — is not for the partisans to settle in this world, but for the One who knows the secrets and the evidence.

In modern life, the verse is a mirror of every community's mutual dismissals. The pattern is universal: each group has its scripture or its doctrine, and each group claims the others stand on nothing. The verse's warning is to take seriously the fact that the dismissing party is itself being dismissed by others, and that the dismissal is as much about belonging as about truth. And the verse's healing is the referral of the dispute to the Judge: the believer does not have to win the argument against everyone in this world; the final verdict belongs to the One who sees what each side actually holds.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 114

Verse 114 says: *"Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah's Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter."*

In simple terms, the verse now asks the sharpest moral question of the section: who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah's Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? The question is not asked for an answer; it is the verdict. The people who do this have no right to enter these places except with fear, and for them there is disgrace in this world and a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.

The occasion is recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim from Ibn Abbas, as quoted by Ibn Kathir: the Quraysh idolaters prevented the Prophet from praying at the Kaaba in al-Masjid al-Haram, and the verse was revealed about them — and about everyone who does the same. The verse's first phrase — "prevent Allah's Name from being mentioned" — is the key: the crime was not the destruction of a building but the prevention of the remembrance of Allah. The mosque is not a building; it is a place where the Name is mentioned, and the one who makes the mention impossible has committed the sin against the Name. The second phrase — "strive to destroy them" — completes the crime: the prevention and the destruction are one movement, the movement of those who want the remembrance to be erased.

"Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear" — the verse describes the reversal: the ones who had tried to bar the remembering of Allah are themselves barred from the places, and the only way they may enter is in fear. The commentators note the double meaning of the phrase: either the wrongdoers can only enter in fear (before the believers' power), or the places of worship are sacred so that no one may enter them except in awe and reverence — and the wrongdoers, whose conduct strips them of the right, are the least entitled to that entrance.

The punishment is twofold: disgrace in this world and the tremendous punishment in the Hereafter. The disgrace of this world — as the history that followed the verse shows — was the deliverance of the house to the remembrance of Allah and the defeat of those who had prevented it. The verse is a statement of divine justice in history: the movement against the remembrance of Allah does not succeed; it brings disgrace on its makers, and the greater account remains.

In modern life, the verse is the basis of the sanctity of worship and the warning against the suppression of the remembrance of Allah. It speaks to every regime, community, or individual that bars the Name from being mentioned, that turns the places of worship into ruins, that makes the remembering of Allah a crime. The verse's answer: no crime is greater, and the history of such movements — whatever their size, whatever their power — ends in the disgrace the verse names and the accounting it leaves for the Hereafter.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 115

Verse 115 says: *"To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse states the ground of the direction of prayer: to Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn, you are facing towards Allah. The verse is one of the Qur'an's most beautiful statements about the relation between the direction and the Divine: the direction is not the object; the One behind every direction is.

The occasion is the change of the qibla. Ibn Kathir's commentary gives the history: when the Prophet migrated to Madinah, he faced Jerusalem (Bayt al-Maqdis) for sixteen or seventeen months, and then Allah directed him to face the Kaaba. The Jews were disturbed by the change — "what made them change the direction of the qibla that they used to face?" — and Allah revealed this verse: to Allah belong both the east and the west. The Jews had claimed that the qibla they faced was exclusive to them — that the Muslims were, in effect, using the Jewish qibla and had no right to it. The verse's answer is that no direction is a possession; the east and the west both belong to Allah.

The scholars also note another understanding of the verse, reported by Ibn Jarir: it permits the one who prays voluntary prayers while travelling, in fear, or facing the enemy to face whatever direction his mount faces — and Ibn 'Umar is reported to have done precisely that, explaining that the Prophet did the same. Ibn Abbas interpreted "wherever you turn, there is the Face of Allah" as "Allah's direction is wherever you face, east or west"; Mujahid interpreted it as "wherever you may be, you have a qibla to face" — meaning the Kaaba. The verse, in other words, is not an abolition of the direction but an establishment of it: the direction is a command, and the command comes from the One to whom all directions belong.

The phrase "the Face of Allah" is one of the Qur'an's theological expressions, and the early commentators handled it with care: the mainstream position is that the face is affirmed as an attribute of Allah in the way that befits Him, and the same texts insist that "nothing is like Him" (42:11). The verse's point for the worshipper is not a spatial claim — that Allah is in every direction — but a spiritual one: the prayer is not an address to a place but a turning to the Lord, and the Lord is not confined by the place. The Allah of the east and the west is the Allah of every turning.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to two errors: the error of making religion a matter of direction and the error of thinking direction does not matter. The verse's first clause establishes the command: there is a direction, and it comes from Allah. Its second clause establishes the meaning: the direction leads to Allah. The believer who prays does not worship the direction; he worships the One the direction points to — and the One who is All-Encompassing and All-Knowing receives the turning from every place on earth.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 116

Verse 116 says: *"They say, "Allah has offspring." Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will."*

In simple terms, the verse now faces the claim that Allah has offspring. The Qur'an begins with "Glory be to Him!" — the exclamation of transcendence, the declaration that Allah is far above such a claim — and then states the refutation: to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and all are subject to His Will.

The claim is one that the Qur'an records from several directions. Ibn Kathir's commentary states that the verse refutes the Christians, and their like among the Jews and the Arab idolaters — the idolaters who claimed that the angels were Allah's daughters. The Qur'an elsewhere records the specific forms: the Christians' claim about Jesus, the Jews' claim about Ezra (9:30), and the pagans' claim about the angels. The verse before us states the matter generally: "They say, 'Allah has offspring'" — the "they" covers the whole range of claimants, and the Qur'an's answer is universal rather than addressed to one sect.

"Glory be to Him!" — *subḥānahu* — is the Qur'an's declaration of the impossibility of the claim. The word *subhan* carries the sense of being free from everything that does not befit Him: from need, from weakness, from companionship, from parents, from offspring, from every attribute of the created. The claim that Allah has offspring is, in the Qur'an's framework, the fundamental error of the attribution: it gives the Creator the characteristics of the creature, and it reduces the One who needs nothing to the condition of those who need everything.

The refutation is then stated in the terms of the relation: "to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth — all are subject to His Will." The word *qānitūn* — "subject to His Will" — means all are submissive, obedient, and in service. The things in the heavens and the earth are not Allah's family; they are His property, His creation, His subjects. Ibn Kathir explains: everything worships Him and is submissive to Him — the sun and the moon, the stars and the mountains, the trees and the creatures — whether in obedience or in the submission of their nature to His laws. The relation is not one of paternity; it is one of ownership and devotion.

The theological point is the deepest one: the claim of offspring arises from a confusion of the Creator with the creation. Those who attribute children to Allah are reasoning from the human order, where a king needs an heir, a father needs a successor, a power needs to be shared. The Qur'an says: the One who owns everything needs nothing; the One who creates everything has no equal (112:1-4); and everything that exists is His subject, not His kin.

In modern life, the verse is the ground of the doctrine of divine unity, and its correction applies to every form of the error: the attributing to Allah any partner, intermediary, or equal — whether a son, a helper, a rival, or a human agent who shares His authority. The verse's formula is the one that the believer carries through life: Glory be to Him, to whom belongs everything, and to whom everything is subject.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 117

Verse 117 says: *"˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth! When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, "Be!" And it is!"*

In simple terms, the verse completes the refutation of the claim of offspring with the strongest argument in the Qur'an's arsenal: Allah is the Originator of the heavens and the earth. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it "Be!" — and it is. The One who creates by a word needs no son to continue His work.

The word *badī'* — "Originator" — is precise. It comes from the root of *ibdā'*, the act of bringing into existence something without precedent, without model, without material. The heavens and the earth were not assembled from existing things; they were originated. The Creator of the new is not like the maker who works with what exists: He is the one who brings forth what had no being. The Qur'an uses the same phrase elsewhere: "the Originator of the heavens and the earth" also appears in 6:101, where the same argument is made.

The second half of the verse is the description of the divine power: "When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, 'Be!' And it is!" The phrase *kun fa-yakūn* is among the most famous expressions in the Qur'an, and it is the answer to every claim that Allah needs an intermediary, a partner, or a helper. The creation of the universe is not a labor that requires assistants; it is an act of decree, and the decree is the act. The verse is used in the Qur'an specifically for the creation of Jesus — the supreme case in which the claimants said Allah had a son: the creation of Jesus is described in the same terms (3:59), and the point is that even the birth without a father — the case the Christians take as the proof of divine sonship — is precisely the case that proves the opposite: it is a sign that Jesus is a creature, brought into being by the word of the Originator.

The verse thus performs the refutation in two movements. The first: the Originator of everything cannot have originated from anything — He has no parents and no children (112:3). The second: if He can create by a single word, He does not need a successor to do what He does — the One who needs no cause cannot be dependent on an heir. The claim of offspring, in the Qur'an's argument, is not merely a false statement about Allah; it is a false model of Allah — the model of the creature projected onto the Creator.

In modern life, the verse is the believer's answer to every suggestion that the divine needs something from the human: the son, the partner, the helper, the intermediary without which the work cannot be done. The verse says the work is done by the word. And it is the ground of the believer's hope in what seems impossible: the same Originator who said "Be" to the heavens and the earth says what He will to the dead, to the barren, to the impossible — and it is.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 118

Verse 118 says: *"Those who have no knowledge say, "If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!" The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith."*

In simple terms, the verse now records the demand of the ignorant: "If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!" The demand is made by those who have no knowledge — and the Qur'an's answer is that the same demand was made by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. And the sign is already clear — for those of sure faith.

The demand "if only Allah would speak to us" is the demand for a direct, personal, and unambiguous communication. The Qur'an's answer begins with the observation that the demand is not original: "the same was said by those who came before." Every generation that received a prophet, a book, and a sign said the same: if only Allah would speak to us. The objection is not new; it is the oldest objection in the history of revelation, and its repetition is the proof that it is not a search for truth but a strategy of postponement. The person who says "if only Allah would speak to me directly" is not asking for guidance; he is asking for an exemption from the guidance that has already come.

"Their hearts are all alike." The verse states the diagnosis: the similarity of the demand is the similarity of the heart. The people who make the demand are not distinguished by their situation — they are distinguished by a heart that will not accept the message unless the message is delivered on its terms. The demand for the sign is not evidence of a seeking heart; it is the evidence of the heart that has already decided what it will accept.

"We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith" — *li-qawmin yūqinūn* — the verse's final clause states the condition: the signs are clear, and they are clear for the people of certainty. The Qur'an's position is not that the signs are ambiguous; it is that the surety of the people is the condition of seeing them. The word *yaqīn* — certainty — is a quality of the heart, not a quantity of data. The person who has the data without the certainty will continue to demand another sign; the person who has the certainty has already seen the signs that the demander is pretending not to see.

In modern life, the verse names the modern form of the oldest objection: "if only there were more evidence," "if only the message were clearer," "if only the proof were airtight." The verse's answer is that the people who said this before had prophets, books, and miracles — and they still said it. The demand is not about the evidence; it is about the heart. The remedy for the demand is the very thing the demander is avoiding: the submission that turns the heart into a heart of certainty — the heart for which the signs are already clear.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 119

Verse 119 says: *"We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire."*

In simple terms, the verse states the mission of the Prophet and its limits: he was sent with the truth, as a deliverer of good news and a warner; and he will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire. The verse defines both the task and the boundary.

The task is defined by three words: truth, good news, warner. "With the truth" — *bil-ḥaqq* — the messenger does not bring the community's preferences, its traditions, or its compromises; he brings the truth. The good news and the warning are the two forms of the truth: the promise for the believers and the word for those who refuse. The Qur'an uses the phrase for the relation between the two: the good news is not the message without the warning, and the warning is not the message without the good news. The messenger is both, and the person who removes either has truncated the mission.

The limit is stated with equal clarity: "you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire." The verse was revealed — as the context shows — at a time when the rejection of the message was painful to the Prophet, and when he might have been tempted to carry the burden of the rejecters' choices. The verse removes the burden: the messenger's responsibility is the delivery, not the conversion. He has done his part when the message has been conveyed; the residents of the Fire are not the product of his failure but of their own rejection.

The verse is thus the Qur'an's doctrine of accountability: every soul bears its own responsibility (the principle the surah will state strongly in the later verses: "Every soul will be held in pledge for its deeds"). The Prophet is not the guarantor of the people's faith; he is the deliverer of the message. The person who rejects the message is responsible for the rejection — and the person who delivers it is responsible for the delivery.

In modern life, the verse is the cure for the two extremes of missionary psychology: the arrogance that claims credit for the conversions and the despair that claims blame for the rejections. The messenger — the teacher, the parent, the preacher, the friend who calls to the truth — has a task and a boundary. The task: to deliver with clarity and with the character of the truth. The boundary: the outcome is not his. The verse's relief is real: the believer who has carried the truth to another and seen it rejected is not responsible for the rejection; and the believer who had no opportunity to hear is not the object of the verse's warning.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 120

Verse 120 says: *"Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you, until you follow their faith. Say, "Allah's guidance is the only ˹true˺ guidance." And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah."*

In simple terms, the verse states the truth about the demand of the People of the Book: they will never be pleased with you until you follow their faith. The statement is not an opinion about their character; it is a statement about the structure of their demand. And the verse then sets the answer and the warning: the guidance of Allah is the only true guidance; and if you follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah.

"Never" — the verse's opening is absolute. The satisfaction of the people of the Book is not achievable by partial conformity; it is not achievable by being closer to their position; it is not achievable by compromise. The demand is total: follow their faith. The verse is one of the most important statements in the Qur'an about the nature of such demands: because the demand is not for agreement but for adoption, no amount of accommodation will satisfy it. The person who gives up one practice to please them will be asked for the next, until the whole religion is given away.

The answer is the sentence at the centre: "Allah's guidance is the only true guidance." The verse's *hudā Allāh* — the guidance of Allah — is not one guidance among several; it is the guidance. The Qur'an is the verse's context: the Prophet and the believers had been given the truth, and the People of the Book were asking them to trade it. The answer: there is no guidance apart from Allah's; the religion is not a market in which the direction is negotiable.

The warning is the most severe in this section: "if you were to follow their desires after all the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah." The clause names the condition precisely: "after all the knowledge." The believer would not be making the trade from ignorance; he would be making it with full knowledge of what he is abandoning — the same situation as the Jews who rejected the messenger they recognized (2:89), and the same result: the loss of every protector and helper.

In modern life, the verse is the Qur'an's answer to the religion of accommodation. The pressure on every community is the same: adopt our practices, soften your doctrines, adjust your message, and we will be pleased. The verse says the satisfaction will not come. The peace of the believer is not in the pleasure of the demanding parties; it is in the guidance of Allah. And the verse's warning is for those who are tempted: the trade made after knowledge removes the protection of the One who was the only protector.
