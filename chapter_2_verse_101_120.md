## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:101

> Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them—confirming their own Scriptures—some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs as if they did not know.

**Commentary:**

### Recognition Followed by Rejection

The verse describes a painful contradiction. A messenger came from Allah confirming the truth of the scriptures they already possessed, yet some of the People of the Book cast Allah’s Book behind their backs. The image is one of deliberate disregard: something valuable is treated as though it were worthless and placed out of sight. Their rejection was not caused by complete unfamiliarity. They recognized enough of the message to know that it challenged their chosen position.

“Confirming their own Scriptures” refers to the Qur’an’s continuity with the original revelation given to earlier prophets. It confirms the oneness of Allah, the reality of revelation, the prophetic tradition, moral accountability, and many signs concerning the final messenger. Confirmation does not mean that the Qur’an accepts every later interpretation or textual alteration without correction. It confirms the truth preserved in earlier revelation and acts as a criterion over what people attribute to it (5:48).

The expression “some of the People of the Book” is important. The Qur’an does not condemn every Jew or Christian as one undifferentiated group. It acknowledges upright people among them who recite Allah’s revelations, believe in Allah, and hasten to good deeds (3:113–115). The criticism here concerns those who knowingly rejected the confirming revelation, not an ethnic or religious licence for collective hatred.

### Casting the Book Behind the Back

To cast the Book behind the back is to refuse its authority after it has become clear. A person may respect scripture as a historical treasure, quote it when convenient, or preserve its outward form while ignoring its message. In that case the Book remains physically present but functionally discarded. The same danger can affect Muslims who honour the Qur’an in speech but place its commands behind political loyalty, commercial interest, family custom, or personal desire.

The image also suggests selective memory. The People of the Book wanted to retain the portions of scripture that confirmed their status while ignoring the passages that required them to recognize the final messenger. This is not faith in revelation as a whole; it is the use of revelation as a tool for protecting identity. The Qur’an later condemns those who believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest (2:85).

The phrase “as if they did not know” emphasizes responsibility. They possessed knowledge that should have led to recognition, but they behaved as though the evidence had never reached them. Knowledge can be pushed aside through pride, social pressure, and fear of losing authority. The heart’s problem is not always lack of information; sometimes it is unwillingness to accept what the information demands.

### The Messenger as a Witness to Earlier Truth

The Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) did not come to erase the genuine missions of Moses and Jesus. He came as the final messenger in a chain that began with Adam and continued through the prophets. The Qur’an calls people to believe in all the messengers and not to divide them into accepted and rejected categories (2:136; 4:150–152). Rejecting the final messenger while claiming to honour the earlier ones creates a contradiction because the earlier revelations themselves prepared people for continued guidance.

The Qur’an’s confirmation also protects believers from a false choice between respect for previous prophets and acceptance of Islam. Muslims honour Moses, Jesus, Abraham, and the other messengers as servants and messengers of Allah. They do not need to reject their truth in order to follow Muhammad. Rather, following the final messenger is understood as continuing the original call to worship Allah alone.

### A Warning About Religious Authority

The people who cast the Book away had influence over others. When scholars, teachers, or leaders reject truth, their personal failure can become a public one. People may follow them because of inherited trust, institutional authority, or fear of challenging the group. The Qur’an therefore repeatedly warns those who conceal scripture or alter it for worldly gain (2:79; 3:187).

This does not mean that every disagreement between scholars is concealment. Honest interpretation, uncertainty, and qualified disagreement are part of serious learning. The sin is knowingly discarding or misrepresenting clear revelation because it threatens a preferred identity or advantage. The believer should therefore distinguish the text itself, its authentic explanation, and the opinions of later interpreters.

### The Cost of Refusal

The verse prepares for the discussion of magic, which follows as another example of turning away from revealed guidance and pursuing forbidden alternatives. When people cast revelation behind them, they do not become neutral. They often replace divine guidance with superstition, political calculation, ancestral custom, or personal desire. The vacuum left by revelation is quickly filled by something else.

The lesson is to keep the Book before the heart rather than behind the back. Read it with humility, seek sound explanation, accept correction, and allow its commands to judge the reader. Recognition is a gift, but acting upon recognition is the responsibility that fulfils it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:75, 2:89, and 2:146 on recognizing truth and then concealing or rejecting it.
- Qur’an 2:85 on accepting some of scripture while rejecting other parts.
- Qur’an 3:3–4 and 5:44–48 on confirmation, correction, and the Qur’an as a criterion.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book and the need to avoid blanket judgments.
- Qur’an 2:79 and 3:187 on altering or concealing revelation for worldly advantage.
- For the image of casting the Book behind one’s back, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:101.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:102

> They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, “We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.” Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!

**Commentary:**

### Solomon Was Not a Magician

After describing people who cast Allah’s Book behind their backs, the Qur’an turns to their pursuit of magic. It first clears Prophet Solomon (Sulaymān, ʿalayhi al-salām) of the accusation that he was a magician or disbeliever. Some people had attributed Solomon’s extraordinary authority and knowledge to sorcery. The Qur’an rejects the accusation categorically: Solomon did not disbelieve; rather, the devils disbelieved.

Solomon’s kingdom was a miracle and a gift from Allah. The wind, the jinn, and other forces were subjected to him by Allah’s command (21:81–82; 38:35–40). A prophetic miracle is not magic, and a prophet does not obtain authority through forbidden occult practices. The distinction matters because magic seeks hidden power through unlawful means, deception, or reliance upon devils, while a miracle is an act Allah grants to support a messenger and establish truth.

### The Devils and the Teaching of Magic

The verse attributes the teaching of magic to devils. Magic is not portrayed as a harmless skill or entertainment when it involves occult claims, unlawful practices, deception, or seeking assistance from forces opposed to Allah. The Qur’an describes people learning what harms them and does not benefit them, despite knowing that this pursuit would cost them their share in the Hereafter.

The verse also says that magic was connected with what was revealed to the two angels Hārūt and Mārūt in Babylon. The wording has been interpreted by classical scholars in several ways, and the reports surrounding the passage vary in strength and detail. The Qur’an’s certain teaching is that the two angels warned anyone who approached them: “We are only a test, so do not abandon your faith.” They did not invite people to magic without warning, nor did they authorize it as a path to spiritual power.

The warning is crucial. A test can involve knowledge of a dangerous matter without making the danger lawful. A physician may explain poison, and a judge may study a crime, without approving either. The people’s responsibility lay in choosing to learn and use what Allah had made a trial after being warned not to abandon faith. The verse does not portray the angels as disbelievers; it identifies the devils’ disbelief and the people’s misuse of tested knowledge.

### The Harm of Magic and Allah’s Sovereignty

The Qur’an specifically mentions magic causing separation between husband and wife. Marriage is a covenant of tranquillity, affection, and mercy (30:21), so attempting to destroy it through occult means is a direct attack upon family life. The example may include psychological manipulation, deception, or a genuine occult harm, but the moral point is the same: a person must not seek to control or destroy relationships through forbidden means.

The verse immediately limits the effect: they cannot harm anyone except by Allah’s permission. This does not make magic permissible or harmless. It places it within Allah’s universal sovereignty. No magician, jinn, or hidden force can operate independently of Allah. The believer therefore neither denies the Qur’anic warning nor falls into superstition that gives created beings absolute power.

Seeking protection from magic is done through lawful means: faith, prayer, remembrance, recitation of the Qur’an, legitimate medical and psychological care, and consultation with trustworthy scholars and professionals. Fraudulent “healers” who exploit fear, demand occult rituals, or accuse innocent people cause additional harm. The Qur’an’s teaching should lead to tawḥīd and responsible care, not panic and exploitation.

### Knowledge That Harms

The people learned what harmed them and did not benefit them. Knowledge is not automatically praiseworthy merely because it is knowledge. A person can learn techniques for fraud, oppression, manipulation, or destruction. The moral value of knowledge depends upon its truth, purpose, means, and use. Islam encourages beneficial learning and condemns knowledge that is pursued to harm people or defy Allah.

The verse says they already knew that whoever bought into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Their sin was therefore compounded by conscious exchange. They traded faith, family security, and eternal hope for an uncertain worldly advantage. The Qur’an uses the language of purchase because the choice was intentional: they bought a temporary illusion at the price of their souls.

This warning extends beyond occult practice. Any knowledge used to corrupt worship, exploit the vulnerable, destroy families, or deceive the public can become a form of the same failed transaction. The believer asks not only, “Can this be done?” but, “Is it lawful, beneficial, and pleasing to Allah?”

### Avoiding Myths and False Certainty

The passage should not be used to validate every story about magic, hidden beings, or alleged supernatural harm. The Qur’an establishes what Allah has revealed and leaves many details unspecified. Reports about the identities, actions, or exact teachings of Hārūt and Mārūt must be assessed by the standards of ḥadīth and tafsīr rather than repeated uncritically.

The safe position is to affirm the Qur’an’s warning, reject magic as a lawful path, recognize Allah’s absolute power, and avoid superstition. A person who is frightened should receive compassion and sound help, not accusations. The true protection is faith and obedience, not fascination with the very practices the verse condemns.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 21:81–82 and 38:35–40 on Solomon’s kingdom as a divine gift and miracle, not magic.
- Qur’an 7:116 on magic and illusion, and 10:81–82 on Allah rendering false magic ineffective.
- Qur’an 113:4 on seeking refuge from those who blow upon knots, a principal Qur’anic reference to harmful occult practice.
- Qur’an 30:21 on marriage as tranquillity, affection, and mercy.
- Qur’an 17:36 and 28:80 on not pursuing unsupported or harmful knowledge.
- For Hārūt and Mārūt, the warning that they were a test, and the evaluation of later reports, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:102.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:103

> If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew!

**Commentary:**

### The Better Alternative

After describing the people who pursued magic at the expense of faith, the Qur’an presents the better alternative: faith and taqwā. The phrase “if only they knew” is not a doubt in Allah’s knowledge. It expresses the tragedy of people who possess enough understanding to choose well but allow desire, fear, or illusion to hide the superior reward from them.

Faith would have redirected them from forbidden power to reliance upon Allah. Taqwā would have restrained them from practices that harmed families and corrupted the heart. Instead of seeking control through magic, they could have sought protection through prayer, honest counsel, lawful treatment, and patient trust. Their loss was not only the punishment of the Hereafter; they also exchanged peace of mind and moral integrity for fear and deception in this world.

### Taqwā Protects Knowledge

The previous verse showed that knowledge can harm when it is pursued for unlawful purposes. Taqwā gives knowledge a moral direction. It teaches the learner to ask whether an ability should be used, whose rights may be affected, and whether the means are pleasing to Allah. Without taqwā, intelligence can become a servant of appetite, pride, or power.

The Qur’an promises that those who are mindful of Allah receive discernment—*furqān*—by which they can distinguish truth from falsehood (8:29). This is not a promise that every mindful person becomes an expert in every technical matter. It means that taqwā protects judgment from being wholly controlled by desire. A person may know how to produce something and still need moral guidance about whether producing it is right.

### A Better Reward with Allah

The reward with Allah is better than any short-term gain magic or manipulation might appear to offer. The Qur’an repeatedly contrasts the little enjoyment of the world with what Allah has prepared for the faithful. “Whatever you have will end, but what Allah has is lasting” (16:96). A worldly advantage obtained through disobedience is unstable; Allah’s reward includes forgiveness, protection from the Fire, and nearness to Him.

This does not mean that the believer will never experience difficulty. Taqwā may require refusing profitable deception, leaving an exploitative occupation, or enduring the loss of a relationship built upon wrongdoing. But the loss is temporary, while the reward is lasting. The believer trusts that Allah does not allow the work of the righteous to be wasted (18:30).

### Knowledge, Faith, and Beneficial Action

The verse joins faith and taqwā because belief must become a governing awareness. Faith identifies Allah as the only true source of power and guidance; taqwā makes that recognition practical. It restrains the hand from harmful acts, the tongue from false claims, and the mind from turning revelation into an instrument of personal control.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that whoever Allah intends good for, He gives him understanding of religion (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Understanding is a gift when it leads to worship and benefit. A person who collects religious or technical information only to dominate others has not received its full good. The best knowledge produces humility and service.

### “If Only They Knew”

The closing phrase invites reflection on consequences before the choice is made. People often recognize the value of a good path only after the cost of a bad path becomes visible. Revelation discloses that cost in advance so that the believer can choose wisely. The warning is especially powerful in relation to magic because the practice may promise hidden control while actually deepening dependence upon deception and exposing the practitioner to spiritual ruin.

The same principle applies to every forbidden gain. If people knew the full value of Allah’s forgiveness, lawful provision, family peace, and eternal reward, they would not exchange them for a temporary advantage. The Qur’an calls the reader to know this now, before the failed transaction becomes irreversible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 8:29 on taqwā bringing discernment.
- Qur’an 16:96 and 18:30 on Allah’s lasting reward and the preservation of righteous deeds.
- Qur’an 49:13 on taqwā as the measure of honour.
- Qur’an 39:18 and 20:114 on beneficial understanding and seeking knowledge.
- Qur’an 64:16 and 3:102 on mindfulness and obedience according to ability.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on Allah granting understanding of religion as a sign of good.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:104

> O believers! Do not say, “Râ’ina.” [Herd us!] But say, “Unẓurna,” [Tend to us!] and listen ˹attentively˺. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Language and Intention

Allah instructs the believers not to use the expression *rāʿinā* and instead to say *unẓurnā* while listening attentively. In Arabic, *rāʿinā* could be intended to mean “consider us” or “give us time to understand,” but it could also be twisted in pronunciation into an insulting expression in another usage. Some opponents exploited the ambiguity to mock the Prophet (ﷺ) while pretending to speak respectfully. The believers were therefore taught a clearer expression that carried no double meaning.

The command illustrates the importance of both intention and social effect. A phrase may be innocent in one language or context but offensive or manipulative in another. Believers should avoid expressions that can reasonably be used to insult, confuse, or undermine a person, especially when a clear alternative is available. Good communication seeks clarity rather than hiding behind technical deniability.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ also mentions people who distort words and use their tongues to attack the Prophet (4:46). The issue is not that Islam forbids every unfamiliar word or every linguistic difference. It is that people must not use ambiguity as a tool of disrespect, and believers should not knowingly provide such a tool when a better expression is available.

### “Look Upon Us” and Attentive Listening

*Unẓurnā* can mean “look upon us,” “give us time,” or “pay attention to us.” It communicates the legitimate request for the Prophet to explain and allow the listeners to understand. The verse pairs the permitted expression with “listen attentively”—*wa ismaʿū*. The believer is not only to choose respectful words; he is also to listen when guidance is recited.

This is an etiquette of learning. Students should ask for clarification without sarcasm, and teachers should give people time to understand. Listeners should not attend merely to find a phrase to mock or a technical error to exploit. The Qur’an praises those who listen and follow the best of what they hear (39:18), while warning against people who possess ears but refuse to understand (7:179).

Attentive listening is especially important in religious discussion. A person may hear a verse while preparing his rebuttal rather than asking what it truly means. He may interrupt, misquote, or judge an entire teaching from a phrase removed from its context. The command to listen is therefore an act of humility before truth.

### Protecting the Prophet’s Honour

The instruction also protects the dignity of the Prophet (ﷺ) and the order of the believing community. The opponents’ wordplay attempted to turn ordinary conversation into an opportunity for humiliation. Allah did not leave the believers to respond with uncontrolled anger; He taught them a precise linguistic alternative and an ethic of listening.

This is a model for responding to provocation. Believers may clarify, set boundaries, and defend sacred matters, but they should avoid becoming trapped in the style of those who mock. The Qur’an commands people to speak words that are upright (33:70) and to respond to ignorance with what is better (41:34). Clear speech and self-control are stronger than reciprocal abuse.

### The Broader Principle of Respectful Speech

Words create social realities. A nickname can humiliate, a legal phrase can conceal exploitation, and a religious slogan can incite hostility. The Qur’an commands believers not to mock one another, insult one another, or call each other by offensive names (49:11). It also teaches that a kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury (2:263).

The replacement of *rāʿinā* with *unẓurnā* is therefore not a trivial vocabulary lesson. It teaches a general discipline: when a phrase is vulnerable to abuse, choose a clearer one; when speaking to a teacher, elder, or vulnerable person, use respect; when a conflict arises, do not hide cruelty behind literal meanings. The believer is accountable for intention and for foreseeable harm.

### Warning Against Disbelief and Mockery

The verse closes with a warning to disbelievers who use language to mock or undermine revelation. It does not command believers to identify every awkward speaker as a disbeliever. It warns those who deliberately turn religious communication into a vehicle for contempt. The Qur’an takes mockery seriously because it trains the heart to despise what should be approached with reverence.

The believer responds by learning, listening, and speaking clearly. He does not use religious vocabulary to make a person feel small, and he does not allow ambiguity to become a hiding place for insult. The tongue is a trust, and respectful speech is part of faith.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:46 on distorting words and using speech to mock or attack.
- Qur’an 33:70 and 41:34 on upright speech and responding to ignorance with what is better.
- Qur’an 39:18 and 7:179 on attentive listening and understanding.
- Qur’an 49:11–12 on avoiding mockery, offensive names, and suspicion.
- Qur’an 2:263 on kind speech being better than charity followed by injury.
- For the linguistic background of *rāʿinā* and *unẓurnā*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:104.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:105

> The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord, but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

**Commentary:**

### Envy of Divine Favour

The verse tells the believers that some disbelievers among the People of the Book and the polytheists did not want them to receive any blessing from Allah. The statement concerns particular opponents and a specific attitude; it is not a command to treat every Jew, Christian, or polytheist as personally hostile. The Qur’an elsewhere commands Muslims to deal kindly and justly with those who do not fight them because of religion (60:8), and it praises upright People of the Book (3:113–115).

The blessing in context includes revelation, prophethood, guidance, the Qur’an, and the formation of a believing community. Some opponents resented that these favours had been given through Muhammad (ﷺ) and to the Arabs rather than remaining within their expected religious or ethnic channels. Envy did not necessarily deny Allah’s existence; it objected to Allah’s choice of recipient.

The Qur’an identifies this illness elsewhere: many of the People of the Book wished to turn believers back to disbelief out of envy after the truth had become clear (2:109). Envy is spiritually destructive because it treats another person’s blessing as a personal loss. It implies that divine generosity should have been distributed according to the envious person’s preference.

### Allah Chooses His Mercy

Allah selects whom He wills for His mercy. Prophethood, revelation, guidance, forgiveness, and opportunities to serve are gifts from Him. Human beings cannot claim ownership over divine grace or demand that Allah send it only through their group. The Qur’an asks, “Do they distribute the mercy of your Lord?” (43:32), reminding people that divine choice exceeds their social calculations.

“Whom He wills” does not mean arbitrary selection without wisdom. Allah’s will is joined to perfect knowledge and wisdom. He knows which messenger, community, circumstance, and moment will fulfil the purpose of guidance. People may not understand every aspect of the choice, but they are not entitled to accuse Allah of unfairness because the blessing reached someone else.

The same principle applies to every blessing: knowledge, wealth, influence, health, children, and the ability to do good. A believer does not resent another’s favour or assume that Allah’s generosity is a limited supply. He asks for blessing, thanks Allah for what he has, and uses his own gifts responsibly.

### Envy and the Health of Community

Envy can become a political force. It may lead a person to spread rumours, undermine a successful neighbour, oppose a righteous reformer, or reinterpret truth simply because someone else is associated with it. A community infected by envy wastes energy fighting the recipients of good rather than benefiting from the good itself.

The cure is to distinguish between permissible aspiration and blameworthy envy. The Prophet (ﷺ) described a form of permissible *ghibṭah*: wishing for a blessing like another person’s without wishing that it be removed, especially in the case of knowledge used well and wealth spent in charity (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This kind of aspiration turns admiration into effort. Envy wants the other person diminished; wholesome aspiration wants oneself improved.

The believer also remembers that the highest mercy is guidance. Material differences may be temporary and difficult to interpret. A person who appears deprived may be rich in faith, and a person who appears favoured may be undergoing a severe test. Allah’s selection of mercy cannot be measured solely by worldly comfort.

### The Lord of Infinite Bounty

The verse ends with *dhū al-faḍl al-ʿaẓīm*—Allah is the Lord of great or infinite bounty. His generosity is not depleted when He gives. The opponents’ envy was based on scarcity thinking, as though another community’s guidance reduced their own possibility of mercy. Allah’s bounty is vast enough to guide, forgive, and provide for all who sincerely turn to Him.

This conclusion also comforts the Prophet and believers facing rejection. Their guidance is not invalidated because opponents dislike it. The blessing came from Allah and is protected by His wisdom. The believer should respond to envy with gratitude, patience, and continued obedience rather than with envy in return.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:90 and 2:109 on envy of divine favour and attempts to turn believers away.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book.
- Qur’an 4:54 on envying people for what Allah has given them of His bounty.
- Qur’an 43:31–32 on Allah’s choice of messengers and the distribution of His mercy.
- Qur’an 60:8–9 on kindness and justice toward those who do not fight believers.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on permissible aspiration toward knowledge and charitable wealth without wishing another person’s blessing removed.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:106

> If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?

**Commentary:**

### Abrogation in Revelation

This verse addresses objections to changes in revealed rulings. *Naskh*—abrogation—refers to Allah replacing a ruling, recitation, or directive with another according to His wisdom. The verse says that what is replaced is followed by something better or similar. “Better” can mean more suitable for the new circumstance, easier for the community, greater in reward, or more beneficial in guidance. It does not mean that the earlier command was defective; it was appropriate for the period and purpose for which Allah revealed it.

The Qur’an itself gives an example in the gradual legislation concerning intoxicants. The community was first told that they contain great sin and some benefits, then prohibited from approaching prayer while intoxicated, and finally commanded to avoid them altogether (2:219; 4:43; 5:90–91). The stages educated a community and prepared it to accept the final ruling. Gradual legislation can be a form of mercy and wisdom rather than contradiction.

Scholars discussed several kinds of abrogation: replacement of a ruling while the verse remains recited, replacement of recitation and ruling, and cases in which a ruling is understood to have been superseded by a later revelation. These technical classifications should be handled carefully, and claims of abrogation should not be made simply because two verses require interpretation or address different circumstances. The Qur’an’s apparent differences are resolved through context, chronology, language, and the prophetic explanation.

### Revelation Is Not a Change in Divine Knowledge

Abrogation does not imply that Allah learned something new or changed His mind. Allah’s knowledge is perfect and eternal. He knew the earlier ruling, the later ruling, the community’s development, and the final wisdom from the beginning. The change occurs in the obligation applied to human beings, not in Allah’s knowledge. A teacher may give different instructions to a beginner and an advanced student without discovering new facts; the variation may be part of the plan from the start.

The Qur’an says, “Allah eliminates or confirms what He wills, and with Him is the foundation of the Book” (13:39). This affirms divine authority over legislation while preserving the stability of His complete knowledge. The verse’s final question—“Do you not know that Allah is capable of everything?”—reminds people that the One who created the universe can legislate for changing human circumstances.

### Better or Similar Does Not Mean Contradictory

The later ruling does not make the earlier revelation false. Both were true when Allah commanded them. The community’s responsibility changes because Allah’s revealed instruction changes. The Qur’an says that each community was given a law and way, and that Allah could have made humanity one community but tests them in what He has given (5:48).

The principle also prevents people from treating religious law as a frozen human system detached from revelation. Allah is the Lawgiver. Human beings must not invent abrogation to remove commands they dislike, but they must also not deny a valid change that Allah and His Messenger established. Sound scholarship requires evidence, not convenience.

### “Forgotten” and the Preservation of Revelation

The phrase “cause it to be forgotten” has been interpreted as removal from recitation or allowing a verse to be forgotten by the Prophet or community in accordance with Allah’s decree. The Qur’an also promises the Prophet that Allah will preserve the revelation in his recitation except what He wills (87:6–7). These passages belong to the doctrine of revelation and its preservation; they are not permission for later people to edit the Qur’an according to personal preference.

The Qur’an’s preservation means that the final scripture remains the authoritative guidance for humanity. Abrogation occurred through revelation during the prophetic period, not through the later opinions of rulers or scholars. After the Prophet’s death, no person may claim new revelation or cancel a clear Qur’anic obligation.

### Obedience and Trust

The verse was relevant to opponents who resented the change of certain practices or the transfer of religious leadership to the final Prophet. It teaches believers to trust Allah’s wisdom while seeking to understand the reason and scope of a ruling. The proper response to a confirmed command is obedience, not the assumption that a change proves inconsistency.

The topic requires humility because abrogation is a specialized matter. A verse that seems to conflict with another may be harmonized through context or may refer to a different audience. The believer should rely on qualified scholarship and authenticated prophetic evidence, not on quick claims that a difficult command has been cancelled.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 16:101 on Allah replacing one revelation with another according to wisdom.
- Qur’an 13:39 and 87:6–7 on Allah confirming, removing, and preserving revelation.
- Qur’an 2:219, 4:43, and 5:90–91 on the gradual legislation concerning intoxicants.
- Qur’an 5:48 on different laws for communities and the Qur’an as a criterion.
- Qur’an 17:86 and 41:42 on Allah’s control and protection of revelation.
- For the categories and limits of *naskh*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and al-Suyūṭī’s al-Itqān fī ʿulūm al-Qurʾān.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:107

> Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?

**Commentary:**

### Allah Alone Owns the Kingdom

After explaining that Allah has authority to replace one ruling with another, the Qur’an reminds the reader that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs solely to Him. Legislation is an expression of lordship. The One who creates, sustains, and knows His creatures has the right to guide and command them. Human beings do not possess an independent authority to overrule Allah’s revelation because they prefer a different system.

The word *mulk* means dominion, ownership, authority, and sovereign control. Human beings may possess property or exercise delegated authority, but their ownership is temporary and limited. Every power, government, institution, and individual remains within Allah’s kingdom. The Qur’an says, “Say: O Allah, Owner of sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will and take sovereignty away from whom You will” (3:26).

This does not deny human responsibility to govern, consult, administer, and make judgments. It means that human authority is a trust under Allah, not an independent source of moral permission. A ruler cannot make oppression just by legal decree, and a majority cannot turn falsehood into truth by voting for it. The believer respects legitimate authority while recognizing that no authority is above Allah’s command.

### No Guardian or Helper Besides Him

The verse says that people have no *walī* or helper besides Allah. A *walī* may be a protector, guardian, close ally, or patron. Created beings can help one another in ordinary and lawful ways, and Islam encourages mutual assistance. The verse denies that any created being possesses independent power to guarantee salvation, control destiny, or protect someone against Allah’s judgment.

This corrects both idolatry and political absolutism. People may seek protection from parents, friends, doctors, institutions, and governments, but they must know that all such means are limited and dependent upon Allah. No saint, angel, ruler, wealth, or social group can become an independent source of ultimate security. “If Allah touches you with harm, none can remove it except Him; and if He intends good for you, none can repel His bounty” (10:107).

The statement also provides emotional strength. The believer may lose human support, face rejection, or see a powerful opponent succeed, but he has not lost the Lord of the heavens and earth. Allah can use created helpers, but the heart relies upon Him rather than upon them. This is the meaning of *tawakkul*: taking lawful means while entrusting the outcome to Allah.

### Authority and Revelation

The verse follows the discussion of abrogation because opponents objected to Allah’s changing a ruling or revealing guidance through Muhammad (ﷺ). Allah answers that sovereignty is His. He may legislate for humanity according to His knowledge and wisdom, and people are not entitled to demand that divine guidance conform to their expectations.

This principle also protects the Qur’an from human manipulation. Scholars explain revelation, but they do not own it. Governments may administer religious institutions, but they cannot rewrite revelation. Communities may preserve traditions, but they cannot make tradition equal to Allah’s command. The proper hierarchy is clear: human authority is accountable to revelation, and revelation belongs to Allah.

### Dependence as Dignity

Human dependence is not a humiliation. It is the truth of created existence. People depend upon food, air, family, knowledge, and one another, and all of these dependencies ultimately return to Allah. The Qur’an says, “O humanity, you are the ones in need of Allah, while Allah is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy” (35:15).

When the believer knows that Allah alone is the ultimate guardian, he can ask people for help without worshipping them and accept help without treating the helper as a rival to Allah. He can resist tyranny without imagining that worldly power is ultimate, and he can accept loss without believing that Allah’s care has ended.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:26–27 and 5:120 on Allah’s exclusive ownership of sovereignty.
- Qur’an 10:107 and 35:15 on Allah as the ultimate source of harm, benefit, and human dependence.
- Qur’an 2:255 on intercession and authority existing only by Allah’s permission.
- Qur’an 6:62 and 17:111 on return to the true sovereign and the rejection of independent partners.
- Qur’an 57:2–5 on Allah’s kingdom, knowledge, and presence over creation.
- For the relation between divine sovereignty and abrogation, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:107.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:108

> Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way.

**Commentary:**

### Questions That Seek Guidance and Questions That Resist

The verse warns the believers against asking their Messenger in the same obstructive manner that Moses was asked. The Qur’an records that the Children of Israel demanded to see Allah, requested extraordinary signs, and repeatedly challenged Moses after clear guidance had come (2:55; 7:138). The warning is not against sincere questions or the search for understanding. It is against questions used to test, delay, ridicule, or avoid obedience.

The Qur’an itself encourages people to ask those who possess knowledge (16:43), and the Companions asked the Prophet (ﷺ) many questions in order to learn their religion. The distinction lies in motive and effect. A sincere question clarifies a duty and makes obedience possible. A hostile question adds impossible conditions, demands a spectacle, or treats the messenger as though he must satisfy the questioner’s ego before the questioner will submit.

Earlier in this sūrah, the Children of Israel’s questions about the cow turned a simple command into a difficult task. Here the warning broadens the lesson. A community must not make revelation difficult by demanding unnecessary details or refusing to obey what has already been made clear. The Qur’an says that Allah has pardoned matters He left unmentioned, so believers should not pursue questions that create needless hardship (5:101).

### The Messenger Is Not a Defendant Before Human Desire

A messenger conveys Allah’s command; he does not negotiate revelation according to the audience’s preferences. The Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) was asked for signs and concessions by opponents, but the Qur’an taught him that his duty was to convey and warn. The messenger can explain, demonstrate, and answer, but he cannot turn divine guidance into a product that must satisfy every demand.

This is also an instruction about the community’s relationship with its prophet. Muslims must ask about worship, ethics, law, and the meaning of revelation, but they should not treat the Prophet as an object of suspicion or as a source of entertainment. Questions should lead to learning and practice. A person who asks only to find an escape route has already decided not to obey.

The verse does not prohibit seeking evidence. Abraham asked Allah to show him how He gives life to the dead, and Allah answered his request because Abraham sought reassurance, not rebellion (2:260). The distinction is therefore not between questions and no questions, but between humble inquiry and defiant testing.

### Trading Belief for Disbelief

The verse concludes that whoever trades belief for disbelief has strayed from the straight path. The commercial metaphor connects this verse with the earlier description of people who traded guidance for misguidance (2:16). A person can lose faith through repeated demands that make obedience conditional upon receiving a particular sign, social advantage, or emotional experience.

The trade may occur when a person abandons a clear command because a friend mocks it, rejects revelation to retain political power, or treats inherited identity as more important than truth. The loss is severe because belief is exchanged for a temporary benefit. The Qur’an says that those who purchase disbelief at the price of faith have gained nothing and will face painful punishment (3:177).

### A Call to Humble Learning

A healthy religious community welcomes questions while preserving reverence. Teachers should make difficult matters clear, acknowledge uncertainty, and distinguish established evidence from opinion. Students should listen attentively, ask respectfully, and act upon what becomes clear. Neither authority nor curiosity should be used to prevent sincere learning.

The believer also asks whether his own questions are serving guidance. Am I trying to understand what Allah wants, or am I searching for a way not to do it? Do I ask about a command because I am responsible to obey it, or because I want to discredit the messenger? This self-examination protects faith from becoming a transaction in which the ego remains in control.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:55, 2:67–71, and 7:138–140 on the questioning and demands made to Moses.
- Qur’an 2:260 on Abraham’s sincere request for reassurance.
- Qur’an 5:101–102 and 16:43 on unnecessary questions, clarification, and consulting people of knowledge.
- Qur’an 3:177 and 2:16 on trading belief for disbelief or guidance for misguidance.
- Qur’an 17:90–93 on demands for extraordinary signs as a means of evasion.
- For the distinction between sincere inquiry and obstinate questioning, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:108.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:109

> Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Commentary:**

### Envy After Clarity

The verse tells the believers that many among the People of the Book wished to turn them back to disbelief after the truth had become clear. The cause named is envy. Some people did not reject Islam because they lacked access to its message; they resented that Allah had granted guidance, prophethood, and community to Muhammad (ﷺ) and his followers. The issue was a refusal to accept divine grace when it appeared outside their expected group.

The wording “many among” is precise. It does not condemn every Jew or Christian, and it does not authorize hostility toward civilians or neighbours. The Qur’an acknowledges upright People of the Book and commands Muslims to act justly and kindly toward those who do not fight them because of religion (3:113–115; 60:8). The verse addresses active efforts to turn believers from faith, not an ethnic or religious population as a whole.

Envy is different from honest disagreement. A person may reject a claim because he is not convinced by its evidence, while another may understand the evidence and still resent the person or community associated with it. The latter is more dangerous because the heart’s real objection is to Allah’s distribution of favour. The Qur’an warns that people may wish to turn believers back to disbelief out of envy even after truth is clear (2:105; 2:90).

### Pardon and Forbearance

The believers are commanded to pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. This instruction was given before later developments in the Medinan community and taught restraint while the believers were vulnerable. It did not require them to approve deception, surrender their faith, or abandon lawful self-defence. Pardon concerns personal retaliation; justice and protection remain legitimate where harm is active.

Forbearance—*ṣafḥ*—means turning away from vengeance and refusing to let another person’s envy corrupt one’s own character. The believer can defend truth, establish boundaries, and oppose persecution without becoming consumed by hatred. The Qur’an says, “Repel evil with what is better,” so that the person between whom and you there was enmity may become like a devoted friend (41:34). This is not naïveté; it is moral strength governed by wisdom.

The phrase “until Allah delivers His decision” indicates patience with divine timing. The believers were not required to force every dispute to an immediate conclusion. They could continue teaching, worshipping, and building their community while trusting that Allah would reveal the appropriate legal and political response. Patience does not mean abandoning action; it means refusing to act outside Allah’s limits because one is impatient for resolution.

### Forgiveness and Justice Together

Islamic forgiveness is not the same as erasing every consequence or preventing every form of accountability. The Qur’an permits justice and praises forgiveness when it leads to reconciliation (42:40). If a person actively harms others, society may need to restrain him. A victim may seek a lawful right while still refusing personal hatred. The command here protects believers from retaliatory excess, not from responsible protection.

This balance is especially important when the hostile person belongs to a religious or ethnic group. The Qur’an forbids hatred from causing injustice and commands Muslims to stand firmly for justice even against themselves or their relatives (5:8; 4:135). A specific warning against those who seek to turn believers from faith must not become a general justification for mistreating people who do not share Islam.

### The Power of Allah

The verse closes by affirming that Allah is capable of everything. The believers may feel socially weak or fear that envy and propaganda will overcome them, but guidance is not protected by human strength alone. Allah can preserve faith, expose falsehood, change hearts, and deliver judgment at the right time. This does not excuse passivity. It frees the believer to act with patience rather than panic.

Allah’s power also gives hope to those who are targeted by religious hostility. They need not answer every insult or win every public dispute. Their responsibility is to remain truthful, display good character, and preserve the faith Allah has given them. The final outcome belongs to Allah, whose judgment is not controlled by the popularity of a claim.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:90, 2:105, and 2:109 on envy of divine favour and attempts to turn believers from faith.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 4:135 on justice despite hatred or personal interest.
- Qur’an 41:34 and 42:40 on responding to evil with goodness while preserving justice.
- Qur’an 60:8–9 on kindness and justice toward those who do not fight believers.
- For *ṣafḥ*, forbearance, and the historical sequence of restraint before later rulings, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:109.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:110

> Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Commentary:**

### Returning to Worship After Conflict

After discussing envy, hostility, and the believers’ need for patience, the Qur’an returns to the essential practices of prayer and alms-tax. This is a practical redirection. Believers are not to allow conflict with opponents to become the centre of their religion. They establish prayer, pay zakāh, and continue doing good. Worship prevents opposition from defining the community’s identity.

“Establish prayer”—*aqīmū al-ṣalāh*—means more than performing isolated movements. It includes observing prayer at its appointed times, fulfilling its conditions, praying with humility, and allowing it to discipline conduct. Prayer places the believer before Allah repeatedly, reminding him that his real allegiance is not to public approval, political victory, or inherited rivalry. The Qur’an says that prayer restrains shameful and unjust deeds when it is established meaningfully (29:45).

Zakāh makes the same truth visible in social life. It purifies wealth and the heart from possessiveness, and directs a recognized share toward eligible recipients. The believer does not respond to hostility by abandoning generosity. He knows that wealth is a trust from Allah and that the needy have rights in it (70:24–25). Prayer without zakāh can become inward devotion disconnected from social responsibility; zakāh without prayer can lose its orientation toward Allah.

### Good Deeds Sent Ahead

“Whatever good you send forth for yourselves” portrays deeds as provisions dispatched ahead to the Hereafter. A person may spend wealth, time, knowledge, and effort in ways that seem to disappear from the world, but nothing done sincerely for Allah is lost. The Qur’an says, “Whatever good you put forward for yourselves—you will find it with Allah; it is better and greater in reward” (73:20).

The wording changes the believer’s understanding of charity and service. Giving is not merely losing something in the present; it is sending an investment to a secure account with Allah. The investment is protected from theft, inflation, forgetfulness, and human ingratitude. Allah knows whether it was given sincerely and will return it as reward in the time and manner He chooses.

Good deeds include public and private actions: supporting family, feeding the poor, reconciling people, teaching beneficial knowledge, restraining anger, speaking truth, and removing harm from a path. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that every good deed is charity (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The verse therefore expands worship beyond formal financial giving while preserving zakāh as a specific obligation.

### Allah Sees the Deed and Its Intention

The verse closes with Allah’s perfect sight. He sees the deed itself, its hidden intention, the person’s circumstances, and the good or harm that follows. Human beings may overlook a quiet act or praise a public act that was done for reputation. Allah’s evaluation is not dependent upon publicity. “The Day when neither wealth nor children will benefit, except one who comes to Allah with a sound heart” (26:88–89).

This awareness protects the believer from both pride and discouragement. He does not become proud when people praise his charity because Allah knows whether it was sincere. He does not stop doing good when people ignore it because Allah sees it. Sincerity is not indifference to correct action; it is freedom from making human applause the final reason for action.

### Goodness Is Not a Substitute for Justice

The command to do good does not mean that believers should abandon justice or allow persecution. Generosity and forgiveness must be joined to protection of rights. The Qur’an praises those who spend in hardship and ease, restrain anger, and pardon, while also commanding justice and resistance to aggression (3:134; 2:190–193). A person may forgive a personal injury while still preventing an aggressor from harming others.

The verse’s order is meaningful: prayer and zakāh first, then all good. Worship establishes the intention and limits of service. Without Allah’s guidance, a person may define “good” according to popularity or personal ideology. With revelation, good is what benefits people without violating truth and justice.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:3, 2:43, and 9:71 on prayer and zakāh as marks of faithful life.
- Qur’an 29:45 on prayer restraining indecency and injustice.
- Qur’an 70:24–25 and 73:20 on recognized rights in wealth and sending good deeds ahead.
- Qur’an 2:261–274 on charity, sincerity, and multiplied reward.
- Qur’an 3:134 and 5:8 on generosity, restraint, forgiveness, and justice.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on every good deed being charity and Allah judging deeds by intention.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:111

> The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.”

**Commentary:**

### Exclusive Claims Without Evidence

The verse records competing claims of exclusive salvation: each group says that Paradise belongs only to its own people. The Qur’an calls these statements *amāniyy*—wishful hopes or unsupported aspirations. A claim does not become true because a community repeats it, attaches it to ancestry, or treats it as a source of collective pride. The Prophet is instructed to ask for proof.

The wording “the Jews and Christians” describes claims made by groups within those communities, not every individual Jew or Christian. The Qur’an elsewhere praises upright People of the Book and recognizes that people differ in faith and conduct (3:113–115). A careful reading must therefore distinguish between a false doctrine of automatic privilege and the real people who may accept, question, or reject that doctrine in different ways.

The claim also resembles the earlier assertion that the Fire would touch them only for a few days (2:80). Both claims provide security without the conditions of sincere faith, repentance, and righteous action. The Qur’an refuses to let religious identity become a substitute for a relationship with Allah.

### “Show Your Proof”

The demand for proof is a central Qur’anic method. Religion is not established by inherited slogans, emotional confidence, or the number of people repeating a claim. The Qur’an asks, “Bring your proof, if you are truthful” (27:64), and challenges those who attribute statements to Allah without knowledge. Evidence includes authentic revelation, sound reasoning, and truthful interpretation.

The proof requested is not merely a quotation taken out of context. A valid argument must agree with the whole of revelation and with Allah’s principles of justice. If a group claims automatic salvation, it must show the divine covenant that establishes it. Instead, the Qur’an repeatedly teaches that honour belongs to those who believe, obey, repent, and act righteously (2:62; 4:123–124).

This is also an ethic for Muslim discourse. Muslims must not assume that their preferred school, organization, family, nation, or religious label is automatically correct. They should ask for evidence without arrogance and be willing to follow it even when it corrects their own group. The Qur’an’s command to produce proof applies to every claimant.

### Wishful Thinking and Moral Complacency

Wishful religious thinking is dangerous because it makes repentance seem unnecessary. If a person believes that his label guarantees Paradise, he may tolerate injustice, neglect prayer, abuse the vulnerable, or reject truth without fear. The Qur’an does not deny Allah’s mercy; it denies that mercy can be converted into an unconditional entitlement invented by human beings.

The opposite of wishful thinking is not despair. Allah promises forgiveness to those who repent and rewards those who believe and do good. Hope becomes sound when it is attached to a path. A person may hope that Allah will forgive him while actively abandoning sin, restoring rights, and increasing in obedience. He may not use hope as an excuse to protect the behaviour that contradicts it.

### Paradise and Submission

The Qur’an’s standard is expressed in the next verse: whoever submits himself to Allah and does good will have his reward with his Lord. Submission is not merely joining a social category. It is directing the self to Allah, accepting His guidance, and allowing faith to produce righteous action. The final revelation makes acceptance of the Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) part of that submission for those to whom his message has been clearly conveyed (3:85).

This does not permit Muslims to declare the final fate of particular individuals whose circumstances are unknown. Allah knows what message reached them and how they responded. It does require communities to abandon the idea that salvation is secured by a name while truth and conduct are ignored.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:62 and 2:80 on faith, action, false hopes, and limited-punishment claims.
- Qur’an 2:112 and 4:123–124 on submission, righteous action, and the rejection of wishful salvation.
- Qur’an 3:85 on the acceptance of Islam after the final message has become clear.
- Qur’an 3:113–115 on upright People of the Book.
- Qur’an 27:64 and 28:75 on demanding proof from those who make religious claims.
- For *amāniyy* and the refutation of exclusive claims, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:111.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:112

> But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Commentary:**

### Submission of the Whole Self

The verse answers the competing claims of exclusive salvation. The criterion is not a label but submission to Allah and righteous action. “Submits themselves” translates *aslama wajhahu lillāh*—literally, he turns or surrenders his face to Allah. The face represents the person’s orientation and whole self. True submission means directing the heart, will, worship, and life toward Allah rather than merely adopting a public identity.

This submission is not humiliation before a human ruler or the erasure of reason. It is the recognition that the creature belongs to the Creator and that Allah’s wisdom is superior to personal desire. The believer thinks, asks, learns, and reasons, but when truth is established he does not make his ego the final judge. Islam is submission informed by knowledge and expressed through obedience.

### Righteous Action Confirms Submission

The verse joins submission with doing good—*wa-huwa muḥsin*. *Iḥsān* means doing what is beautiful, excellent, and correct, with sincerity toward Allah and good treatment of creation. Submission that never changes conduct is only a claim. The Qur’an repeatedly joins faith with righteous deeds because the heart’s orientation appears in the life it produces.

Good action includes worship, honesty, justice, generosity, family responsibility, protection of the weak, and restraint from harm. It does not mean that a person earns Paradise through personal merit independent of Allah. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that no one enters Paradise solely through deeds without Allah’s mercy (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Righteous deeds are the evidence and means through which divine mercy is sought.

The phrase also protects against an empty form of religious universalism. “Doing good” in the Qur’anic sense is not whatever a person personally approves. It is action grounded in submission to Allah’s revealed guidance. A person cannot define oppression as good merely because his group benefits from it. The standard includes sincere intention and conformity to truth.

### The Unity of the Prophetic Message

Abraham is presented elsewhere as one who submitted to the Lord of all worlds (2:131), and the Qur’an says that he was neither a Jew nor a Christian in the later communal sense but an upright monotheist (3:67). Jesus, Moses, and the other prophets called people to Allah. The verse therefore points to the common essence of authentic religion: submission to the one Lord and righteous living according to the revelation given to each community.

For the final community, submission includes accepting the Qur’an and the prophethood of Muhammad (ﷺ) once his message has been clearly conveyed. The continuity of the message does not make all later religious claims identical; it means that the source is one and the final revelation confirms and completes the earlier guidance. The Qur’an is the criterion by which claims about previous scripture are evaluated (5:48).

### No Fear and No Grief

The promise that there will be no fear or grief repeats the comfort given to those who follow Allah’s guidance (2:38) and the people described at the beginning of the sūrah (2:5). It refers ultimately to the Hereafter, where the righteous will be protected from the fear of punishment and the grief of final loss. In this life, submission does not remove every trial, but it gives trials meaning and prevents them from becoming despair.

The person who submits to Allah may still experience illness, bereavement, poverty, or persecution. The promise is not emotional numbness. It is the assurance that Allah knows the effort, rewards patience, and brings the believer to a final home where fear and grief are removed. The believer can grieve without losing hope and fear without abandoning principle.

### A Standard Against Sectarian Pride

The verse corrects the idea that Paradise is the private property of a group. It also corrects the idea that all religious differences are irrelevant. Allah asks for submission and good, not for a label detached from truth. The believer respects people, acts justly, and leaves the final judgment to Allah, while holding firmly to the revelation he has received.

The proper response to the verse is humility. No one should say, “My group is safe because of its name,” while neglecting the conditions Allah has stated. Nor should anyone despair because he was not born into a particular social class or family. The door is open to the person who sincerely turns to Allah and allows that submission to produce good.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:131, 3:67, and 3:84 on Abraham’s submission and the unity of prophetic faith.
- Qur’an 4:125 and 31:22 on submitting the self to Allah while doing good.
- Qur’an 2:62, 2:111, and 4:123–124 on labels, wishful claims, and actual accountability.
- Qur’an 5:48 on the Qur’an as a confirming and overseeing criterion.
- Qur’an 23:1–11 and 16:97 on faith, righteous action, and success.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on Paradise being entered through Allah’s mercy, with deeds as evidence of sincere faith.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:113

> The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on” and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,” although both recite the Scriptures. And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺. Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute.

**Commentary:**

### Religious Rivalry Despite Scripture

The verse describes Jews and Christians making mutually exclusive claims while both recite scripture. It does not deny that their doctrines contain real differences or that the Qur’an may correct false beliefs. It condemns the attitude in which each community dismisses the other without acknowledging any truth, responsibility, or shared prophetic heritage. Possessing scripture should produce knowledge, humility, and moral seriousness—not automatic immunity from sectarian arrogance.

The statement “have nothing to stand on” is an expression of total dismissal. It resembles later claims that only one community can enter Paradise (2:111). The Qur’an challenges these sweeping declarations because they are not established by sound proof. It also reminds the disputants that their own scriptures testify to revelation, prophecy, judgment, and the need to worship Allah. Religious knowledge should make a person more careful in judgment, not more reckless.

The verse’s mention of those who have no knowledge is significant. People without scripture repeat the same dispute, each declaring that the others have no basis. Ignorance can imitate religious certainty. A person who does not understand the evidence may still make absolute claims because group loyalty supplies confidence. The Qur’an places all such disputes before Allah, the One who knows the truth and the degree of responsibility in every case.

### Scripture Should Unite Around Truth

The original revelations came from one Lord and shared essential teachings. The Qur’an calls the People of the Book to a common word: that they worship none but Allah and do not take one another as lords besides Allah (3:64). This common ground does not erase doctrinal differences, but it creates a basis for honest dialogue, justice, and recognition of shared moral responsibilities.

The Qur’an also says that Allah gave different communities a law and a way and could have made them one community, but He tests them in what He has given; therefore they should compete in good deeds (5:48). The command is not to pretend that all beliefs are identical. It is to avoid using difference as a justification for arrogance and wrongdoing. Truth is pursued through evidence and worship, while human beings remain accountable for how they dispute.

The community that possesses scripture must also face its own failures. It is easy to list another group’s errors while ignoring hypocrisy, injustice, or disobedience within one’s own. The Qur’an’s criticism of the Jews and Christians in this verse is therefore also a warning to Muslims. Religious rivalry can become a substitute for repentance and action.

### The Day of Judgment Resolves the Dispute

Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment. This means that final judgment does not belong to competing communities, political powers, or popular opinion. Allah knows which revelation reached whom, what was preserved, what was altered, what a person understood, and how he responded. His judgment will be complete and fair.

The promise of final judgment is not an excuse to abandon truth in this life. Believers still explain their faith, defend justice, and distinguish truth from falsehood. But they do not pretend to possess Allah’s knowledge of every individual’s final state. They avoid persecution and leave the hidden outcome to the Judge who cannot be deceived.

The verse also gives relief from endless religious rivalry. A person may be unable to persuade an opponent despite clear evidence. He is responsible for truthfulness and good character, not for controlling another heart. Allah will settle what human debate cannot settle, and He will reveal which claims were grounded in truth and which were driven by pride.

### The Ethics of Disagreement

The Qur’an commands Muslims to debate with the People of the Book in the best manner, except with those who commit injustice, and to say that Muslims believe in what was revealed to them and to earlier prophets (29:46). This is the proper balance: clarity without insult, conviction without injustice, and dialogue without surrendering truth.

The Prophet (ﷺ) warned against disputation that is pursued merely to win and praised those who leave argument even when they are right, when doing so protects good character (Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 4800, with supporting narrations). Not every dispute deserves endless engagement. The believer asks whether his words serve truth and guidance or merely strengthen the ego.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:111–112 on exclusive claims to Paradise and the criterion of submission and good action.
- Qur’an 3:64 and 5:48 on common ground, different laws, and the Qur’an as criterion.
- Qur’an 22:17 on Allah judging between religious communities.
- Qur’an 29:46 on debating the People of the Book in the best manner.
- Qur’an 49:13 on human diversity and the measure of honour being taqwā.
- For the historical and theological context of the mutual claims, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:113.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:114

> Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.

**Commentary:**

### The Gravity of Attacking Worship

The verse asks who could be more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s name from being mentioned in places of worship and strive to destroy them. The question expresses the enormity of the crime: it attacks both worship and the public space in which people practise it. A place of worship is not merely a building. It is a sanctuary of remembrance, prayer, community, learning, charity, and refuge.

Classical exegetes differed about the immediate historical referent. Some related the verse to the people who obstructed the Prophet and the believers from the Sacred Mosque; others connected it to the destruction or prevention of worship at earlier sanctuaries. The wording is broad enough to establish a general principle while the historical circumstances explain its force. Whoever actively prevents sincere worship or works to destroy sanctuaries commits a grave wrong, regardless of the name of his group.

The verse is not a command to attack other religions’ buildings or to punish people for their beliefs. Rather, it condemns those who obstruct Allah’s worship and destroy places dedicated to worship. The Qur’an mentions monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques as places where Allah’s name is remembered and says that they would have been destroyed if Allah had not repelled some people by means of others (22:40). Protection of worship spaces is therefore part of resisting corruption.

### The Sacredness of Places of Worship

Mosques have a special status in Islam because they are dedicated to the worship of Allah, but the principle of protecting sanctuaries includes respecting the rights of other worshippers and their places. The Prophet (ﷺ) established agreements that protected religious communities, and Islamic law developed rules concerning non-combatants, houses of worship, and the prohibition of unjust destruction. A building’s religious identity does not justify violence against the people inside it.

Preventing worship can take many forms: physically barring people, destroying a sanctuary, threatening worshippers, confiscating a site unjustly, banning prayer without legitimate reason, or creating terror that makes worship impossible. It can also be done through propaganda that portrays every religious space as an enemy target. The verse speaks to both direct violence and systems that make remembrance of Allah dangerous.

The phrase “prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned” includes obstructing the call to worship and preventing believers from praying. It does not mean that human beings can literally prevent Allah’s name from existing or being remembered. Allah’s remembrance is greater than any building, but the oppressor is still accountable for depriving people of a place and security in which to worship.

### Entering in Fear

The statement that such people have no right to enter these places except with fear has been understood as a promise that they will be humiliated and afraid as a consequence of their aggression, or as a reference to the sanctity of the Sacred Mosque and the fear that should govern those who enter it. The phrase should not be used to justify random intimidation or vigilante punishment. It describes divine judgment and the loss of false security, not a licence for individuals to terrorize others.

A sanctuary should be a place where fear is reduced, not increased. The Qur’an calls the Sacred House a secure sanctuary (2:125), and the broader ethic of worship spaces requires protection from violence. Those who attack sacred places may temporarily appear powerful, but the verse promises disgrace in this world and tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.

### Worship and Public Freedom

The verse protects the public dimension of faith. Worship is not always a private activity that can be confined without harm. Communities need spaces for prayer, teaching, gathering, caring for the poor, and remembering Allah together. Preventing these activities weakens both individuals and society. At the same time, public religious presence must be exercised with justice and must not become a pretext to oppress others.

For Muslims, the verse demands that mosques be maintained as places of sincere remembrance rather than political manipulation, commercial exploitation, or factional intimidation. It is possible to keep a building open while preventing the name of Allah in spirit—by using the mosque to promote hatred, corruption, or unjust power. The purpose of a sanctuary is fulfilled when it protects worship, knowledge, mercy, and justice.

### A Warning Against the Destruction of Sacred Trusts

The final warning applies to rulers, mobs, armies, and individuals. No power is justified in destroying a place of worship merely because it belongs to a disliked group or because its existence challenges a political narrative. If a genuine security concern exists, it must be addressed through lawful, proportionate, and evidence-based means rather than collective punishment.

The believer should also support the rebuilding and protection of places where people worship Allah. The Qur’an’s language makes clear that preventing remembrance is a form of corruption. Protecting worshippers, preserving sanctuaries, and allowing sincere devotion are forms of justice that honour the Creator and the dignity of human beings.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:125 on the Sacred House as a sanctuary and place of prayer.
- Qur’an 22:40 on monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques being places where Allah’s name is remembered.
- Qur’an 8:34 and 9:17–18 on obstructing the Sacred Mosque and maintaining Allah’s places of worship.
- Qur’an 5:2 and 5:8 on cooperating in righteousness and upholding justice.
- Qur’an 60:8 on kindness and justice toward those who do not fight Muslims because of religion.
- For the historical interpretations and the meaning of the threat of disgrace, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:114.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:115

> To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### All Directions Belong to Allah

The verse reminds the reader that east and west, and every direction between them, belong to Allah. No direction possesses sacred power independently. Allah is not contained by a place, and He is not absent from any direction. The created world is His kingdom, while He is exalted above being confined within creation.

The immediate historical context is connected to questions about the direction of prayer and circumstances in which a person cannot face the qiblah precisely. When the qiblah was changed to the Sacred Mosque, some opponents tried to use directions as an argument against the believers (2:142–144). The verse answers that Allah owns all directions. The qiblah is an act of obedience and communal unity, not a claim that Allah is physically located in the Kaʿbah or on one side of the world.

Islam still requires the able person to face the qiblah in obligatory prayer. The verse does not cancel that command. It teaches that direction is appointed by Allah and that the sanctity of the direction does not come from the compass point itself. In conditions of fear, travel, illness, or genuine inability, the law provides concessions. A person does what he can, and Allah knows the circumstance.

### Facing Allah in Worship

“Wherever you turn, you are facing Allah” can also express the believer’s awareness that Allah knows and receives his worship in every place. It does not mean that Allah is physically present in every direction or that all directions are interchangeable in every ritual. It means that no geographical location excludes Allah’s knowledge, power, or mercy, and that the believer’s ultimate orientation is toward Him.

The Qur’an says that Allah is with people wherever they are through His knowledge and that He knows what they do (57:4). It also teaches that righteousness is not exhausted by turning the face east or west; it includes faith, charity, prayer, covenant, and patience (2:177). Physical direction has meaning because it expresses obedience, but the direction of the heart is equally important.

A person may face the Kaʿbah while his heart faces wealth, reputation, or other people’s approval. Another may be physically unable to face the qiblah accurately while sincerely turning his heart to Allah. The law takes account of both outward responsibility and inward intention. Allah is not deceived by ritual posture without submission.

### The Qiblah and Communal Unity

The qiblah gives the Muslim community a shared direction across geography, language, and culture. Muslims in Abuja, Makkah, Jakarta, London, and countless other places stand in different physical locations but orient themselves toward one House. This creates unity without making the House an object of worship. The Kaʿbah is honoured because Allah commanded that it be honoured; worship belongs to Allah alone.

The change of qiblah was also a test of obedience. People who followed the Prophet accepted a new direction because Allah commanded it, while others treated it as evidence of inconsistency. The Qur’an explains that the former direction was assigned to distinguish those who would remain faithful from those who would turn back (2:143). Ritual direction can therefore reveal whether a person follows revelation or his own expectation.

### All-Encompassing and All-Knowing

Allah is *Wāsiʿ*—vast, all-encompassing, and unlimited in bounty and knowledge—and *ʿAlīm*—All-Knowing. His mercy is not restricted to one land, and His knowledge does not depend upon a sanctuary. He knows the prayer of the traveller, the sick person, the one who faces correctly, and the one who is genuinely unable. He also knows whether a dispute about direction is motivated by sincere inquiry or by a desire to undermine revelation.

The names reassure the believer who cannot fulfil a physical detail perfectly. Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity (2:286). At the same time, they warn the person who uses the verse to dismiss a clear obligation. Divine vastness is not permission to ignore divine instruction; it is the reason the instruction is applied with knowledge, mercy, and appropriate concessions.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:142–150 on the change of qiblah, obedience, and the meaning of facing the Sacred Mosque.
- Qur’an 2:177 on righteousness being more than physical direction.
- Qur’an 2:286 and 64:16 on ability, responsibility, and Allah’s mercy.
- Qur’an 57:4 on Allah’s knowledge and encompassing presence over creation.
- Qur’an 16:48–50 on creation’s submission to Allah.
- For the contextual and legal interpretation of “wherever you turn,” see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī, and the relevant chapters of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī concerning prayer during travel.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:116

> They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will.

**Commentary:**

### Allah Is Above Need and Dependence

The verse rejects the claim that Allah has offspring. The Qur’an does not name only one group here because different communities made different forms of this claim. Some associated a son with Allah, some claimed that angels were daughters of Allah, and others attributed divine sonship to Jesus. The common error is to apply a creaturely category to the Creator.

“Glory be to Him”—*subḥānahu*—declares Allah’s transcendence above every imperfection and limitation. Offspring arise from biological generation, material composition, or a need for continuation and inheritance. Allah is the Eternal Creator, independent of creation, not a body that reproduces or a ruler who needs an heir. “He neither begets nor is born” (112:3), and “How could He have a child when He has no spouse?” (6:101).

The rejection does not deny the honour of Jesus (ʿalayhi al-salām). The Qur’an calls him the Messiah, a word from Allah, a messenger, and a sign of Allah’s power, while refusing to make him divine or the literal son of God (3:45; 4:171; 19:30–36). Honouring a prophet means accepting what Allah revealed about him, not adding claims that compromise the Creator’s uniqueness.

### Everything Belongs to Allah

The verse answers the claim by stating that whatever is in the heavens and earth belongs to Allah. Ownership here includes creation, possession, authority, and dependence. If all things are created and owned by Allah, none of them can be His offspring in the literal sense. They are servants and signs, not extensions of His essence.

The statement also establishes the basis of worship. Created beings may be respected, loved, and helped within their proper limits, but none shares Allah’s divine rights. The Qur’an asks why people attribute to Allah a child while choosing children for themselves, and why they assign Him what they dislike (16:57–62). Such claims reveal human projection: people imagine the divine through categories derived from their own social experience.

Everything belongs to Allah, including human bodies, wealth, knowledge, and authority. This corrects human pride. People cannot claim absolute ownership of what Allah created and may not use His gifts as though they were independent of His command. Ownership is a trust, and the true Owner will ask how the trust was used.

### All Are Subject to His Will

“All are subject to His Will” translates the idea that the creatures are devoutly obedient or submissive to Allah. The created order obeys Allah’s sustaining command whether creatures recognize it or not. The sun, earth, angels, animals, and physical processes operate within the order He established. Human beings and jinn possess a special form of moral choice: they can submit willingly or rebel, but neither choice places them outside Allah’s power.

The Qur’an says, “To Him submits whoever is in the heavens and the earth, willingly or unwillingly” (3:83). Voluntary worship is the honour offered by believing humans and jinn; involuntary submission describes the fact that no creature can escape Allah’s decree. This distinction protects both divine sovereignty and moral responsibility.

### The Error of Making Allah Like Creation

The claim of offspring confuses the Creator with the created. The Qur’an says, “There is nothing like Him” (42:11), while also affirming that He is near, hears prayer, and knows His servants. Allah may describe Himself with names and attributes that human beings understand in meaning, but His reality is unlike created realities. He is not subject to biological processes, spatial limitation, fatigue, lineage, or need.

The same principle applies to all theological language. Believers should affirm what Allah and His messenger revealed without imagining Allah as a creature. They should also reject claims that attribute weakness, injustice, or dependence to Him. Transcendence and nearness are both affirmed in ways befitting His majesty.

### Worship Belongs to the Owner

If everything belongs to Allah, worship cannot be divided among competing owners. Idols, saints, angels, prophets, ancestors, and natural forces are all within His kingdom. They may be honoured as created beings where appropriate, but none can independently forgive, provide, protect, or determine destiny.

The verse therefore returns to the central call of Sūrah al-Baqarah: worship the Lord who created and provided (2:21–22). The denial of divine offspring is not an abstract theological correction. It protects the purity of worship and restores the proper relationship between Creator and creation.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 6:101, 16:57–62, and 19:88–93 on rejecting claims of divine offspring.
- Qur’an 112:1–4 on Allah’s oneness, independence, and transcendence.
- Qur’an 3:45–59, 4:171, and 19:30–36 on Jesus as messenger and servant, not a divine offspring.
- Qur’an 3:83 and 30:26 on all creation belonging to and submitting to Allah.
- Qur’an 42:11 on the uniqueness of Allah and the impossibility of comparing Him to creation.
- For the theological meaning of *subḥānahu* and the refutation of divine sonship, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:116.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:117

> ˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth! When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is!

**Commentary:**

### The Originator Without Precedent

Allah is the Originator—*Badīʿ*—of the heavens and the earth. The name indicates that He brought the universe into existence without a previous model that constrained Him and without needing a material partner or helper. Creation is not the result of divine birth, biological generation, or an inherited substance. Allah existed before creation and originated the whole order upon which every creature depends.

This directly answers the claim of divine offspring. Offspring belong to created systems that depend upon generation, continuity, and material conditions. Allah is the originator of those systems and is not contained by them. He is independent while creation is dependent. The Qur’an says, “Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Prevailing” (39:62).

The heavens and earth are mentioned together because they represent the entire visible order. The One who originated the vast universe can create, guide, resurrect, and legislate without being limited by human assumptions. The scale of creation should not make the believer imagine Allah as distant or unable to attend to individual prayers. His knowledge and power encompass the whole without effort or division.

### “Be!” and It Is

“When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ and it is.” This is a Qur’anic expression of effortless divine power. Allah does not require tools, fatigue, preparation, or assistance in order to bring about what He wills. The command is not necessarily a spoken sound like created speech; it communicates that creation occurs through Allah’s will and command, in a manner befitting Him.

The phrase appears in relation to creation, resurrection, Jesus’ miraculous birth, and other acts. Allah says that the likeness of Jesus before Him is like Adam: He created him from dust, then said “Be,” and he was (3:59). If Allah can create Adam without human parents and Jesus without a human father, divine sonship cannot be used as an explanation for either. Miraculous creation proves divine power; it does not make the created being divine.

The expression also supports belief in resurrection. People may imagine that restoring decayed bodies is difficult, but Allah’s command is not limited by the processes through which the first creation occurred. “Does the One who created the heavens and the earth not have the power to create the like of them?” (36:81). Nothing is difficult for the Originator.

### Divine Will and Human Responsibility

Allah’s effortless command does not mean that human choices are meaningless. He created human beings with capacity, knowledge, and choice, and He holds them accountable for how they use these gifts. The universe operates by His decree, but moral responsibility remains real. The believer neither denies divine sovereignty nor uses it as an excuse for injustice.

The phrase “Be, and it is” also reminds people that no created authority can compete with Allah’s will. Governments, wealth, technology, and social majorities may appear powerful, but they exist only by His permission and can disappear by His command. This does not mean that believers wait passively for miracles. They take lawful means while knowing that success is ultimately from Allah.

### Creation, Revelation, and Resurrection

The argument of the passage moves from Allah’s ownership of creation to His ability to originate it and command it. If He is the Originator of the heavens and earth, then He has the right to guide humanity. If He can create by command, then He can send revelation and raise the dead. Theology, worship, and accountability are connected.

The believer sees every new life, growing plant, changing season, and resurrection of the earth as an ongoing reminder of the Originator. These signs should produce gratitude and humility. Allah is not one power among many; He is the source from whom all created power derives.

### The Limits of Creaturely Comparison

The verse should not be reduced to a childish image of Allah speaking a magical word to matter. Qur’anic language brings the reality of divine command close to human understanding while preserving Allah’s transcendence. Allah’s creative act is unlike human speech and production. Humans need materials, tools, time, and effort; Allah needs none of these.

At the same time, the verse is not merely symbolic. It affirms that creation truly depends upon Allah’s will. The believer holds together divine transcendence, divine power, and the real existence of the created world. Creation is not Allah, nor is it independent of Him.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:47, 3:59, 6:101, and 36:82 on creation by Allah’s command.
- Qur’an 16:40 and 40:68 on Allah saying “Be” and the matter coming to be.
- Qur’an 36:77–83 on creation and resurrection.
- Qur’an 39:62 and 35:3 on Allah as Creator and the source of all provision.
- Qur’an 42:11 on Allah’s incomparable nature.
- For *Badīʿ* and the theological meaning of “Be, and it is,” see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:117.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:118

> Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!” The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith.

**Commentary:**

### Demanding a Sign While Ignoring Signs

The verse describes people without knowledge asking why Allah does not speak to them directly or send them a sign of their choosing. The question resembles earlier demands made by the Children of Israel and by the polytheists of Makkah. The problem is not that Allah has failed to provide evidence. The problem is that the questioners want a sign on their own terms, often after ignoring the signs already before them.

The Qur’an records demands that the Prophet bring Allah and the angels visibly before them, cause springs to flow from the earth, or ascend into heaven and return with a book they could read (17:90–93). These are not ordinary requests for clarification. They are conditions designed to delay faith. When one demand is met or answered, another is introduced. The person is not pursuing truth; he is preserving the right to reject it.

The Qur’an itself is a sign and a revelation. It invites reflection, offers arguments, and challenges humanity to produce a sūrah like it. The created world is another field of signs: life, death, rain, conscience, and the order of the heavens. A person who says “bring us a sign” may be overlooking the fact that he is already surrounded by signs and sustained by the One whose existence he questions.

### Repeated Objections Reveal Similar Hearts

“The same was said by those who came before” shows that the objection is not new. Different communities repeat similar demands because human resistance often follows familiar patterns. A person who does not want to submit seeks an extraordinary event that would remove the need for trust, moral choice, or humility. Yet even Pharaoh saw clear signs and still rejected them out of wrongdoing and arrogance (27:13–14).

“Their hearts are all alike” does not mean that every person in different communities is identical in every belief. It means that the inward disposition behind the demand is the same: arrogance, suspicion, and a refusal to accept ordinary evidence. The same heart can appear in a pagan, a scripturalist, or a nominal believer whenever he makes faith conditional upon satisfying his own terms.

A sincere person can ask Allah for reassurance without sharing this rebellious attitude. Abraham asked to see how Allah brings the dead to life, and Allah affirmed his belief while granting the request to reassure his heart (2:260). The difference lies in whether the question is a path to stronger obedience or a pretext for avoiding it.

### Signs Are Clear to the Receptive Heart

Allah says that He has made the signs clear for people of sure faith. Clarity is not only a property of the sign; it also concerns the readiness of the person receiving it. A clear road is useless to someone who refuses to look, and a clear argument is unhelpful to someone determined not to be persuaded. The believer approaches the sign with humility and asks what it requires.

This does not mean that faith is irrational or that people must suppress every question. The Qur’an repeatedly invites reasoning and reflection. It means that the will can interfere with perception. Pride may make a person reinterpret clear evidence, while taqwā helps him recognize and follow it. The opening of this sūrah described the Qur’an as guidance for the mindful (2:2); here it describes the signs as clear for those with certainty.

### Allah Speaks Through Revelation

The request that Allah speak to them should not be understood to mean that Allah never communicated with human beings. He spoke to Moses in a manner befitting His majesty, sent revelation through angels, and addressed humanity through His revealed Books. The issue is the demand for a direct sensory encounter as a condition for belief. Allah has chosen the forms of communication by which people are tested, and they cannot force another form.

The Qur’an says that it is not for any human being that Allah should speak to him except by revelation, from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger (42:51). This verse affirms divine communication while preserving the distinction between Creator and creature. The proper response is to receive the revelation Allah has sent rather than reject it because it does not conform to a preferred experience.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:55, 2:97, and 17:90–93 on demands to see or hear Allah directly and the forms of divine communication.
- Qur’an 2:260 on Abraham’s request for reassurance, distinguishing sincere inquiry from defiance.
- Qur’an 27:13–14 on recognizing signs and rejecting them through arrogance.
- Qur’an 41:53 and 51:20–21 on signs in the horizons, the earth, and the human self.
- Qur’an 42:51 on Allah’s modes of communication with human beings.
- For “their hearts are alike” and the difference between faith-seeking questions and rebellious demands, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:118.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:119

> We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire.

**Commentary:**

### The Prophet Sent with Truth

Allah tells the Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) that He sent him with the truth. The truth includes the Qur’an, the reality of Allah’s oneness, the truth of prophethood and resurrection, the moral law, and the way of worship. The Prophet did not create the message or tailor it to win popular approval. He was entrusted to convey what Allah revealed, even when it challenged the beliefs and interests of his audience.

The Qur’an describes the Prophet’s mission with two complementary roles: a bearer of good news and a warner. Good news gives hope to those who believe, repent, and do good; warning protects people from denial, injustice, and the consequences of rejecting Allah. A message containing only warning could drive sincere people to despair, while a message containing only promise could make the heedless complacent. The prophetic balance addresses the whole human condition.

The good news is not limited to material prosperity. It includes forgiveness, guidance, inner peace, divine companionship, reward for patience, and Paradise. The warning is not merely a threat detached from evidence. It explains the consequences of rejecting truth and harming others. Together, the two roles make the message morally complete.

### The Prophet Is Not Responsible for Forced Belief

The statement that the Prophet will not be accountable for the residents of Hell means that he is not responsible for forcing people to believe or for carrying the burden of their chosen rejection. His duty is to convey clearly. “You are only a warner; you are not a controller over them” (88:21–22). Allah will judge each person with complete knowledge of what message reached him and how he responded.

This verse consoles the Prophet because many people rejected him despite clear evidence. Their rejection did not mean that he had failed to convey the truth or that the truth had become uncertain. He could teach, warn, answer questions, and model obedience, but he could not open a heart by force. Guidance in the sense of conveying the path belonged to him; guidance in the sense of creating faith belonged to Allah (28:56).

The verse also sets a limit for every teacher, parent, and caller. They must speak truthfully, patiently, and wisely, but they cannot control another person’s final response. This protects them from despair and from the temptation to manipulate. It also protects those they teach from coercion disguised as religious guidance.

### Truth and Compassion Together

The Prophet’s mission combines truth with mercy. He warned people because he wanted them to be saved, not because he delighted in punishment. The Qur’an describes him as deeply concerned for the believers and compassionate and merciful toward them (9:128). Good news and warning should therefore be delivered with wisdom, not arrogance or cruelty.

Believers who inherit the prophetic task of teaching must preserve this balance. They should not hide the consequences of disbelief and injustice, but they should also not speak as though they personally own the keys to Paradise and Hell. They invite, explain, advise, and leave final judgment to Allah. The purpose of warning is reform before the consequence arrives.

### No Compulsion in Faith

The verse also fits the Qur’anic principle that there is no compulsion in religion (2:256). Faith is not genuine if produced by physical coercion or social terror. The messenger presents the truth, and the hearer is morally responsible for the response. Coercion may produce outward compliance, but it cannot create the inward certainty and submission that Allah seeks.

This principle does not mean that societies have no laws or that every public act is beyond accountability. It means that the existence of faith itself cannot be forced into a heart. Justice, public order, and religious invitation must not be confused with manufacturing belief through compulsion.

### A Message for All Humanity

The Prophet was sent with truth as a deliverer of good news and a warner, and his message is not restricted to one ethnicity or region. The Qur’an says, “We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds” (21:107), and describes him as a messenger for all humanity (7:158). The final message completes the chain of revelation and calls every people to worship the one Lord.

The verse therefore asks the reader to decide how he will respond. Will he accept the good news through faith and action, or treat the warning as an inconvenience? The Prophet has conveyed the message; responsibility now belongs to the hearer, while final judgment belongs to Allah.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 18:56, 33:45–46, and 34:28 on good news, warning, and the universal mission of the Prophet.
- Qur’an 7:158 and 21:107 on the Prophet’s mission to all humanity and as a mercy to the worlds.
- Qur’an 28:56 and 88:21–22 on conveying guidance without controlling hearts.
- Qur’an 2:256 on the absence of compulsion in religion.
- Qur’an 9:128 on the Prophet’s compassion and concern for people.
- For the relationship between truth, good news, warning, and accountability, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:119.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:120

> Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you, until you follow their faith. Say, “Allah’s guidance is the only ˹true˺ guidance.” And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah.

**Commentary:**

### The Limits of Seeking Universal Approval

The verse warns the Prophet (ﷺ) that the religious factions opposing him will not be satisfied with him merely because he compromises. They would prefer that he abandon the guidance brought by Allah and follow their religious direction. The statement addresses groups and leaders who were actively disputing the Prophet’s message; it should not be turned into a claim that every Jew or Christian in every time and place is personally hostile to Muslims.

The lesson is about the danger of seeking universal approval by surrendering truth. A person who tries to satisfy every audience may gradually exchange revelation for the expectations of people. No amount of compromise can guarantee approval from those whose real demand is that the believer cease to be a believer. The Qur’an asks, “Do you wish to guide those whom Allah has allowed to stray?” (4:88), reminding the Prophet that guidance is not obtained through abandoning the truth.

This does not mean that Muslims should refuse respectful dialogue, cooperation, or peaceful relations with Jews and Christians. The Qur’an commands Muslims to debate with the People of the Book in the best manner (29:46), to act kindly and justly toward those who do not fight them because of religion (60:8), and to recognize that some of them are upright (3:113–115). Conviction and justice are compatible.

### Allah’s Guidance Is the Standard

The Prophet is instructed to say, “Allah’s guidance is the only true guidance.” The repetition of “guidance” emphasizes that ultimate direction cannot be determined by public approval, inherited custom, or the pressure of religious authorities. Allah’s revelation is the standard by which claims are measured. It does not mean that every Muslim opinion is automatically divine guidance; it means that the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah remain the criterion.

The Qur’an presents itself as guidance that brings people from darkness to light (14:1), and as a criterion distinguishing truth from falsehood (25:1). The believer must therefore seek knowledge, consult qualified scholarship, and distinguish clear revelation from personal interpretation. Loyalty to Allah is not loyalty to every cultural practice performed in His name.

The verse also strengthens the Prophet against emotional pressure. Even a messenger could naturally desire that opponents accept his message and stop resisting. Allah teaches him that their approval cannot be purchased through compromise. The truth is not measured by whether its opponents are pleased with it.

### Following Desire After Knowledge

The warning is severe: if the Prophet were to follow their desires after knowledge had come, no protector or helper could save him from Allah. The statement is a hypothetical warning to the highest-ranking recipient of revelation, so ordinary believers should take it seriously. Knowledge increases responsibility. Once truth becomes clear, following desire is no longer an innocent preference; it is a form of betrayal.

The phrase “their desires” shows that the issue is not all that Jews or Christians believe, nor every point of religious difference. It concerns the demands that conflict with Allah’s revealed guidance. Desire may appear as pressure to dilute tawḥīd, abandon a command, accept a false doctrine, or define faith by political convenience. The believer evaluates claims by revelation rather than by the identity of the person making them.

This warning applies within the Muslim community as well. A person may follow the desires of a political faction, family, scholar, majority, or market after recognizing that Allah’s guidance requires something else. The Qur’an commands believers to stand for justice even against themselves and to respond to Allah and the Messenger when called to what gives them life (4:135; 8:24).

### Conviction Without Injustice

The verse should be balanced with the Qur’an’s commands of justice and kindness. Holding firmly to Islam does not permit Muslims to insult Jews or Christians, break agreements, oppress minorities, or treat ordinary disagreement as aggression. The Prophet’s refusal to compromise revelation was accompanied by honesty in contracts, fair dealings, and protection of those who were not fighting the believers.

A believer can say, “Allah’s guidance is the true guidance,” while also acknowledging that other people possess dignity, rights, and freedom from unjust harm. Conviction concerns worship and truth; justice concerns how people are treated. The two are not enemies.

### The Prophet as a Model of Steadfastness

The Prophet’s task was to convey the revelation faithfully, not to redesign it until every opponent was satisfied. His steadfastness teaches believers that truth may be unpopular and that a messenger’s mercy does not include altering the message. He continued to invite, explain, forgive, and make treaties where appropriate, but he did not exchange tawḥīd for acceptance.

The verse closes the section by returning to the opening theme of guidance. Some hearts receive it, some reject it openly, some pretend to accept it, and some try to make the believers abandon it. The believer’s protection is to remain attached to Allah, seek knowledge, practise justice, and refuse to make people’s approval the measure of truth.

- Qur’an 29:46 and 60:8–9 on respectful dialogue, kindness, and justice toward non-hostile People of the Book.
- Qur’an 4:135 and 8:24 on justice and responding to Allah and His Messenger.
