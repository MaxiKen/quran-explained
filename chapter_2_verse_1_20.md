## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:1

> Alif-Lām-Mīm.

**Commentary:**

### The Meaning of the Disjointed Letters

This āyah consists of three isolated Arabic letters: *alif*, *lām*, and *mīm*. They are known in the Qur’anic sciences as *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*—the disjointed or isolated letters. They occur at the beginning of twenty-nine sūrahs, sometimes singly, sometimes in combinations such as *Alif-Lām-Mīm*, *Alif-Lām-Rā*, *Ḥā-Mīm*, and *Qāf*. Their exact intended meaning has not been decisively disclosed by Allah (ﷻ), and the safest position is to affirm them as revelation while refusing to assign a certainty that revelation itself has not supplied.

This restraint is not a weakness in interpretation. It is part of the Qur’an’s training in humility: the believer knows that the divine Word is true even where the limits of human knowledge prevent a complete explanation of every detail. The opening of Sūrah al-Baqarah therefore begins by teaching the attitude with which the whole Book must be approached. One does not subject revelation to the condition that every mystery must first be decoded by the human mind. Rather, one receives what is clear, seeks understanding where understanding is possible, and entrusts what remains hidden to the One who revealed it. The Qur’an itself praises those who say, “We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord” (3:7).

The early authorities transmitted several explanations, but they did not agree on one definitive meaning. Some of the Companions and later exegetes regarded these letters as among the matters whose ultimate meaning Allah alone knows. Others suggested that they draw attention to the miraculous nature of the Qur’an: it is composed of the same ordinary Arabic letters known to its first hearers, yet no human being can produce a discourse comparable to it. Sūrah al-Baqarah soon follows these letters with a declaration about “the Book,” and later challenges humanity to produce even one sūrah like it (2:23–24). On this reading, the letters function as a challenge by implication: these are the very sounds and characters available to you, yet the revelation formed from them remains beyond imitation. This is an interpretive insight, not a claim that every isolated letter has a separately known lexical meaning.

The letters also arrest the listener’s attention. The opening does not begin with a familiar narrative or a human argument; it begins with sounds that compel the hearer to stop, listen, and recognize that the speech which follows is not ordinary speech. In a culture where the Qur’an was first heard aloud, this opening had a powerful rhetorical effect. It moved the audience from the familiar world of human oratory into the presence of a recitation whose source and scope demanded a different kind of attention. The next āyah identifies that recitation as “the Book” and describes its relation to doubt, guidance, and taqwā.

The believer should therefore avoid two opposite errors. The first is to dismiss the letters as meaningless simply because their full significance is not available to us. Revelation is not rendered meaningless by a mystery within it. The second is to speak with unwarranted certainty, claiming that one particular numerical, historical, or esoteric interpretation must be what Allah intended. The Qur’an condemns speaking about Allah without knowledge (7:33), while it repeatedly directs people to reflect upon its signs with honesty and reverence. The proper response is confident faith joined to intellectual modesty.

There is also a practical lesson in beginning with letters. Human language is a gift from Allah (ﷻ), and the Qur’an uses that gift to guide, warn, reason, narrate, and console. Yet the Creator is not confined by the creature’s ability to analyse language. The same Book that can be understood by the ordinary believer contains depths that continue to occupy the most learned scholars. Its accessibility and inexhaustibility are not contradictions: it gives guidance to the sincere while continually revealing further horizons to those who study it with knowledge, worship, and humility.

The Prophet (ﷺ) also taught that reciting the Qur’an is rewarded at the level of its letters: “Whoever recites a letter from the Book of Allah will have one good deed for it, and each good deed is multiplied tenfold. I do not say that *Alif-Lām-Mīm* is one letter; rather, Alif is a letter, Lām is a letter, and Mīm is a letter” (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2910). The hadith is not an explanation of the hidden meaning of these initials. It does, however, show that even the believer’s encounter with these apparently simple sounds is an act of worship and not an empty exercise.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:7 on clear and allegorical matters and the humility of saying that all revelation is from Allah.
- Qur’an 7:33 on the prohibition of speaking about Allah without knowledge.
- Qur’an 10:38 and 11:13 on the challenge to produce a discourse or sūrah comparable to the Qur’an.
- Qur’an 17:88 on humanity’s inability to produce the like of the Qur’an.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2910, on the reward for reciting each letter of the Qur’an.
- For the principal interpretive approaches, see the discussions of the *ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt* in Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī. None of these approaches should be presented as a certain statement from Allah where the revealed text has left the matter open.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:2

> This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Commentary:**

### A Book Beyond Doubt

After the arresting opening letters, the revelation points to itself: “This is the Book!” The demonstrative *dhālika*—literally, “that”—does not indicate physical distance. In Arabic rhetoric, a distant demonstrative can express exaltation, greatness, and eminence. The meaning is that this is the elevated Book, the one whose rank is beyond the reach of ordinary human composition. It is not a private spiritual impression or a collection of the Prophet’s (ﷺ) reflections. It is the revealed speech of Allah (ﷻ), sent down for guidance and judgment.

“There is no doubt about it” translates *lā rayba fīh*. The statement is not a claim that every person will be psychologically free from questions, nor does it deny that people may raise objections or experience uncertainty while learning. It declares that doubt does not belong to the Book itself: its source, truth, and authority are not defective. The problem may lie in a person’s information, motives, assumptions, or refusal to follow the evidence, but no uncertainty can be attributed to Allah’s revelation as though it were an unreliable human document. The Qur’an describes itself elsewhere as “a Book whose verses are perfected and then explained in detail” (11:1), and as “the truth from your Lord” (2:147).

This certainty has an important companion: the Qur’an repeatedly invites reflection rather than demanding an unthinking leap. It asks, “Do they not reflect upon the Qur’an?” (4:82), points to the harmony and coherence of its message, and challenges its opponents to produce a comparable sūrah (2:23). Faith in the Qur’an is therefore not credulity. It is trust grounded in signs—its language, message, preservation, moral seriousness, fulfilled guidance, and agreement with the truth brought by the earlier prophets. The believer may not know the answer to every question, but he has sufficient reason to trust the One who speaks.

### Guidance Is Received According to Taqwā

The Book is described as *hudan lil-muttaqīn*: “a guide for those mindful of Allah.” This does not mean that the Qur’an was sent only for a narrow class or that its invitation is closed to those who do not yet possess taqwā. Allah calls it “a guidance for humanity” (2:185) and says that the Qur’an was sent to clarify the way for all people (14:1). Its universal address remains open. However, guidance becomes transformative only in the heart that is willing to be guided. The same rain can fall on fertile soil and barren rock; the difference is not in the rain but in what receives it.

*Taqwā* comes from a root associated with protecting oneself. It is commonly rendered as God-consciousness, reverence, or mindfulness of Allah. It is not merely fear, and it is not a claim of sinlessness. The *muttaqī* is the person who places a moral protection between himself and Allah’s displeasure by believing, obeying, repenting, and remaining alert to the consequences of his choices. The opening description in the next verses gives concrete signs of this disposition: faith in the unseen, prayer, generosity, belief in revelation, and certainty in the Hereafter (2:3–5).

The sequence is significant. The Qur’an does not first promise that every question about life will disappear. It first forms the posture of the reader. A proud reader approaches revelation as an object to be defeated; a sincere reader approaches it as truth to be understood and lived. This does not excuse weak arguments or silence honest questions. Rather, it explains why two people can hear the same āyah and emerge with opposite responses. One asks what the truth requires of him; the other asks how the truth can be made harmless to his desires. The Qur’an guides the first because he has opened himself to moral correction.

Guidance in the Qur’an has both explanatory and practical dimensions. It reveals who Allah is, what human beings are, why they exist, what is right and wrong, what happens after death, and how justice and mercy should be embodied in individual and communal life. It also guides the reader through stages: it corrects false beliefs, purifies intentions, establishes worship, disciplines conduct, and directs society toward justice. A person who reads only for information may acquire concepts without transformation; the one who reads with taqwā allows the Book to examine him.

The verse therefore places the reader before a choice. The Qur’an is certain in itself, but its benefits are proportionate to sincerity. The proper response is not merely to admire its eloquence or collect quotations. It is to recite it, understand it, submit to its commands, seek refuge in its warnings, and let its promises reorder one’s priorities. In this way, “the Book” becomes “guidance,” and guidance becomes a lived path.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:185: the Qur’an is “a guidance for humanity” and contains clear proofs of guidance.
- Qur’an 3:138: it is a clear explanation, guidance, and admonition for the mindful.
- Qur’an 4:82: the invitation to reflect on the Qur’an’s coherence.
- Qur’an 11:1 and 17:88 on the Qur’an’s perfected nature and inimitability.
- Qur’an 14:1 and 39:23 on revelation bringing people from darkness to light and affecting receptive hearts.
- Qur’an 49:13 and 65:2–3 on *taqwā* as a conscious relationship with Allah, not a claim of ethnic or social superiority.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:3

> who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,

**Commentary:**

### Faith Begins Beyond the Visible

The first quality of the *muttaqūn* is that they “believe in the unseen”—*yu’minūna bil-ghayb*. The unseen is not simply whatever a person finds mysterious. It is the realm of realities beyond ordinary human sense perception and independent discovery, known because Allah and His Messenger have truthfully disclosed them. It includes Allah Himself, His angels, revelation, the resurrection, Paradise, Hell, divine decree, and the realities of the intermediate life. Belief in the unseen does not mean believing without evidence; it means accepting reliable revelation about realities that the senses cannot exhaustively reach.

Human beings already live by confidence in realities they cannot directly see. We trust that other minds exist, that moral obligations are real, and that causes will ordinarily produce effects, even though none of these convictions is established by sight alone. Yet Qur’anic faith is more disciplined than vague spirituality. It is grounded in the signs of Allah in creation, the truthfulness of the messengers, the integrity and inimitability of revelation, and the fitrah—the natural disposition that recognizes dependence upon a Creator. The unseen therefore corrects the arrogance of a worldview that treats the measurable as the whole of reality. “They know only the outer surface of the life of this world,” the Qur’an says, while remaining heedless of the Hereafter (30:7).

Belief in the unseen also protects worship from becoming a performance for spectators. If a person prays, gives, restrains anger, or refuses injustice only when people are watching, his conduct is controlled by the visible audience. The one who believes in the unseen knows that Allah sees the hidden intention and the deed that no human records. This is why the Qur’an connects faith in the unseen with sincerity, and why the Prophet (ﷺ) defined *iḥsān* as worshipping Allah as though one sees Him—and, if one cannot reach that spiritual presence, knowing that He sees the worshipper (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8, the ḥadīth of Jibrīl).

### Prayer Turns Faith into a Daily Covenant

The verse then moves from inward conviction to the most regular bodily expression of faith: “establish prayer.” The Arabic is not merely *yuṣallūn*—they pray—but *yuqīmūna aṣ-ṣalāh*, they establish it. Establishing prayer includes performing it at its appointed times, fulfilling its conditions and essential acts, guarding its inward reverence, and allowing it to reform one’s conduct. A prayer that is technically performed but deliberately kept disconnected from obedience has not achieved its full purpose. “Indeed, prayer restrains from shameful and unjust deeds” (29:45).

Prayer is the believer’s repeated return to Allah throughout the day. It interrupts work, appetite, distraction, and self-importance, placing the human being in a posture of standing, bowing, and prostration before the One who gave him life. It also gives faith a stable rhythm. Spiritual enthusiasm rises and falls, but the obligatory prayer remains a covenant that carries the servant back even when emotion is weak. The Prophet (ﷺ) described the five daily prayers as like a river at the door of a person in which he washes five times a day; no dirt remains after such regular washing (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

### Spending from Provision, Not Possession

The third quality is generosity: they “donate from what We have provided for them.” The wording shifts the believer’s understanding of ownership. Wealth, health, skill, time, land, knowledge, and opportunity are all provisions originating from Allah. Human beings may possess and lawfully use them, but they are trustees rather than absolute owners. This perspective cures both pride in abundance and despair in deprivation. The generous person does not imagine that he created his own capacity to earn, and he does not imagine that giving makes Allah indebted to him.

The command includes obligatory alms-tax (*zakāh*) and voluntary charity (*ṣadaqah*), while the broader principle includes every beneficial expenditure made for Allah’s sake. The Qur’an later defines righteousness by placing financial care beside belief and prayer: giving cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers, and captives (2:177). It also warns that charity is spoiled when followed by reminders of generosity or injury (2:262–264). Giving, therefore, is not merely the transfer of money; it is the purification of the giver’s relationship with wealth and the protection of the dignity of the receiver.

The order of the three qualities is instructive. Faith in the unseen gives the believer his horizon; prayer gives him a repeated vertical bond with Allah; spending gives that bond a horizontal consequence in the lives of other people. The genuine worshipper does not retreat into private spirituality while ignoring suffering, and the activist does not replace worship with public service. Belief, prayer, and generosity together form a person whose heart is oriented toward Allah and whose resources become a mercy to creation.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:177 on the connection between faith, prayer, charity, and social responsibility.
- Qur’an 23:1–4 and 70:22–25 on prayer and spending as qualities of successful believers.
- Qur’an 29:45 on prayer restraining indecency and wrongdoing.
- Qur’an 30:7 and 52:35–36 on the limits of reducing reality to what is immediately visible.
- Qur’an 57:7 and 64:15–17 on wealth as a trust and charity as a means of purification.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8, the ḥadīth of Jibrīl on faith in the unseen and *iḥsān*.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the parable of the five daily prayers as a river that washes away sins.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Commentary:**

### One Source of Revelation

The *muttaqūn* believe in what was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) and what was revealed before him. This places Islam within the continuous history of divine guidance. The Qur’an does not present itself as an isolated religious invention, nor does it teach that God began speaking to humanity only with the final Prophet. Allah sent messengers to every people (16:36), and the revealed path brought by them was one in its foundation: worship Allah alone, live righteously, and prepare to meet Him.

The believer therefore affirms the original revelations given to prophets such as Mūsā, Dāwūd, and ʿĪsā (peace be upon them), as well as the revelation given to Ibrāhīm and the other messengers. This is not the same as declaring every later manuscript, interpretation, or religious claim to be untouched revelation. The Qur’an says that earlier scripture was entrusted to religious scholars, some of whom preserved part of it while others concealed, distorted, or forgot what they had been taught (5:44, 5:13–15). The Qur’an confirms the truth that remains in the earlier revelations and acts as a criterion over them (*muhaymin*, 5:48). Thus Muslim faith honours the genuine source of previous revelation while allowing the final revelation to correct human alterations and disputed claims.

This principle prevents two opposite errors. One error is religious exclusivism that denies every truth outside one’s later community and insults the prophets who came before. The other is an indiscriminate relativism that treats all texts and all doctrines as equally authoritative, even where they contradict the Qur’an’s decisive teaching about Allah’s oneness, prophethood, and judgment. The Qur’an instead calls Muslims to say: “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and what was given to Moses and Jesus and what was given to the prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them” (2:136). The distinction is not between prophets in faith, but between the truth of their original revelation and later claims that must be tested by the final criterion.

### Believing in the Messenger and What He Brings

Belief in what was revealed “to you” includes belief in the Prophet as the trustworthy recipient and conveyer of revelation. It is not enough to admire a scripture in the abstract while rejecting the messenger appointed to explain and embody it. Allah says, “Whoever obeys the Messenger has truly obeyed Allah” (4:80), and the Qur’an describes the Prophet’s function as reciting revelation, purifying people, and teaching them the Book and wisdom (2:151). The Sunnah is therefore not a rival revelation or an optional biography; it is the prophetic explanation and lived application of the Qur’an, preserved and assessed through the methods of ḥadīth scholarship.

The verse’s final quality is *yaqīn* concerning the Hereafter—certainty, not a passing assumption. The Hereafter gives moral reality its complete horizon. In this world, the unjust may prosper, the truthful may suffer, and many deeds remain hidden from public judgment. Without resurrection and accountability, human history would often end with injustice apparently victorious. Belief in the Last Day assures the believer that no act is lost: “Whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it” (99:7–8).

Certainty in the Hereafter changes the meaning of present life. It teaches the believer to resist immediate temptation for a lasting reward, to endure hardship without concluding that Allah has abandoned him, and to forgive without believing that wrong has been ignored. It also restrains oppression: the hidden bribe, the private betrayal, and the unrecorded injury are all known to the Judge before whom every soul will stand. The Qur’an calls the worldly life a temporary enjoyment and the Hereafter the lasting home (40:39; 13:26).

The four qualities in verses 3–4 therefore complete one another. Faith in the unseen gives access to realities beyond the senses; prayer enacts submission; spending proves that faith has loosened the grip of possession; belief in all revelation joins the believer to the prophetic chain; and certainty in the Hereafter prevents the present world from becoming the final measure of truth. This is not a loose collection of religious preferences. It is a coherent worldview centred on one Lord, one moral purpose, and one final return.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:136, 3:84, and 4:136 on believing in all divinely sent prophets and revelations.
- Qur’an 5:13–15 and 5:44–48 on earlier scripture, human concealment, and the Qur’an as a confirming and overseeing criterion.
- Qur’an 16:36 and 35:24 on the universality of the prophetic mission.
- Qur’an 2:151 and 4:80 on the Prophet’s role in teaching, purifying, and conveying divine guidance.
- Qur’an 23:115–116, 40:39, and 99:7–8 on the certainty and moral consequences of the Hereafter.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8, the ḥadīth of Jibrīl, which defines faith as belief in Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, and the Last Day.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:5

> It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.

**Commentary:**

### Guidance from Their Lord

The preceding verses described the inward and outward qualities of the mindful: faith in the unseen, prayer, generosity, belief in revelation, and certainty in the Hereafter. This verse gives the divine assessment: “It is they who are guided by their Lord.” The guidance is attributed to *rabbihim*—their Lord, Sustainer, Educator, and Nurturer. It is Allah who creates the capacity to recognize truth, sends revelation, raises messengers, opens the heart to faith, and gives the believer strength to continue upon the path.

At the same time, the verse does not turn human response into a meaningless illusion. The people described did not receive guidance while remaining indifferent; they believed, prayed, gave, and cultivated certainty. The Qur’an repeatedly joins divine guidance with a person’s willing response: “Those who strive for Us—We will surely guide them to Our ways” (29:69), and “As for those who are guided, He increases them in guidance and grants them their mindfulness” (47:17). Guidance begins as a gift, is received through humility, and is strengthened through obedience.

Scholars distinguish between guidance in the sense of showing and clarifying the way, and guidance in the sense of enabling a heart to follow it. The Qur’an itself says to the Prophet (ﷺ), “You cannot guide whoever you love, but Allah guides whoever He wills” (28:56), while also saying of the Prophet, “Indeed, you guide to a straight path” (42:52). The first verse refers to the inward granting of faith; the second refers to conveying, teaching, and directing people to the path. Both truths stand together: the messenger delivers the evidence and invitation, while Allah opens hearts and grants steadfastness.

### Success Is More Than Worldly Gain

The verse calls these people *al-mufliḥūn*—the successful, the people who attain *falāḥ*. In ordinary language, success may mean wealth, influence, health, victory, or public recognition. The Qur’an does not deny that these can be temporary blessings, but it refuses to make them the final measure. A person can possess the world and still lose his soul; another may suffer deprivation while being among the truly successful. The Qur’anic standard is escape from ultimate loss and attainment of Allah’s pleasure, forgiveness, and Paradise.

The same term appears in the call to prayer: “Come to prayer; come to success.” This connection is revealing. Success is not merely what the believer receives at the end; it is also the direction in which he orders his life now. Prayer repeatedly summons him away from a narrow definition of prosperity and toward worship. The Qur’an says, “Successful indeed are the believers” and then describes their humility in prayer, avoidance of vain speech, purification, chastity, fulfilment of trusts, and guarding of prayers (23:1–11). Success is therefore a moral and spiritual pattern before it becomes a final reward.

The wording “it is they” is emphatic. True guidance and success belong to the people whose faith is made visible in these qualities, not to those who merely claim a religious identity. The Qur’an warns against empty profession in many places: “It is not your wishful thinking nor the wishful thinking of the People of the Book; whoever does evil will be recompensed for it” (4:123). This does not mean that deeds earn Paradise independently of divine mercy. Rather, genuine faith produces obedience, and Allah’s mercy accepts and multiplies that obedience.

These opening verses also establish a pattern repeated throughout the Qur’an: revelation describes the path, identifies the people who take it, and announces their end. The reader is not left with abstract theology. He is invited to examine himself. Does he acknowledge realities beyond what he can see? Does prayer have a protected place in his day? Does his provision benefit others? Does he honour all true revelation and follow the final messenger? Does the Hereafter govern his choices? The answer to these questions indicates whether he is merely reading about guidance or actually travelling under it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:3–4 for the qualities that precede this verdict of guidance and success.
- Qur’an 23:1–11 on the characteristics and outcome of successful believers.
- Qur’an 24:51–52 on the response of the believers and the connection between obedience and success.
- Qur’an 28:56 and 42:52 on the distinction between conveying guidance and Allah’s granting of inward guidance.
- Qur’an 29:69 and 47:17 on striving, receptivity, and increase in guidance.
- Qur’an 103:1–3 on the universal danger of loss and the qualities that preserve a person from it.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:6

> As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.

**Commentary:**

### The Persistent Rejecter

After describing the people who receive guidance, the sūrah turns to those who persist in disbelief. The Arabic phrase *inna alladhīna kafarū* refers to people characterized by *kufr*—deliberate rejection, denial, or concealment of truth—not to every person who has not yet heard the message clearly or who is still sincerely seeking. The verse describes a settled posture of refusal. It should therefore not be used carelessly as a judgment upon particular individuals whose circumstances, understanding, or final state are known only to Allah (ﷻ).

The Prophet (ﷺ) is told that warning will not benefit such people. This does not mean that revelation is ineffective or that the messenger failed. Warning is meant to clarify the truth, remove excuses, awaken conscience, and invite repentance. But a person can reach a point at which he repeatedly rejects every reminder, not because the evidence is absent, but because accepting it would require surrendering pride, habit, power, or desire. At that stage, the warning exposes the condition of the heart even when it does not reform it. The Qur’an says, “It is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts within the breasts that are blind” (22:46).

### Not a Doctrine of Arbitrary Fatalism

This verse must be read alongside the many passages in which Allah invites disbelievers to repent and promises forgiveness. “Say to those who disbelieve: if they desist, what has already passed will be forgiven for them” (8:38). The people of Makkah who opposed the Prophet in the early period were not all permanently closed; some later became among the strongest believers. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (raḍiyallāhu ʿanhu), Khālid ibn al-Walīd (raḍiyallāhu ʿanhu), and many others moved from opposition to faith. The verse is therefore not saying that every unbeliever is metaphysically incapable of believing. It describes those who have chosen persistent rejection and, in the context of the complete warning, refuse to respond.

The Qur’an presents the hardening of the heart as connected to human choices. “When they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate” (61:5). The initial deviation is attributed to them; the further deviation is a just consequence and divine abandonment of those who insist upon it. Similarly, “Allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves” (13:11). Divine knowledge and decree do not make obedience and disobedience morally meaningless. Allah knows what people will choose without compelling them to love or hate the truth.

The verse also clarifies the task of the messenger and the teacher. Guidance cannot be forced into a heart. The duty is to convey with wisdom, sound argument, patience, and good character: “Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in the best manner” (16:125). The messenger is not charged with manufacturing belief or controlling the unseen response of another person. His responsibility is faithful proclamation; the response belongs to the hearer, and the final judgment belongs to Allah.

### The Danger of Becoming Unteachable

There is a warning here for believers as well. A person may have faith in general and yet become selectively resistant to a command, correction, or truth that conflicts with his interest. Every repeated refusal can make the next refusal easier. The heart does not usually become sealed in a single dramatic moment; it may become less sensitive through accumulated excuses, concealed sins, mockery of the righteous, and rejection of reminders. The believer therefore asks Allah for firmness and does not treat the ability to respond to guidance as a permanent personal possession.

Warning remains meaningful even when some reject it. It protects the truth from being redefined by its opponents, gives the sincere a way back, and establishes justice on the Day of Judgment. The Qur’an says messengers were sent “so that mankind would have no argument against Allah after the messengers” (4:165). The messenger’s warning is not measured only by the number of immediate conversions; it is also a mercy to those who accept and a decisive proof against those who knowingly reject.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:165 on messengers establishing proof and removing excuses.
- Qur’an 8:38 on forgiveness for those who desist from disbelief.
- Qur’an 13:11 and 61:5 on human deviation and the consequence of continued deviation.
- Qur’an 16:125 and 28:56 on the messenger’s duty to convey and the fact that inward guidance belongs to Allah.
- Qur’an 22:46 and 47:16 on blindness and hardness as conditions of the heart.
- Qur’an 39:53 and 66:8 on the continuing door of repentance and the prayer for steadfastness.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:7

> Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Commentary:**

### A Seal Produced by Persistent Rejection

The previous āyah described those who persist in disbelief; this āyah explains the consequence of that persistence. “Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered.” The heart in Qur’anic language is the centre of understanding, intention, moral perception, and spiritual response. The verse does not describe a physical defect. It describes a person whose inward faculty of recognition has been closed through sustained refusal, while his eyes and ears remain physically functional.

The verb *khatama*—to seal—suggests closure after something has been placed inside. A seal protects a container, but it also prevents access to what is within. In this context it depicts the settled condition of a person who has repeatedly rejected clear truth until reminders no longer enter as guidance. The Qur’an elsewhere gives the process in moral terms: “No! Rather, a stain has covered their hearts because of what they used to earn” (83:14). Sin and rejection leave an effect; the effect can become a covering; and the covering can become a seal when a person refuses to turn back.

This is not arbitrary punishment imposed before a choice. The Qur’an states that when people “deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate” (61:5), and that Allah does not guide a wrongdoing people (2:258; 61:5). Divine sealing is therefore both an act of Allah’s judgment and the consequence of a person’s chosen direction. It is comparable to a path that becomes increasingly difficult to leave the more often it is travelled. Allah is never unjust, and the verse must be read with the numerous calls to repentance addressed to those who have not yet reached this final persistence.

### Hearing and Seeing Are Moral Faculties

The reference to hearing and sight is especially significant. The disbelievers heard the recitation and saw signs, but they did not hear and see in the sense of allowing perception to produce submission. The Qur’an distinguishes between physical reception and beneficial understanding: “They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear” (7:179). It also asks, “Are those who know equal to those who do not know?” (39:9), showing that true knowledge is knowledge that reaches the moral will.

This does not denigrate the senses. The Qur’an repeatedly directs people to look at the heavens and the earth, listen to revelation, and reflect upon the signs in creation. The problem is not that the sealed person lacks data. It is that he has trained himself to neutralize the meaning of the data. He can hear a warning and immediately turn it into a joke; see a blessing and attribute it solely to himself; witness injustice and defend it because it serves his group. The sensory organs remain open while the conscience is closed.

The image of covered sight also exposes the limits of materialism. Sight can measure surfaces but cannot by itself recognize the purpose of life, the obligation of justice, or the reality of resurrection. The Qur’an says that many people know the outward appearance of worldly life but are heedless of the Hereafter (30:7). Spiritual blindness is therefore not lack of intelligence. A clever person may construct arguments in defence of what he already wants, while a humble person with little formal learning may recognize truth and act upon it.

### The Seriousness of the Punishment

The consequence is “a tremendous punishment”—*ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm*. Qur’anic warnings are not rhetorical intimidation detached from mercy. They are a form of mercy because they disclose the end of a road before the traveller reaches it. The punishment is tremendous because the rejection is not a harmless private preference: it is rejection of the Creator, denial of accountability, and persistence in a condition that corrupts the person and often harms others. The Qur’an joins disbelief with moral and social consequences in many passages, including 5:33, 16:88, and 47:1–9.

Yet the believer should not use this verse to pronounce that a living person’s heart is sealed. The Prophet (ﷺ) was not commanded to investigate hidden hearts; he judged people by their outward professions and conduct while leaving their inward realities to Allah. The appropriate response is fear for oneself, compassion in calling others to truth, and confidence that Allah’s judgment is perfect. A heart that still feels remorse, asks questions sincerely, or longs for guidance has not been abandoned from the path of repentance.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 6:110, 7:179, and 22:46 on hearts, hearing, and sight as instruments of spiritual perception.
- Qur’an 13:11 and 61:5 on deviation and the consequence of choosing it.
- Qur’an 30:7 and 83:14 on outward perception, heedlessness, and the stain upon hearts caused by wrongdoing.
- Qur’an 39:53 on Allah’s open invitation to repentance, which must be read alongside the warning against persistent rejection.
- Qur’an 47:1–9 and 16:88 on the consequences of knowingly rejecting divine guidance.
- For the relationship between sin and the darkening of the heart, see Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 3334, the ḥadīth describing a black mark placed upon the heart through repeated sin and removed through repentance.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:8

> And there are some who say, “We believe in Allah and the Last Day,” yet they are not ˹true˺ believers.

**Commentary:**

### The Third Category: Hypocrisy

The opening passage has now presented three broad responses to revelation: the mindful who accept it, the persistent rejecters who openly refuse it, and the hypocrites who present an outward claim while concealing inward denial. The Arabic word *munāfiq* is connected with *nifāq*, hypocrisy. Classical scholars described a hypocrite of belief as one who displays Islam while hiding disbelief. Such a person is more difficult to identify than the open disbeliever because his public language resembles faith while his private allegiance opposes it.

The hypocrites named here appeared in the Medinan community after the Prophet (ﷺ) and the believers gained social and political strength. Open opposition could carry a cost, so some people entered the public ranks of Islam for protection, influence, information, or the hope of weakening the believers from within. Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn later records their claim, “We bear witness that you are certainly the Messenger of Allah,” followed by the divine declaration that Allah knows they are liars (63:1). Their words were formally correct, but their testimony was not supported by belief in the heart.

This verse does not say that every believer who struggles, sins, or experiences a fluctuation in faith is a hypocrite of creed. Islam distinguishes between major hypocrisy—concealing disbelief beneath an outward claim of faith—and hypocrisy in conduct, such as lying, breaking promises, and betraying trusts. The Prophet (ﷺ) said: “The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; and when he is entrusted, he betrays” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Another narration adds that when he quarrels he behaves wickedly. These traits are acts of hypocrisy and are extremely dangerous, but possessing one of them does not automatically place a person outside Islam.

### Mere Profession Is Not the Whole of Faith

The hypocrites say, “We believe in Allah and the Last Day.” They mention the two foundations that should govern every other belief: Allah’s oneness and the certainty of accountability. Yet the claim remains verbal. Faith in the Qur’an is not reduced to a public slogan. It is a conviction of the heart expressed by the tongue and confirmed by conduct. When faith enters the heart, it produces reverence, prayer, honesty, sacrifice, and loyalty to truth even when those qualities are costly.

This does not mean that a person must be flawless before he can be called a believer. The believers of the first generation sinned, became afraid, grieved, and sometimes fell short, but they returned to Allah and did not make deception their religious identity. The hypocrite’s defining feature is not ordinary weakness; it is the deliberate division between public profession and private allegiance. He wants the benefits of belonging without the surrender that belonging requires.

The mention of the Last Day is particularly revealing. A person who truly believes that every concealed motive will be exposed and every deed judged cannot treat public appearance as the final reality. Hypocrisy is sustained by living as though the audience of people matters more than the knowledge of Allah. The Qur’an warns that the hypocrites attempt to deceive Allah and the believers, but they deceive only themselves (2:9). Their supposed advantage is therefore temporary and self-destructive.

### A Warning Against Suspicion and Self-Righteousness

Because hypocrisy is hidden, this passage should produce vigilance rather than a culture of accusation. The believer examines his own intentions and conduct before assigning hidden motives to others. The Prophet (ﷺ) did not permit people to treat accusations as proof, and he continued to deal with the outward claims of members of the community according to justice. Hidden judgment belongs to Allah, who knows what hearts conceal and what circumstances surround a person’s choices.

At the same time, the community must not be naïve. Public religious language cannot be allowed to sanctify betrayal, corruption, or violence. Actions may reveal patterns of hypocrisy, and leaders must protect people from harm through fair evidence and due process. The balance is Qur’anic: do not claim knowledge of the unseen, but do not allow eloquent claims to replace truth, trustworthiness, and accountable conduct.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:142–146, 9:67–68, and 63:1–4 on the attitudes and fate of hypocrites.
- Qur’an 2:9–20, which develops the difference between their public claim and hidden reality.
- Qur’an 49:6 and 49:12 on verifying reports and avoiding suspicion and backbiting.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the signs of hypocrisy: lying, breaking promises, and betraying trusts.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8, the ḥadīth of Jibrīl, on faith as more than a verbal claim.
- For the distinction between hypocrisy in belief and hypocrisy in conduct, see the discussions of nifāq in Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr and al-Nawawī’s commentary on the relevant ḥadīths in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:9

> They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.

**Commentary:**

### The Impossibility of Deceiving Allah

The hypocrites imagine that their outward profession allows them to manipulate the divine judgment and the believers’ perception. The Qur’an says that they “seek to deceive Allah and the believers.” The wording does not mean that Allah can actually be fooled. It describes their intention and their attempted strategy as it appears from their own limited perspective. Allah knows the unseen and the concealed: “He knows the stealthy glance and what the breasts conceal” (40:19). A person may hide a motive from people, but he cannot hide it from the One who created the motive, the heart, and the person himself.

The phrase also conveys a form of divine response. In Arabic, the verb *yukhādiʿūn* has the sense of attempting a deceptive exchange. Allah responds to their attempted deception by exposing its futility and allowing their own strategy to become evidence against them. Similar expressions occur when the Qur’an says that the hypocrites try to deceive Allah, while “He is deceiving them” (4:142), meaning that their false confidence is answered with a just requital, not that Allah practises a human vice. Divine attributes are understood in a manner befitting Allah’s perfection: His response to deception is justice and the unveiling of truth, never ignorance or moral defect.

### Self-Deception and the Divided Self

Their real victim is themselves. They may gain access to a community’s protection, wealth, influence, or information, but these temporary benefits cannot save a person from accountability. The more successfully they maintain an external religious image, the more deeply they train themselves to live by falsehood. They begin by deceiving others and end by believing the story they have constructed about themselves. The Qur’an describes this broader human danger: “They forget Allah, so He makes them forget themselves” (59:19), and “they wrong no one but themselves, though they do not perceive it” (2:57).

Self-deception operates through selective attention. The hypocrite notices the advantages of appearing faithful but refuses to consider death, resurrection, and the exposure of hidden motives. He may interpret patience as weakness, sincerity as naivety, and religious law as a tool to be used against others rather than a judgment upon himself. In this way, the intelligence that could lead him to repentance is recruited to defend his duplicity. The Qur’an’s phrase *wa-mā yashʿurūn*—“they do not perceive”—indicates not a lack of physical sensation but a moral unawareness.

A person can also deceive himself without being a full hypocrite of belief. Every believer is vulnerable to confusing intention with achievement, appearance with character, or religious vocabulary with submission. Someone may give publicly and call it service while secretly seeking admiration; speak of justice while excusing injustice committed by his own group; or claim trust in Allah while arranging his life around the fear of people. The cure is honest self-examination, repentance, and asking Allah to purify the heart. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that deeds are judged by intentions (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 1; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1907), which means that hidden motives are not secondary details in the divine evaluation.

### Protecting the Community from Deception

The verse also establishes a social principle: sincere communities need both compassion and discernment. Believers should not become so suspicious that they destroy trust, but neither should they treat every pious claim as proof of integrity. The Qur’an commands verification of reports (49:6), fairness even toward opponents (5:8), and the fulfilment of trusts (4:58). These safeguards protect public life without pretending to know the unseen.

The believer’s confidence rests in the fact that Allah’s justice does not depend upon human exposure. A corrupt person may escape investigation, and a hypocrite may receive praise, but neither outcome changes the record kept by Allah. The day will come when “the secrets are tested and exposed” (86:9), and when what people tried to conceal becomes visible. The short-term success of deception is therefore not success at all; it is a postponement that makes the eventual loss more severe.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:57 and 59:19 on people harming or forgetting themselves while failing to recognize it.
- Qur’an 4:142 on the hypocrites’ attempt to deceive Allah and the believers.
- Qur’an 40:19, 64:4, and 86:9 on Allah’s knowledge of hidden motives and the disclosure of secrets.
- Qur’an 49:6 and 5:8 on verifying claims and maintaining justice.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 1, and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1907, “Actions are only by intentions.”
- For the rhetorical explanation of divine “requital” in expressions involving deception, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 2:9 and 4:142.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:10

> There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.

**Commentary:**

### Spiritual Sickness

The “heart” in Qur’anic discourse is the inward centre of belief, understanding, intention, and moral choice. The sickness mentioned here is therefore not a diagnosis of the body. It is a spiritual disease: doubt that is cultivated rather than honestly resolved, envy of truth and its people, love of deception, cowardice before accountability, and a willingness to prefer worldly advantage to sincere submission. The hypocrites’ problem is not simply that they lack information. Their inner orientation is diseased, so information that should heal becomes material for further manipulation.

The Qur’an uses the same language for a range of inward conditions. It calls some hearts diseased because they pursue immoral desire (33:32), others because they harbour doubt and suspicion (9:45), and describes the hearts of believers as finding tranquillity through the remembrance of Allah (13:28). The metaphor is powerful because a physical illness weakens the body’s ability to function, while spiritual sickness weakens the person’s ability to recognize, love, and follow what is true. In both cases, neglect can turn a manageable condition into a destructive one.

### Allah’s Allowing of Increase

“Allah lets their sickness increase” is a warning about the moral law of consequence. When people knowingly prefer falsehood, each preference prepares them for the next. A lie requires another lie for protection; hidden envy becomes resentment; resentment becomes mockery; mockery becomes open hostility. The increase is attributed to Allah because nothing occurs outside His knowledge and decree, but it is connected to their chosen response. The Qur’an explains this pattern: “When they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate” (61:5), and “We turn their hearts and eyes away because they refused to believe in it the first time” (6:110).

This divine abandonment is not a denial of Allah’s mercy or a declaration that repentance is impossible for everyone who sins. The Qur’an opens the door of repentance even to those who have committed grave wrongs (39:53), and history records people who left opposition and hypocrisy for sincere faith. The warning applies to those who continue to nurture the disease and die without turning back. It teaches that spiritual states are not static. The heart can be purified, or it can be further darkened by what a person repeatedly chooses.

### Lies as the Disease’s Public Symptom

The verse concludes by identifying lying as a principal symptom. The hypocrites’ public statement of faith is a lie because it is deliberately used to conceal the opposite inward position. Their words are not merely inaccurate; they are instruments of harm. False testimony can deprive people of rights, corrupt communal decisions, and make trust impossible. This is why the Prophet (ﷺ) placed truthfulness at the centre of moral formation: “Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise; lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

The warning includes more than formal religious declarations. A person lies whenever he knowingly presents falsehood as truth, conceals a material fact in order to exploit another, promises what he has no intention of fulfilling, or uses spiritual language to disguise a worldly motive. Not every mistake is a lie; the decisive factor is deliberate misrepresentation. Repentance requires more than regret. It requires abandoning the falsehood, correcting its harm where possible, and rebuilding trust through truthful conduct.

### Healing the Heart

The Qur’an does not merely expose the disease; it teaches its cure. “O humanity, there has come to you instruction from your Lord and healing for what is in the hearts” (10:57). Sincere faith, remembrance, prayer, truthful companionship, lawful nourishment, and repeated repentance all oppose the habits that sustain hypocrisy. The believer asks not only whether his words are technically correct, but whether his heart is aligned with them.

The description of the hypocrites should consequently produce fear without despair. A person who is troubled by his own insincerity and seeks correction is in a different condition from the one who celebrates deception and refuses to repent. Fear can be a medicine when it drives a person toward Allah; it becomes another disease when it is used to justify hopelessness or accusation. Allah is the One who purifies hearts, and the way to that purification begins with acknowledging the sickness honestly.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 9:45, 13:28, and 33:32 on different states of the heart.
- Qur’an 6:110 and 61:5 on deviation increasing through repeated rejection.
- Qur’an 10:57 on revelation as healing for what is in the hearts.
- Qur’an 24:21 and 39:53 on resisting Satan and never despairing of Allah’s mercy.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on truthfulness leading to righteousness and lying leading to wickedness.
- For the classical treatment of the heart’s diseases and their increase through hypocrisy, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ghazālī’s Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn, Book of the Marvels of the Heart.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”

**Commentary:**

### Corruption Hidden Beneath the Language of Reform

The hypocrites are confronted with a direct moral instruction: “Do not spread corruption in the land.” Their answer is not repentance or clarification but self-certification: “We are only peace-makers.” This is one of the most persistent patterns in human history. Harmful people rarely describe their own actions as harmful. They give them noble names—security, reform, progress, liberation, order, realism, or peace—and expect the label to settle the moral question.

The Qur’anic word *fasād* is broad. It includes damage to faith, life, family, honour, justice, trust, public order, and the natural balance Allah has established. It is not limited to one type of political disorder. Sūrah al-Rūm says that corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people’s hands have earned (30:41), linking social and environmental breakdown to human wrongdoing. Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ records the counsel, “Do not seek corruption in the land; indeed, Allah does not love the corrupters” (28:77). A person spreads corruption when his action systematically produces harm, even if his rhetoric claims a benevolent purpose.

### Peace Is Not Merely a Slogan

The hypocrites’ claim to be peace-makers does not mean that every peace proposal is false or that disagreement itself is corruption. Islam commands reconciliation, justice, and the removal of conflict. “If two groups of believers fight, make peace between them,” the Qur’an says, adding that reconciliation must be carried out with justice and not by protecting the aggressor (49:9). A peace that preserves oppression, silences the injured, or permits repeated aggression is not the Qur’anic ideal of *iṣlāḥ*—repair and reconciliation.

The difference between genuine reform and corrupting propaganda is measured by truth, method, and outcome. Genuine reform begins by acknowledging reality, respects human dignity, avoids forbidden means, and aims at justice rather than merely the preservation of power. Corruption often begins with false information, tribal or ideological incitement, betrayal of trusts, and the destruction of institutions that restrain abuse. It may speak constantly of peace while making ordinary peace impossible.

The Qur’an repeatedly exposes this inversion. Pharaoh presented himself as a reformer while accusing Moses and the believers of threatening the land (40:26–29). The people of Shuʿayb were told not to give short measure and not to spread corruption after the land had been set right (7:85). Reform, in Qur’anic terms, is not whatever the powerful declare it to be; it is restoration of the order of justice, honesty, worship, and mercy established by Allah.

### The First Cure Is Honest Recognition

The final words of the hypocrites’ answer reveal their deeper disease: they may no longer recognize corruption as corruption. A person who benefits from an unjust arrangement can become sincerely convinced that his defence of it is responsible. Repeated self-justification dulls moral perception. This is why the believer is commanded to weigh his conduct against revelation and to listen to sincere counsel, not merely to the praise of allies.

The verse also warns communities against judging claims by presentation alone. The polished speaker, the religious slogan, and the public declaration of good intentions are not substitutes for evidence. The Qur’an commands verification of reports (49:6), justice even toward those one dislikes (5:8), and consultation in communal affairs (42:38). These commands provide a way to resist both naïveté and reckless suspicion.

Finally, the verse teaches that peace-making is an act of worship only when it is truthful and just. The sincere reformer does not seek applause for appearing peaceful while quietly deepening division. He accepts correction, protects the vulnerable, and is willing to name wrongdoing even when it comes from his own side. The hypocrite’s tragedy is that he has made the language of virtue a shield for vice; the believer’s task is to make virtue real before he names it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:12, which gives Allah’s judgment on the claim made in this verse.
- Qur’an 5:8 on standing firmly for justice even toward people one dislikes.
- Qur’an 7:56 and 7:85 on not spreading corruption after the land has been set right.
- Qur’an 28:77 and 30:41 on the scope and consequences of *fasād*.
- Qur’an 42:38 and 49:6, 49:9 on consultation, verification, and just reconciliation.
- Qur’an 40:26–29 on the recurring political language of “reform” used to defend oppression.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:12

> Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.

**Commentary:**

### The Divine Verdict Corrects the Public Claim

The hypocrites have called themselves reformers, but Allah exposes the reality: “Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors.” The emphatic Arabic construction—*alā innahum hum al-mufsidūn*—places the responsibility directly upon them. The people who speak most confidently about peace are not necessarily the people producing peace; the decisive question is what their beliefs, alliances, and actions actually bring about.

This is a recurring Qur’anic method. It does not allow a person to define himself by his preferred title while ignoring the consequences of his conduct. A tyrant may call himself a guardian, an exploiter may call himself a benefactor, and a deceiver may call himself a reformer. Revelation returns the discussion to truth and result. “Allah does not amend the work of corrupters” (10:81), while He promises that the reward of those who fear Him and reform their conduct will not be lost (7:170; 18:30).

### Corruption Has Spiritual and Social Dimensions

The hypocrisy of these people is a spiritual corruption because it replaces sincere submission with calculated appearance. It is a social corruption because their duplicity damages trust within the community. They carry one message to the believers and another to their hidden allies, spread suspicion, expose private matters, and encourage conflict while claiming neutrality. The damage is therefore not measured only by a single act. It lies in the network of falsehood that makes cooperation, counsel, and justice difficult.

The Qur’an connects faith and social repair. The believers are instructed to reconcile disputing parties, care for the vulnerable, keep trusts, and speak words that are upright. Conversely, disbelief and hypocrisy can become engines of disorder when they make personal loyalty more important than truth. The Qur’an warns that a trial or corruption may not affect only the people who initiated it: “Beware of a trial that will not exclusively afflict those among you who have done wrong” (8:25). Public corruption spreads through silence, imitation, and the normalization of wrongdoing.

The phrase “they fail to perceive it”—*wa-lākin lā yashʿurūn*—shows that moral perception itself can be damaged. They are not merely hiding the truth from the believers; they have created a story in which their own corruption appears virtuous. This is more dangerous than ordinary error because an honest mistake can be corrected by evidence, while self-righteous self-deception treats correction as hostility. A person who cannot imagine that he might be wrong is difficult to guide, whatever his vocabulary may be.

### A Mirror for Believers

Although the immediate subject is the hypocrites, the verse is also a mirror. The believer asks whether he uses good language to cover harmful behaviour. Does he call gossip “concern,” domination “leadership,” cowardice “prudence,” or vengeance “justice”? Does he condemn corruption in opponents while excusing it in his own group? The Qur’an calls believers to be witnesses for justice, even when the testimony is against themselves, parents, or relatives (4:135).

This self-application prevents the passage from becoming an excuse for collective arrogance. It is easy to read “the corruptors” as a description of people outside one’s own circle. The Qur’an, however, repeatedly turns historical narratives into moral tests for its immediate audience. A Muslim institution, movement, family, or government can reproduce hypocrisy if it conceals wrongdoing behind religious language. Names and affiliations do not protect anyone from the consequences of injustice.

### Reform Requires Repentance and Repair

The opposite of *fasād* is not mere public respectability but *iṣlāḥ*—repair, restoration, and making things sound. Genuine reform begins with truthfulness about the damage already done. It seeks forgiveness from Allah and, where people have been harmed, restitution from those people. It abandons the methods that produced corruption instead of merely changing the public description of those methods.

This is why the next verse will show the hypocrites insulting the sincere believers as fools. Their unwillingness to recognize corruption is connected to their contempt for those who obey Allah. A community remains healthy when it honours truthful counsel and welcomes correction. It becomes corrupt when it rewards flattery and labels every moral objection an attack on peace.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:11, 8:25, and 10:81 on corruption, its spread, and Allah’s rejection of corrupting works.
- Qur’an 4:135 on standing for justice even against oneself and one’s own relations.
- Qur’an 7:56, 7:170, and 18:30 on repair, righteousness, and the reward of reformers.
- Qur’an 30:41 on the social and environmental consequences of human wrongdoing.
- Qur’an 49:9 on reconciliation that is governed by justice rather than appearances.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on the hypocrites’ claim to be reformers and the emphatic divine refutation in 2:12.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:13

> And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.

**Commentary:**

### The Arrogance of Looking Down on Faith

The hypocrites are invited to believe “as others believe.” The expression points to the sincere believers around them, especially the Companions who accepted the Prophet’s (ﷺ) message without treating faith as a badge of social superiority. The invitation is not to imitate a crowd blindly; it is to join people whose belief is grounded in revelation, truthfulness, sacrifice, and obedience. The hypocrites answer with contempt: “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Their insult reveals that they measure intelligence by worldly calculation rather than by the ability to recognize truth and act upon it.

The Qur’anic word *sufahāʾ*—fools, shallow-minded people—can refer to those whose judgment is deficient or whose priorities are upside down. The hypocrites apply it to believers because they see faith as a loss of status and material advantage. In their view, the believer’s willingness to pray, give, endure persecution, and obey a messenger is irrational. Yet the Qur’an reverses their judgment: true foolishness is to trade eternal reality for a temporary image, to possess evidence and refuse it, or to sacrifice conscience for the approval of people.

### The Qur’anic Standard of Intelligence

The Qur’an does not oppose faith to thought. It repeatedly calls people to reason, observe, remember, compare consequences, and reflect. “Only those who listen and follow the best of it are guided by Allah; it is they who are people of understanding” (39:18). The Qur’an criticizes those who refuse to use their hearing, sight, and hearts (7:179), not those who ask honest questions. Its condemnation falls upon intellectual arrogance: the decision to reject truth before examining it because submission would threaten an identity or interest.

A person may be highly educated and still be a fool in the Qur’anic sense if he cannot distinguish what is lasting from what is temporary. Pharaoh’s court possessed power and technical ability, but its arrogance did not make it wise. By contrast, the believing magicians who recognized the truth of Moses’ sign accepted faith despite the threat of torture (7:120–126). Their intelligence was demonstrated not by winning an argument but by refusing to let fear prevent them from following what they knew to be true.

The Qur’an describes the successful believers as those who listen, then follow the best of what they hear (39:18). This is an active intellectual virtue. It requires attention, discrimination, courage, and the willingness to change. The hypocrites, by contrast, are not seeking truth from the believers; they are protecting their own superiority. Their question is rhetorical and derisive. The insult is a defence mechanism against a call that exposes their divided loyalties.

### “As Others Believe” Does Not Mean Uncritical Conformity

The phrase should not be misused to demand that all Muslims imitate a group in matters where the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah leave legitimate room for difference. The believers are not defined by a personality cult, political party, ethnicity, or fashion. They are defined by their common submission to Allah and His messenger. The Qur’an praises those who believe in Allah and His messengers without rejecting some and accepting others (2:285), while also commanding believers to verify reports and avoid blind partisanship.

Thus the verse condemns elitist contempt, not thoughtful scholarship. A sincere questioner who seeks evidence is not a hypocrite merely because he does not yet understand. Nor is every person who makes a mistake a fool. The target is the person who dismisses faith and its adherents through ridicule, refusing to engage the truth because he assumes that worldly sophistication places him above revelation.

### The Inversion of Reality

The closing words, “Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know,” expose the deepest effect of hypocrisy: it reverses the moral order while concealing the reversal from the person himself. The people who are willing to sacrifice temporary advantage for truth are called irrational, while those who sacrifice truth for temporary advantage call themselves wise. Such inversion is visible in every age whenever integrity is mocked as weakness and opportunism is praised as intelligence.

The remedy is to ask Allah for sound judgment. Wisdom is not merely the possession of information; it is placing each thing in its proper rank. The Hereafter must be valued above the passing world, truth above group loyalty, and Allah’s approval above the applause of people. Whoever learns this ordering has escaped the foolishness described here, even if the world considers him unsophisticated.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:142, 39:18, and 67:10 on understanding, listening, and the misuse of reason.
- Qur’an 7:120–126 on the magicians who recognized truth and accepted it despite worldly danger.
- Qur’an 8:22 on those who possess hearing but refuse to understand.
- Qur’an 17:36 on not following what one does not know.
- Qur’an 28:77 and 63:9 on placing lasting accountability above worldly pride and distraction.
- For the explanation of *safāhah* as defective judgment and misdirected priorities, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:13.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:14

> When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”

**Commentary:**

### Two Faces and Two Audiences

This āyah shows hypocrisy in action. In the presence of believers, the hypocrites claim faith; in private with their own associates, they disown that claim and call it mockery. Their religion changes with the audience. They are not confused seekers who speak imprecisely. They understand the two messages they are delivering and deliberately use each one to secure a different advantage.

The expression “their devils”—*shayāṭīnihim* in the Arabic—has been explained by the classical exegetes as including the leaders and influential companions who encouraged their hypocrisy. The word can also carry its broader Qur’anic meaning of rebellious tempters among jinn or human beings. The Qur’an says that every prophet was tested by enemies—devils from humankind and jinn—who inspire one another with decorated speech (6:112). In this context, the hypocrites’ private circle is a network that reinforces contempt for revelation and treats deception as cleverness.

The verse does not require that every private friend of a sinner be literally a jinn or that every non-believing person be called a devil. Its point is moral association: the people who encourage a person to mock truth, betray trust, and maintain a double identity are functioning as tempters, while the hypocrite willingly adopts their companionship. Company shapes conscience. A person who repeatedly sits where faith is ridiculed may gradually begin to regard ridicule as normal and sincerity as embarrassing.

### Mockery as a Defence Against Truth

Their statement, “We were only mocking,” reveals that they understand the seriousness of what they are doing. Mockery is often used to avoid answering a truth. Rather than respond to the Prophet’s message, the signs of Allah, or the conduct of the believers, they turn the matter into entertainment. Humour can be wholesome, but ridicule that degrades revelation or sincere worship is not neutral. It trains the heart to laugh at what it should fear and to despise people for obeying their Lord.

The Qur’an later gives a severe warning to those who say they were merely joking after mocking Allah, His signs, and His Messenger: “Was it Allah and His revelations and His Messenger that you were mocking? Make no excuses; you have disbelieved after your belief” (9:65–66). This does not authorize individuals to make reckless accusations on the basis of every awkward remark. It does establish that claiming humour does not automatically erase the meaning or consequence of deliberate religious contempt.

### Sincerity Does Not Change with Company

The believer’s character is not supposed to split into a public self and a private self. He may speak differently according to context—wisdom requires appropriate speech—but the underlying values remain stable. “O you who believe, fear Allah and be with the truthful” (9:119). Truthfulness includes consistency between conviction, speech, and conduct. A person can repent from a bad habit, conceal a voluntary good deed, or refrain from exposing every private struggle without being hypocritical; hypocrisy is the intentional presentation of a false allegiance.

The Prophet (ﷺ) warned against the person who goes to one group with one face and another group with another face, describing such a person as among the worst people in the sight of Allah (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This is not a condemnation of diplomacy or the effort to reconcile people. A reconciler may convey good words between disputing parties without betraying either. The blameworthy two-faced person changes principles for advantage and feeds each side what will make it trust him.

### The Test of Private Conduct

Private conduct is often a clearer test of sincerity than public speech. The hypocrites are courageous before their own associates because they expect approval there, but they do not have the courage to present their true position openly before the believers. The believer, by contrast, knows that Allah is present in private as well as public. He may fear his own weakness, but he does not build a deliberate system of deception around it.

This verse should lead to self-examination rather than obsessive surveillance of others. Do I praise faith in public and ridicule its obligations in private? Do I change my moral standards according to the group whose approval I seek? Do I call sincerity naïve and duplicity intelligent? The cure is truthful companionship, repentance, and the remembrance that the private conversation is not hidden from Allah: “He knows what they conceal and what they reveal” (2:77).

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:77 and 4:142–145 on Allah’s knowledge of concealed hypocrisy and its consequences.
- Qur’an 6:112 on human and jinn tempters who beautify deceptive speech.
- Qur’an 9:65–66 on the seriousness of mocking Allah’s signs and the Messenger.
- Qur’an 9:119 on remaining with the truthful.
- Qur’an 63:1–4 on public testimony that conceals inward rejection.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the two-faced person who approaches different groups with different faces.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:15

> Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.

**Commentary:**

### Divine Requital, Not Human Mockery

The verse says that Allah “will throw their mockery back at them.” The Arabic expression *Allāhu yastahziʾu bihim* must be understood in a way that befits Allah’s perfection. Allah does not engage in frivolous or ignorant joking. In Qur’anic usage, the statement means that He requites their mockery, exposes its emptiness, and allows the consequences of their contempt to return upon them. Their attempt to make faith appear ridiculous becomes evidence of their own moral degradation.

The Qur’an uses similar language elsewhere: “The hypocrites mock the believers, but Allah mocks them” (9:79), and “Those who committed crimes used to laugh at those who believed... but today those who believed are laughing at the disbelievers” (83:29–36). In each case, the apparent advantage of the mockers is temporary. Allah’s judgment reverses the outward appearance and reveals who was truly foolish. Classical exegetes commonly explained the divine “mockery” as recompense in kind—justice that corresponds to the act—rather than as an attribute resembling human amusement or ridicule.

This manner of expression is part of the Qur’an’s rhetorical precision. A person who uses deception is answered by exposure, a person who plots evil is caught in his own plot, and a person who mocks truth discovers that the object of his mockery is not diminished. “The evil plot only encompasses those who make it” (35:43). Allah’s response does not make evil good; it demonstrates that no evil strategy can ultimately escape the moral order established by Him.

### Left to Wander in Rebellion

The second part of the verse describes Allah leaving them to “continue wandering blindly in their defiance.” The Arabic *yamudduhum* conveys extension or prolongation, while *ṭugyān* means rebellious excess, crossing the limits, and acting as though one is answerable to no higher authority. *Yaʿmahūn* depicts a person wandering without clear direction. The picture is not of a sincere traveller temporarily lost; it is of someone who has chosen defiance and is then left to wander within the consequences of that choice.

The Qur’an frequently describes guidance as light and misguidance as darkness (14:1; 57:28). The hypocrites possess enough outward knowledge to navigate social situations, but their moral compass is damaged. They can calculate how to win approval, conceal allegiance, or exploit a dispute, yet they cannot recognize the destination toward which these choices are taking them. Their apparent cleverness becomes a form of blindness.

Allah’s allowing a person to continue is itself a severe trial. Immediate punishment might force a person to stop, but the extension of time can create the illusion that he has succeeded. The Qur’an warns that the respite granted to wrongdoers is not proof of approval: “We only delay them for a Day when eyes will stare in horror” (14:42). The time given to repent is mercy; the time used to deepen rebellion becomes evidence against the one who uses it.

### The Believer’s Response to Mockery

Believers will inevitably encounter ridicule. The Qur’an does not instruct them to answer every insult with anger or to imitate the style of the mocker. It commands patience, dignified withdrawal from gatherings where Allah’s signs are mocked, and wise speech. “When you hear Allah’s revelations denied and ridiculed, do not sit with them until they engage in another conversation” (4:140). Elsewhere it says to respond to ignorant provocation with words of peace (25:63).

This does not mean tolerating harm or abandoning justice. A community may protect its members and hold people accountable through lawful means. But personal retaliation, vindictive mockery, and uncontrolled rage often reproduce the very moral disorder that the believer is meant to resist. Confidence in Allah’s judgment allows the believer to defend truth without becoming captive to the emotional economy of the mockers.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 9:79, 35:43, and 83:29–36 on mockery, requital, and the reversal of apparent advantage.
- Qur’an 4:140 on withdrawing from deliberate ridicule of Allah’s revelations.
- Qur’an 14:1 and 57:28 on revelation as light and disbelief as darkness.
- Qur’an 14:42 and 16:61 on respite not being proof that wrongdoing is approved.
- Qur’an 25:63 and 41:34 on responding to provocation with dignified goodness.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on divine “mockery” as just recompense and exposure, in a manner befitting Allah.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:16

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Commentary:**

### A Deliberate Exchange

The Qur’an portrays the hypocrites’ choice as a commercial transaction: they “trade guidance for misguidance.” The verb *ishtaraw* means to purchase or exchange. The image emphasizes that their condition is not an accidental misfortune. They have preferred one thing over another. Guidance was available through the Qur’an, the Prophet (ﷺ), and the visible example of sincere believers, but they exchanged it for the temporary advantages of hypocrisy—security, influence, status, access, and the approval of people who shared their hostility to truth.

The metaphor of trade is repeated throughout the Qur’an because human beings understand profit and loss. “Indeed, Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their wealth in exchange for Paradise” (9:111). That is the successful transaction: a temporary trust is surrendered to Allah and returned as an everlasting reward. The hypocrites reverse it. They risk the Hereafter to preserve a short-lived worldly position. Their transaction may look profitable while they are negotiating it, but its final account is ruin.

### Misguidance Is Not a Neutral Commodity

The verse does not present guidance and misguidance as two equally valid consumer choices. Guidance is alignment with reality: knowing Allah, worshipping Him, living by truth, and preparing for judgment. Misguidance is estrangement from that reality, even if it is accompanied by intelligence, sophistication, or worldly success. The Qur’an warns, “Those are the ones who have purchased error in exchange for guidance, so their trade has brought no profit, nor were they guided” (2:175, in closely related wording).

A person may believe he has gained freedom by rejecting divine limits, but freedom without truth can become enslavement to appetite, fear, fashion, or power. He may think he has gained safety by concealing his convictions, but a life built on constant performance becomes a prison. He may think he has gained community by joining a circle of mockery and intrigue, but that community is unstable because it is held together by shared hostility rather than shared truth.

The trade also includes the exchange of moral clarity for group loyalty. A hypocrite may know that an action is wrong yet defend it because acknowledging the wrong would endanger his position. Each such defence purchases a little more social comfort at the price of inner honesty. Over time, the heart becomes unable to distinguish the price paid from the benefit received. That is why the verse ends by saying they are not guided: the choice has not merely resulted in a single error; it has damaged the faculty by which errors are corrected.

### No Profit and No Guidance

The phrase “their trade is profitless”—*fa-mā rabiḥat tijāratuhum*—does not deny that hypocrisy may provide short-term benefits. It denies that those benefits amount to true profit. The Qur’an’s definition of profit includes salvation, forgiveness, moral integrity, and a sound return to Allah. Worldly gain that ends with divine displeasure is a loss, even if people applaud it. Conversely, worldly hardship borne in obedience can be gain if it leads to Allah’s acceptance.

The verse’s final phrase, “they are not rightly guided,” shows that the failed trade has a direction as well as a result. They are not simply without a map; they are moving away from the destination while imagining that their manoeuvring is wisdom. Their social flexibility conceals spiritual directionlessness. The Qur’an contrasts them with those who “hold firmly to Allah”—for them guidance and a straight path are secured (3:101).

### Examining One’s Own Transactions

The commercial image invites every reader to examine what he is selling and buying. What truth do I sacrifice to retain a friendship, job, reputation, political alliance, or personal comfort? What command do I describe as outdated because obedience would cost me? What religious principle do I publicly affirm but privately exchange for praise? The questions are not meant to produce despair; they identify the places where repentance must begin.

The profitable trade is to surrender the ego, not the truth; to spend wealth for Allah, not to spend faith for wealth; and to prefer lasting guidance to immediate applause. “Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We increase for him his harvest; and whoever desires the harvest of this world, We give him some of it, but he will have no share in the Hereafter” (42:20). The believer’s task is to keep the final market in view.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:175 and 3:177 on trading guidance or faith for error and punishment.
- Qur’an 9:111 on the successful exchange of life and wealth for Allah’s pleasure and Paradise.
- Qur’an 14:3 and 42:20 on preferring the temporary harvest of this world to the lasting harvest of the Hereafter.
- Qur’an 3:101 and 47:17 on holding firmly to Allah and receiving increase in guidance.
- Qur’an 103:1–3 on the universal nature of loss and the qualities that preserve a person from it.
- For the commercial metaphor and the hypocrites’ failed exchange, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Saʿdī on 2:16.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:17

> Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see.

**Commentary:**

### The First Parable of Hypocrisy

The Qur’an now presents a parable. Parables translate an inward reality into a scene that the mind can see and remember. Allah says that He sets forth examples for people so that they may reflect (14:25; 29:43). The example begins with a person kindling a fire. The fire gives light, warmth, and the ability to see one’s surroundings. Then, precisely when the flame has illuminated what is around him, Allah takes away his light, leaving him in darkness.

Classical exegetes explain the fire as the initial outward benefit the hypocrites receive from associating themselves with Islam. Their public declaration places them among the believers, gives them a measure of protection, and allows them to participate in the life of the community. The light can also represent the momentary exposure to revelation: they hear the Qur’an, recognize some of its force, and move for a time in the atmosphere of its guidance. But because their hearts do not submit, the benefit does not become stable illumination. What looked like religious belonging is revealed to be a borrowed appearance.

The parable is carefully worded. The Qur’an says Allah takes away their *light*—*nūrihim*—not necessarily the fire itself. A fire may continue to burn while no longer providing useful illumination. This distinction suggests a person who retains speech, activity, intelligence, and outward religious forms but loses the inner light by which truth is recognized and followed. He may remain socially active while spiritually blind. The outward shell survives, but the guidance that should have directed it is gone.

### Light in the Qur’anic Imagination

Light is a central Qur’anic image for revelation, faith, understanding, and divine direction. Allah brings people “out of darknesses into the light” (14:1), and the Qur’an describes the believer whose heart Allah has opened to Islam as walking upon a light from his Lord (39:22). The opposite is not simple ignorance. Darkness in the plural often indicates layers of confusion, desire, fear, and false allegiance. The hypocrite is surrounded by signs but unable to use them as a path.

The person in the parable is not described as having never seen light. He has seen it and benefited from it briefly. That makes his condition more serious than the condition of someone who has not received a clear message. He has experienced enough guidance to know what it demands, but he wants its social or emotional advantages without surrendering his inner loyalties. The light is taken away as a just consequence of treating guidance as an instrument rather than a truth.

The parable may also describe the unstable experience of a hypocritical community. When faith is used as a public costume, it can create the appearance of unity, but a crisis exposes the absence of shared conviction. Sūrah al-Ḥadīd describes a future scene in which hypocrites ask believers to wait for them so that they may borrow their light; a barrier is placed between them, and the hypocrites are left to seek a light they rejected in the world (57:13). The imagery in both passages teaches that borrowed faith cannot illuminate the soul.

### Temporary Illumination and Sudden Darkness

There is a psychological realism in the scene. A person who walks from a bright place into darkness may be more disoriented than someone who has always been in darkness. The hypocrites have learned religious language and witnessed the moral power of the believers, but their inner allegiance remains divided. When revelation demands sacrifice, they retreat; when worldly advantage appears, they advance. Their intermittent exposure to light does not mature into certainty because they will not remain with the guidance long enough to be transformed by it.

The verse also comforts believers who see falsehood temporarily prosper. The hypocrite may appear enlightened, informed, and socially secure, while the sincere believer suffers. The parable asks the reader to distinguish visible access from actual light. True light is not measured by confidence, popularity, or public display. It is measured by whether a person can see the right path when desire, fear, and pressure surround him.

The remedy is to ask Allah for enduring light and to protect it through obedience. The Prophet (ﷺ) used to ask Allah to place light in his heart, hearing, sight, and before and behind him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the reported supplications for prayer). Light is preserved by truthfulness, prayer, remembrance, learning, and repentance; it is weakened by duplicity and sustained disobedience.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 14:1, 24:35, and 39:22 on revelation, faith, and understanding as light.
- Qur’an 57:13 on the hypocrites seeking to borrow the believers’ light on the Day of Judgment.
- Qur’an 6:122 on the one who was dead and given light by which he walks among people.
- Qur’an 14:25 and 29:43 on parables as means of reflection and understanding.
- Qur’an 66:8 on the believers’ light and their prayer for it to be perfected.
- For the principal interpretations of the fire and light in this parable, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:17.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:18

> They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**Commentary:**

### Moral Deafness, Not Physical Disability

The verse describes the hypocrites as deaf, dumb, and blind. These are spiritual metaphors, not a condemnation of people who live with sensory or speech disabilities. The Qur’an honours human beings and does not make physical impairment a measure of a person’s worth. Its criticism is directed at those who possess functioning senses but refuse to use them in the service of truth. They hear revelation but do not accept its instruction, see signs but do not draw a lesson, and remain silent or speak falsely when truthful testimony is required.

The Qur’an makes this distinction explicit: “They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear” (7:179). It also says, “It is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts within the breasts that are blind” (22:46). The heart is the faculty that turns perception into recognition and recognition into moral response. Without that inward act, information accumulates without guidance.

### Silence and Speech

Calling them “dumb” includes their failure to speak truthfully. The hypocrites are not necessarily physically mute; they are unwilling to use speech as a trust. They can speak when a lie protects them and remain silent when truth would cost them. The Qur’an commands believers to speak words that are upright (33:70) and warns that concealing testimony is a sin of the heart (2:283). Truthful speech is not an ornament of faith; it is one of the ways faith becomes public and protects other people from harm.

The tongue can also be used to create the appearance of piety while hiding hostility. The hypocrites’ profession of belief in verse 8 shows that words alone cannot establish faith. Speech becomes sound when it corresponds to conviction and conduct. This does not require a believer to announce every private thought or expose every weakness. It requires that what he deliberately presents as his belief not be a mask for its opposite.

### “They Will Not Return”

The statement that they will not return—*lā yarjiʿūn*—describes the direction of their chosen path. They have not merely stumbled and become temporarily confused. They have rejected the means of return: listening to revelation, acknowledging their condition, and responding with repentance. As long as a person turns back, the door remains open. But the hypocrites’ persistent refusal makes them like travellers who repeatedly turn away from the road and eventually lose the ability to recognize it.

This must again be read with the Qur’an’s open invitation to repentance. Allah says, “Except those who repent, reform, and hold fast to Allah and make their religion sincere for Allah; those will be with the believers” (4:146). The same passage that threatens hypocrites identifies the way out. The finality in verse 18 concerns those who persist in their hypocrisy until judgment, not a living person who sincerely abandons it.

### The Danger of Becoming Selectively Receptive

There is a lesson here for every believer. One may be attentive to revelation when it confirms personal preferences and deaf when it corrects them; eloquent about another person’s faults and silent about one’s own; quick to see social injustice but blind to injustice within one’s family or group. Selective receptivity is a small beginning of the same moral pattern. The remedy is to ask whether one is willing to obey even when the command is inconvenient.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that the believer is like a mirror to his fellow believer, receiving sincere counsel and helping correct defects rather than flattering them. A heart remains alive when it accepts admonition. “Remind, for the reminder benefits the believers” (51:55). The hypocrite’s tragedy is not that he never encounters reminders, but that he repeatedly encounters them without allowing them to reach his will.

- Qur’an 7:179 and 22:46 on the metaphorical blindness and deafness of the heart.
- Qur’an 2:283 and 33:70 on truthful speech and the sin of concealing testimony.
- Qur’an 4:146 on repentance, reform, and sincerity as the way out of hypocrisy.
- Qur’an 16:108 on hearts, hearing, and sight being sealed after deliberate rejection.
- Qur’an 51:55 on the benefit of sincere reminder to believers.
- For the relation of this verse to the first fire parable, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 2:17–18.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:19

> Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺.

**Commentary:**

### The Second Parable

The Qur’an now presents another image for hypocrisy. The first parable showed a fire whose light was taken away; this one shows people trapped in a violent rainstorm, surrounded by darkness, thunder, and lightning. The second picture is more complicated and more dynamic. Rain can be a blessing because it gives life, yet a storm can also be frightening and disorienting. In the same way, revelation is mercy and life for the receptive heart, but it becomes a source of fear for the person who wants its benefits without its demands.

The Arabic *ṣayyib* commonly denotes heavy, pouring rain. Classical exegetes related the rain to the Qur’an because revelation descends from above and brings life to dead hearts just as rain revives dead land. The darkness represents doubt, confusion, and the overlapping pressures of falsehood; thunder represents the force of warnings and commands; lightning represents the flashes of truth that briefly illuminate the way. These correspondences are interpretive explanations of the parable, not claims that each weather phenomenon has only one fixed symbolic meaning.

The imagery of rain as life-giving revelation is found elsewhere in the Qur’an. “He sends down rain from the sky and the valleys flow according to their measure” (13:17), and the Qur’an gives the example of water as a means through which Allah brings life to the earth. Revelation likewise separates truth from falsehood and revives hearts that respond. But the same rain may expose a traveller’s weakness if he has no shelter. The storm is not defective; the traveller is unprepared.

### Fear of the Thunder

The hypocrites press their fingers into their ears whenever thunder strikes, fearing death. The exaggerated image communicates an attempt to shut out the very warnings that could save them. They do not merely fail to hear; they actively protect themselves from hearing. This is the condition of someone who avoids sermons, correction, evidence, or serious reflection because these things disturb a comfortable falsehood.

The fear of death is especially fitting. Revelation repeatedly reminds people that they will return to Allah, and this reminder is terrifying to the person who has built his life on concealment. The hypocrites want to benefit from the believers’ social world but do not want to stand before the Judge to whom that world belongs. Their terror is not produced by revelation’s injustice; it is produced by the exposure of their own condition.

A person cannot permanently silence the signs. He may avoid a recitation, ridicule a warning, or surround himself with people who confirm his desires, but the realities of death, dependence, and accountability remain. The Qur’an says that the rejecters would put their fingers in their ears and cover themselves with their garments when they were called to forgiveness (71:7). The physical image describes a spiritual manoeuvre: refusing to allow truth even the opportunity to make a claim.

### Allah Encompasses the Disbelievers

The verse closes by stating that Allah encompasses the disbelievers. They may imagine that the storm gives them a hiding place, but no darkness conceals them from Allah. His encompassing includes knowledge, power, and the certainty that their final judgment is within His control. The statement is both a warning and a correction to their illusion of escape. They are not outside the created order, nor can they negotiate with the One who governs the storm, life, and death.

The phrase does not mean that every person who experiences fear, doubt, or emotional distress is a disbeliever. The storm is a parable of a deliberate refusal to let revelation guide. A believer may fear a warning and still benefit from it; in fact, wholesome fear can be a sign of faith when it brings repentance. The problem is not feeling alarm. It is placing one’s fingers in one’s ears so that alarm cannot lead to reform.

### Revelation as Both Mercy and Test

The Qur’an is described as a mercy, healing, and guidance, but it also tests the hearer. The same passage may comfort one person and disturb another. To the humble, the thunder of warning awakens vigilance; to the hypocrite, it threatens the carefully maintained image. The difference lies in the response. The believer says, “We hear and obey” (2:285); the hypocrite seeks a way to hear only what will preserve his independence.

A healthy response to the storm is neither panic nor denial. It is to seek shelter in Allah, listen to the warning, and follow the flashes of guidance until the path becomes clear. The next āyah will complete the image by showing that the hypocrites walk when the lightning offers temporary light but stop whenever darkness returns. Their problem is not that revelation never illuminates; it is that they will not continue with it.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 13:17 and 24:43 on rain, water, thunder, and the signs of Allah in creation.
- Qur’an 71:7 on people physically and spiritually attempting to shut out the call to forgiveness.
- Qur’an 14:1, 16:65, and 39:22 on revelation as light, life, and healing.
- Qur’an 8:2 and 23:57–61 on wholesome fear of Allah as a quality of sincere believers, distinct from hypocritical evasion.
- Qur’an 2:285 on the believing response: “We hear and obey.”
- For the two principal parables of the hypocrites, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:17–20.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:20

> It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight. Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Commentary:**

### Moving Only When the Light Serves Them

The storm parable continues. The lightning is so intense that it almost snatches away their sight, yet whenever it flashes they walk in its light. When darkness returns, they stand still. The image presents people who accept guidance when it is convenient, illuminating, or socially useful, but stop when obedience requires patience, sacrifice, or trust in what cannot immediately be seen.

The flashes of lightning can be understood as the moments when the truth becomes unmistakable: a clear proof, a compelling verse, a visible success of the believers, or a benefit that the hypocrites wish to share. In those moments, they move with the community. But guidance is not a series of disconnected advantages. It is a continuous path. When the command becomes difficult, when the believer must give up an interest, endure ridicule, or wait for Allah’s promise, the hypocrite stands still. His movement is controlled by immediate conditions rather than by conviction.

This does not mean that every believer who feels spiritually weak is a hypocrite. Faith rises and falls, and the believer may struggle while sincerely wanting obedience. The decisive contrast is between weakness that is resisted through repentance and a calculated willingness to follow truth only when it serves one’s interests. The hypocrites do not merely stumble in the darkness; they make their allegiance conditional upon the presence of a convenient flash.

### The Need for Steadfastness

The Qur’an repeatedly joins guidance with steadfastness. “Indeed, those who say, ‘Our Lord is Allah’ and then remain steadfast, the angels descend upon them” (41:30). Steadfastness—*istiqāmah*—means maintaining the direction of obedience through changing circumstances. A person does not need to see the entire road at once. He needs enough light to take the next faithful step and the trust to continue when the next section is not yet visible.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that the most beloved deeds to Allah are those that are consistent, even if they are small (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Consistency protects the believer from living by spiritual impulses alone. A small regular prayer, a fixed practice of charity, daily Qur’an recitation, truthful speech, and steady service can preserve light more effectively than occasional displays followed by long periods of darkness. The hypocrite wants the flash without the discipline of walking.

The verse also clarifies why divine commands are sometimes experienced as a test. If every consequence were immediately visible, people could move by calculation rather than trust. The unseen requires the heart to follow Allah because He is worthy of obedience, not merely because the next step is already rewarding. The believer’s certainty in the Hereafter, mentioned at the beginning of the sūrah, gives him the ability to continue through temporary darkness.

### Allah’s Power over the Faculties

The warning is direct: had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. The faculties they misuse are gifts from Him. If He can give hearing and sight, He can remove them physically; if He can allow a person to perceive revelation, He can also leave him in the spiritual darkness he has chosen. The closing statement, “Allah is Most Capable of everything,” places the entire parable under divine power and reminds the hearer that no one is independent of the Creator.

This is not a statement that every disability is a punishment or that physical loss of hearing or sight indicates disbelief. The Qur’an honours believers with disabilities and measures people by faith and righteousness, not bodily condition. The point is that the hypocrites’ apparent control is an illusion. They cannot guarantee that their opportunity, intelligence, health, reputation, or access to guidance will remain. A person should respond to these gifts with gratitude and obedience before they become evidence against him.

### The Two Parables Together

The fire parable showed the loss of light after an apparent illumination. The storm parable shows intermittent movement—advance during a flash and paralysis in darkness. Together they describe the instability of hypocrisy. It borrows the language and benefits of faith without accepting its discipline; it desires safety without surrender, insight without accountability, and community without loyalty to truth.

The sincere believer is not promised that every moment will feel bright. He is promised a Lord who guides, a revelation that illuminates, prayer that renews, and a final gathering in which all temporary darkness will end. The proper prayer is the one taught by the Qur’an: “Our Lord, perfect our light for us and forgive us; indeed, You are over all things competent” (66:8). The opening twenty āyāt of Sūrah al-Baqarah thus close by returning the reader to the central choice: to walk consistently under Allah’s light or to remain dependent on flashes that disappear when obedience becomes difficult.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 14:1, 24:35, 39:22, and 57:28 on divine light and guidance.
- Qur’an 41:30 and 46:13 on steadfastness after affirming Allah as Lord.
- Qur’an 66:8 on the believers’ prayer for their light to be perfected.
- Qur’an 3:101, 47:17, and 64:16 on holding firmly to Allah, increasing in guidance, and obeying according to ability.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on consistent deeds, even when small.
- Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on the lightning as temporary illumination and the darkness as the hypocrites’ hesitation and confusion.
