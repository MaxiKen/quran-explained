# Sūrah an-Nabaʾ (Chapter 78) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:1

> What are they asking one another about?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah That Opens by Eavesdropping**

The new sūrah begins not with oaths this time but with an overheard street-scene: *ʿamma yatasāʾalūn* — about what are they asking one another? The verb is in the reciprocal form — *tatasāʾalūn*, they question each other mutually — and it is priceless as a genre-marker. After the previous sūrah's solemn winds, here the discourse begins downstairs, in the Meccan alleys, where the deniers are already talking. God opens His address with a record of *their* address.

Something is being conceded by this structure, and cleverly: the news has achieved what all news wants. It is being discussed. The mission's success is silently indexed in the very first sentence — people do not trade back and forth over what leaves them indifferent. Before the sūrah has said one word in defense of its message, it has documented the message's grip on its opponents.

**Who the Questioners Are, and in What Register**

The *they* is the Meccan opposition — the Qurayshite gatherings who had now heard, repeatedly, Muhammad reciting warnings of a Day that would sort mankind. Their mutual questioning, the commentators note, was of two registers: some mocking — asking each other mischievously about the *new* resurrection tale, as one trades over a curiosity — and some genuinely unsettled, probing what they had heard with the anxiety of people who suspect it might be true. The verb catches both registers without deciding between them.

Notably absent from the scene is the Prophet. He is not the conversant; he is the conversation's object at one remove, filtered through their reports. The revelation does not step down into the street to engage them directly — it overhears, and then it answers *over their heads*. The dignity of the truth is staged in this distance: God will not be cross-examined by the people; He will exhibit, and let the knowing arrive on schedule.

**A Question That Confesses an Itch**

Look closer at what their mutual questioning already concedes: the report has reached the center of their attention. A community that had gods, trade, poetry and pedigree now spends its assemblies on *this* — the resurrection claim. The sūrah's first verse thereby gives a strange comfort to the bearer of any unwelcome message: that the mockery of the audience, the continual asking *about it*, is itself the symptom of penetration. Indifference would have been the burial.

The reciprocal grammar also suggests rehearsal: they were not asking *for* information so much as asking *at* each other — trading the report in tones that reassured the group. Doubt manufactured socially is weaker than doubt reasoned, but it is far more durable, because it is maintained by belonging rather than by argument. The sūrah will not try to out-argue this social production; it will simply predicted and then display its Day.

**Why God Begins With Their Question Rather Than His Answer**

Beginning here is a strange honour to the opponents' discourse. The Qur'an throughout treats its challengers as people whose talk is worth recording — not because it is profound but because the record of human response belongs, permanently, in scripture. Their street-chatter is canonized, fossilized mid-ridicule, and that fossil ends up preaching the longest sermon of the sūrah: here is what denial sounded like in its native dialect, in the very generation to which the warning was freshest.

The device also builds immediate interest for the answering believer. What question could be worth opening a sūrah upon? The next verse names the object — the momentous report itself. A reader drawn from the street into the scripture is thereby taught the first lesson of the chapter: the world's talking-points and God's talking-points concern the same thing, now and at the end. The index of their evening assembly is the index of the Day.

**The Question That Will Be Answered to Them**

There is a judicial irony ready in the wings: people who ask *what are they asking about* are going to be made to know what they were asking about. The doubleness of asking and knowing governs the sūrah's first movement — they inquire in the optative mood, and God answers in the predictive mood: *they will come to know*. The question they pose in jest becomes, at the Day, the question answered in full — with the questioners standing inside their own answer.

The same widening happens between their time-scale and God's. To the Meccan club, the news was evening entertainment, to be asked about until newer gossip displaced it. The sūrah arrests that natural decay of attention with one verb of future print: whatever else happens to this report, it has a day of resolution, and the walk from the evening gathering to that Day is short enough to be called soon. Asking-time is short; knowing-time is fixed.

**The Door of the Sūrah Stands Open**

Structurally, 78:1–3 forms one complete phrase — the question, its object, the division over it — before the gavel of *kallā* drops in verse four. Those three opening verses proportion a listener for what follows: first the world's exhibits (78:6-16), then the world's terminus (78:17-20), then the day in both its doors (78:21-36), then the majesty that stamps the end of speech (78:37-38), then the name of that Day and a standing invitation (78:39), and finally the warning's return to the present tense (78:40).

The sūrah of the Momentous News has, in one eleven-syllable sentence, revealed its entire economy: a question already circulating, already splitting the audience, already pressing toward its own resolution. All that remains is for the revelation to walk the same ground again — but this time on oath-grade footing, in the indicative voice, with the evidence parade of the created world moving past the stand. The street asked; the sky will now answer.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:2

> About the momentous news,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Object of All the Asking**

Answer now supplied: *ʿani n-nabaʾi l-ʿaẓīm* — about the momentous news. After the street-scene of mutual questioning, the sūrah names what the talk had as its object. Two things are settled by the naming. First, the matter was real enough to hold a city's cycles of conversation. Second, its weight is registered in advance by God Himself: the thing being handled with levity downstairs carries the attribute *ʿaẓīm* — vast, momentous — upstairs.

Two principal identifications stand in the commentary tradition: the *Resurrection* — the majority view, confirmed by the sūrah's own unfolding, since everything after verse 17 reports the Day in its furniture — and the *Qur'an*, the very recitation that was carrying the news into Mecca. The two are not rivals: the revelation's content at the moment was the resurrection-news; asking about one was asking about the other. Behind both identifications stands one reality — the return of all creatures to their Maker, delivered first as speech and later as event.

**Nabaʾ Is Not Khabar: The Arabic of Consequence**

The word-selection is a lesson in itself. Arabic reports can ride on *khabar* — any information, true or idle — or on *nabaʾ*: news of real significance, news that changes the situation of its hearer. The Book chooses *nabaʾ*, and then intensifies it with *al-ʿaẓīm*. The report of the resurrection is thus classified, by divine linguistic act, as the heaviest class of report there is: news upon which outcomes stand.

That classification indicts the handling, not the report. The Meccan assemblies treated the nabaʾ as khabar — banter-grade material, traded and discarded. The sūrah's correction lies entirely in the adjective's placement: no new evidence is introduced; the *same* news is re-labeled at its true weight. Much of revelation is exactly such re-weighing of what everyone already has in hand: the words are old; the accent is new.

**The Qur'an Calls Itself by the Name**

The second reading — the news as the Qur'an — enjoys a startling proof: in Sūrah Ṣād, the Prophet is commanded to say, in the first person: *“Say, ‘This ˹Quran˺ is momentous news, from which you ˹pagans˺ are turning away’”* (38:67-68). There the identical doublet *nabaʾun ʿaẓīm* is worn by the recitation itself, and its possessors are described by their response — turning away. The two passages together establish both readings as one doctrine whenever the term appears: the Book and the Day are the one momentous news at two points of its itinerary.

Which is fitting, since the Qur'an's own account of itself is that its content is the Day. To ask dismissively about a Revelation whose theme is the resurrection is to ask dismissively about the resurrection; to turn away from the Day's warning is to turn away from the new-arriving Book. The identity protects the Prophet, too: their quarrel is not really with a man from Mecca but with the largest news in heaven and earth, which the man merely delivers.

**Too Big to Be True — the Skeptic's Arithmetic**

Behind the mocking register stands a recognizable mental calculation: the claim was so large that it sounded self-refuting. Dead generations, decomposed bones, whole epochs of the buried — all summoned back, all assembled, all judged? The magnitude that marks the news as momentous *for* God functioned, in the skeptics' quick arithmetic, as a case *against* it: surely nothing that big could be on the schedule.

The Qur'an identifies this exact move everywhere — *“Who will revive ˹these˺ bones when they have decayed?”* (36:78) is the objection quoted with its own author silenced by His own origin-scene: the One who first composed man from nothing has no difficulty with composition from dust. The sermon of 78:6-16 will run the same correction at greater length: the evidence of divine capability is precisely what the doubters are living on, sleeping under, and standing on all along.

**A Report That Keeps Its Name in Both Theatres**

Track the word through the Book's two theatres. In the world, *nabaʾ* = the preached report, mocked at evening assemblies. On the Day, the same content arrives *in person* — and the sūrah at its far end will name that arrival *the Day of ultimate truth* (78:39), where what was news becomes what is seen. News and event keep one identity across the theatres; the bridge between them is nothing but time, and time is exactly what the sūrah says is appointed and short.

This is why the report's momentous quality outlasts every conversation about it. Whether the Meccans talked solemnly, joked, or dropped the subject, nothing about the news depended on its reception. Its name in heaven was fixed: the greatest tiding between creation and return. The sūrah, by printing the name here at verse two, warns the talkers what category of object they are tossing in jest.

**Measured by the Response It Demands**

Finally, the weight of the news is a scale for the response it asks. A khabar of rain deserves an umbrella; a nabaʾ of the resurrection deserves examined life, repentance, the rearranged table of one's priorities. Greatness in the object makes smallness in the answer a kind of theft — withholding from the truth a response proportioned to its announced mass.

And there is the sūrah preparing its pivot: immediately after naming the object's great weight, the text will note the variety of answers the object received (78:3). Disagreement over a mountain does not shrink the mountain; disagreement over the momentous news measures only the disagreeing parties. The mountain stands; the news stands; the next verse watches the human response scatter into camps — the permanent sociology that meets every divine address before the Day resolves it.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:3

> over which they disagree.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Whole Future Compressed in Five Words**

The street-scene completes itself: *alladhī hum fīhi mukhtalifūn* — about which they are in disagreement. With that clause the sūrah prints Mecca's spiritual weather for posterity: not rejection alone, not acceptance alone, but *ikhtilāf* — a scatter of positions, arguments, moods and camps. The city was divided over the news; the revelation's first portrait of its audience is a people at variance.

The verb *mukhtalifūn* carries more juice than simple difference. It is in the intensive participle of difference-over-time: they are *occupied in* differing, habitually at variance, maintaining their plurality as an institution. The disagreement about the news had itself become a social fact of Meccan life, an argument kept warm by repetition, like the songs of the gatherings. When scripture takes notice of the sociology, the sociology is thereby told it will be examined.

**The Camps of the Ikhtilāf, Drawn From the Record**

Who stood where? The record of the early Meccan period, inside and outside the Qur'an, gives the disagreement its familiar constituencies: the small band of believers who had accepted the news and begun arranging their lives by it; the hardened instigators who traded in mockery of it; and between them the large wavering mass, troubled, half-curious, half-dreading — asking others about it at night and concealing their interest by day.

The sūrah's own sequel will address each camp in turn without addressing anyone: the mockers receive the doubled arrest (78:4-5); the wavering are addressed by the evidence-parade of the world itself (78:6-16); and the believers find their portion in the garden-furniture of the Day (78:31-36). The one verb *mukhtalifūn* thus quietly sets the chapter's entire rhetorical itinerary: a divided assembly is about to be argued with, warned by, and sorted.

**Disagreement Proves Contact, Not Falsehood**

An instinct common to every age runs: if the news were true, truth would command consensus — so disagreement itself discredits the message. The Qur'an reverses the inference at its root: a report that produces such convulsion in a city built on inherited orthodoxy has, by that convulsion, proven its arrival. Dead letters are not argued over. The scale of the ikhtilāf measures the scale of the impact; the noise around the news is the signature of the news's weight.

Besides, the disagreement's pattern told its own sociology: nearly everyone agreed the *messenger* was honest and the words were powerful; the dispute raged precisely over the consequences — over accepting a report that would rearrange the city's gods, markets and hierarchies. The camps divided roughly where interests differed. The ikhtilāf was never information-shaped; it was interest-shaped, and that shape is visible across the whole record of the prophetic missions: not truth but the response-to-truth has always splintered where the truth touched standing arrangements.

**Meanwhile the Object Refuses to Wait on Its Reception**

The deepest lesson of the clause is asymmetry. The disagreeing is all on the human side; on the report's side there is no disagreement — the news announces a fixed event with an appointed time (78:17). The city can send its measure of the matter into as many camps as it pleases; the matter does not multiply to meet them. Truth, in this sūrah's physics, does not divide to accommodate its audience.

Which is why the sūrah can proceed so unbothered. Having registered the ikhtilāf, it never returns to referee between camps, quote party-platforms, or out-argue the disagreement in kind. Instead it reloads the interrogation: *have We not...?* follows *have We not...?* — the evidence parade of bed, pegs, pairs, sleep, night, day, heavens, sun, rain, orchards — as if to demonstrate that the camps are all camped on the same Earth-gift while they quarrel about the Giver's promise. The questioners will be shown what they had in common all along: the furnishings of a temporary lodging.

**A Sundown Clause Hidden in the Clause**

There is also a mercy tucked inside the word. *Mukhtalifūn* is present-tense — *are* disagreeing — which means the disagreement still has time in it. The camps, as described at verse three, are not yet overrun by the event; there is still an interval during which positions can change, trades can be re-examined, and parties can dissolve. The verse preserves the moment when every camp still contained people who could walk to another camp.

That interval is exactly what the next two verses will name without naming: the coming *knowing* that closes the market. The sūrah gives the disagreeing city advance notice that its divisions have an expiry printed on them — and here lies the verse's lasting orientation for every divided religious culture since: camps and arguments are all riding inside a window, above which stands one event that will count them, weigh their jerry-built certainties, and remand each camp to the furniture of its chosen door.

**Disagreement as the Sūrah's Unifying Subject**

It is worth one final look at how tightly the sūrah holds its thread. The dispute opens the chapter at verse one; the evidence of One Maker in verses six through sixteen answers it with the unity of design; the Day's appointment in verse seventeen overrides it with the unity of the ending; the two doors of verses twenty-one and thirty-one permanently un-do it; and the permission-to-speak clause at verse thirty-eight dissolves it altogether, because on that Day only licensed speech remains.

The ikhtilāf, then, is the sūrah's wound which the whole chapter dresses. It is a magnificent opening diagnosis for a sūrah named the News: the first thing said about the news in the world is that people divide over it; the last thing said is that the warned-of punishment is near — and the distance between those two statements is the entire curriculum the disagreeing parties had left to study. Verse four will now state the expiry of the debate in the sharpest grammatical form the Arabic possesses.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:4

> But no! They will come to know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā: The Gavel Falls on the Conversation**

The arrest-word arrives: *kallā* — by no means, no indeed, enough of that. In Arabic usage *kallā* is the particle of desistance: it halts a line of talk or a state of mind and orders it to stop. God does not here refute an argument; He dismisses a posture. The ikhtilāf of verse three, the entire climate of traded scepticism, is called to order by a single two-consonant gavel-stroke, the way a presiding voice ends disorder in an assembly before sentencing.

The commentators note *kallā*'s range: it issues a rebuke (a *stop that*) against the mockery, simultaneously warning the mocked-of news's opponents that their register of handling is over. What follows the arrest is not a counter-argument but a sentence about the future: *sayaʿlamūn* — they will come to know. Discussion time is hereby closed; knowing time is officially on the calendar.

**Sayaʿlamūn: The Verb of Deferred Certainty**

*They will know* — future tense, bare, without a stated object. The eloquence of the ellipsis is famous in this sūrah's clan: the knowing's object is so obvious that stating it would weaken it. Know *what*? The truth of the news; the weight of what was mocked; the furniture of the Day; the identity of the Speaker. All of it, at once, at the appointed time — a knowing so comprehensive that the syntax leaves the blank for the imagination to fill.

The future-tense choice is itself the substance of the warning. Had the sentence been *they are wrong*, it would have been merely another move inside the ikhtilāf — one more position among the camps. By sentencing them to a future *knowing*, God steps out of the debate entirely and speaks from beyond it. The question is not whether their position will be refuted but when their certainty will be installed. Debate has a horizon, and this verse posts its date in the grammar.

**Why the Arrest Is Not Followed by Argument**

Notice the pattern of the divine response: *kallā* — and then, no refutation. For modern ears trained on the debate-club model of truth, this seems abrupt. The method is precise, though: where genuine error of information holds a person, the Qur'an elsewhere argues at scholarly length; where the disagreement is a posture kept for standing and comfort, argument only provides the posture new furniture. The arrest-word classifies Meccan disagreement as the second kind.

The refusal to argue is, in its way, the harshest sentence of the passage: the street's objections were not even worth retaining as objections. Nothing of the evening assembly's cleverness is preserved against them except the bare fact that they disagreed (78:3) — the content of the mockery is not even transcribed. The sūrah responds the way eternity responds to a season's chatter: not with a debating-society win, but with a calendar entry. *They will come to know.*

**The Knowing Will Come to Everyone**

It is important that *sayaʿlamūn* runs wider than the mockers. Every camp in the ikhtilāf — believer, waverer, instigator — is carried by the plural. For the believers, the future-knowing is joy-long-expected; for the waverers, a resolution; for the instigators, the dread of certainty arriving. The same verb serves three destinations because knowledge is one, and the Day's economy consists precisely in the collision of every party with the one body of facts.

The Qur'an elsewhere counts the stages of that certainty with surgical terms: certainty by report, certainty by seeing, certainty by being in the event. The sūrah's next movement begins installing the first stage by evidence: *˹But˺ you will surely see the Hellfire* (102:6) illustrates, in a companion sūrah of this family, how the knowledge that arrives as grammar today arrives as spectacle tomorrow. *They will know* is thus the briefest possible form of the whole doctrine of the Day of Decision compressed into two words.

**Deferral as Both Terror and Mercy**

A deferred certainty has two spiritual effects, and the verse trades on both. On the terror side: a future knowing that is *promised by God* is more certain than any present knowing; the condemned may, in the interval, continue their trading and their assemblies, but they do so inside an appointment already entered in the register (78:17). The deferral does not relax the certainty; it suspends only the timing.

On the mercy side — and the sūrah will keep this half in view until its last verse — the interval of not-yet-knowing is precisely the interval of *ippana* repentance. A God who sentenced the disagreeing city to a future knowing, instead of an immediate demonstration, was giving the city daylight in which the mockers could still step into the believing camp before the knowing arrived. *They will come to know* is the most generous possible framing of doom: it leaves the door open exactly up to the one specified hour.

**The Hammer Will Strike Again**

This sentence will toll twice — the sūrah repeats it in full at the very next breath, with the doubling that never occurs idly in this Book. Repetition is the rhetoric of the absolutely settled: what God affirms twice, no conference will reopen. The question at the sūrah's opening — *what are they asking one another about?* — is answered, in two strikes of the same formula, with the only reply the question ever deserved: they will know.

Between the first strike and the second, no new content intervenes — as if the divine voice paused merely for the sentence to echo down the alley of the assemblies where the news was being joked away, then struck again. When the text resumes, the register will have shifted entirely: from street-argument to the quiet cataloging of the world's furnishings — bed, pegs, pairs, sleep — the evidence parade on which the promised knowing will be built. The gavel has fallen twice; the exhibits are wheeled in.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:5

> Again, no! They will come to know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence, Struck Twice**

And then — *thumma kallā sayaʿlamūn*: again, no indeed, they will come to know. The sentence of verse four recurs, unaltered, with *thumma* (moreover/again) prefixed. This is deliberate, authorized duplication, and the Qur'an never spends duplication idly: its reprises are structural stresses, placed where the hearer's attention is to be pinned for an extra beat before the discourse moves on.

The reposition carries a signal. In Arabic oratory as in the Qur'an's, doubling marks settled matters — the point beyond which repetition is needed not to add information but to fix gravity. After the first strike, some hearer might hope the verdict was rhetorical, a flourish; the second strike eliminates that hope. The news's opponents are given the sentence twice so that none of them can later say they heard it once, in the middle of noise, and missed its finality.

**Two Strikes, Two Knowings?**

The commentators have long asked whether the repetition merely intensifies, or whether each *kallā sayaʿlamūn* is bound to its own moment of knowing. On the second reading — attributed broadly in the tradition's early layers — the first strike concerns the knowing that arrives at death, when the mockers meet the *barzakh*-state and the mocked-of report begins wearing personal address; the second concerns the Day of Decision itself, when the knowing becomes public conviction before the assembly.

The doubling by occasion has Qur'anic precedent: the companion-sūrah of Rivalry sounds the same doubled arrest — *“But no! You will soon come to know. Again, no! You will soon come to know”* (102:3-4) — and then maps the stages of certainty onto seeing (102:5-7). The two readings are not competitors: doubling for emphasis and doubling for two appointed knowings reinforce one another, since the doom arrives graded — first privately at death, then cosmically at the resurrection — and each grade deserved its own strike of the gavel.

**What the First Knowing Will Prove**

If the first knowing is at death, its content is the truth of the warner: the moment the veil of the visible world parts, the dying mocker knows, beyond argument, that the recitations he laughed at were divine speech — and knows it one hour too late for amendment. The Book's dread formula for this is everywhere: remembering comes exactly when remembering can no longer change anything: *“but what is the use of remembering then?”* (89:23).

This first knowing also explains a curiosity of Meccan persistence: the instigators watched companions die — some as believers, some as mockers — and nothing visible reported back from the grave. The second verse's doubling says: nothing reported back *to the gatherings*, but everything reported *to the dead*. The grave is the first courtroom, silent and private, and its knowing is already in session beneath the same earth the assemblies were standing on while they joked.

**And What the Second Knowing Will Add**

The second knowing is the cosmic one — the Day the sūrah will call, from verse seventeen onward, the appointed time: when the trumpet is blown and the graves release their account-holders, and the mocked-of news stands assembled in crowds. At that stage the knowing is no longer private conviction but shared spectacle: the heavens opened into gates, the mountains passed off as mirage, and every soul present to what its hands sent ahead (78:40).

The two knowings are one education, delivered in two classrooms. The first cures the tone — the laughter ends at the grave's edge; the second cures the doubt — the event itself stands enrolled and undeniable. The doubled *kallā* thus traces, in four words, the entire itinerary of the punishment's arrival: arrest of the present register, promise of the private curtain-lift at death, arrest again, promise of the public curtain-lift at the Day. Rhetoric could not travel further on fewer syllables.

**The Cadence Is Part of the Content**

Read the sequence aloud and the method becomes physical. Verses one through three move in the discursive, sandaled gait of street conversation — question, object, disagreement — and then two identical hammer-blows land, back to back, where a sentence stands perfectly still and sounds a second time exactly as it sounded the first. The reciter feels the form: the talk is over; the verdict-piece has been struck; nothing of the evening assembly survives but the assembly's knowing-future.

The rhythm matters for the sūrah's whole design. This is a sūrah that will eventually deliver a doubled stress-system of its own — the Fire-block and the Garden-block, the two statutes of reward — and the double gavel at its opening is the count-off of that larger structure: two strikes here, two doors there, two knowings between. God announces doubled certainties at the start of a sūrah that will exhibit the world's certainties in pairs, and sort its audience into two camps at the end.

**After the Gavel, the Exhibits Are Rolled In**

The courtroom metaphor runs the whole sūrah. Charges registered (78:1-3); arrest and sentencing of the register (78:4-5); and now, at verse six, the evidence-parade begins: a long chain of questions — have We not — proceeding through the very floor underfoot. The transition itself is a lesson in the Book's pastoral method: silence the posture, then teach the evidence. Not evidence first, then silence — because posture defines hearability.

Everything after verse five is, in one sense, the content of the promised knowing, spelled out while there is still time to benefit from it. The streets of Mecca disagreed about the news; now the sūrah will show the disagreeing city the world it stands on — constructed like habitation, governed like a household, pegged, paired, covered, commissioned — until the question reverses on the questioners: given *this* architecture, what exactly was implausible about the Builder's promise of an accounting Day? The parade begins at the ground.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:6

> Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Evidence-Parade Begins at Ground Level**

With the gavel struck twice, the sūrah opens its interrogation-exhibits: *a-lam najʿali l-arḍa mihādā* — did We not make the earth a resting-lay, a spread-out bed? The form is *a-lam* plus a past-tense fact: a question whose answer is too obvious to require an answer. God's opening exhibit in the case of the resurrection is not a miracle or a prophecy. It is the floor beneath the sceptics' sandals.

The rhetorical design is load-bearing: from what is incontestably visible and granted, the sūrah will argue to what is invisibly appointed. If the deniers can be brought to say *yes* — and the interrogative chain will corner that yes eleven times through earth, mountains, pairs, sleep, night, day, heavens, sun, rain, crops, orchards — then the Maker established by the agreed exhibits is the same Speaker of the mocked news. Capability gets admitted before the promise is re-examined.

**Mihād: The Word of a Living-Room for a Planet**

The selected noun, *mihād*, is the word for a bed or a cradle — the laying-place prepared for a person, especially the smoothed spot on which an infant is set down. God did not, in this image, merely *create* the earth; He *prepared* it — spread it out with the deliberateness of someone making a bed for an expected guest. The planetary surface is thus described from the vocabulary of hospitality: a readied floor, a cradle-lay for every creature set down upon it.

The image converts the world at once from mass to *furnishing*. The sceptics were disputing the Giver of feast-schedules while walking on the largest single act of housekeeping ever performed. *Mihād* reaches deeper than flatness: it includes suitability — soil that receives seed, ground that bears weight, floors that a body can rest on every night of its lifespan without once thanking the floor. The question is: since when does raw accident make beds?

**Smoothness as the Mercy Nobody Ordered**

The translation's *smoothed out* catches what the first audience would have felt hardest: for people whose world was rock, dune, lava-field and scrub, the fact that Arabia also offered broad walkable valleys, plantable hollows and pasturable flats was not trivial. The earth's habitability is a miracle of moderation — enough rock to stand on, enough soil to plant, enough level to build — a mixture that no one ordered and everyone uses.

The Qur'an returns to this exact exhibit with its purpose named elsewhere: *“˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for ˹all of˺ you, and set in it pathways for you, and sends down rain from the sky”* (20:53). Laid out for you — the purpose-clause is printed there, and assumed here. The mihād is not neutral property; it is *addressed* property, made *for* the creatures who will be questioned about what they did upon it.

**The Floor Where Their Own Bodies Were Founded**

There is a sharper point under the surface, and the sūrah's parallel-argument does not even need to state it aloud: the deniers of the resurrection disputed a God who could not reassemble dust — while standing on the very dust from which their assemblage came: *“From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you”* (20:55). The mihād is simultaneously their origin-material, their lodging, and their grave-in-waiting.

The interrogation thus carries a prosecutor's trap inside the comfort-word: the bed the deniers acknowledge will also be their custody until the Day; the earth that receives them comfortably now is the same one that will hold them for the appointment. Every *yes* the a-lam question extracts is a double yes — yes to the Maker's hospitality, yes to the Maker's custody. The bed is the first exhibit and the last.

**The Question That Preaches at Every Step**

The verse's enduring pedagogy is training in how to read a floor. The faithful tradition ever since has taught its students to find the signs at ground level — the walk between doorstep and market as a lesson in divine laying-out. Habitability is not background; it is the first and most continuous revelation, upon which all spoken revelation builds its questions. Nothing about the mihād needs poetry to be astonishing; it only needs to stop being invisible.

There is also comfort for the believer in this first exhibit's scale. Whatever else the sūrah will say about heaven's gates, dissolving mountains and appointed days, the Speaker of all of it is the same power that laid out a planet like a bed for creatures He had not yet made. A God of that demonstrated capacity is not to be doubted over the lesser acts of waking sleepers or raising graves: the earth underfoot is the standing certificate of everything the sūrah is about to promise.

**From the Floor, Upward to the Stakes**

The evidence-parade climbs next to the world's most visible furniture — the mountains — and with it the interrogation starts to work on the hinge of the whole sūrah's argument: if these mountains, which look permanent, were *made* as pegs holding the bed steady, then their stated unmaking at the Day is no surprise. The parade has barely mounted and already the resurrection's difficulty is dissolving into the world's Maker's ordinary powers.

The bed, then, is exhibit one of a house: floor-lay first, stakes next, then the inhabitants' life-cycles in pairs and sleep. The question the sūrah will finally ask the house's tenants is what they did in such a furnished lodging as guests — because a house this deliberately made implies a Householder. And a Householder implies an accounting. The first yes has been extracted; the chain interrogation now ascends from the floor to the mountains.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:7

> and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Awtād: The Desert's Most Familiar Fastener**

Interrogation-exhibit two: *wal-jibāla awtādā* — and the mountains as pegs. *Watad* is the tent-stake: the short, sharp, deeply-driven pin that holds a dwelling's cords against every wind. The image was intimate to people whose architecture was canvas and guy-ropes — a stake looks small above ground, but everything depends on its buried length and angle. Mountains, God says, are the world's tent-pegs: their visible mass is only the head of the fastening; the anchoring runs below.

The figure instantly re-scales the landscape. What Meccan eyes read as the scenery of permanence — the grey hulks standing around the valley, older than every genealogy — is re-described as *installations*: placed, driven, purposed. Permanence-appearances are just the heads of stakes; the question under every a-lam asks the viewer to think about who drives stakes.

**The Purpose of Pegging Is Stated Elsewhere in Full**

The sūrah names the function by the image, and the Book states it explicitly in companion passages: *“He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you”* (16:15), and again, *“And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way”* (21:31). The pegs are *restraint upon movement*: the earth-gift would pitch and stagger without its anchors, and dwelling would be impossible on a rolling, unsecured crust.

So the stakes are both stability and mercy. The tent of the world holds through storms of geology because its pegging is deep; the same hand that spread the bed steadied it. And the pair of exhibits works as a unit — bed and pegs together complete the single idea of a *dwelling made ready*: floor first, fastening second, occupancy afterward. The order of the furnishings is a builder's order, and the questions accumulate like the rooms of a house.

**The Stakes Are Exhibit A for the Day Their Case Is Won**

But the mountain-image carries a secret verdict the sūrah will pronounce over itself within twenty verses: those pegs, at the appointed Day, are *plucked*. The same chapter that installs the mountains as anchors will say of them, at the resurrection, that they are set moving and pass *like a mirage* (78:20). Making-and-unmaking, in one sūrah, of the world's most permanent-looking furniture: the most silent, massive rebuttal possible of the sceptics' claim that resurrection outstrips divine capability.

The parallel passages then take over the courtroom: *“And ˹if˺ they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the mountains, ˹then˺ say: My Lord will wipe them out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen”* (20:105-107). Asked about resurrection's plausibility, the Book points at the mountains and says: those will be dust first. The pegs are not just mercies; they are dated exhibits — temporary stakes in a temporary tent of the world, whose withdrawal-time is already set by the Peg-driver.

**Reading Mass as Message**

The spiritual training of the verse is to see through *appearances of mass*. The sceptic's instinct equates solidity with self-subsistence: what is that huge cannot depend on anything; what is that settled cannot be moved by any Speaker's promise. The word *awtād* executes a demolition of the instinct in one image: the hugest things in the landscape are precisely the *inserted* things — held in place the way a pin is held, by the will that pinned them.

The faithful tradition has mined this for endurance: when the world's fixtures look unshakable — when tyrannies, fortunes and certainties appear mountain-grade — the believer remembers what the mountains really are in God's bookkeeping: pegs, on loan, awaiting the Day of being rendered a mirage. Nothing visible is load-bearing except as it is held. The view of the range from the valley is a picture of how the universe is kept, and by whom.

**The Pegs and the Pathways of Life**

The Book's extended treatment adds a homely sequel to the mountain-exhibit: *“and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way”* (21:31) — the pegged landscape is also passable, valleyed, travelled. The stakes stabilize, and the passes between them connect; stability and mobility are the two conditions of human habitation, and the Book attributes both, clause by clause, to the mountain-furniture. Even the routes of commerce — the very caravans of the Quraysh — ran in the passes of God's driven stakes.

That detail quietly repositions the audience's own biography: the traders being addressed with the resurrection-news spent their working lives *inside* the mountain-exhibit, filing between the pegs on the way to Syria and Yemen. Their *ijārah* and profit were earned in the hallways of a building they denied was a building. The evidence-parade does not tour a museum of the hidden; it is drawn entirely from the roads the audience drove every season.

**From the Stakes to the Sleepers**

The interrogation now turns from the world's outer furniture to its inhabitants' inner cycle — pairing and sleep, the mercies closest to the skin. The design of the parade is visible: after the house's floor and frame, the household's daily rhythm, each item as granted and unearned as the one before. The sequence is asking, at every step: what landlord ever furnished a tenancy at this standard without planning an accounting?

The pegs stand, then, as exhibit two in a graduated lesson — from the macro-mercy that anchors continents, to the micro-mercy that stills nerves at midnight. Each yes extracted strengthens the chain, and the chain is being forged for one purpose: to drag the sceptical imagination, item by item, from what it cannot deny underfoot, past what it cannot deny in its own bedrooms, toward the one appointed item it has been denying all along — the Day when the pegs come up. The floor; the stakes; next, the pairs of sleepers upon the bed.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:8

> and created you in pairs,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parade Climbs Onto the Bed Itself**

Exhibit three moves from the house to its tenants: *wa-khalaqnākum azwājā* — and We created you in pairs. After floor and pegs, the evidence turns to the audience's own constitution: humanity exists in *azwāj* — pairs, couples, kinds. The primary reading is the duality of the sexes: male and female, made as complements, each half of a whole that completes, shelters and continues the other.

The shift of scale is rhetorically exact. The questioner has been walked past the planet's lay and the mountains' stake-work — the furnishings largest and most impersonal — and now the parade arrives at the exhibit no person can look away from: oneself, twinned. The evidence-generation of the sūrah is running from cosmology into the skin; the questions are getting harder to deflect because the exhibits are getting harder to leave.

**Pairing as Mercy, Twice Over**

The Book's own extended reading of this exhibit names two embedded gifts: *“And one of His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them. And He has placed between you compassion and mercy. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect”* (30:21). Pairing addresses solitude first — *comfort*, *sukūn*, the settling of one life into another's presence — and continuation second: through the pair, the generations pass down the bed of the world.

Note where the verse locates the sign: not merely in the pairing's mechanics but in what God *places between* the pair — affection and mercy, *mawadda* and *raḥma*, invisible endowments no pairing-contract can produce by itself. The sceptics debating the Day's plausibility were each of them carried, daily, through — and by — this exhibit: born of a pair, comforted by a pair, sustained by an endowment of placed mercy. The interrogation is narrowing to the point of personal inescapability.

**The Pair-Principle Stamped All Over Creation**

The sūrah's *azwāj* also rhymes with the principle the Book proclaims cosmically: *“Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know!”* (36:36). The created-pairs motif is not human-only: plants come in pairs; the not-yet-known orders come in pairs; the signature is on all the registers of made things. Humanity's sexual duality is one chapter of a book of pairing that fills the created world.

The pairing-principle then becomes a pointer of its own: paired things stand in created complementarity, with neither half self-sufficient — and the uncreated Maker stands above pairing entirely (the Book's formula against every *walad* and *ṣāḥiba* assigned to Him). The question of the sceptics — how could one God without partner rule all pairing creation? — inverts: creation is *in* pairs precisely because its Maker is singular, and everything made exists as one of a set that completes only with its other.

**From Pairing the Generations Come, and the Graves Fill**

There is a prosecutorial second edge to this exhibit, quiet but present: pairing is also the mechanism by which the world fills with *bodies of appointment* — each new birth another soul with a record opening, another will presented with the delivered-word. The believers and deniers of Mecca were alike the products of pairs, and so were their parents, back to the first pair — the entire procession of generations generated out of this single divine datum.

Each procession-member is also a procession toward the grave: the pairs of the world are unpaired at death — one goes first — and the surviving half stands at a burial that whispers the whole sūrah's argument. The pairing-exhibit thereby swings directly into the topic of the news itself: continuation *by* the pair in the world; summons of *every one of the paired* at the Day. The mechanism that multiplied humanity for life is the same that will multiply the assembly for judgment.

**Duality Designed, or Duality Happened?**

The examiners' question under this exhibit is the simplest one the sceptical age can be asked: is sexual duality an engineered arrangement, or a blind persistence? On the sceptic's hypothesis, innumerable accidents across countless species converged on complementary pairs, sustained by deliberately-placed impulses of comfort and mercy; on the sūrah's attribution, one Maker intends all of it, and each household is a standing *āyah* — a sign to be read, not just used.

The Qur'an's own inflection is reflection: *“in this are signs for people who reflect”* (30:21) — the signs withhold themselves only from the unreflecting. The verse's interrogative form (*have We not...?*) presumes the reflectors will answer *yes*. For Meccans listening under the night of revelation, each in a household made possible by this very pairing, it was an intimate cornering: the Giver of all of it stands in the recitation asking — did We not make *you* in pairs?

**From the Pair to the Pause: Sleep Is Next**

The parade's design now descends from social pairing to personal rhythm: the next exhibits are sleep and night and day — the circadian mercies that pair activity with rest the way the spouses pair man with woman. The sūrah's world is running on *pairs* at every level: earth/sky, bed/pegs, male/female, waking/sleeping, day/night. A questioning audience already knew the pattern of the chapter before the pattern was ever named.

What the pairs-of-sleep exhibits will add is the most devotional point in the sequence: nobody *asks* for sleep, no one earns the night — these arrive as pure, un-ordered provision, exactly like the pairing that put the hearers in the world in the first place. A Maker whose provision runs on gifts and instinct, not on invoices, is precisely the God whose promised Day can be trusted to contain everything its warning contains: the Giver of the world's pairs keeps the accounts of what the pairs did. The procession continues into the bedroom.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:9

> and made your sleep for rest,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mercy That Arrives Unordered**

Exhibit four steps into the bedroom: *wa-jaʿalnā nawmakum subātā* — and We made your sleep a rest, a cutting-off. The evidence has now reached the diary of every listener: whatever they doubted at the assemblies, each of them that very night would fall helpless into a mercy he did not request, could not command, and cannot survive without. Sleep is the daily example of divine government over creatures who imagine they self-govern.

The detail *your sleep* is pressed personally: the Giver addresses what is most *yours* in experience — your nightly unconsciousness, the thing no one can outsource or delegate — and calls it His making. The interrogation-chain has come all the way from the planet-scale to the eyelid-scale, and the method's cumulative force is exactly this convergence: planet, mountains, pairs, and now the thing that will close over the doubter's own eyes within hours of the recitation.

**Subāt: The Vocabulary of a Merciful Severance**

The chosen word, *subāt*, is built on the root of *s-b-t* — to cut, to cease, as the Sabbath (*sabt*) is the day of cutting work. Sleep, in this word, is the divinely-installed *severance*: the tensions of the daylight hours are cut; the nerves' files are closed; the mind's continuous self-torment is interrupted by an appointed command the creature cannot override. God's name for sleep is a mercy-word before it is a biology-word.

The Book's parallel proclamation makes the triplet coverage explicit: *“He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover, and ˹made˺ sleep for resting, and the Day for rising”* (25:47). Cover, rest, rising — the three terms of the night-cycle, each one a gift-item, each unearned. The pairing of the same phrase-cluster in two sūrahs marks this as standing doctrine: the daily cycle is itself a revelation, preached nightly whether recited or not.

**A Daily Rehearsal of Death and Rising**

Here the sūrah's argument comes to its sharpest edge, and the Book makes the connection openly: *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ calls back the souls ˹of people˺ upon their death as well as ˹the souls˺ of the living during their sleep. Then He keeps those for whom He has ordained death, and releases the others until ˹their˺ appointed time. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect”* (39:42). Every night, souls are *taken*; every morning, most are returned. The world's most common transaction is a miniature of the Day feared and denied.

The sceptic who says the dead cannot be brought back has spent every night of his existence inside a counter-example: taken, held, returned, and never by his own machinery. The One who manages this operation across all of mankind — hundreds of thousands simultaneously each night, soul-economies of infants and kings processed in the same dark hour — is being doubted over a single, one-time, mass-operation scheduled for a fixed morning (78:17-18). The evidence parades itself in bed each night; the doubter sleeps in the proof.

**The Economics of Unearned Rest**

Gratitude-economics again: sleep is the purest form of provision-mercy in the sequence, because it cannot be mistaken for something the creature produced. Sleep cannot be bought against its appointed onset, cannot be resisted by strength nor summoned by will; the emperor and the porter fall under it in the same helplessness. It arrives as hospitality — the covering and severance — and it departs as wakefulness, a *return* that no one remembers performing.

The sūrah thereby deals the critics a subtle syllogism: provision of this intimacy (gift-cycles inside one's own pulse) makes no sense in a theology of an absent or unconcerned God — but perfect sense for a Lord who counts, keeps, and plans to call to account. If He tends every soul nightly, He is tending the books daily (78:29 will claim exactly this). The bedroom ends up being the exhibit-room where a careless mankind's *unwatchedness* hypothesis is refuted first, nightly, personally.

**Why This Mercy Preaches Both Comfort and Fear**

For the believer, the verse's comfort is immense: the same hands that close the eyes nightly keep custody of the sleeper — nothing of him is lost in the process, and the waking he greets is itself a return from the divine keeping. For the objector, it is warning: custody during sleep proves power to retain at will. The distinction between *sleep* and *death*, God's voice says, is not located in the creature at all, but in the Keeper: He keeps whom He wills, releases whom He wills (39:42).

The next verse will extend the mercy outward from the body's severance to the world's covering: the night as a garment spread across the day-cycle, under which the cutting-off occurs. Sleep is the soul's withdrawal; night is the world's. The two are providentially synchronized, and the next verse will name the synchronization a garment — one put over the whole of mankind at each turn of the turning world.

**Four Exhibits Deep, and the Chain Grows a Spine**

The interrogation now has a shape: floors below (78:6), fixtures around (78:7), fellows beside (78:8), and the person's own nightly taking-up (78:9). Direction and set exhibit one consistent Speaker — one bedside manner across all scales. The sceptic's cumulative position has become this: a God who cannot raise the dead *made* the bed they are lying on, *drove* the stakes around it, *created* their marriage-partner, *takes* their soul nightly, and *returns* it every morning.

The interrogation is not finished. Night's covering next, then daylight's commission, then the heavens and the lamp and the rain and the provision — and then, in verse seventeen, the named appointment itself. The sequence is openly building a single courtroom sentence: *did We not... did We not... and yet they doubt?* The sleep-verse is the quietest, most patient item in the chain — every night, sleeping through the evidence of exactly the power they spent the day disputing.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:10

> and made the night as a cover,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Libās: A Garment Spread Over the World**

Exhibit five pulls a covering over the sleeper: *wa-jaʿalnā l-layla libāsā* — and We made the night a clothing, a covering-wrap. *Libās* is the garment-word — what a body is wrapped in, worn next to the skin. The whole world's nightfall is thereby described as God clothing His creation: darkness lowered not as absence of light but as *raiment* — deliberately fitted, deliberately lowered, for covered rest.

The metaphor preaches at once. A garment implies both covering and comfort: the night hides the sleeper's sprawled form, the weeper's face, the laborer's slackened posture — concealment as one of its mercies. At night, undignity is draped; the world can stop posing. The great cover also quiets: sight is removed, and with it most of the noise that daylight trades in; the world's marketplace shutters under one cloth, house by house, at God's sundown command.

**A Garment-Word With a Household Echo**

The word's domestic resonance is one the Book develops elsewhere with unforgettable tenderness: *“Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them”* (2:187) — *hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna*. Husband and wife are each other's garments: covering, nearest-touching, warmth-keeping. The same noun for night's covering quietly binds the exhibits of this sequence (spouses in verse eight, cover in verse ten) into one household: the day's workers paired and covered, the world's family bedding down under its shared cloth.

The household-echo also keeps the theology of the night in the right key: the night-cover is not a hiding-place *from* God drawn over the world — God is never covered from — but a covering of the world *by* God, guilt-wrapped and shame-wrapped, lowered over the clamor so that the day's sins can be repented under hush before the next daylight's commission. The garment is a mercy-transparent one: opaque to the world's eyes, never to the Giver's.

**The Triplet Complete: Cover, Rest, Rising**

The Book restates this exact mercy in its full triple form: *“He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover, and ˹made˺ sleep for resting, and the day for rising”* (25:47) — cover, then rest within it, then rising after it, all attributed to one Hand at each station. The two sūrahs together carve the circadian cycle into a standing proclamation: the cycle is not physics-that-happens but worship-window that happens *every* night — provision, wardrobe, resurrection-rehearsal in one dark shift.

And the rising matters as much as the covering: *nushūr* (rising) is the Book's own resurrection-word. Each morning is a small rising from the night's dust-sheet — the sleeper gets up, bedded and covered, into his reclaimed day. The verse's garment-term thereby leans forward into the sūrah's main subject: the One who covers with the night will lift with the morning — and will one day lift all the covered at a single trumpet-blow.

**Darkness Fitted to the Body's Needs**

Physiology, in this image, is served by cosmography. Eyes, muscles and nerves were fashioned for a half-turn of darkness; the body's repair-crews work the night shift. The cover's gift includes the end of five-day-type ambitions — the sun's work-list is closed at sundown not because the world's light failed but because *the cover was lowered*: a divine economy that orders officers of toil off their posts each evening, apportioning fatigue its legal refuge.

The denier hearing this in Mecca was a person for whom darkness had certain very present risks — cold, wolves, raiders — yet the Book emphasizes the reverse relation: the night as shelter-dress, rest-garment. The sūrah's attribution argues from the audience's *best* experience of a phenomenon, not their worst: yes, the cover is lowered every daybreak-turn; has it been reduced to terror? No — it is received by households as the signal of sleep, prey for prayer before dawn, the hour when remembrances are made.

**Night as the Daily Outage of the World's Ambitions**

The garment-image also audits the daytime self. Under the cover, the proud plans laid at noon are all suspended without appeal; the market's confident prices cannot be revised until the cloth is lifted. The night is thus a standing limit on human pretension: whatever a creature's rank at midday, the evening finds every rank equally wrapped, equally inactive, equally dependent on a covering set by Another.

Whole industries of the modern world are dedicated to defeating this mercy — light flooding the cover, wakefulness stretched past design — and the ancient verdict is not reversed by the engineering: the body still pays its night-duty whether or not the city sleeps. The garment cannot actually be removed; it can only be defied at cost. Meanwhile its original purpose still stands for any household that receives it: to end the day, to cover the day, to hold the sleepers until the Rising.

**A Cover With an Expiry Print**

Nothing in this mercy-chain is unqualified, though; the garment also preaches its own coming withdrawal. A cover, by definition, is not the thing covered — the world is the thing, night is its temporary cloth; Day is coming in which every covering is lifted: graves open, records uncovered, skies split into gates (78:19), and the hidden things shone upon by the court's light. The circadian cover and the Day's uncovering stand at opposite ends of one doctrine: modesty first, exposure at the appointment.

So far has the interrogation climbed: below, a laid earth; around, driven stakes; beside, paired fellows; within, souls nightly taken; above-all, a cover lowered over the whole household at dusk. The chain of questions has quietly mounted the sceptics inside a boarding-house of mercy with nothing unpaid and nothing unearned, and is about to walk them to the morning-window and look out at the day — at livelihood — and at the roof of the whole boarding-house: seven heavens, with a lamp burning in them.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:11

> and made the day for livelihood,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Maʿāsh: Daylight Hours as a Wage-Window**

Sixth exhibit: *wa-jaʿalnā n-nahāra maʿāshā* — and We made the day a livelihood, a time for making-a-living. *Maʿāsh* derives from *ʿāsha*, to live: the day is named after its appointed function — the opening in which sustenance is sought. The sequence’s household logic completes itself here: the night covers (78:10), the day commissions. Neither half is accidental; both are *made* — and made consecutively, in the order the creature actually experiences them.

God could have created a humanity without need of provision, or a daylight usable for nothing; He instead installed the need and the window together. The day, in this attribution, is not *time that happens* but *work-time that is granted*: the market's true opening-bell is the sunrise, set by Another, and the true closing-bell the sundown. Every trader who ever rose to earn has worked inside a shop whose hours he did not set.

**The Two Signs and Their Two Offices**

The Book's own fuller proclamation of this pair names the offices side by side: *“We made the day and night as two signs. So We made the sign of the night devoid of light, and We made the sign of the day ˹perfectly˺ bright, so that you may seek the bounty of your Lord and know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺”* (17:12). The day is for bounty-seeking; the night-day alternation, together, is also for *reckoning* — calendar-keeping, the numbering of years.

Two offices, one gift: livelihood and countability. The alternation that feeds the household also provides the clock by which all terms are measured — debts, journeys, ages, and, ultimately, the appointed term of the world itself. The Meccan merchants multiplying measurements by day were, without noticing, living inside the same *measure*-doctrine the sūrah preaches at the cosmic scale: a God of counted days has the mechanism of the Final Appointment built into every sunrise (78:17).

**The Wage-Window Preaches the Wage-End**

Making a livelihood is itself a prophetic parable when read aright: one seeks, one earns, one is given — with effort real and result granted. The day's economy is the world's small-scale model of divine economy: work done in God's opened hours, provision arriving from beyond one's control — harvests not made by the harvester, prices not set by the seller's breath. *Maʿāsh* names the day as the classroom where reliance is studied without being named.

The believers' tradition has always prayed at this window's two edges — the dawn-remembrance before the striving, the sunset-remembrance after it — exactly because the window's gift-character is most legible at its opening and closing. The day's middle is where the gift becomes invisible; hence the sūrah's interrogative: did He not make it? The question is sharpest at noon, when the least likely hearer supposes the daylight self-evident.

**The Commission Is Also the Test**

The wage-window carries its embedded hazard: employment. Hours ordained for seeking provision are, by density, the hours most prone to swallowing the seeker's attention — the daylight's roster filling until the Giver of daylight is forgotten in His own provisions. The Book's sting-lines about diversion locate themselves exactly here: *“Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺, until you end up in ˹your˺ graves”* (102:1-2). *Maʿāsh* occupied becomes *maʿāsh* idolized — the window loved for itself till it becomes the wall.

Which is why this exhibit appears inside a sūrah about the Day: livelihood-hours are the courtroom's chief exhibit of what the defendants were busy with. At the resurrection, no one will plead ignorance of the alternation's lessons; the ledger records what occupied each day, and each day arrived already merchant-shaped, already sermon-shaped — the wage-window preached its doctrine of grant-and-gratitude from the hour the shutters lifted.

**Eleven Questions Later, One Corner**

Count the interrogation's holdings as this verse completes them: the earth-bed below, the mountain-pegs around, pairing beside, sleep within, the night-cover overhead, daylight for winning bread — and still to come, the heavens above everything, the lamp in the heavens, the clouds, the rain, the vegetation, the orchards. Eleven a-lam exhibits (some counts vary by clause, but the chain is one continuous question), arranged from the ground beneath to the roof above, with the hearer's own life in the middle of the inventory.

The prosecutorial design becomes visible exactly here: not one of these exhibits can be answered *no*. And with that many yeses admitted — admitting a Maker of floors, stakes, partners, sleep, covers and wage-hours — the sceptic is cornered into the sūrah's only remaining question: on what grounds, then, does the denier of such carefully counted *days* believe there will not be a *Day?* The case has been built entirely from gifts; the gift-catalogue is complete; the court is about to be named.

**Six Exhibits Deep, Looking Upward**

The parade so far has toured the horizontal world — floor, landscape, partners, sleep-cycle. The verse after this one breaks the horizontal plane and looks up: the roof of the house enters the evidence, and with it, the lamp hung in the roof, and the rain dispatched from above it. The house's upper storey is the interrogation's final gallery.

The movement mirrors the day's own practice: the worker at midday does not look up; the morning's market keeps the eyes level and busy. The sūrah lifts the audience's gaze through the roof, as if to say: the wage-window you labor under has a ceiling, and the ceiling has an Owner's signature seven storeys high. Livelihood, then, is not the day's explanation but its assignment — the bright hours are working-hours inside Someone else's workshop, and the work is weighed upstairs. The next exhibit is the architecture overhead — and after the architecture, the promised day when the roof itself is moved aside and the Court sits openly.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:12

> and built above you seven mighty ˹heavens˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Banaynā: Construction-Language for the Cosmos**

Exhibit seven lifts the eyes to the roof: *wa-banaynā fawqakum sabʿan shidādā* — and We built above you seven firm ones. *Banaynā* is the build-verb — the cosmos is not said to have a sky but an *erected construction*, with all the vocabulary of deliberate architecture: foundation, raising, fastening. The sky is evidence not because it is vast but because it is *built* — a thing appears designed exactly insofar as it behaves like a building.

*Fawqakum* — above you — keeps the audience located: the interrogation places the listener under the roof it describes, in the posture of inspection. The tenant stands inside his tenancy; the ceiling is directly overhead. Nothing in the wording lets the heavens drift into abstraction; they are introduced as *the structure over this audience's heads*, the house's upper works — as close to hand and as inescapable as the earth under the sandals two exhibits ago.

**Seven Storeys, One Above Another**

The number is given exactness: *seven*. The heavenly storeys are not the-atmosphere-as-haze but enumerated levels — seven shidād, one above another, as the Book states through Noah's own preaching: *“Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other?”* (71:15). Storeyed creation implies Builder-order; count implies construction-daybook; the correspondence of levels declares the heavens a planned structure rather than a drift of vapor.

The number seven in this Book also carries the teaching that created realities have *definite measure* — bounded, numberable, known. The sky that stretches past all roads is still countable to its Maker: seven storeys, held, stacked, roof above roof. What appears limitless to the tenant lies under a definite inventory upstairs; the sūrah is slowly assembling the theology of a God who numbers what men call immeasurable — for the same God will soon number the deeds those men called unremembered (78:29).

**Shidād: The Word for the Roof's Strength**

The adjective *shidād* — mighty, firm, tightly-wrought — is the quality the interrogation fastens on. The heavens hold. No crack has appeared in the roof through the lifespans of all the nations; no sag has been reported in the rafters of the sky. The roof's strength is habitability's guard: the stores held aloft, the courses of the lights kept in their lanes, the upper order preserved against the chaos it would otherwise pour onto the floor.

The preservation is credited as an act of continuous, untiring custody: *“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him”* (2:255). The strength of the sky is thus twice attributed — once in its making (firm-built), once in its keeping (preserved without fatigue). The sceptical imagination asks where this world goes; the interrogation answers first: it is *held* — and its being held at all, firmly, across ages, is itself evidence that the Holder's appointments will be kept with equal firmness.

**The Roof as Scale-Preacher**

The rhetorical second duty of *shidād* is to humble the scale-sense of the disputing tenants. Meccan assemblies settled their affairs inside a valley between hills; drilling one's gaze upward past the hills, past the weather-roof, one meets a seven-tiered firmness that renders the heralded Day proportionately ordinary. Against such Builder-scale, the promised unbuilding of mountains and splitting of the ceiling at the Day (78:19) loses its outlandishness: a seven-storeyd house is not astonished at the disclosure of its builder's further acts.

Awe is the intended product, and the verse's engineering of it is economical: the roof that silently awes every dawn and dusk — the ceiling every infant notices before anything else of the sky — is registered as the God-evidence the audience had forgotten they possessed. The strongest signs are not distant; they are immense and therefore unmentionable by habit. The interrogation returns them to evidence-status: did He not *build* these?

**Above the Storeys, the Unbuilt Throne**

The seven shidād are the highest *created* architecture; beyond them, the Book's own survey continues upward to what is not of their kind: the Seat that encompasses the heavens (2:255), the Throne borne above all boundaries. The sūrah's later verse will call the Day-hour *the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, the Most Compassionate* (78:37) — Lordship named over exactly these storeys here exhibited. The ceiling has a Landlord.

That direction of reference matters to the sūrah's destination: the Day of Decision will not occur in a void but *beneath* this very roof's opened gates, with the ranks of angels standing in the newly passed storeys (78:38). The house-architecture of verses six through sixteen is thus the map of the place where judgment will convene: floor holding the procession, roof opened to pass the hosts. The evidence parade builds the courthouse at the same time as it proves the Builder.

**And in the Highest Gallery, a Lamp Burns**

The parade's last celestial exhibit follows within the same roast: the roof was not left dark. The construction includes lighting — a blazing, steadied lamp hung in the storeys, whose offices to the tenants below include their wage-window, their warmth, their year-counts and every harvest the next two verses will trace back to it. The ceiling has an engine.

After the lamp, the interrogation descends the rain-chain to the table — clouds, pouring water, grain, plants, intertwined orchards — until the house stands fully audited from foundation to lamp. The sequence's rhythm is the believer's still: look down at the floor He laid, up at the roof He raised, then back to the table He spreads between them — and then answer the sūrah's one accumulating question, pressing harder with each exhibit: what doubt remains about the Householder's appointed visitation?

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:13

> and placed ˹in them˺ a shining lamp,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sirājan Wahhājan: The Sun Reduced to Its Job Description**

Exhibit eight: *wa-jaʿalnā sirājan wahhājā* — and We set a glowing lamp. The sun — the most magnificent object in the visible experience of any human being — is here introduced not by name but *by function*: *sirāj*, a lamp. The naming is a deliberate demotion of the magnified and a promotion of the function over the idol. What men might call a god, the revelation calls household lighting: an appliance, hung in the house's roof, burning on fuel not its own, serving tenants not its own.

The parallel passage through Noah's preaching runs the same service-name across both sky-lights: *“placing the moon within them as a ˹reflected˺ light, and the sun as a ˹radiant˺ lamp”* (71:16). In God's catalogue, the greatest luminary reads as a fixture — sited, lit, maintained, and (the sūrah will later imply) extinguishable at the appointment.

**Wahhāj: The Adjective of Steady, Scorching Light**

The qualifying adjective *wahhāj* doubles the instruction: blazing, searing, shining with burning intensity. The sun's office is not decorative gleam; it burns — a lamp with heat in its light, warming as well as showing, continuous without flicker. The twin adjectives of the two lamps at 25:61 and 71:16 make the same praise steady: it does not fail, it does not gutter, it does not leak smoke; the world's one great lamp is also the one lamp that never needs trimming.

Steadiness is the sermon: the lamp has burned through all remembered mornings, through every civilization's record including the deniers' own genealogies, without once missing an appointment. If the lamp's Maker keeps such precision in a fixture, why would He be suspected of imprecision in a promised Day (78:17)? The sun is the standing alibi of the divine calendar — the most regular thing in sight attesting the Most Regular's own appointment.

**One Lamp, One Household: The Tawḥīd Inscription**

The choice of *a* lamp — singular — is not accidental grammar. One sun lights this household; one source rules its light; one schedule governs its burn. The cosmic fixture inscribes the doctrine of unity at the planet's obvious skyline: there is no committee of lamps, no contested currents between rival suns. The arrangement's simplicity is its sign-ness: the roof of the house testifies, by its lighting-plan, to the single Light-giver.

Extend the thought downward, and the sun-as-lamp becomes the house's resident preacher: no matter where a worker stands in the day's field, the lamp is above all equally, portioning its service to all — the rich tent and the shepherd's rock under the same distribution. The divine economy of light prefigures the divine economy of provision and also of judgment: one source over all camps, whose light does not consult the quarrels of the campfires below it.

**The Lamp's Payroll: Offices Named in Later Exhibits**

The verse stands bare of a benefits-list, but the chapel of offices is catalogued in the sequence itself. The lamp makes possible the wage-window (78:11) — daylight *is* the lamp at work; it feeds the production-chain of vv15-16, since no grain, plant or orchard repeats its annual miracle without the burning lamp's energy; and it paces the very calendar the mockers lived by, setting their fasts, months and pilgrimage-times.

Each office, traced back, lands on a Keeper: *“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him”* (2:255). A lamp that burns without cease across aeons is burning *on* conservation — its continued lit-ness is a continuous act of being held. The attribute *wahhāj* thereby unleashes the whole doctrine of providence: the blazing is sustained, the sustaining is wakeful, and the Wakeful One is the being asked to be believed about a morning when sleepers rise.

**Fire From Above, Fuel From No One**

The interrogation's sharper edge under this exhibit is the sceptics' own dependence: they disputed the resurrection *by lamplight* — argued at councils beneath a sun they could no more make, light or refuel than they could unburial their own fathers. The fixture overhead was at once the standing proof of divine capability (a fire hanging in the roof without fuel-train) and the standing exposure of creaturely incapacity (no arm can reach its wick).

The classical readers also hear in *sirāj* a polemic against sun-cults old and new: gods are not lit; gods light. By importing the lamp-word, the revelation relocates the object of wonder from the sun to the Lighter: not *look at the lamp* but *look at the fact that it was placed there* — *jaʿalnā*, We made and set. Every later sun-worship is pre-emptively dissolved at the level of vocabulary: one does not prostrate to the candle of one's own room.

**After the Lamp, the Rain-Service Descends**

The interrogation, having toured the house from floor to roof to lighting, now comes to the house's plumbing — the water-service: clouds pressed, water poured down, and by it the whole spread of provision set on the table below. The sun-lamp and the rain-service work in relay: light for growth, water for life. The Maker of the roof is also the Steward of the cistern, and the two services will converge in one closing image — the dense orchard — before the question-chain ends at verse sixteen with a question mark.

The audience-locating remains constant: *above you* (78:12), *for you* implicitly — the house contains them; the exhibits are all features of their own lodging. The interrogation is building to a landlord's closing question: given the furnishing-standard of My house, at what clause of the lease did you imagine there would be no audit-day? The rain-chain arrives next: the house's water-supply carried down from the storeys to the table.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:14

> and sent down from rainclouds pouring water,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Anzalnā: Rain as a Dispatched Consignment**

Exhibit nine shifts from the roof to the water-service: *wa-anzalnā mina l-muʿṣirāti māʾan thajjājā* — and We sent down, from the pressed clouds, water pouring forth. The verb *anzala* — to send down — is deliberate consignment-language: the rain is not moisture that happened, but a dispatch, released from above by order. And the lexical echo is one the hearers knew: the same verb governs the revelation itself — *sent down* the rain; *sent down* the Book. God's two consignments share grammar, sharing origin.

The duality preaches quietly: as rain revives dead land, revelation revives dead hearts; both descend from above by measure, at seasons, upon prepared receptacles. The sceptical audience lived at the intersection of the two down-sends, standing under one while disputing the other. The interrogation's corner tightens accordingly: they trusted the rain-consignment's Giver for every growing season, while doubting the same Giver's revelation-consignment about the season of all seasons.

**Muʿṣirāt: The Clouds Worked Like a Press**

The descriptive noun *al-muʿṣirāt*, from *ʿaṣara* — to press, to squeeze, as juice from the grape — images the rain-clouds as presses worked to yield. Two readings run through the classical register: the clouds themselves, heavy and near-dripping, squeezed of their load; or the winds as the pressers that work the clouds until the water streams out. Both readings preserve the same doctrine: rain is *produced* by a worked process overhead, managed and timed — not a self-starting accident of the air.

The image dignifies the withheld moments too: a cloud that sails past without rain is not an empty one but an un-pressed one — the store remains above, under governance, until its appointed press-time. The desert audience, for whom a year of drought could end a livelihood, lived closest to the theology carried in this word: mercy overhead, real and near, but issued at command — the same pattern the sūrah states for the Day: near, real, held, and scheduled (78:17) — pressed at no pleader's haste.

**Thajjāj: Water Poured Past Measure**

Then the pouring-adjective: *māʾan thajjājā* — water cascading, gushing in abundance, outpoured beyond drop-economy. When the press releases, the release is generous: the rain-word here carries the sound of volume, of the roof-stores emptying lavishly over the fields. God's open hand in the sky, opened at *this* adjective's pressure, comes with no ration-book: thajjāj is the opposite of drizzle-tally.

The extravagance of the rain-consignment sets the measure for the whole provision-theology of these verses: the Giver's custom is abundance against desert, generosity against scarcity. This matters against the terrified suspicion natural to survival-cultures — that heaven's stores are scarce, that every prayer is overheard counting — and the sūrah answers with an adjective: no, the custom overhead is *outpouring*. The later reward-furniture of the sūrah (78:32-36) will wear the same lavishness on another register.

**The Descent's Destination Is the Table**

Rain's telos arrives in the next two exhibits — grain and greenery raised *by it* — and the Book's own full statement of the chain is worth setting beside: *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”* (36:33). Dead land, pressed clouds, cascading water, life, grain, eating: the entire procession of provision is displayed as a single continuous dispensation from the same Hand — from the inert soil to the morsel.

Traced upward, every meal on every table in Arabia was delivered *through* this consignment-system: the loaf begins at a pressed cloud. The sceptic's daily bread — the actual bread — ran on a supply-chain whose head office was the sky. The interrogation is assembling the meals as evidence now; the meals were always answers waiting for their questions. Did We not send down? The eater's yes is already in his mouth.

**The Sky's Storehouse Doctrine**

A settled comfort of the tradition gathered here: the rain is kept *above*, in a storehouse no creature administers. Drought therefore never proves the store empty — the pressed-cloud image itself teaches withholding-without-absence, the mercy suspended in governance. The people so addressed knew droughts as their standing dread; the verse relocates the dread from scarcity to scheduling. What is kept is in hand; what is in hand arrives at its pressing-time.

The praying-of-rain custom that the believers later institutionalized grows from exactly this verse-logic: the address for more is not the cloud but the Cloud-keeper; not the storehouse but the Steward of the pressing. And the season-teaching completes the comfort: a delayed consignment is not a cancelled one — the pressing-vocabulary itself guarantees that the stores exist long before the streets see water. Appointments under God's sky are always closer than the drought suggests.

**Water, the Element of Life and of Counter-Life**

The Book's water-doctrine is a pillar: the same voice that says life came from water *“˹and˺ made from water every living thing”* (21:30) teaches by the same element the pattern of revivification — dead earth quickened by descent, season upon season. The rain-consignment is, in God's curriculum of analogies, the resurrection rehearsed annually: what looks terminally dead drinks and stands green. *He who revives the land after its death* is the recurrent formula the Book attaches to this cycle.

The verse thereby appoints the rain-service as the sūrah's transition-witness: a God certified by every wet season can conduct one greater wet season — the graves' opening — with no new machinery. The mockers burnt their objections under a lamp (78:13) and watered them down with rains (78:14) they could never create; the evidence now totals sky, roof, lamp, clouds — the upper storey fully audited — and the chain descends finally to the field, the garden and the mouth, in the verses that complete the house's inventory.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:15

> producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bi-hi: The Pronoun That Keeps God on the Controls**

Exhibit ten completes the chain: *li-nukhrija bi-hi ḥabban wa-nabātā* — to bring out thereby grain and vegetation. The little instrument-pronoun, *bi-hi* — *by it* — carries the column of causation: the water is the instrument; the bringing-out belongs to *Us* — God. The revelation never lets its audience slip into instrument-worship: clouds do not farm; water does not germinate; the consignment-mechanism overhead is handled from beyond the mechanism.

The chain's placing is also its sermon. The verses have now run the full production-line — pressed clouds (78:14), poured water, grain and green — and the personnel at every grip-point of the line is the same divine We. Modern machinery-thinking would split the line into processes; the Book keeps the line unified under one Will, and draws the hearer's attention to the unity precisely by stringing the pronouns: *We* sent down... *by it* We bring out...

**Ḥabb: The Named Staple**

*Grain* — ḥabb — comes first because it comes first in survival: the staple upon which cities stand, the caravan-load by which Mecca itself ate. Yemeni wheat, Syrian barley — the commerce of the peoples being addressed was, at bottom, grain-logistics; their pride of trade was a re-routing of God's pressed-cloud consignments into sacks. The sūrah names the staple as exhibit: city-wealth is granary-mercy routinized.

The closer look grain invites is famous in the Book's pedagogy: the kernel must go into the ground, break, seemingly *die* in the soil, before it multiplies thirty-, seventy-fold. Buried and raised again with increase is seed-metaphysics older than every sceptic's objection: the economics of all bread is a tutorial in resurrection. The Qur'anic analogies elsewhere say it in so many words — the caller to scepticism was a farmer before he was a doubter; the sowing he practices is the doctrine he refused to recite.

**Nabāt: The Category Without Census**

Then the green generality: *wa-nabātā* — and vegetation, the uncountable order: fodder for the herds (without which no caravan moves), the medicine-shrubs, the timber-trees, the cover of the earth that the nomad reads like scripture for pasturing. After the staple, the unbounded branch: God does not enumerate the plants because the point of the category is its open-endedness — all the green that ever meant life to flesh is rolled into one word and attributed to one Agent through one pronoun.

Grain and vegetation together compose mankind's complete life-support: bread taken directly, meat carried up through the grazing-chain, shade and wood and figs between. Two words inventory an ecology. And the theological payoff stands in the pairing: the same consigned water feeds both word-classes — the cultivar and the wild cover — which teaches the splendor-point, *diversity from one delivery*. Pressed clouds indistinct from one another produce, through one pronoun's governance, food on ten thousand stems.

**Dead Earth Is the Film the Sceptics Watch Yearly**

The Book names the setting with precision: *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”* (36:33). Dead — that is the soil between seasons: cracked, grey, apparently finished; then the consignment falls, and the stand of life rises where death was the only analysis. The sceptics' entire homeland experiences this film continuously: wadis that bake in August run in February; hillsides that seemed mineral break green.

The sūrah's argument is not analogical whimsy but standing Qur'anic method: *He revives the earth after its death* — that is how He will conduct *your* revival, and the film is subtitled as such across the Book. Verse fifteen is thus the load-bearing exhibit in the resurrection-proof: the interrogation's entire spectacle — pressed stores, poured water, risen green — argues the one point the audience was disputing, in material they could not possibly dispute. Dead becoming alive under consignment is not a belief; it is an annual observation.

**The Kitchen as Mosque of Remembrance**

Tradition drew the table-consequence at once: the meal never arrives innocent of the chain — hence the remembrances upon eating, the naming of God over bread. This verse gives the liturgical reflex its full theological content: at the loaf, the chain stands behind it — pressed cloud, poured water, split seed, hands reaping, fire baking — and every link reads *We*, *We*, *We*. The kitchen becomes a daily recitation of the provider-scene, the meal a benediction-cue.

Where meals are taken without that remembrance, provision becomes anesthesia: full stomachs recruiting against the reminder. The sūrah will soon display the sharpest form of that inversion — the condemned receiving, from the same Provision-source, the counter-provision of scalding water (78:24-25) — the two tables of God in the two doors of the Day. The blocked heart that denied over full bread will drink with the same dependence and none of the mercy; the link traced to the Giver is the whole difference between provision and torment.

**One Item Remains Before the Question-Mark Falls**

After grain and generality, the inventory closes with the most luxurious item on the desert-hearers' list — the orchard, densely interplanted, green stacked upon green under its own woven shade (78:16). The approaching of the *jannāt alfāfā* is fitted to be the final exhibit, the superlative that seals the yes-chain before the chain's question-mark is pressed home.

Then the interrogation ends forever — within a verse the sūrah will turn from have-We-not to indeed — and the promise these exhibits were gathered to underwrite will be delivered in its appointment-vocabulary: the Day, the scheduled Day of the greatest of all separations, which the provisioned and the sceptical alike will attend without a note of rain able to be ignored. The orchards of the world are next; then the orchards' pledge of another Garden.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:16

> and dense orchards?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jannātin Alfāfā: Green Woven Upon Green**

The inventory's closing exhibit: *wa-jannātin alfāfā* — and gardens, orchards, densely-entwined. *Alfāf*, from the root of wrapping and binding, describes foliage grown so thick that its branches interlace — shade stacked over shade, green upon green until sunlight barely reaches the orchard floor. The sequence's superlative has been saved for last: not a sprout or a field but abundance at the level of *embarrassment*, vegetation overgrowing itself.

And the word *jannāt* is the sūrah's slyest choice. It is the ordinary word for orchards here — and the selfsame word for the Garden of reward in the verses ahead (78:31-32). The earth's interlaced orchards deliberately share the Garden's name: God's previews are distributed in the world's backyards before His main promise lands. The sceptic who strolled a vineyard at Ṭāʾif was strolling through the pledge's advance-gallery — *jannāt* here, *jannāt* there, and the difference precisely the one the sūrah is about to define.

**The Orchard-Evidence in Reach of Every Meccan**

The exhibition requires no imagination: the hearers had seen them. The date-plantations of Yathrib's basins, the grape-vines and fruit-trees of Ṭāʾif's walled gardens — green islands amid volcanic plains — were the peninsula's standing marvels. The Book's own exhibit-text runs parallel: *“And We placed in it gardens of date-palms and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth, so that they may eat of their fruit”* (36:34-35). Palm, vine, spring, eating: orchard theophany was a weekend's ride away.

An orchard, of all God's spreads, carries the strongest *signature-of-care*: grafting-ages of wooden patience, water-channels hand-dug, walls against the sand-wind, and then — under one woven roof of branches — fruit in impossible plenty within a hand's reach. The very interlacing of *alfāfā* is the luxury — not fruit here and there, but a *construction* of abundance, self-shaded, self-mulched. That such architectures should stand amid Arabia's stone is the exhibit's whole point: the desert maps its margins with gardens.

**A Word That Means to Conceal**

The root of *janna* is covering — *j-n-n*, to veil, to enwrap: the orchard is so named, the linguists say, because its density covers the ground beneath it. The word's own etymology thus rehearses the orchard's meaning: God's generosity concealed beneath his creatures' feet, concealed green covering concealing dark soil — provision wrapped inside provision.

Out of the same verb-root also come *junnah* (a shield), *jinn* (the covered-from-sight ones), and *al-jannah* — the Garden-that-covers. The sūrah, without pausing for philology, thereby gathers the entire lexical family into one exhibit: the shaded orchard was a small garden-named shield under the sun; the promised Garden ahead is the final such covering. The verse itself does the pivoting by vocabulary alone — orchard-experience handed forward as promise-language.

**The Question-Mark of a Parliament of Evidences**

The chain ends here, where the punctuation stands in the verse itself: after eleven continuous exhibits, the question that began *have We not* finally closes with its printed *?*. God's interrogation-parade has ended. Earth-lay, mountain-stakes, pairs, sleep-severance, night-cover, day-commission, seven storeys, blazing lamp, pressed clouds, cascading water, grain, green, orchards: the audit of the visible is complete, and the answer expected of the listener — eleven times over — is *balā* — yes, You did.

The function of the question-mark, now that it finally arrives, is the trap: an audience that has conceded this list eleven times has intellectually signed the words *such a Maker cannot be doubted*. The interrogative chain is the closest thing in the Book to a signed confession-scene: the exhibits do not argue toward a conclusion; they *extract* one. And at the end of the yes-chain, the next verse will not ask anything at all — after so many questions, the sūrah will at last deliver a statement, in the strongest possible particle: *inna*.

**What the Interrogation Has Purchased**

Pause over the price of those eleven yeses. They have bought, for the resurrection-doctrine, three immovable admissions: a Maker demonstrated at every scale of creation; an order of provision so systematic that miracles of recurrence (seasons, rains, harvests) pass among men as ordinary; and a household whose comforts are continuous deliveries from above, not self-produced fixtures underneath. With those three walls stood, the sceptics' objection *it is too big to happen* is structurally demolished: the biggest visible acts are already occurring as invisible routine.

The chain has also purchased the correlate mercy: no one in the audience can claim the case went unseen. The evidence was beneath them, around them, above them, in their marriages, sleep, bread and orchards — election-day notices issued from every dimension of the house. After this, the term *momentous news* (78:2) is no longer a claim about a strange report; it is a summary of a housebuilt world whose Owner visits.

**From the Orchard's Shade to the Appointed Day**

The pivot-edge is deliberately the sharpest in the sūrah: weave the shade and water of verse sixteen directly alongside the next verse's *indeed*, and the scene's grammar changes from indicative-present to decree-future without one word of transition. The interrogative closes; the imperative arrives. At seventeen, the sūrah leaves the furnished world it just audited and steps over the threshold of the house's final event — the landlord's own scheduled visitation of His completed property.

The orchard holds a quiet foretoken within its shade: *jannāt* again — the Garden-block (78:31-36) will open with the same word's plural promise for the righteous; the sūrah's closing third will thus be seeded in the same noun as its evidence-block. God's final question of the interrogation delivers His greenest exhibit, as if to say: what I grow beside your walls in this world, understand well — for I am able to grow it, larger, on the other side of the Day. The last amen of the yes-chain murmurs into the orchard's interlace; then the trumpet-readiness begins.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:17

> Indeed, the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is an appointed time—

**Expanded Commentary**

**After Eleven Questions, the Statement**

The interrogation's yes-chain has ended, and now the sūrah delivers what the whole evidence-parade was purchasing: *inna yawma l-faṣli kāna mīqātā* — indeed, the Day of Decision stands appointed. Emphatic *inna* closes the questions with a declaration: not an argument, a scheduling order. The form alone preaches — verses six through sixteen asked; this verse decrees. What the audience conceded under interrogation is now entered against them in the plainest assertive grammar the Arabic possesses.

The structure is the sermon: a world whose furnishing is undeniable leads inexorably to a Day whose appointment is undeniable. The sūrah's logic is here declared complete — with no intermediate step, no softening transition. The mercy-economy of the house has a closing-hours posted; the tenancy's answering event has a fixed entry on the calendar of the Landlord. *Kāna mīqātā*: it is, and has long been, set.

**Faṣl: The Day Named After Separation**

The Day's name here deserves its full weight. *Faṣl* is decisive separation — the cutting between true and false, deserving and undeserving, claimant and claimant; also *settlement*, the closing of a case. The Book's family of occurrences is thereby summoned into view: the day that settles what was disputed (44:40), the day the mocked communities first named at its dawn (37:20-21). This is the sūrah of the momentous news's own name-rise: the news, still today receivable as controversy, will on that day be unraveled without remainder — and every ikhtilāf of verse three will stand resolved by the event itself.

That function pre-dates denial: dispute has always existed among men, and God's remedy has always been the same — deferral to a set court, where the evidences stand on oath, and the gavel comes down for the last time. Judgment Day in this naming is nothing but God's long patience contracting at last into God's single sentence; the deferred argument of every street is given one scheduled hour to end.

**Mīqāt: The Calendar-Word of Appointments**

The noun *mīqāt* is the everyday appointment-word: the ḥajj pilgrim's scheduled and located stations are called *mawāqīt*; a meeting-time fixed in advance is a *mīqāt*. God borrows the machinery of ordinary life for His greatest scheduling: the Day is not described as a vague eventuality but as a *fixed entry* — placed, dated, unchangeable. The audience's own calendar-instincts supply the meaning: an appointment from God has the same status as an appointment in trade or pilgrimage, but it is one that no courier can cancel and no negotiator renegotiate.

And the word implies the full content that appointment-words carry: an appointed time with an appointed Keeper, who will be exactly where He scheduled. The promise's weight rests on its Namer: the same voice through the whole sūrah has shown itself punctual at every deliverable — sunrises, rains, harvests all delivered on time since the world began. The mīqāt of the Day is guaranteed by the only calendar in existence that has never missed a dawn. God's prompt-record is the security of the appointment.

**Why a Set Time Preaches Both Directions**

Scheduling preaches mercy first: a set appointment implies an *interval* — the now between announcement and arrival — and the interval is exactly where the sūrah's audience still stands. The pinned date carries an open space before it in which the disagreeing parties may still revise their camps. Elsewhere the Book states the deferral-logic in a simile of utmost restraint: *“And We only delay it for a fixed term”* (11:104) — the waiting is the wait of justice so complete it will not arrive even one innocent lifetime early.

And the same scheduling preaches terror: postponed-only-until. A fixed mīqāt cannot drift away; it approaches the disagreeing city with the exact necessity that the city observed in the sun's morning arrival (78:13). What is set does not have to be hastened by the mockers or slowed by their treaties; it simply *faces* them, calendared, advancing one sunrise every sleep-interval they are granted. The appointment-language thus transfers the argument from plausibility to mathematics: time-to-live between here and a fixed entry.

**The Sūrah's Hinge-Stone**

Note the verse's structural position: it stands precisely at the hinge. Everything before it (78:1-16) was world and talk: the street's question, the audit of the house. Everything after it (78:18-40) is the Day and its two doors. *Yawm al-faṣl* is named in the exact center, the axis on which the whole sūrah turns — the sentence to which the evidence points and from which the decree unfolds. Form and doctrine coincide with unusual purity: the settled statement at the sūrah's center announces the settlement of all unsettled things.

The recurring rhetoric of this Book names other hinge-stones at other sūrah-centers, and the device is always the same: the reader, arrived mid-sūrah, stands at an hour-hand calibrated to the Day. The arrangement itself says the news is closer than it looks, standing at the middle of a recounting that began at creation's furniture. The story of the house is more than half told; only the Day remains.

**An Appointment Written Singular**

The appointment's count is also its doctrine: *mīqātā*, one appointment. Not a rolling schedule of judgments, not a succession of audition-days in which camps might hedge their participation across several attempts — one fixed hour, universal of attendance. The singularity of the date is the singularity of the King: history ends once; each soul's arrival at it is scheduled simultaneously with all the others; no soul re-schedules, no generation lodges for a later sitting.

And the dash at verse's end — the suspended *time—* — is the Arabic's sense as well as the English translator's stroke: the appointment-name leans forward into the next verse's own announcement, the day-of detail: the trumpet blown, and you will come. The naming of the day here is the breasting of a hill: at the top, the whole landsccape of it opens — the crowds, the sky opened, the mountains unmade — in the sequence of verses that attends. The mīqāt has been raised like a standard; the next verse begins its parade.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:18

> ˹it is˺ the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will ˹all˺ come forth in crowds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yunfakhu fi ṣ-Ṣūr: The First Sound of the Last Day**

The appointment now receives its program of events: *yawma yunfakhu fi ṣ-ṣūri fa-taʾtūna afwājā* — the Day the Trumpet is blown, and you come in companies. *Al-ṣūr* is the Horn — the Day's opening instrument, sounded at God's command to re-summon every sleeper and every grave-guest. The Book's own program-poster for those moments is one of its most staggering verses: *“The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead... Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺”* (39:68).

The two blasts — the end-blast and the rising-blast — stand outside this verse's fast sequence, but the sūrah assumes them: the blast mentioned here is the assembly-blast, the one whose air reaches the turned earth and the scattered bones, and whose first product is stated immediately: *you come*. The trumpet-the image is masterful: a summons that no agent can refuse, a convocation dispatched not by courier but by sound through every tomb at once.

**Fa-taʾtūna: The Second Person Finally Lands**

*And you will come forth.* Mark the pronoun-shock: verses one through five discussed them — *they* ask, *they* will know; from the interrogation onward God was the We and the audience an addressed *you* listening about the world. Here, at the trumpet's edge, the procession contains you — *taʾtūna*, second person plural: the assembly will be made of the presently-listening assembly. The debated news has become the addressee's personally scheduled arrival.

This grammatical pivot is standard in the sūrah-final style of revelation: the case is prosecuted at a distance (they), the verdict is delivered to the face (you). The Meccan hearers, overheard in the first verse as *they*, stand addressed at verse eighteen in the actual procession. A sūrah that began by eavesdropping has converged, within eighteen verses, to speaking to the overheard — and it will hold the singular by the end (78:40), until the procession is not even a crowd but one person beholding his own hands' dispatch.

**Afwāj: Arrival in Sorted Companies**

The procession comes *afwājan* — crowds, companies, large successive droves. The word images the arriving of humanity as an immense caravan-season, generations and peoples in waves: nations filing with their prophets, cohorts arriving by era, the whole numbered species in ordered movement. Procession-vocabulary replaces individual biography: in the convoy, a person travels with his *kind* — the companies of the like, the file of his works and his opinions.

The Book's parallel uses mark the sorted character: the deniers *“driven to Hell in groups”* (39:71) wear the same procession-noun on the dark leg of the journey; and the company-formation serves a purpose beyond mere crowd-handling. Companionship at the arrival is itself a first disclosure of the file: the honest recognize who they marched beside, the mocked-of recognize who sneered beside them, and the sūrah has already named the lanes of the sorting — transgressors one way, the God-wary the other (78:21, 78:31).

**The Sound That Ends the World's Noise**

The trumpet-as-image preaches with special sharpness against the *ikhtilāf* that opened the surah: the markets and assemblies were full of voices — the voices of mutual questioning. One blast, and the whole distributed argument ends absolutely: buying stops mid-price, the sneer stops mid-word, the earth's noise yields to one unanswerable Voice. The Book marks that same finality: *“the Day all will hear the ˹mighty˺ Blast in ˹all˺ truth, that will be the Day of emergence ˹from the graves˺”* (50:42) — heard in truth, as against all the hearings in doubt that preceded.

Sovereignty's speech is not, this time, a recitation that can be debated; it is a sound that makes further debate physically impossible — and the distinction is the difference between the world of the remonstrating Book and the world of the executing King. The voice that asked *what are they asking one another about?* has, by verse eighteen, assumed the single register in which no questions stand: the trumpet. What the evening-assembly talked over, this sound ends — and begins the era of verdicts.

**Whoever Lived Lined Up for This**

The universality of the coming is total: *fa-taʾtūna* addresses all the living of every age. Prophetized and unprophetized, the caravan-leaders and their camel-boys, the disputants of Mecca's clubs and the peasants of terraced valleys who never heard the argument — one summons, one procession. The procession-verse thereby quietly overthrows the last shelter of pride: standing beside the mocked-of news was never a question of status-exemption; at the convocation all ranks walk.

And it also finishes the reply to the sceptic's arithmetical objection — the too-many-dead objection. The question was numeracy: how can the uncountable generations re-emerge? The verse answers with movement-vocabulary: they are not *assembled by clerks*; they *come*, called by a sound, arriving in their natural companies, faster than the counting-schemes of their doubters. The trumpet's arithmetic works by summons rather than by transportation, and the summoner is the one who gathered the first time, from dust, without assistance.

**Above The Procession, the Sky Is Already Being Opened**

The program-poster continues with what stands over the marching: the ceiling of the house (78:12) — audited as the strongest architecture in existence — is at this hour *being opened into gates*, unbolted in all its storeys to let the Day's traffic pass. The raging of processions below and passages above runs simultaneous: the house is dismantled from above as the tenants are emptied from it below.

After the sky, the mountains: the pegs driven at verse seven pulled up in the blast-schedule, marched into mirage. The order of the appointed program is exactly the inverse of the interrogation's furnishing order — roof and stakes unmade first (78:19-20), then the house's two appointed occupancy-states (the ambush and the garden, 78:21ff). The tenancy audited is surrendered in the same order in which it was furnished; and the trumpet's blast is the key turning in that surrender.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:19

> The sky will be ˹split˺ open, becoming ˹many˺ gates,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Strongest Thing in the World Is Unbolted**

The program of the appointment continues overhead: *wa-futiḥati s-samāʾu fa-kānat abwābā* — the heaven is opened, so it becomes gates. The reverse-witness here is exact: verse twelve's seven shidād — the mighty, tightly-wrought storeys — are now *opened*, and the roof of the whole house proves to have been a door or set of doors the whole time, closed until its hour. The most permanent-looking feature above the heads of every creature is reclassified, in half a verse, as *passage*.

*Kānat* — it *became* — performs the change without a process: there is no gradual architecture of damage, no siege of the firmament; the sky's nature at this hour is simply *being-opened*, as if opening had been its latent property awaiting command. Divine renovation is not construction-time; the storeys turn gateway by fiat. Every later reader of 78:12 must now re-read it in this light: the heavens' strength was always the strength of *doors kept shut by their Keeper*, never of a structure independent of His latch.

**Why the Sky Has Gates at All**

Gates exist for traffic. The Day's operations require passage between the upper and lower economies of the created order: the descending ranks of angels (78:38 will show them standing in rows below), the delivering of the records, the passage of command and authorization. The ceiling's opening routes the administration of the Day: the government above moves unimpeded into the courtroom below — not as raiders through a breach but as officers through opened gates.

The register of *abwāb* — plural, many gates — also unwrites the sky's inaccessibility in a second direction: what had always been the utmost *beyond* of the human gaze becomes traversable. Men asked their whole lives what lay above the ceiling of sight; at the Day the answer is delivered not by answer but by removal — the gate-many sky stands open, and the order beyond it stands at work in view. The Book holds another announcement of the same event brevity: *“When the sky bursts open”* (84:1) — the splitting registered in one line, like the final page of a ledger being turned.

**The Unmaking of the House Proves the Made-House Doctrine**

Here is the argument the sūrah has constructed so smoothly that it nearly goes unseen: the interrogation furnished the world as a house (floor, stakes, cover, roof, lamp), and the appointment's program un-furnishes it in the same vocabulary — sky opened, stakes marched off (78:20). The condition of being *made* is continuous between both states: built and dismantled by the one Landlord. God's proof of capability for the Day does not stand on the Day's novelty but on the Day's belonging to the same authorship.

Which is why the gates-verse is not spectacle for spectacle's sake; it is the completion of the sermon begun at *a-lam najʿal*. The temple-that-was-a-house proves to be the way-station it always was. Every gift of the furnishing — the cover, the lamp, the peace of the roof — was a *tenancy-fixture*, not an endowment, held on the Landlord's warranty, and the warranty's term runs out exactly at the mīqāt (78:17). The architecture's opening is the lease's expiry.

**The Mocked-of Above Is Now the Scene Below**

There is a polemical undertone for the original audience: demands that the sky be made to fall were among the Meccan taunts — and here the sky behaves, at last, *even more* than the taunts imagined: not crashed but *opened*, administratively, into gates that serve the very judgment the taunters had demanded be hastened. Ironies of this kind mark the Book's Day-programs throughout: every mocking requisition is granted in a form that indicts the requisitioner.

The awesomeness is also deliberately *seen*. The verses speak as though the procession below — *you coming in crowds* (78:18) — has eyes lifted upward to this ceiling: the crowd's first sight at arrival is the world being unmade above it. Awe at the Day is a designed part of the assembly's education: denial ended not by syllogism but by skyline, futile ikhtilāf silenced under a ceiling of doors. The street that asked and joked now stands in the concourse of the opened heaven — and for the transgressors, another destination's ambush is already waiting in the description one verse away.

**Gates as the Counterword to the Sealed**

Abwāb also preaches against every sealed exit of the world: the sky, which never once opened for any tribe's prayers to circumvent the appointed order, *opens for judgment alone*. All the shutters of the world — the grave's sealing, the record's sealing, the hearts' sealing — have their master-image in this first unsealing of the greatest boundary. The gate-plural preaches one doctrine: at His hour, no closure holds.

The pattern has devotional edges: the believer who has prayed under the closed heaven as under a listening roof is not surprised at the final unfastening; the praying people always knew the ceiling could open — the gates-being-many was their expectation, not their shock. The procession-sky thus strikes the two crowds differently: for the transgressors, it is the first view of the arrival of their file's administration; for the God-wary, the first view of the travel-corridor toward mafāz.

**Below the Opened Sky, the Mountains Are Already Melting**

The program's next frame is the unmaking at ground level: the storeys opened above are matched by the pegs pulled below; the sky's traffic-gates swing while the world's most stubborn mass turns to shimmer — the mountains, those awtād drilled through the earth's crust, moved off until they resemble a mirage (78:20). Roof and anchor are unmade in the same two verses, and the procession marching in crowds walks under an opened heaven across a dissolving geography.

Such is the appointed program of the visitation: the house is not merely left, it is *dismantled in the leaving* — as if the Landlord wished no tenant, afterward, to imagine the tenancy could be resumed or the closing delayed. The Day's traffic-points are gaping overhead; the landmarks are rolling underfoot; hold only on the file you marched with. The ambush-verse is two verses away; the garden-verse is nine verses away; the gates and the mountains lie between, unbuilding the world so that only the sorting remains to be built.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:20

> and the mountains will be blown away, becoming ˹like˺ a mirage.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Suyyirat: The Stakes Are Called In**

The last frame of the world's unmaking: *wa-suyyirati l-jibālu fa-kānat sarābā* — and the mountains are set in motion, so they become a mirage. *Suyyira* is the march-order verb: the most deeply-rooted objects in creation receive movement-orders and move. The verse is the sūrah's own back-reference executed: those *awtād* drilled into the earth-bed at verse seven — the pegs of the whole house — are here pulled up, and the bed they steadied ripples with their extraction.

Every believer reciting the sūrah feels the hinge: the very exhibits of stability from the evidence-parade return as exhibits of mutability in the appointment-program. God had argued from the mountains' emplaced firmness for His power of keeping; now He argues from their *removal* for the Day's certainty. Both directions prove the same Lordship; both are one decree away from each other, at all times. A fixture is a peg only until the Peg-driver summons it.

**From Bedrock to Heat-Shimmer: The Word Sarāb**

The endpoint-word is chosen with desert-eyes: *sarāb* — the mirage, the quavering false-water on hot plains that looks like substance and is air. Mountains become *that*: the most granite-certain feature of the visual world reclassed into the category of *things that never really possessed the substance they seemed to*. The metamorphosis named is more than pulverization; it is a verdict on appearances themselves — the solid was provisional; the shimmering departure is nearer its truth than its rootedness ever was.

The classical images arrive in dues order: the Book elsewhere says the mountains are moved off, crushed, carded like wool, flattened until the land lies bare — *“My Lord will wipe them out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen”* (20:105-107) — and in another register — *“when the earth is flattened out”* (84:3). The end-state is a plain without landmark: the muster-field requires flatness because nothing is left to hide behind, no highland for the high, no crag for the shelter of settlements. Geography's advantages are wound up before the judgment sits.

**The Mirage-Metaphor Audits Every Fixture of Confidence**

The word's polemic is wider than geology. If *mountains* — the byword for permanence in every tongue of the region — close their account as *mirage*, by what name are the passing fixtures of human confidence to be classed? Fortunes, dynasties, fortresses, personal plans commanded so solidly that a generation builds its certainties upon them: in the Day's optics they are sub-mountain, more vaporous than mirage. The verse is calibrating the hearer's sense of what *substance* means.

At the street-level of the sūrah's opening this audit lands exactly: the Meccan assemblers took their pride from standing arrangements — houses, markets, tribal guarantees, the very hills of the sanctuary-valley as emblems of inviolability. Verse twenty answers that pride at its deepest nerve: the hills themselves are at the appointment converted to shimmer. Whatever is not God, the mountains teach by their exit, has only a deferred shimmer-date; only the file of one's deeds will accompany one across the levelled field.

**Twenty Verses, One Sweep From Furnishing to Shimmer**

The sūrah's structural masterpiece stands visible at this point: verses six through sixteen furnish a world — bed, pegs, household, lamp, orchards; verses seventeen through twenty unmake it — appointed trumpet, flattened mountains, opened roof. The two moves, furnishing and unfurnishing, are the whole theatre of creation as this Book sees it: a made-house, inhabited on trust, and called in at term. The interrogation-chain and the appointment-program enclose each other like brackets; everything the mīqāt will do is already narratively contained in what *We did* at the furnishing.

The sweep also identifies the resurrection's real difficulty, and it was never divine capacity: between the two brackets stands only the human interval, the street with its talk. Against a world arrivable and departible at command, the sceptics' dispute concerned not God's arm but their own appointment — and verse twenty is the sūrah's promise that no landscape will stand between them and facing it. The solid is marched off so that only *truth* retains solidity in the plain that remains.

**At the Flattened World, Two Enclosures Await**

Now the sūrah's travel-sequence brings the two destinations within one horizon: with the mountains gone, the terrain visible from the muster-field has only two landmarks — the ambush-station of the Fire (78:21) and the enclosure of gardens (78:31-32). The levelling of the world is the removal of everything except what is *for* or *against* the assembled; the geography of what matters is all the geography left.

Notice that both destinations are described as *waiting*: hell lies *in ambush* — mirṣādā — and the gardens stand prepared under the same Giver; the two outcomes pre-exist the arrivals, as places must that were *made ready* before the making of mankind's last generation. The flattened plain thus reads like the unveiled truth of the world's own moral geometry: all the while the sceptics traded talk among their hills, two enclosures stood beyond the hills, already built. The hills hid them; the hills are now mirage.

**The Mirage as the World's Self-Portrait, Mainly**

A last reflection for those still walking the interval before the appointment: the mirage-word in the mouths of desert people named a known cruelty — the thirsty rush toward shimmer, and arrive at nothing. The sūrah has been applying the same cruelty-theology from its first verse: the disagreements, mockeries and postponements of the street were shimmering guarantees of more time; the pursued little-while enjoyments (as its sister-sūrah put the sentence) shrink under the same sun. Verse twenty universalizes the metaphor: not merely their comforts but *the hills they stood on* prove in the last frame to be mirage-grade.

Everything, then, depends on whether anything in life was ever *not* mirage — and the sūrah's answer is ready and will occupy its remaining twenty verses: the works sent ahead (78:29-30, 78:36, 78:40), the God-wary's savings of taqwā, the Lord named Merciful and the Lord of all the storeys (78:37). Against a world whose mountains are shimmer, the only solidities are the ones the Book has been preaching since its first sentence: the Day, the news about it, and the path back to the Lord who fixed the appointment. The ambush-verse announces itself next from beyond the flattened plain.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:21

> Indeed, Hell is lying in ambush

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mirṣād: The Abode That Hunts**

The two destinations open, and the sūrah gives the darker one first: *inna jahannama kānat mirṣādā* — indeed, Hell lies in ambush, waits watching. *Mirṣād*, from *raṣada* — to lie in wait, to keep watch for prey — is predator-vocabulary applied to a place: the Fire is not merely a destination at the end of the plain but a watching, waiting hunter. Two classical inflections meet in the one word: Hell itself depicted as the lurking observer of those who pass, ready to seize its own; and Hell as a posted look-out, kept on station for God's enemies.

The desert-eared audience felt the word's full weight: an ambush (*mirṣād* is the marauder's station) is what turns a route into a death-trap. One does not walk toward an ambush; the ambush receives. The verse's power is exactly this reversal of agency: a mankind convinced it is *travelling toward* outcomes discovers, from the verse onward, that the outcome has been *posted ahead, watching*, the whole time — the traffic is prey-traffic whether the travellers knew it or not.

**Kānat: The Ambush Was Already Posted**

The tense is past: *kānat* — Hell *has been* — is lying in wait. The ambush was posted before the summons, before the warning, before the first dispute in Mecca. This pre-existence is doctrine, reported also in the register of readiness: *“Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers”* (3:131) — *prepared*, *uʿiddat*, the same finished-state word that introduces the Garden (3:133). The two destinations stand built; the procession of the Day files into what is already standing.

The age-old question of *when* the two abodes were made finds here its sūrah-local answer: whenever — *they already await*. The sūrah is not interested in the theology-debate; its pastoral point is sharper: nothing remains to be *prepared* for the disbeliever's doom except the disbeliever's arrival. The ambush is already staffed — time is not building the case against them; time is only walking them toward it.

**Ambush-Theology: The Old Enmity Made Visible**

The hunter-image also completes a theme the Book has kept warming: hostility between the Fire and the sacred destiny of man. The enemy that waylaid Adam at the tree now has its own place of ambush; the plan that began with a whisper in a garden ends — for the transgressors — with a station-master's noose in an unlit ravine. The mirṣād is Satan's project, completed as an address.

But ambush-theology preaches its own consolation, too: for the God-wary, the ambusher is posted *not against them*. Watching stations do not trouble travellers who are not the quarry. The very people the mirṣād awaits — the *ṭāghīn*, the excess-runners — are named in the following verse; and for the other company, another prepared-place has its own verse coming (78:31). The ambush's existence is selective doom — the prey knows itself; and the sūrah's whole interrogation was issued precisely so that no reader would remain, unknowing, on the prey-path.

**Waiting as Greater Dread Than Arrival**

The power of *mirṣād* over any mere statement of location is its tempo: waiting, watching, *for ages* (78:23). Horror amplified by foreknowledge is the conventional wisdom of all literature of doom, but here the foreknowledge belongs to the place, not the hunted: the Fire already knows its people; the only ignorant party is the traveller. The verse's mercy hides right inside its terror: a warning delivered *this early* can still reroute the file.

Indeed, the sūrah's entire arrangement — exposé of the ambush before anyone marches — is sovereign generosity: kings do not ordinarily telegraph their ambushes to the enemy. The advertised ambush is God's un-ambush: the station's coordinates are published, the quarry-groups named, the alternative address printed three verses below. An ambush so announced is, in truth, a signpost; only determined prey keeps walking.

**The Tenancy's Last Landmark**

Within the sūrah's architecture, verse twenty-one is where the world's removal ends and the sorting begins: roof opened, mountains shimmered (78:19-20), and then — the first standing structure in the world-to-come is the ambush-station. The mirṣād is, starkly, the first durable landmark of the hereafter's landscape — the building that rises at the end of the flattened plain, prior to the garden-report.

Its positioning in the two-destination economy also has pedagogy: hell is described first (78:21-30) and the garden after (78:31-36), so that the terror can do its work before the hope perfumes the ear; the sequence matches the sūrah's own earlier couplet — Fire described long (77:28-40 in the sister-sūrah), garden brief and bright. Preaching to the unpersuaded requires dread's full paragraph first. The flight from the ambush must outrun, in the hearer, the saunter toward the garden — and for that, the Fire needed ten full verses before the Garden could receive any.

**A Home Readied for the Oversteppers**

The ambush's target-class is named immediately after: the oversteppers, the ṭāghīn — those who crossed every boundary laid down in the furnished world for them. The mirṣād waits specifically for them — for boundary-crossers by profession, whose whole way of being was *excess*: crossed limits of God, of decency, of gratitude. The ambush watches, because the transgressing pattern it awaits is by habit straight-ahead: men who have crossed every boundary already will not keep from crossing the last one.

The verse thus works as the sūrah's own sentinel — mirṣād posted at the gate of the Hell-block itself, warning the reciter: beyond this point lies the inventory of the home of the heavy-footed, their drink and their ages and their verdicts. Any soul still on the road has the station-coordinates in hand. The sūrah now walks slowly through the ambusher's lodging, room by room, as if leaving no one the excuse of not knowing what waits there.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:22

> as a home for the transgressors,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Maʾāb: The Address That Takes Them In**

The ambush has a postal code: *lil-ṭāghīna maʾābā* — for the transgressors, a place of return, an abode. *Maʾāb* derives from *āba* — to come back, to resort to — and it names a dwelling by its function of *receiving-returners*. The word is haunting for its homeliness: hell is here called a *home-coming*, the address people come back to, as though the march on the plain had been a long way *home* — home to where their file, all along, said they belonged.

The home-word's irony then deepens: the transgressors spent their world fleeing every summons-home of God — prayer-calls left unanswered, scripture-calls unread — and the destination that finally takes them in names itself by the return they refused elsewhere. *Maʾāb* carries also resort-senses: the lodging one stays at, the camp one falls back to. It is the last lodging of people whose whole record of movement was *away*, on every offered road, from the only true Resort.

**Al-Ṭāghīn: The Class-Name From the Flood-Word**

Their designation is drawn from hydrology: *ṭāghīn*, plural of *ṭāghī*, from *ṭughyān* — the swelling of water past its banks, flooding past its channel. The transgressor, in the Qur'an's image, is overflow-being: the self exceeding its created limits, poured out beyond the boundaries God set around it. From the same root comes the idol-name *al-ṭāghūt* — the transgressing object-of-worship. The flood-word names the deep grammar of their sin: injustice as boundary-crossing, pride as depth exceeded.

The flood-image is also precise about the social form of their sin: floods do not merely overtop; they overtop *and carry off* — the transgressor's excess drags others in its current, sweeps the little channels of the weak. Nations drowned this way before: the Book's drowned flood-nation of Noah's day was overwhelmed by actual water as the mirror of its own overflowing — ṭughyān answered by ṭughyān — a lesson the sūrah's first hearers knew in their own tribal chronicles.

**Why the Fire Has a Class-Name Already**

That hell receives *the ṭāghīn* by name is one of the sūrah's telling clues about the Day's operations: the filing at the destination is already fully categorized. The abode does not receive vague sinners, nor mixed crowds awaiting triage in its courts: it opens its mouth to the *class*, under their class-name. The judgment has, in effect, already happened by the time the reception begins — the procession companies (78:18) arrive at doors that know them.

Class-names are, in the Book's filing, only ever collections of *verified file-patterns*: transgressing is a repeat-offence category, not an incident-report. God does not classify by slip but by posture — the ṭāghī are those whose excess was their structure. The translation's phrase *for the transgressors* preserves the specialization: the abode is *ṭāghīn*-configured; its heat, its drink, its ages-provision all matched to the overflow-natures it houses.

**The Two Addresses of the Sūrah Stand Drawn**

With this verse, the sūrah's double-iconography is complete: *ṭāghīn* here (78:22), *muttaqīn* there (78:31) — transgressors toward one abode, the God-wary toward another. The Book's dyads always preach: the classes are not arbitrary circles but opposite postures, both available to every member of the audience for the length of the interval. The reader's every fresh day is a fresh enrollment opportunity — the class-names sit open even as the abodes stand ready.

The dyad also marks the sūrah's honesty: it does not only threaten. The same care that describes the ambush's lodging describes the other lodging — so the verse's real function in the reciter's ear is to present a fork with addresses posted at both ends. No denier of the momentous news can, after 78:22 and 78:31, claim lack of clarity about the deuxitous map. The ambush watches onward only for those who decide, knowing all this, to walk in its direction.

**Crossing Back: The Boundaries Were Mercy**

If transgression is boundary-crossing, then the revealed boundaries themselves were protective mercies — river-banks, not prison-bars: the banks that keep a river useful and alive. The ṭāghī treated mercy-banks as tyranny-walls; the final address thus completes a symmetry: those who could not abide banks are now contained, ages-long, in the *unbankable* place (78:23) — in a flood with no land to overflow onto.

The pastoral word for all who hear: every limit God posts is a posting on someone's behalf; prayer-hours, appetite-banks, speech-banks, wealth-banks — all shoreline, not wall. The sūrah's parade of furnishings earlier taught the world's graced order; this verse teaches the congruent truth that overflow ruins: excess in any channel — profit, appetite, pride-of-state — is the early phase of the *ṭāghī*-trajectory, homeward-bound for a home fitted for exactly that condition, age upon age.

**A Lodging Prepared Before Its Tenants**

The furnishing-thread that began at the earth-bed ends, in the darker direction, here: *maʾāb* — a readied lodging — answers *mihād*, the readied earth-bed (78:6). Two prepared dwellings for man's two stays: the smooth lay for the brief sojourn, the resort-home for the long one. God prepared the first without consulting the tenant and the second *equally* without consulting him; and between the two preparations stands exactly one variable: the tenant's own works.

The sūrah will now move inside the lodging and begin its tour of the interior — the ages, the tasting, the two drinks, the matching verdict, the charge-sheet, the archive and the increase-sentence — ten verses of tenancy in all, before the other prepared lodging receives its six bright ones. The ambush has opened its door; the class-name is understandable to every reciting generation; and every reciting generation is still at liberty, while the street days last, not to join its tenancy. A lodging already furnished is a warning already issued: the resort does not need building once the overflow has been crossing its banks, only arriving at — and arrival is what the partnership of striving and delay quietly books.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:23

> where they will remain for ˹endless˺ ages.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lābithīn: They Settle In**

The tour begins with duration, and the first word is the terrible one: *lābithīna fīhā aḥqābā* — abiding in it for ages. *Lābithīn*, from *labitha*, is the verb of staying-put, of settled remaining — the vocabulary of habitation, not of passing through. The transgressors are not imagined as briefly processed or temporarily detained: they take up residence. The verse's tone is one of paperwork finality — inhabitants, not visitors; the ambush's captives are installed.

The contrast with the world's tenancy is immediate: in the smooth-laid earth they were lodgers on a short lease, restless with plans, and they died still scheming. In the maʾāb, they are settled at last — given exactly the permanence their vanity always assumed it had. The transgressors' dream of durable establishment is granted and fulfilled — in the one address where establishment is the punishers' instrument, since nothing ends the punishment like impermanence does everywhere else.

**Aḥqāb: Ages Stacked, Not a Single Number**

The duration-noun the verse chooses is a strange and heavy one: *aḥqāb*, plural of *ḥuqb* or *ḥuqba* — long stretches, ages. Not *forever* stated bluntly, but *ages*, compounded and pluralized — durations stacked upon durations, eras upon eras. The early commentators are preserved explaining scale: some held each ḥuqba to be a great age — and some reports speak of each as eighty years, the year itself counted at a thousand — while others held the term open: ages with no final term, one after another endlessly.

The theological precision inside the plural matters: the punishment's duration arrives not as one timeless verdict but as *successions* — even at that Day, time (or its answering structure) remains countable, tracked, age upon age. The judgement-content of aḥqāb is exactly this: however much the captive counts, there is always another age ahead — the arithmetic of despair, the anti-calendar, where every completed era renews the prospect of the next identical era.

**Ages Against Their Little While**

The sūrah's own time-economy converges here. The transgressors' duration-trust in the world was *qalīlan* — the little while of enjoyment that God's promise kept annotating; they lived as though the little while were the era. The mirror-verdict of aḥqāb is scale-justice: those who refused to *expect* a reckoning during their short days will now measure *ages*, age upon age, in which expectation is the only content of experience. Time, the one mercy whose cessation made worldly pain survivable, is exactly what is extended past all hope in the abode.

Elsewhere the Book states the closure explicitly: no death there to end it, no lightening of sentence — *“Neither will it be decreed for them to die, nor will its torment ever be lightened for them”* (35:36). And in yet another register, the wish that the punishment be revoked is granted only refusal — *“Our Lord! Remove us from it!”* — with only the sentence's own stability as answer. The ages-barrier is impassable; the settled residence has no forwarding address within it.

**The Endlessness Debate, Handled With Open Eyes**

The community of interpretation has always handled this verse inside a wider framework of God's freedom: for the people of pure denial, the consensus holds the ages endless; and yet the Book itself has seeded the hope-doctrine that God *increases what He wills* and pardons what He wills — and the authentic stream of tradition records exits: the Prophet's own intercession leading to the removal, from the very Fire, of whoever carries *faith equal to a mustard seed*, who is then thrown into the River of Life and grows again like a seed by a rainwater stream (Saḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6560).

The two horizons must be held as the Book holds them: verse twenty-three states the sentence as standing, ages upon ages; and the wider economy reserves to God Who is the absolute Pardoner. The ṭāghīn of this verse — the professional transgressors — are not the marginal believers of the mustard-seed report; the sentence states their case, with the ages stacked as stated, and all hope here belongs earlier, on the side of the street with its calls — exactly where the sūrah has been sounding them since verse one.

**Duration as the Fire's Primary Instrument**

Why begin hell's inventory with duration before its drinks and verdicts? Because duration is the terror that unites all the others: pain age-stacked becomes a different metaphysical object than pain-at-an-hour; the mind can endure any *when* it can count toward, but the aḥqāb abolish counting-toward. The verse's order preaches this precisely: the worst thing about the ambush's lodging is not first its heat but its *permanence* — and only after permanence is established do the appurtenances add their detail.

The worshipping imagination takes the same lesson in the other direction: the duration doctrine dignifies every hour of the warning-interval. Whatever repents, believes and works *during* the little while accumulates its wages across cycles that will themselves never end — for the other lodging has its own ages, with the direction reversed, a permanence no one will ever want revoked (78:31-36). The stacked ages of the maʾāb are the dark shadow of a duration-doctrine whose bright side — a Garden without exit — is promised in the same Book to the God-wary.

**Inside the Lodging: What the Inhabitants Will Taste**

The tour continues with the senses. Permanence assigned, the verse next strips the two merciest stations of bodily existence — coolness and drink — the two things desert existence ran entirely on. The negation is total, addressed not as metaphor but as negation of *taste*: the intimate bodily verb of sensing is used for the deprivation itself, giving the starvation of the senses its grammar.

The sūrah is walking word by word now, and slowly: age upon age, then thirst upon thirst, drink by drink. The register of the account is unhurried — as if the voice describing the place were pronouncing a leisurely, complete sentence over the class whose whole world was *qalīlan*, a little while, because the sentence of the ages requires exactly the opposite of haste. What remains un-tasted in their ages will be named first; what is left to them to taste comes after, in the exception-clause.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:24

> There they will not taste any coolness or drink,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words, Two Lifelines Cut**

The interior tour reaches the senses: *lā yadhūqūna fīhā bardā wa-lā sharābā* — they taste therein neither coolness nor drink. Two negations, two lifelines: *bard* — cooling, the relief of heat, the touch of what soothes; and *sharāb* — drink-in-general, the elemental answer to thirst. For people whose land made these two words the condition of survival, the verse names the withholding of exactly what Arabia prayed for. The ambush's lodging offers neither.

The listing is a bare-minimum catalogue: not luxuries denied, but the *essentials* — a place whose climate is never cool and whose fluids never salve. Paradise's paired promises are precisely climate-governed and drink-governed (78:31-36); hell's mirroring negation is the same two dimensions, voided. The Book keeps its two houses in strict architectural rhyme: everything the Garden-household multiplies, the maʾāb strikes from inventory.

**Why Taste Is the Chosen Verb**

*Yadhūqūn* — they taste — is the deliberate verb of the Fire-passages: the sensory-bodily word, innermost of the knowledge-words. The punishment is not merely *present*; it is *present to the subject at the root of sensation* — heat in the throat, burning at the skin, tastelessness for the cool that never comes. The negation *lā yadhūqūna* uses the same verb: deprivation itself is textured, felt, experiential — a positive gap registered continuously, ages-long, by the faculty that exists to receive mercies.

The taste-verb also rules out all distancing: no account of hell in this Book is a spectator's report from outside experience. Whatever coolness one has known in life — breeze, water, shade — the verse says the inhabitants of the lodging will know its *absence* with exactly the same intimacy with which one knows these comforts' presence now. The two sensory poles of desert mercy become the two felt poles of the counter-mercy, permanent.

**The Two Mercies' Double Exposure**

What exactly is denied unfolds in both clauses. *Bard* is the cooling relief — not merely the absence of burning but the positive touch of what eases: cool shade, cool cloth, cool wind, cool water against heat-flushed skin; all denied. *Sharāb* is the fluid-category itself: whatever one drinks; all denied. The first negation is against the body's outside, the second against its inside — and together they mark hell as the place where the two founding-humiliations of the body (heat without, thirst within) are raised into permanent conditions.

And the negations do not even complete the sentence: an exception-clause is already loaded. What *will* be available for drinking and tasting arrives in verse 25 — and the Book's audiences learned early that the exceptions of hell are worse than its defaults: the lodging has drinks after all, but they are not drinks; they are the getting-drunk of the punishment itself, water given the temperature of rage and fluid given the colour of the wounds of its people.

**The World Where Coolness Was Free**

The verse lands hardest on habitual thought: in the world, bard and sharāb arrived unearned: shade was free to the scoffer, water drunk without remembering. The sūrah's early exhibits — pressed clouds, pouring water (78:14) — are precisely the mercies now revoked in this verse; the same Book stating *We sent down water* now states *they will not taste drink*: ministry and austere withdrawal spoken by one Voice. The accounts of provision remain in the audit; the flowing has simply changed destination.

The sensory memory of the world thereby becomes the lodging's first instrument: the inhabitants have tasted coolness; they know precisely what nothing means. The verse's description, like the whole hell-block, is built less from exotic contents than from *familiar absences* — coolness known and denied, drink known and denied — because absence-of-known is intelligible where strange torments would be abstract. The apophatic method of hell is the horror of known mercies gone.

**The Garden's Climate Ban as the Counterlamp**

The opposite household's climate-terms are held nearby for comparison: its inhabitants recline *“never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold”* (76:13) — neither extreme admitted into the Garden's air; the two poisons of climate drawn off there, the two essences drawn off here. Paired, the verses preach the sūrah's kindness-doctrine precisely: peace lies in moderation and provision; torture lies in the negation of moderations and the extremes that rush in to fill them.

Every verse of the hell-block therefore keeps the garden-block glowing by echo ahead: *not taste coolness* is answered by shaded couches; *not drink* is answered by the full cup, diḥāq, diḥāq-filled to the brim (78:34). The Book's symmetry-architecture preaches with the reciter's own memory — the reciter who hears one room cannot but measure it against the other. The two destinations rhyme in every verse so that choosing can proceed by rhymes.

**The Exception That Is the Menu**

The next verse gives the exception-clause the full cruelty of its small relief: there will be something after all to put to the lips — boiling water, and the murk-fluid named *ghassāq*. The negation, it turns out, was total only regarding mercies; the lodging has its own beverage-list, ready now to be named — the drink-list of a household kept in torture, where the very exception issues from the punishment's inventory.

The pause between the two verses is the ear's most chilling half-second in the sūrah: the flow of mercy withheld, then the sound of its allowance — and the allowance arrives *worse than the denial*, for the lodging's drinks are not drinks. The tour continues: the beverages of the ambushed household, named and matched to the transgressors' file, in the verse that follows — and then the seal-word that closes the inventory, *fitting*, in the verse after that.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:25

> except boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception Clause: A Beverage-List of Torture**

The negation now receives its exception: *illā ḥamīman wa-ghassāqā* — except scalding water and ghassāq. The lodging, after all, has its drinks: two of them, and both are instruments. *Al-ḥamīm* is the water brought to ultimate heat — the fluid that can no longer be called refreshment, because possessors of it find it functions as fire made pourable. The allowance of drink, in this exception, is only the delivery-form of further torment: thirst answered by the one liquid that re-ignites it.

The Book keeps this same menu consistent across its hell-passages, establishing the doctrine that the Fire's drinks are themselves from the fire: *“Let them then taste this: boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus”* (38:57) states the identical pairing in the sūrah of Ṣād; and another passage describes the mechanics of what such drink does to the drinker: *“made to drink boiling water that tears their bowels”* (47:15). The lodging's meal passes through, and the passage itself is described anatomically — its entrails make the proof of the menu.

**Ghassāq: The Verse's Murkiest Word**

The second fluid needs the commentary-tradition's full attention: *ghassāq*, from *ghasaq* — intense darkness, the deep dusk. The classical reports register, in the main, two readings. In one, it is the gatherate flowing ooze of the people of the Fire — their wound-discharge, sweat, tears and pus — a liquid definition of the place's own population, served back to them; so Ibn Kathīr transmits in his commentary. In the other, it names an unbearably cold, murky liquid — the zenith of cold as ḥamīm is the zenith of heat — serving the fire-tenant the opposite extreme in the same cup.

Some early commentators kept both: the punishment engineered of the two poles, the scorching and the freezing-dark; the word itself, carrying darkness in its root, supports the murk-and-cold register. And the verse's parallel, 38:57, joins it in either interpretation. What is certain across the reports is the convergence: the endowed fluid is no drink; whichever gloss is taken, *ghassāq* names a beverage that functions entirely as the furthering of the punishment — the lodging's cellar held for its people.

**A Place of Extremes, Against the House of Balance**

The verse yields the deepest architectural insight into the Fire yet: hell is not merely hot — it is *extreme*, the place of the poles, where every moderated comfort has been drawn off and only the spectrum's ends remain. The Fire scalds; its cold alternative also burns; the drinks are the poles poured into a cup. The commentators of the tradition, drawing the contrast, observe that this is exactly the inverse of the Garden's condition, whose every description is of *temperate* plenty: no sun-scorch there, no frost (76:13); and for its drink, *“a drink ˹of pure wine˺ from a flowing stream, that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* (56:18-19).

Balance — blessedness; extremes — punishment. The verse's murk-word thus contains an entire theology of the Two Houses: the same created universe whose worldly mercy-structure is all moderation (day and night apportioned, heat and cold alternated) is at the last Day shown resolved into its two final samples: one house at perfect median, one house at both poles. The interval, the world, is where the poles are held apart by God for tenants still choosing their house.

**The Cup of the Arrogant, Answered**

There is also mirror-retribution in the beverage-list. The transgressors' social signature included drink-pride — the wine-cups of the elite assemblies, the boasting over shared bowls; and the lodging sets its cups accordingly: ḥamīm and ghassāq, passed at the maʾāb's tables with the same hospitality-language, now filled from the cellar of hell. The drinker's culture of Mecca's clubs is answered by the drinker's culture of the abode: same gesture, same rituals of passing, new contents measured to the old company's file (78:26).

Nor is it merely social metaphor: the Book's own account of the transaction runs through 47:15 — the righteous in a scenery of rivers, including a *river* of wine delightful to its drinkers, beside the transgressor with his one tax-cup of scalding water that cuts through. The rivers and the cup stand at opposite ends of the same leaflet; the mockers mocked when the first household was described; at the lodging, the mockers drink. The parallel stands complete with the sūrah's own garden-block (78:34): the full cup, the pure cup — there, and the pole-poured cup here.

**Whose Drinks, and for How Long?**

A grammarian's question that occupied the commentators also hangs on this verse: does *therein* (78:24) point to the Fire itself, or to the *ages* of the verse just before? If the negation scopes the ages, the dread reading shifts: ages pass with nothing but ḥamīm and ghassāq — and then *other* ages, with still other punishments following. On the second reading, the two fluids are simply the lodging's perpetual supply, forever. The first makes the drinks witnesses of succession — punishment renewing its kinds down the eras; the second makes them fixtures of permanence.

Both readings are held inside the tradition, and the verse strains neither — for the horror of the two is structurally identical: whether these cups alternate with other tortures or stand as the eternal beverage-list, no cup in the lodging ever once relieves. The ambush's household has no mercy-menu of any era; the ages differ in what they add, never by giving relief. That is the invariant all the commentaries agree on, however their grammar divides.

**Two Drinks as the Inventory's Lowest Point**

The tour of hell's interior has travelled duration (78:23), deprivation (78:24) and now the allowance-submenu (78:25) — and the exception-clause stands as the abysmal low-point of the description: it is the moment the hearers first saw that the doomed were being *kept alive to drink it*. The lodging is inhabited, ages-long; and the drinks of the lodging serve the habitation, delivering the torment *through* the lifeline by which life is maintained: what sustains them is also what punishes them, poured through the single opening of the throat.

At the inventory's close the sūrah now places its verdict-word — short, adminstrative, sealing: *fitting*. The fitting-word is all the commentary the verse-list receives, because the tradition's whole doctrine of divine justice is contained in that one adjective, already prepared and waiting at the next verse — the answer to the pity-question every flesh feels at any talk of ḥamīm and ghassāq, stated before the question is asked with the full tranqulity of perfect proportion.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:26

> a fitting reward.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inventory Sealed With One Adjective**

After the ages, the negations and the two drinks, the whole interior is stamped with a concluding formula of just two words: *jazāʾan wifāqā* — a fitting reward, a recompense exactly corresponding. The brevity is the seal. Where the list of furnishings needed verses, the judgement on the whole list needs one attributive phrase — the enormous apparatus of the lodging stands under a single quality-word: fitting. The verse is the accounting office's stamp pressed onto the described building.

*Wifāq*, from the root of *w-f-q* — to match, to be in accord, to tally — names a recompense measured so that it *answers* its object: the cup fits the hand that pours it, the age fits the arrogance that occupied with the world as though it owned time. The lodging described from verse 21 through 25 is not cruel excess but calibrated correspondence; God's seal over the entire description asserts proportion where flesh had already begun to feel revulsion. The sentence also reads almost as a title-line: as though the inventory just completed were handed back signed *fitting* — a whole dwelling of horror filed under the one heading of exact correspondence.

**Fit as the Whole Doctrine of Divine Justice**

The adjective confronts the perennial objection squarely: is any duration-ages punishment *fitting* for acts committed in a little while? The wifāq-word carries the standing answer of the Qur'anic jurisprudence of reward: recompense is computed against real measure — not surface-duration but full weight: the Warning ignored, the signs denied every day of the little while, the self's own confession signed at the end (77:35 in the sister-sūrah), the choices locked in at death.

The objection's premise is also silently exposed: men measure by spans what God measures by *files* — and His file on each transgressor is complete (78:29). The one who matches with full knowledge of every circumstance — mitigations counted in, aggravations counted in — is the only being whose decree of *fitting* can be absolute. Human recoil at the inventory is the recoil of partial files; God's wifāq runs on total ones.

**The Same Stamp Over Both Houses**

The identical formula-word binds the two destinations architecturally: the Garden, too, receives its *jazāʾ* and its full-measure gift (78:36), and the earlier sister-sūrah stated the statutes in matched grammatical sentences — thus We deal with the wicked; thus We reward the good-doers. *Jazāʾan wifāqā* is the dark side of that pairing; fittingness is the one principle that runs under both apparatuses, fire and garden.

The bright side exposes the mercy-slope of the identical principle: for the God-wary, the reward explodes proportion upward — bounties beyond the file, gift folded into reward (78:36) — whereas for the transgressors, proportion is held exact: no gift, precisely the file. God over-fits in only one direction, and that one direction is grace. The wifāq-stamp on the hell-block thereby testifies simultaneously to the generosity of the other lodging: of His gifts, He adds; of His justice's dues, He neither adds nor removes.

**No Injustice: The Book's Centre-Held Claim**

The fitting-word anchors one of the Book's most dogmatic themes: at the Day, no one is wronged. *“So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:7-8) — the atom-floor under all accountability; *“We will set the scales of justice for the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least”* the Book states of the scales; and the verdict-scene's self-aware cry is: *“You will not be wronged at all”* (18:49) — patterns of the same proclamation.

The stamp on hell's inventory is thus the stamp on the whole court: what is served at the lodging is not rage but case-law, not conquest but correspondence. *“So taste, for your deeds — Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation”* (3:182) is the same formula completed one clause further: taste *because* fitting, and fitting *because* just — with justice itself rooted finally in the defect-free file.

**Proportion Gives the Warning Its Latin**

Fittingness is not only defence; it is also the severest possible preaching: destiny as *self-matching*. The lodging is not arbitrarily horrible — its horrors correspond — that is, they are *generated from the files of the habits* of its inhabitants: drink-pride answered with the drinks (78:25), ages-planned answered with ages (78:23), no-expectation-of-audit answered with the completed audit (78:27-29). The lodging is the transgressors' own record, extended into place.

The mirror-structure of recompense, once grasped, converts every reflection on the inventory into practical instruction: read the lodging's features backward, and one is reading the file-patterns that generate them — pride, overflow, heedlessness, rejection. The wifāq-word is the key the sūrah gives its reciters for reading: punishment is biography rendered in architecture; examine the architecture and reverse-engineer the lives.

**Now the File Is Read Aloud**

With the seal stamped, the sūrah turns to the grounds: because they did not *expect* — here comes the cause — and because they rejected every sign. The next two verses are the charge-sheet of the class named at verse 22, and then the great archive-verse of verse 29: the enunciation of why their own file is as complete as God's claim. The fitting-word's full argument unfolds — proportion demonstrated from its evidences rather than left to bare assertion.

The verse's poise between inventory and cause is its final eloquence: the horror-list closed, the grounds following, the archive after — the sūrah's hell-block reads like case-documents in proper order: description of sentence, statement of charges, proof of records, and then — last — the execution-formula in God's own voice, addressed in the second person, in verse 30: Taste. The cause-list begins with the interior charge: the expectation they never maintained.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:27

> For they never expected any reckoning,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Charge: An Expectation They Never Maintained**

The charges begin: *innahum kānū lā yarjūna ḥisābā* — they did not use to expect a reckoning. *Rajāʾ* is expectation — the forward-look faculty — and the verb's negation describes not a philosophical position held in the head but a *missing orientation*, a faculty never exercised: the transgressors lived with no felt relation to any coming audit. Their days ran unwatched; their gains ran uncounted; their sleep came without reflection — expecting nothing past it.

Note how gently, and how tragically, the charge is framed: not *they performed an argument against the audit* but *the audit-expectation was absent* — an organ never developed rather than an opponent fought. And that gentleness is more damning than polemics: the case against them is that *they never looked*. The whole sūrah's evidences — pressed clouds over them, appointed days ahead of them, graves filling around them — passed by a class that simply never brought the future-account into view.

**Rajāʾ: The Word of Hope Turned to the Sense of Awe**

*Rajāʾ* is ordinarily hope — the brightness of anticipated good. Here its absence means withdrawal of *awe*: never reckoning-on reckoning. Some of the early readers gloss the sense as fear — *never feared the reckoning* — and the double valence is the word's own richness: expectation and apprehension ride the same verb, because anticipating the audit is itself the hope-and-fear of the God-wary, working together.

The God-wary of verse 31, by mirror, are defined exactly by maintenance of that expectation: they lived with the account in prospect. The verse thus divides mankind by one diagnostic question alone: does the expectation of reckoning form part of one's ordinary psychology — consulted at the market-voice, at the appetite's whisper, at the signing of every act? Or is the landscape of the mind simply missing it? The class-name ṭāghīn settles onto those for whom it was missing.

**Audit-Expectation as the Human Watchman**

What exactly was lost by lacking that expectation? Everything the world calls self-policing: restraint, foresight of consequence, conscience-machinery before the fact rather than remorse after it. Audit-anticipation is the watchman God installs in the intending self, the faculty that whispers *this will be asked about* before the hand completes its grab. Denying the audit annuls the watchman; the watchman slain, every boundary runs undefended — and the whole anatomy of ṭughyān, overflow, follows naturally.

The interior life without expectation is thus not neutral or merely careless: it is open ground where every appetite operates unwatched. The transgressors' outward acts (denial of signs, cruelty, excess) are v28's charge and the brother-sūrahs' charges; verse 27 names the *interior enabler*: the closed door through which all other faults entered. No expectation of reckoning → no watchman at the gate of the self → flood past every bank → the class-name earned and the abode that is *for the ṭāghīn*.

**Expectation's Absence Is Fitted With Its Mirror-Punishment**

The fitting-word of verse twenty-six stands ready to be applied, and verse twenty-seven carries its first application: those who never expected the audit received the audit-complete — were assigned to the only place in existence where *nothing but* audit-expectation remains: expectation of the next age, the next drink, the next re-flaring. The abode is a forced eternity of forward-looking at last: rajāʾ perverted into dread of the continuing.

At the world's end, expectation is the one faculty that cannot be withdrawn: every inhabitant of the ages is defined by looking forward — learned too late, maintained involuntarily, on the wrong side of the door. The inverse psychology in hell mirrors the inverse psychology of the garden-dwellers, whose expectation was also perfected — into fulfilled certainty. Punishment and reward both crown expectation; the difference is only which side of *hoped* the crown sits upon.

**No Expectation: A Verdict on Wealth-Cultures**

The verse's pastoral targeting is precise: audit-ignoring flourishes where audit-culture is weakest — and it is weakest wherever the daily occupation is accumulation itself. The Qurayshite merchant-house that counted a hundred measures nightly for profit maintained exactly zero for the end of all measures; their ledgers were immaculate while their hearts' ledger was unopened. The same cultural diagnosis stands: facility with accounts coexisting with refusal of the Account is the signature of stockade-worlds in every century.

The charge lands instead as a meditation for practice: *expectation* must be built. It does not arrive with wealth or with gray age, nor is it inherited with creed; it is the actual daily exercise of imagining one's own small measures within the full measure of the Day. The verse is preaching the repair explicitly: form the habit of the forward-book — audit-expectation as discipline — because its absence was itself the first charge presented at the lodging.

**The Second Charge Follows: The Signs Themselves**

From the interior absence the sūrah turns to the exterior act: the signs were present, and they were rejected — totally rejected, as verse twenty-eight's wording will insist by grammar as well as by fact. The two charges are a matched pair: what they never held in prospect (the audit) and what they put away in the present (the signs) — inside and outside, posture and deed — the charge-sheet completed in one dual statement.

The sequence ends at the archive: within three verses, the lodging's charges read, the Fire has produced what no earthly defendant expects at his trial — complete documentation, *everything* numbered (78:29). The no-expectation charge and the archive verse stand deliberately back to front: they did not expect because they did not expect to be numbered; the verse of the numbered file exposes that their whole no-expectation strategy depended on being unnumbered — a hope that only ever held up in a world where no one was counting. God was counting all along.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:28

> and totally rejected Our signs.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Outward Charge: Rejection Proclaimed by Grammar**

Charge two: *wa-kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā kidhdhābā* — and they rejected Our signs with utter rejection. Arabic nails the totality grammatically: the internal-object form (*kidhdhābā* as the absolute-object of the same root) is the language's instrument for intensifying a verb through itself — they denied, *denial-ly*, to the limit of the word. The translation's *totally* answers the form exactly: not a case of single acts of denial but of denial as their comprehensive mode toward the sign-economy of God.

The significance of the grammatical doubling is structural: God will not let the charge rest on one refusal or one bad week; He cites the *pattern*. Takdhīb — the very class-word the sister-sūrah tolled ten times over (the mukadhdhibīn) — is here stamped onto the charge-sheet with its accompanying intensifier: interior expectation had died (78:27); outwardly, every sign that arrived was met with a No.

**Āyātinā: Exactly the Signs the Sūrah Showed**

The object is intimate: *Our signs* — āyātinā — and the possessive stresses the claim of the signs to the Rejectors' attention: His signs, sent and set among them. But the pronoun-scope also snaps the charge back onto the sūrah's own exhibition — the signs paraded from verse 6 to verse 16 are *āyāt*: earth-laid, pegs-driven, pair-making, sleep-severing, night-clothing, day-opening, storeys-built, lamp-lit, clouds-pressed, water-poured, grain-raised, orchards-intertwined. Verse 28 is the hell-block looking back at the evidence-block and noting: *those* were the signs, and the rejection reached them all.

And beyond the furnishing-signs stand the recitation-signs themselves: the sūrah they were hearing — its very verses — is also covered by āyātinā. The charge lists the rejection of the very signs currently being recited — a meta-inclusion: God charges them, within the recited signs, of rejecting recited signs. The verse confesses the full plight of the preaching: the instruments of proof are the instruments denied — and it is the recitation itself that delivers the indictment.

**The Two-Column File Stands Complete**

Verses 27 and 28 compose the complete charge-sheet. Column one: interior — no expectation of reckoning (the watchman absent); column two: exterior — rejection of every sign (the available guidance refused). The two columns form the full anatomy of the class: to reject outwardly the signs while going inwardly unexpectant of any account is the double defect that makes the transgressor: absent watchman within, barred doors against guidance without.

Charge-sheets, in God's jurisprudential style, are characteristically short. Two charges stand here; the first Day's whole case against the ṭāghīn runs on these two. The economy is deliberate: conviction of mankind does not depend on itemized millions of failures but on two governing postures from which all failures stream — pride's posture (no expectation of audit) and denial's posture (rejection of signs). God's case-law cites the root, not the branches: the twofold root, once established, accounts for every item in the file numbered at verse 29.

**Total Rejection and the Social Manufacture of Denial**

The intensifier also records a social fact from the sūrah-world: rejection by this community was performed through every instrument of denial available to a literate culture: mockery in assemblies, arguments in councils, taunts at the reciter, demands for alternative signs, appeals to the ways of the fathers. The kidhdhāb-form compresses them all — *in every manner you denied* — as one embrace-word over the varied catalogue of Meccan tactics.

Totality of manner matched totality of object: nothing of God's sign-making was credited: not the recitation's challenge (produce something like it), not the cosmos' exhibits, not the Prophet's known truthfulness, not the death-scenes of deniers, not their own rescue-adventures (as the Book elsewhere counts them: at sea they call sincerely, then return to denial). Rejection had become their total posture toward the unseen — so the verse names it with the totalizing object: denial *comprehensively*.

**At the Lodging, the Denial Itself Is Fitted**

Wifāq reads this charge too: to the class who rejected every communication that God sent, the fitting abode is the place where all further addresses arrive only as verdicts. No call goes in there but the summons that executes: the taste-command of verse 30; the interrogating calls of its keepers (39:71); the plea-deflecting pronouncements of its management (43:77: *they will cry: O Mâlik! Let your Lord finish us! He will answer: You are here to stay*). Communications continue at the lodging, but their genre has become verdict-only — because the addressees had, in their kidhdhāb-season, communicated one total rejection to every genre of guidance.

Even the drink becomes confirmation: they rejected the tasting-gifts, the merciest mercies — the cool water, the shade — so the lodging's drinks answer with the mirror-fluids (78:25). Rejection's oceanic totality is mirrored in reply by the abode's totality: nothing in the lodging is un-torment; nothing there is un-after-their-denial. The kidhdhāb-object thus haunts every room of the maʾāb, as though the place itself were the final shape of their rejections, standing on the ground they had earned for it.

**Between the Charges and the Archive**

With the two charges entered, the file lacks only one thing before sentence-execution: the documentation — and verse 29 will produce it in the strongest possible phrase: *everything* accounted, number by number, in writing. The four-verse nesting — sentence-descriptor (26), charges (27–28), archive (29), execution (30) — composes a complete specification of the court of the Day, arranged so that no listening person can miss that the court works from evidence, files, and records, rather than from heavenly rage.

Indeed the archive-verse is the verse between the charges and the taste-command — *the record standing between charge and sentence* — and this placement is itself the doctrine: no judgment in God's court runs without the record. The next verse is therefore the most terrifying verse of the block for the practised modern unbeliever: not the flames, not the drinks, but — We have recorded *everything* — because everything is the one word no defendant in the world hopes to hear from the prosecuting side.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:29

> And We have everything recorded precisely.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kullu Shayʾ: Nothing Outside the Ledger**

Between charges and sentence, God produces the file: *wa-kulla shayʾin aḥṣaynāhu kitābā* — and everything, We have numbered it, recorded. *Kullu shayʾ* is the unbounded formula — every thing — the same phrase the Book reserves for the totality of divine knowledge and the bounds of creation; applied now to the deeds of creatures, it means: nothing done has fallen outside the record. The secret and the public, the word and the gesture, the intention matured and the glance half-formed: everything, encompassed in a count.

The terror of the phrase for the defendant-class is exactly their hope destroyed. Verse 27's charge was *they never expected a reckoning* — and verse 29 states the infrastructure of what they never expected: an archive that had been filling on their case from the first breath they spent. The no-expectation strategy was a bet on being unremembered; *everything recorded* overturns the bet with one administrative word. The file existed; the file was full; the Day is its audit.

**Aḥṣaynāhu: Enumeration, Not Approximation**

The verb *aḥṣā* is counting-terminology — to number exhaustively, to tally until nothing remains outside the enumeration. God's record is not a narrative summary with the small items elided; it is a numbered inventory where every entry has its own place. The Book's own cry on the Day confirms the granularity: *“What is this record that leaves no deed, small or great, unaccounted for!”* (18:49) — the small included, the great included — with the observed content inducing the unbeliever's famous exclamation of dismay at the report.

Enumeration also dignifies the good half of the record with equal exactness: the iḥṣāʾ that totals the transgressor's whole file is the same iḥṣāʾ that keeps every secret kindness of the God-wary, every atom's weight of good (99:7-8). Counting is not the instrument of wrath; it is the instrument of *justice*, run in both directions, with no favorites and no forgettings. The verb's use in a hell-context is the specialized dark side of a universal promise: God counts *everything* — which means the overlooked good of the weakest worshipper is as permanently numbered as the loud sins of the proud.

**Kitābā: In Writing, Where Nothing Shifts**

The mode-noun *kitābā* — recorded, in writing, by the book — completes the machinery: the count is not held in memory's possible-flux but *written down*, fixed, in archive-form. The description of the Day's procedure elsewhere completes the scene: the angel-scribes who write in the world, the individual volume handed to each soul: *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺”* (39:69); then the reading-out addressed personally — *“Read your record! Your own soul is sufficient to take account of you this day”* (17:13-14) — the archive opened under the owner's own fingers.

Written language removes every ambiguity-of-flux: words can be argued, but a record is *entered*. The fire-block's ordering — charges, then written count, then execution-command — is God's own dramatization of due process at the scale of the eternal: not arbitrary power, but *recorded justice executed by command*. The form of the file gives the lie forever to the suspicion of invention, embellishment or tribunal irregularity at the Day: the charges stand written from the very moments of the deeds, by the appointed writers.

**The Surveillance That Is Also guardianship**

The unbounded record preaches in both directions, and the elders of the tradition always read it both ways. To the transgressor, it is the removal of the fantasy of privacy; to the God-wary, it is the guarantee that nothing beautiful is ever lost. The same archive that convicts the ṭāghīn preserves the widow's two coins and the hidden tears — *no deed, small or great* is outside it. The knowledge that one is fully counted is terror only to a certain class; it is, to another, the deepest consolation available in the world.

And there is a third direction — the correction of living-critics who caricature the divine attentiveness as burden: the uncounted life is the unattended life. To be fully recorded is to be fully *taken seriously*; the Book's doctrine is that no breath of any creature is lost as meaningless — the count is itself the dignity of being answerable. God's creatures matter enough to be numbered, and the counter-claim — a God too distant to count — is, in this theology, not comfort but dismissal of every creature as beneath notice.

**The Strategic Placement: Between Charge and Sentence**

The verse's structural position in the block is deliberate: after statement of the fittingness and the two charges, before the execution-command. That order — correspondence, cause, archive, sentence — maps a complete juridical movement whose fixedness preaches the lesson: God judges from records, not from presentation; from written tallies, not from argument-combat. Nothing at the Day will hinge on courtroom eloquence; everything hinges on what stands written.

The condemned's famous wish — *would that I had* — targets precisely the archive-verse's territory: if only entries could be amended, added to, erased. But the record's nature is definitive: the entered stands entered. Hence the interval-mercy shouted by the whole scripture: the record is *editable now* — the only edition-window there will ever be is the remaining stretch of the interval — and the avenue of amendment is repentance, the appointed mercy by which entries are replaced before the ledger's closing. The archive-verse at the Day reads all it records; the archive, this side of the Day, still receives revisions from the trembling.

**The Verse After: The Executor's Voice**

With correspondence stated, charges entered, and archive exhibited, what remains is the sentence in the second person — God's own voice implementing the inventory so carefully documented: a command-form addressed direct to the inhabitants, without angel intermediary naming itself: the taste-command, whose content is escalation itself. The full apparatus of the court has been shown so that the final word of the block can be understood as what it is — not a caprice of the court but the case's completed course: Taste.

The four-verse prosecution (26–30) thus stands as one of the Book's most compressed judicial liturgies: fittingness, charges, record, command. Nothing in the arrangement is redundancy; each element abolishes one specific refuge of the accused: correspondence abolishes *excess* as a plea, charges abolish *the uncharged*, the archive abolishes *the unrecorded*, and the final command abolishes the last refuge — that the court would not, in the end, actually execute. It will.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:30

> ˹So the deniers will be told,˺ “Taste ˹the punishment˺, for all you will get from Us is more torment.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taste: The Case's Final Word Is a Command**

The block closes on God's own voice: *fa-dhūqū fa-lan nazīdakum illā ʿadhābā* — So taste! For We shall not increase you except in torment. The imperative arrives after sentences of pure description — ambush, home, ages, drinks, fitting, charges, archive — and the change of register is itself part of execution: the court's description-phase is closed; what remains is only enactment. The Book's hell-passages ring again and again with this same taste-command, and the consistency is deliberate: punishment in God's record is never narrative alone but concludes in direct address — the file closed, the file enacted.

And the verb's cruelty is precise: taste — back to the sensory verb of verse 24. There, the inhabitants could not taste the two mercies; here, they must taste the only thing on the inventory's real menu: the torment itself, in the register of mouth-and-throat. Dhawq is the Book's chosen sensory-word because this punishment is not an abstract penalty-status; it is experience, experienced forever — the soul tasting and tasting, age after age, what its file compiled.

**The Increase-Clause: The Day's Darkest Sentence**

The attached clause is the darkest sentence in the description of hell anywhere in the Book: *fa-lan nazīdakum illā ʿadhābā* — you will have no increase from Us except torment. Every other economy of God runs on increase of some bounty for its inhabitants: ordinary time brings new days; prayer brings increases of light; the Garden runs on increase upon increase — *“with Us is ˹even˺ more”* (50:35). Here, ontological increase continues as God's rule does not change — *We will increase you* — but the only currency of the increase is the abode's own: more torment.

Commentators across the centuries have preserved, at this verse, a judgement of utter dread: as transmitted through the learned chain (Qatādah, from Abū Ayyūb al-Azdī, from Ibn ʿAmr), a report records: *no verse was revealed that is harder on the people of the Fire than this one*. Preserved in the tafsīrs of Ibn Kathīr and others, the judgement reflects what every reciter feels at the clause: it is hope's mathematical closure. A system in which the only increase is increase of penalty is a system with no gradient of escape remaining anywhere in it — not by diminution, not by abatement, not by plea.

**The Mechanics of Ever-Rising Torment**

How such increase stands the Book details elsewhere, in verses that read as schedules to this sentence. Abatement answered by flare: *“Hell will be their home. Whenever it dies down, We will flare it up for them”* (17:97) — the Fire's own capacity maintained by rekindling, the heat re-fed whenever its level begins to fall. Skin answered by skin: *“Whenever their skin is burnt completely, We will replace it so they will ˹constantly˺ taste the punishment”* (4:56) — endurance itself renewed, so that tasting does not diminish by callus or by neurology's mercy.

Growth-of-form: ages bring fresh varieties of the inventory — the early commentators, glossing the lodging, imagined the successors of the drinks: new torments kept in reserve, following one after another down the ages. Each of these is a mechanics of *lan nazīdakum illā ʿadhābā*: the fire-tended Flaring is increase; the re-skinned tasting is increase; the succession of fluids is increase. The verse is not rhetoric-itself but the *administrative standing order* of the abode, pursuant to which all those mechanisms are operated.

**Why Increase-Only Is the Finished Justice**

The reflection the verse asks of its believers-listeners is not to gloat but to *understand what justice is when completed*. In the world, all economies are mixed — pain and ease alternating, sentences remitting, prices fluctuating: because the interval-world is the world of *choice* and therefore of mixture. The final abodes end choice; ends-of-choice are ends-of-mixture. Increase in hell purely of torment means: from their abode's angle, *every last channel of mitigation has already been fully honored* — in the world, at the warning-tables, at the sign-parades, at the rap-sheet's writing. What remains after total completion of mitigation is precisely an economy of which the only direction is increase, and the only direction of increase is the deserved.

It is also the logical completion of the fitting-word (78:26): increase-only-torment fits the class whose only direction was increase-in-overflow — as they added transgression to transgression in the world's file, the abode increases torment to torment, age on age. The correspondence-measure is the same as their own trajectory, extended: they plotted increase; they inherited increase. The verse's finished justice is chilling precisely because it is *familiar* — the family-likeness of what they built for themselves in life, completed at last with infinite resources and no interference from the world's brakes.

**The Register of the Pronoun-Shock**

Note the addressee-slip in the verse's quoted translation: *you* — the second person — with no earthly addressee in view. The sentence is reported speech, given before the event as though already at the event. God's courtroom command is thus delivered twice: once future-real at the Day, once now-present in recitation — and the rehearsal is the mercy. Every reciter who says these words to himself at prayer is, for a holy second, standing in the execution-line of the abode, reading his own non-membership notice: this command is *not yet for me* — and the grace of hearing it commanded at others while still outside its jurisdiction is what the recitation exists for.

The pronoun-slip also performs the sūrah's narrow-door psychology: the audience that began as *they* (78:1) and entered the procession as *you* (78:18) now hears the lodging's execute-command as *you* again — the recitation training ears to hear all Day-speech as addressed-to-me, in advance, so that today's registration might prevent tomorrow's pronunciation. The verse is terrifying in recitation because it was *meant* to be terrifying in recitation; terror now is the appointed alternative to tasting then.

**At the Block's Edge, the Other Door Begins to Glow**

With the increase-sentence, the hell-block completes: ambush, home, ages, negations, drinks, fitting, charges, archive, command — ten verses without one softening clause. And exactly here the sūrah performs its balancing turn: the very next verse opens with the same emphatic *inna* that opened the ambush — Indeed — but the announced subject changes to the God-wary and their salvation: the prison's mirror-door with gardens behind it.

The hinge is one of the Book's most beautiful moments of stark relief: reader, the *same* Voice that said *We will only increase them in torment* will now announce *salvation* (mafāzā) to another class — and the clause is linguistically open in both directions from the same throne. The next verses linger on what increase runs in the other household: gardens in increase, vineyards, companionship, full cups, and then one enormous gift-review — reward and generous gift from your Lord — where increase from God is all bounty. The weight of the hell-block falls, in one verse, from the scale's dark pan, to be replaced, with overflowing justice, by the light one.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:31

> Indeed, the righteous will have salvation—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror-Opening: The Other Inna**

Across the divide of one stroke the whole register changes: *inna lil-muttaqīna mafāzā* — indeed, for the God-wary, salvation. The particle that opened the hell-block — *inna* (78:21) — is struck again in the identical position, as though the text were deliberately rhyming the two announcements: one Indeed for the ambush, one Indeed for the winning. God's voice speaks both certainty-forms in one grammatical estate — the Book's habit of matching the two great truths at every formal level is nowhere cleaner.

And the rhyme reaches further back into the charge-file: the ṭāghīn-named inhabitants of the ambush are answered by the *muttaqīn* — transgressors matched with God-wary, the two class-names that have been sorting the whole chapter since the charge-sheet (78:22, 78:27-28). Between the two names ran the entire content of the world's probation: a person of the street could migrate camps while the street lasted; at the Day, the names govern two fully-built estates.

**Mafāz: The Word of Winning and of Being Saved**

The reward's heading-word is chosen from the root of victory-and-deliverance: *mafāz*, from *fāza* — to win, to attain safety, to come through. It is the Book's complete-success word: not luxuries or languors but *making it*: winning-out at the Day's deciding, escape and triumph fused into a single noun. The Garden that follows is introduced plainly as *what* the winning consists in — not a theme-park of perks but the actual condition: the deliverance itself so complete that it is expressed in garden-form.

In this way, verse 31 is a sentence that defines before it describes. Before any inventory of reward, the hearer is given the *name* of the promised condition: salvation. The world of the sceptics mocked the promise with images of hedonism; the verse's first word answers the mockery's whole theological distortion: the Garden is not appetite-at-last; it is *removal-from-evil-at-last*: fawz first, fruit after. The place named mafāz is the one address where nothing needs ever again to be fled.

**For Such Honour Let the Workers Work**

The Book sets the same word at the climax of its story of confrontation in Ṣāffāt: *“For such ˹honour˺ all should strive”* (37:61) — mafāz-grade victory named as the proper goal of all striving. The verse at our sūrah's garden-gate announces the same object in its hometown scene: for the God-wary of this contested world, the winning registered as arrival has an address, and it is about to be displayed. The worker's vindication verse and the garden-gate verse state the two ends of one doctrine: strive here; the winning is real, named, and standing.

The mafāz-word also corrects the scales by which striving is judged in the world's hour: the believers of Mecca looked like those who had *lost* — property abandoned, standing surrendered, mockery borne. The pronoun-attached promise reverses the optical illusion produced by the little while: at the Day the visible losers are revealed as the only ones who ever truly won. Worldly loss in the interval was, in the Book's final accounting, the price of mafāz; and the household that holds the mafāz was prepared, long before, hand-in-hand with the ambush itself.

**The Class-Name Finishes Its Work**

*Al-muttaqīn* — those who ware wary of God — completes the chapter's own definition-by-pairing exercise of vv27-31: the transgressors were they who expected no reckoning and rejected the signs (78:27-28); the wary are those who expected the reckoning and received the signs with watchful yeses — the two postures that were open to every Meccan ear from verse 1 onward. The sūrah's charge-sheet of one class and the prize-state of the other are written on one shared axis: expectation of the audit as the mark of taqwā.

The wary-class is generous in its inclusions: not saints only, not prophets only, but the simply-God-fearing — people whose lives were run by the sense that they would be asked. The Book delights in making honest people into the terminal class of salvation, pledging again and again, in the pattern of *“For Allah loves the good-doers”* (5:93), that belief joined to wariness is the whole entrance-requirement; the prerequisites for the mafāz were always the plainest possible disciplines, hewn daily, by plain people, in the plainest conditions.

**Before the Items, the Intended Name**

The verse's dash — the way it ends so that the next verse's list becomes its own content — makes the grammar perform the preaching: the mafāz, when it comes, does not even need an explanatory sentence; the furnishings *are* the explanation. God does not say, 'the salvation is pleasant' or 'the salvation is tremendous'; He names it, stops, and then begins to recite its contents as a flow of raw nominatives: gardens, grapevines, companions, cups. The salvation speaks itself through its furniture.

The telegraphic style that follows (one unverbed catalogue of joy) is itself a literary mercy-offering: the Book is exhibiting the direct, unpredicated solidity of the Garden — its blessings do not depend on sentences about them; they exist like nouns, before any description. A salvation so real that it may be discharged in inventory-language is fitted precisely against the ambush's architecture of deprived negations: there, the inventory ran on extinguished mercies; here, on extant ones.

**A Standing Vindication at the Sūrah's Turning**

For the sūrah's first hearers in the beleaguered Meccan community, verse 31 was the official announcement, within the recitation's own surah, that their strange endurance had a registered upside: their God put the word *salvation* in the Book by name, over their heads, while the street-mockery was still ringing in the alleys. The verse belongs to the chain of consolations that ran through the Meccan years: not riches now, not victory now, but a named condition at the appointed Day — and no one's mockery could un-name it.

The comfort is transferable by design: every later age of small, mocked faith inherits the promise with the name intact — mafāz remains in the Book, unworn, until the Day arrives. And with the name delivered in one breath, the recitation now descends the other side of the door to describe, item by item, the household's furniture: first gardens of a specific earth-borne kind, then companions, then the cup, then the silence — because God's face-to-face gift is best told in the humble vocabulary of what His listeners already knew how to imagine as the best.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:32

> Gardens, vineyards,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inventory of Joy Begins With Earthly Names**

The salvation's furniture arrives as a noun-list: *ḥadāʾiqa wa-aʿnābā* — gardens, grapevines. Notice the Book's chosen vocabulary here: not candle-dripping abstractions, but the best words the audience had for planted delight — ḥadāʾiq, walled lush gardens; aʿnāb, the vine and its clusters. The Garden of the Day speaks in the idiom of the world's gardens because God's kindness, at the description-stage, condescends to the categories of human longing: the hearer is told the winning in words whose worth he already knows in his mouth.

The choice is also an abortion of drab conceptions of the afterlife: what is coming is planted, tended, bearing — a working green, not a pearly waiting-hall of vagueness. The Book's eschatology is insistently horticultural: God sows reward the way a gardener plants, and waits: the believer's works were seeds in the world; the harvest is the garden of the Day. The imagery that told the interrogation's evidence (78:15-16) becomes the imagery of the reward — the same God-growing, now unending.

**Ḥadīqa: The Particular Word for Walled Green**

The noun ḥadīqa is particular in Arabic — not any garden but the dense, water-fed, close-planted one, often walled-round, hedged in heavy palm or vine: the imagery of intensity of green, enclosure for privacy, enclosure for lushness kept in. The Book reserved *jannāt* for the generic gardens of world-and-Garden; *ḥadāʾiq* here carries the further hint of the tended and the held-close: the walled garden, with its inner shade and its concentrated scent of watered soil.

The enclosure-sense answers an unspoken worry of rural folk of every age: would the great place be too vast, too impersonal to belong to? Ḥadāʾiq are not the infinite plain of public geography but the near, beloved, kept parcels — and they are promised in the plural, one for each residence, as the fuller parallel passages detail (two gardens, four gardens). The Garden of the Day is private space worked into the architecture of divinity: God, infinite, assigns His saved household the scale of courtyards.

**Aʿnāb: The Specific Fruit That Crowned the Arab Table**

Among the trees, the single fruit named: the grape. To the addressed desert-economy — for whom the Taif-vine was the summit of orchard-luck, the caravan-delicacy, the grape-press of wealth — the naming of aʿnāb was the naming of electric delight: the grape, that most wastefully-pleasant of fruits, the one that could be dessert or drink or dried-wealth, is planted in the winning household as standard flora.

The Book's worldly promise-chain runs in one unbroken straight line from the nursery-name in the interrogation — *And We placed in it gardens of date-palms and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth* (36:34) — to the winning inventory of this verse: the same pairing, grapes and greenery, earthly and post-earthly. God plants in the Day the exact pairing with which He proved Himself in the world; the proof-item (36:33-35) is thereby delivered as prize — continuity of mercy-items on both sides of the veil, durability the only difference.

**The Chemistry of the Worldly Vine, Perfected Upstairs**

The named grape also hooks one of the Qur'an's dearest arguments: the vine in the world is already a sermon — one wooden creeper producing, out of dust and water, a berry whose juice can be bread-softness or else forbidden-intoxication; and the Book several times names this doubling as deliberate sign-craft. In the winning-inventory, the whole debate withers: the grape of the Garden yields only its unambiguous face — 76-style cups share their wine with no headache in them, no blur at all (56:18-19).

And so the aʿnāb-verse quietly settles the question that must have murmured through the prohibition-years: is the Day-jurisdiction merely ban-oriented? The answer in verse 32's brevity: no — the same God who forbade the world's cup names the grapevines in His own household's planting-plan. God's direction with the plant was never contempt for it, nor temperance as permanent deprivation, but the right jurisdiction for each world: the vine filtered out of harm there, the harm filtered out of the vine in the garden.

**Rapid-Fire Nominals: The Sprint of the List**

The grammar of the garden-block steps fully into its own style at this verse: two nouns, no verb, no qualifier — the famine of syntax itself joy-making. After the ambush's prose of negations (they shall not taste — exception clauses — addendums), the saving-household's description is cathedral-brief: items. The stylistic contrast is itself doctrine-made-literature: hell needed explaining; paradise only needs naming. The saved need nothing argued for them at their home — the garden's reality outruns syntax without exhausting itself.

The interior effect of the rhythm on the reciting ear is notable: a series of accusal-absence, object-cases like water-drops — gardens, vines, companions, cups, silence — each separated by the smallest pauses the recitation permits. The Book teaches the heart by tempo: the ambush was walked-through slowly, dread by dread; the winning is walked-through brightly, gift by gift, like a delighted host opening doors in quick succession — door, next door, next door — until the listener is out of breath before the household is.

**Two Botanical Words Against Ten Fire-Verses**

The garden-block will run six verses against the hell-block's ten — the sūrah's arithmetic deliberately lopsided, and the verse-division teaching its own pastoral doctrine: warning needs length, and promise only barely stops to be tasted. Two botanical nouns at verse 32 open an estate that the believer will pre-live joyfully through decades of difficulty; the hell-block's ten verses are the homework; the garden's verses are the home.

And the botanical specificity is comfort for the plainest reciter: a list a farmer can understand. God has not, at the Day's prize-table, stocked anything that a drinker-of-wells cannot name from his own irrigation-plot dreams — and yet He has stocked *that* beyond comparison, since two words from his own soil-vocabulary now refer to gardens whose planter is God. The list continues: after the gardens and the grapevines, the companions are named, with the adjectives attending them — because blessing without someone to bless-with is not yet mafāz.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:33

> and full-bosomed maidens of equal age,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kawāʿib: The Description the Book Is Unashamed Of**

The companions are named with two adjectives, the first bodily: *kawāʿiba* — full-bosomed, swelling-breasted; *kāʿib*, singular, is the maiden whose form has come to its mature fullness. The Book does not blush at its own biology-language here — the recitation that describes cosmic courts also describes companionships, and at both registers it stays exact. The adjective's register is not leering but stating: perfected forms, not desiccated by age nor unfinished by immaturity, companions at the prime-point of created bodily grace.

The clause belongs to the Book's consistent commitment: desire is God's made-thing and His address does not flee it. The warnings of the sūrah were issued to creatures of appetite; the promise answers the same creatures in the same register of integrity: the Garden is not a discontinuation of human embodied joy but its extraction-from-harm. God, who made the forms of longing in the first place, is their trustworthy Describer at the other address.

**Time-Proofed Forms: The Adjective's Accounting Consequence**

The commentators carry the first adjective one step further: forms at their fullness, *not subjected afterward to the vicissitudes of time* — that is, the point of maturity held permanently. The body in the world is a ledger of depreciation — every prime carried forward into entropy; and the promise of companionship-kāʿib is the reversal of the ledger: the prime described as state, not as phase. The hour the garden's doors open, the entropy-rule is left outside.

The tradition's wider teaching converges here: the people of the Garden altogether, men and women, are re-created to a prime estate. A report recorded in Musnad Aḥmad and elsewhere — its transmission graded variously, with some of the critics noting weakness along its chain — describes the people of the Garden as entered at the age of thirty-three, hairless, beardless, kohl-eyed and at the height of their formedness. The verse speaks of companions hypothetically aged alongside the saved themselves; the report extends what the verse signals: time stopped at the prime, forever.

**Atrāb: The Equality That Makes Companionship**

The second adjective is the social one: *atrāb* — of one age, co-aged, matched in years (singular *tirb*. The promise is generational harmony: no companionship-strife of disparity between the saved person and their companions, no conversation lost across age-gaps, no watchfulness of one side ageing away from the other. Co-ageing in the world was the gift of the peer-years that one only keeps for a while; in the Garden it is made permanent and universal.

The parallel verse in al-Wāqiʿah, founding the same promise with open vocabulary, states the making itself: *“We have created ˹their companions˺ of noble character, making them virgins, loving and of matching age, for the people of the right”* (56:35-38). Note the two moral adjectives around the matched-age one there: *loving*, *devoted* — the companionship's beauty is not attributed to symmetry alone, but to love built into it, character made for accord. Co-aged + loving + full-formed = the Book's complete brief of the companionship-blessing.

**Who Are These Companions? The Two Answers Held Together**

The readers have kept two frames on the question, across the sūrah-tradition: companions understood as the pure consorts of the Garden — the ḥūr, created, as 56:35 states, for the people of the right — and the wider frame within which the Garden receives as consorts the righteous spouses of the world too, joined to their believing mates in faith (52:21's promise of family-elevation, 13:23's embrace of righteous spouses, 40:8's prayer). The two frames do not compete: the Book promises both companionships newly-created and families reunited; the Garden's household accommodates every honourable bond.

Which answers, at last, the Meccan wives and mothers who believed in the early years, whose agonizing question about their own position in the promised place the revelation repeatedly takes up: the promises are general in scope — companions for the saved, of co-age and perfect grace — and the believer-family of the world enters within those promises. The verse's feminine adjectives describe the promise from the standard address-angle; the wider Qur'anic witness (recompense for every soul, women addressed throughout the Book) holds the promise open in full for all of the saved.

**Against Two Maladies of Reading This Verse**

The Book's sensuous-honest verses have always faced two maladies of reception. On one side, prudery flinches: can revelation speak this way? — an anxiety the Book itself answers by its composure: the God who created these forms does not need euphemism to mention them, and the reduction-of-embarrassment is itself part of the point — desire is creaturely, and the Garden's companions are promised to creatures. On the other side, vulgarity-commercialization converts the verse into the all-sufficient image of reward, and this the Book answers architecturally: two adjectives in one clause, then the list moves on to the cups of pure wine — and to the far larger blessing of undistorted hearing (78:35), and finally to the only unpriceable item in this world or the next: a reward *from your Lord* (78:36), the Giver overshadowing every gift named.

The proportion keeps desire in place: companionships in the Garden are God's complete honouring of the body-spirit unity He created — not the whole of the reward, not the point of the reward, but an unashamed real part of the furnishings. The mature tradition reads the verse and thanks God for approval-of-pleasure; the same tradition then takes the list's motion into the fuller blessings and follows them.

**After the Companions, the Cup**

The inventory's next furnishing follows immediately — the beverage of the household: cups, and not sips in them but cups *full*, filled past ordinary measure — the Book will soon describe their effect-opposites (no headache, no intoxication) as part of the perfection (56:18-19). The companions and the cup stand side by side in the catalogue as the two classic symbols of eastern festivity: company, and the circulating drink — the evening perfected at the Day.

The sūrah has already shown the lodge at the other door with two drinks — ḥamīm, ghassāq — and the same cup-gesture that the revelling class prided itself on in the world reappears at both addresses, in only two sizes: full-to-brim of pure delight here, boiling-and-murk there. God's catalogue-book of reward is, movingly, all counter-written from the same register as His catalogue of penalty; and the panorama's aim is not sensation but choice — verse 39 will shortly re-issue the invitation in a hortatory key while the street-time still holds.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:34

> and full cups ˹of pure wine˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kaʾsan Diḥāqā: Cups That Are Never Low**

Fourth furnishing: *wa-kaʾsan diḥāqā* — and a cup, brim-filled, overflowing. *Kaʾs* is the celebratory cup — the proper name for the drinking-vessel of joy — and *diḥāq*, the adjective of the filled-to-the-brim and continually-replenished: it denotes fullness so sustained it reads as successiveness — cup after cup, always filled. The inventory's rhythm continues: two nouns, an adjective; but the adjective chosen performs the whole theology of supply at the winning household — the cup level that does not drop.

The detail-of-endlessness answers the one sorrow that hushes every feast on earth: the last of the vintage, the bottom of the bowl, the approaching end that the guests pretend not to check. God's festivity-noun in this verse is the one-word annihilation of banquet-end: *diḥāq* — topped-up-ness as the permanent estate of the cup. At the winning household, the approach of the end is the one guest never admitted.

**The Counter-Cup of the Ambush's Cellar**

The verse's force doubles the moment one sets it beside the drinks of the lodged sinners three verses above: boiling water and ghassāq there — scorched and murk-fluid, the two poles — cup and cup; and here the brim-filled cup of pure wine. The cup-gesture is identical across the two households: raised in both; swallowed in both; but one cup punishes at the throat and one cup celebrates at the mouth. The abodes rhyme right down to the vessel.

The parallel-darkness extends to the servers: at the Garden the cup is circulated by attendants whose description is luminous from the companion-passages — *“They will be waited on by eternal youths with cups, pitchers, and a drink ˹of pure wine˺ from a flowing stream, that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* (56:17-19). The circulation-service named, the streams named, and then — the two negative adjectives that hold the whole superiority of this cup over every cup that men ever trusted: without hangover; without the thought-portion being carried away.

**The Two Negative Adjectives Are the Wine's Crown**

The Book repeatedly finishes its wine-verses with those negations — no headache and no intoxication (56:18-19); no harm and no drunkenness (37:46-47) — because they are the theological edge of the promise: wine fully itself, with its penalty removed. The drink of the Garden keeps the oenophile's whole desire — hue, bouquet, coolness, lift — and disconnects the mortal surcharge that earthly wine has always exacted: blurred reason, damaged mornings, the slow corruption of the drinker by degrees.

This pairs naturally with the same Book's complete law concerning the earthly cup: the wine of the world was forbidden precisely for those surcharges — the harm-quantified outweighing its boon — and God's removal of the surcharge in His own household is His way of saying: the affection behind the cup was never despised; only its entanglement with reason-corruption was. Heaven's vintage comes with the entanglement dissolved: the drinker remains luminous-minded, in full command of his own judgment, while the cup's joy circulates. The prohibition thereby receives its true context: deprivation now against lawful delight then: the temporary withholding embraced in the world is vindicated at the Day by a drink better than drink.

**A Feast That Circulates, Never Monopolizes**

The verse's last social note: the cup is passing ware. The parallel feast-verses describe the drink in motion — *“They will pass around to each other a drink ˹of pure wine,˺ which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23) — circulated services, shared rounds, the banquet's classic gesture. The winning-household's joy is thus designedly *table fellowship*, not private hoarding: the fellowship imagined in the shared cup is itself one of the finest furnishings.

In the world, such circulating vessels were the class-sized toys of the landed rich — the wine-halls of the well-born, guarded by rivalry and turned sour by boasting. At the winning household the same act is restored to its uncorrupted objectivity: shared delight with no proprietor smaller than God, no rivalry, no reckoning of who stood whom a round. The society of the Garden is the first society whose festive circulation carries no politics — its cups pass because its Host topped them, diḥāq, and no guest can ever recoil into the creditor-debtor thread of earthly feasting.

**The Cup as Sentence in the Legal File of the Transgressors**

A further sober thread: the same persistence-verb of the taunt is here run in delight — the Book's two imperatives-of-drinking (Taste there; Drink-deep here) keep their form because justice speaks to both files in qualified symmetry: to the class of the lodging, taste-of-boiling with increase; to the winning class, brim-cups with increase — the increase that at 78:30 was locked to torment in one household is at 78:36 unlocked into gift in the other. The cup-filling of diḥāq is the beverage-form of increase — and the lodging's flare-up of 17:97 is its dark visualizing form.

The student of the sūrah is taught at this point to see that the two destinations differ not in their modifiers but in their referents: both receive increase; both receive cups; both receive ages; both receive keeping-in; each element of the two lists exactly mirrored, with the sign simply reversed at formation. To know which list any person belongs to is, therefore, the single question worth one's life; every other listed-furniture-question is secondary.

**Past the Description: The Serving-Hand Above Every Cup**

The verse stands next to one of the chapter's best-kept pivot-traditions: the company-of-youth who serve the cups immediately recall the service-throng of the Garden, while the place where the wine streams from recalls the named springs the believers were shown earlier (76:17-18's Salsabīl); and every stream-source traces finally to the Grantor whose name comes trending up in verse 36 and arrives in verse 37 as *your Lord, the Lord of the heavens and the earth* — the feast-item and the Host-name delivering each other, as the Book keeps planning.

The pious recitation has never read these verses as drink-verses primarily but as *Him*-verses: every cup raised in the description raises the question — whose is the cellar? whose the feast? whose the table? The verse's own conclusion will do the naming within two verses: a recompense from *your Lord*, a gift. The cup, diḥāq-filled, is the Host's pouring; the Host is only postponed by two lines of furnishings, so that the inventory itself may first instruct every reciting servant: whatever is loved here is loved because the Loved pours it.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:35

> never to hear any idle talk or lying therein—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Furnishing No One Thought to Ask For**

Fifth furnishing — and it is not an item but an *absence*, the sole negation of the winning-household, precisely where the hell-household's descriptions were all negations: *lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan wa-lā kidhdhābā* — they shall not hear therein any idle talk nor any lying. The Garden's descriptions are litanies of additions until this clause, which stands alone: the only texture of heaven that God describes by what it *lacks* — noise and falsehood — the two acoustic pollutants of every earthly society, eradicated from the air.

The native charge of the verse deepens once its verbs are held against the hell-block's ritual repetition of tasting: here the negated faculty is *hearing* — the sense that suffered most in Mecca's early streets. The believers of the first years spent their believing half-lives hearing precisely the two things this verse abolishes: idle mockery of the message, and open lying against it. Heaven's *acoustic climate* is the Book's custom remedy addressed to the memory of that suffering: at the Day, the ears of the saved are never again assaulted by what they heard in the street.

**Laghw: The Empty Wind of Words**

*Laghw* is the idle, the vain, the sound with no content under it — talk said only because silence might be thought-provoking, chatter whose whole meaning is that it fills time. The Book consistently classifies laghw as hallmark of heedlessness — of a life with too much noise in it to reflect — and then, with this verse, reports laghw as the first abolition of eternity. The saved will not merely avoid idle talk; they will not *hear* it — the world of laghw moved out of earshot altogether, placed beyond their experience.

The parallel of rescue from laghw is stated with characteristic economy elsewhere too: *“Only those are saved who turn away from pointless talk”* (23:3's formula); and of the Garden of the Elevated it states: *“where no idle talk will be heard”* (88:11) — the same rule in two sky-places. The pious reader hears therein a double-register promise: deliverance from one's own stray words, and deliverance from everyone's — the curse of empty talk that fills worldly public squares lifted from the saved forever.

**Kidhdhāb: The Word That Haunts the Sūrah**

The second banned acoustic is sharper: *kidhdhāb* — falsehood — the very noun-root worn by the hell-file of verse 28, where the ṭāghīn were charged with utter rejection (*kidhdhābā*) of the signs. The word appearing here as the banned acoustic of heaven is the same word the sinners were charged with in their lives — an interior rhyming of the sūrah's two estates: in the abode of the chargees, the sounding-list is torment-fluids; in the winning household, the world's old falsehood never once reaches the ears of the delivered.

For the community that had lived within earshot of the proclamation's being called *a lie*, *a poet's invention*, *an old tale*, with the reciter himself called liar in the streets — the verse must have rung like early rain: God's formal, permanent answer to the street-slogans is a household in which the word for falsehood cannot sound. Their wounded ears, that kept the account during the street-years, are precisely what the mafāz heals first. Every believer who has since kept faith while hearing the truth reviled inherits that same promised room, walled-round with the silence that God's earth always owed them.

**Peace as the Final Ambient Sound**

With the two noises abolished, what remains to be *heard*? The Book tells the acoustic content positively in its twin passage: *“There they will never hear any idle talk — only ˹greetings of˺ peace”* (19:62) — salām as the standing lawful noise of the household; and the inter-cursory cups of the parallel passage carry the same acoustic order: the drink passes *“which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23). The feast stays articulate, even intimate — but it never becomes noise, never slides into falsity; the banquet-conversation of the winning household remains salām-shaped, by construction.

The heavenly quiet is not absence-of-talk, then, but *talk so perfectly governed it no longer hurts*: speech weighed by mouth of every speaker there who cannot co-author laghw or kidhdhāb because none of them any longer *contains* it. The infection of speech with emptiness and falsehood was a soft spot of the fallen world - once hearts are finished remodeling, the air behaves itself. The verse thus promises the repair of sound at the level of the repair of hearts, rather than by externally imposed quiet.

**The Pierced-Ear Folk Now Rest Their Hearing**

The social-pastoral freight of this verse deserves fuller note, because it answers the daily condition of religion's faithful across ages: the believer's ears are his most-burned organ — wherever truth lives unroofed among its mockers. The promised silence of the household comes as comfort explicitly fitted to that burn: those who once could not stop their ears from collecting insult now receive streets, gatherings and banquets that manufacture no insult at all.

Contemplatively, the verse also tutors the hearing already in the interval: if heaven is where laghw and kidhdhāb cannot be heard - the God-wary make their own acoustics resemble it early: refusing to retail noise and untruth within their own houses, steering their households' conversations from the two pollutants, building small walled gardens of true-speech while the world's air still runs hot with both. The household of heaven is furnished even now in miniature wherever true words and peace are layed down where the noise used to sit.

**The Nominals Complete, the Charter Approaches**

The sprint-list of the inventory closes with this quiet abundance — gardens, vines, companions, cups, silence — five nouns and two adjectives performing what prose could never hold at this level of joy without gaudiness. The absolute understatement of these verses is part of their elocution: after the steam and urn of the ambush-block, the winning-household is conveyed in the plainest possible diction, as though the feast need not call attention to itself.

And then, at verse 36, the sūrah turns grammatical face from inventory to charter: all the items just displayed are named in one governing phrase — a reward with a Giver, a gift with an amount: reward from your Lord, and a generous, measure-filled gift. The items were furnishings; verse 36 is the deed of the house, signed by the Host. And with the charter, the voice's addressee pivots — toward your Lord, toward the Prophet — for the garden-block ends not at the feast but at the naming of the name of Him who prepared it.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:36

> a ˹fitting˺ reward as a generous gift from your Lord,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charter Signed Over the Inventory**

The sprint of nouns ends, and now the estate receives its deed: *jazāʾan min rabbika wa-ʿaṭāʾan ḥisābā* — a recompense from your Lord, and a bestowal full-measured. The inventory of vv31-35, which ran on raw nouns, is gathered up inside one governing formula: all of it — gardens, grapevines, companions, cups, silence — is here legislated under two relations: *jazāʾ*, the reward-earned; and *ʿaṭāʾ*, the gift-freely-given. The household is simultaneously wages and generosity.

The distinction preaches the Book's settled balance on reward: to honour the works, God calls it jazāʾ — what was verily worked for; to keep the house humble, God calls it ʿaṭāʾ — bestowal, in excess of wages. The two names together are the defence against both pride-of-entrance and presumption-of-guarantee: the believer is genuinely rewarded for genuine deeds, and the estate genuinely exceeds every accumulation those deeds could honestly claim.

**Min Rabbika: The Giver Steps Forward By Name-Relation**

The pronoun turns the charter intimate: *from your Lord* — the address aimed, over the feast described, at God's own Messenger, the hearer of the whole sūrah in His night of revelation. All the household's furnishings, the cups and the companions and the silence, are assigned as what proceeds *from your Lord*: the sender of the provision is named in the vocative-relation as though God were saying to the Prophet, alone in the recitation: it is I who prepared this — for them, and for you.

The comfort-purport for the reciting listener is the chain of the pronoun: a promise delivered to one man in Mecca becomes, by the text's own widening, the charter of all the saved of every people. The garment *your Lord* is worn first by Muhammad, and it is the same Lord who names Himself at the next verse in His universal title: Lord of the heavens and the earth (78:37) — the personal pronoun and the cosmic title adjoining so that the two registers of God are never experienced separately: intimate Giver, universal Sovereign.

**Ḥisābā: Three Readings of the Amount-Word**

The charter's amount-word calls the commentators to their registers, and the tradition preserves three overlapping readings. First, *ḥisābā* as *sufficient* — an amount adequate against every wish, enough and unending: the household measured-out to the fullest extent of satisfaction. Second, ḥisābā as *abounding*, generous — generous abundance marked by the lavishness already previewed in diḥāq's overflowing cups (78:34). Third — as noted by commentators joining the two destination-blocks — *fitting*, full-account: in parallel with hell's wifāq (78:26), the believers received measured recompense, *as true account* — their earnings precisely honoured.

The three are not three answers but three faces of the one charter: sufficient because God measures at generosity's scale, abundant because the same measurement is the One-Who-Gives' measurement, and fitting in that nothing of the saved one's file of righteousness is under-read, not an hour, not a coin, not a hidden intention. A reward given by the most delicate Accountant on earth would already surpass imagination; a reward given by *your Lord*, well-measured in the charter-word, completes what can be said in Arabic about sufficiency.

**The Jazāʾ-ʿAṭāʾ Pair Against the File-Model**

This pair also closes, definitively, any thought that the household is purchased by the works themselves. The model of the fire-block was: file produces home — sin for sin, drink for drink (78:22-26). The garden-block's model is declaredly richer: the works place you at the household; the household's content outstrips the works by the gift-clause — so that the saved, entering, do not survey the garden as contractors checking delivery of an order, but as guests overwhelmed by their host's generosity at a table they could never have commissioned.

The prophetic tradition's own balance always kept both: deeds matter — nobody enters without them — and no deeds ever suffice as price — entrance is by mercy. The dual noun structure of this verse is the Qur'anic seat of that balance: *jazāʾ* ties the banquet to the works in genuine causality; *ʿaṭāʾ* ties the banquet's scale to a Giver whose giving exceeds all works. Both nouns are from *min rabbika* — from your Lord — so that every trace of the estate's origin runs to the same place: the Lord's own custody of both wage and grace.

**The Charter as Daily Medicine for the Interval**

What does a believer still walking the world do with a verse like this? Hold it as the anchor-daydream: that the estate of the saved is defined not by its items but by its *issuer* — from your Lord. Every worldly shortfall, every slight and privation and unanswered longing, is measured in the interval against a charter signed by the Lord of the Day: the mouth that claims on the feast need only work for the *issuer's approval*, and the gift-clause covers what the works do not.

The pious practice gathered around the verse since the earliest generations has a simple profile: when the world disappoints — when houses crumble, when enterprises sink, when the heart's wishes stand queued at a shut door — recite the charter. The disappointment is measured against *ḥisābā*: there exists a sufficiency assigned already, in full account, waiting at a fixed location. A heart that believes this can pass years of short rations quite steadily; it knows what neither the rich market nor the full table can supply is neither lost nor dropped, only deferred, by gift.

**From the Feast's Charter to the Sovereign's Title**

The verse ends mid-clause — the relative description of your Lord continues without breath into verse 37, where the charter's Giver is crowned with His universal title: *Lord of the heavens and the earth and all that is between them, the Most Compassionate* — the feast-Giver and the Holder of the cosmos established as the same person in one sentence spanning two verses.

That sentence-bridge is the garden-block's last architecture: the banquet's Giver is not a feaster-deity within the Day; He is the Ruler of all the territories of the Day — of the seven storeys that the sceptics saw opened (78:19), of the earth they saw levelled (78:20) — and yet the name by which He bestows is, before any title of might, the Mercy-Name. The gift-verse leads directly into the majesty-verse so that no one may imagine the gift issuing from smallness: the most lavish Householder is the Holder of everything, whose very next words regulate the speech of every creature at His Day — by permission alone, and in truth.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:37

> the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, the Most Compassionate. No one will dare speak to Him

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Title Runs Over the Feast**

The sentence of verse 36 continues without pause, and the Giver of the garden is now named in full power: *rabbi s-samāwāti wal-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā r-raḥmāni lā yamlikūna minhu khaṭabā* — Lord of the heavens and the earth and all between them, the All-Merciful: they possess no addressing of Him. The feast's Giver is crowned, in the same breath as the feast, with the universal title — so that the hosts of the banquet are registered as the Sovereign of the Day. The structure guards against the smallest theological error: that the promise might come from a lesser chair than the Throne.

The interior order of the names is itself the sermon: Lord-of-total-domain first, *al-Raḥmān* — the Mercy-Name — held adjacent, and then the verse of the complete hush of all creation before Him. Might-Nearest and Mercy-Nearest stand side by side here, because the coming scene will need both: permission to speak will be issued from His mercy (78:38), and the inability to speak will be imposed by His majesty. The verse names the two keys of the Day before handing out their uses.

**Lord of the Storeys the Sūrah Audited**

The title cites exactly the territories the sūrah has been touring from verse 6: heavens — the seven storeys built above (78:12) — and earth — the mihād below (78:6) — and everything between: the lamp, the clouds, the rain, the orchards, the moving caravan-world of the listeners. The garden-charter's Giver is therefore not a stranger-saviour from outside the audited world but its own Landlord, re-named at the point of reward as Lord-of-the-furnished-cosmos. Everything between is in the formula — the transgressors and the God-wary alike included, for they too are *between* — which is itself the silent import of the Day: every inhabitant of the interval will now face the Landlord of the interval.

The parallel verse elsewhere crowns the title's breadth one stage further: *“The Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, and the Lord of all points of sunrise”* (37:5) — every east and all that rises — so the listener understands: not one territory, not one horizon; the Day's Ruler is Lord of every direction from which light comes and every realm the light visits. The garden promised is therefore mere property of the Owner of all; its being bestowed is the smallest imaginable exercise of His holding.

**Al-Raḥmān: Mercy Adjacent to Majesty**

The mercy-name's placement just before the silence-verse delivers a deliberate juxtaposition across the etiquette of the Day: the Being before whom no one can speak a word is *the Most Compassionate*. The terror of the speech-ban is thus folded inside the name most associated with favour: the hush is not the hush of a tyrant's court but the hush of a Lord so great that His mercy itself has majesty — and the mercy, far from being cancelled by the hush, is about to be manifest through the regulated permissions of the next verse.

The name also states the ground of the banquet just chartered: the Giver is al-Raḥmān, because mercy-personality and not reward-bookkeeping is the deepest reason for the feast. Verse 36's gift-relation — bestowal, exceeding wages — was, all along, the Raḥmān-relation in fiscal form. The sūrah's God is unitary across the two destinations: the lodging's fittingness (78:26) is His justice; the garden's gift is His mercy; and both stand under a Lordship whose totality is mercied, not indifferent.

**Lā Yamlikūna: The Address-Right Withdrawn**

Then the speech-law of the Day: *lā yamlikūna minhu khaṭabā* — they will possess no khiṭāb with Him, no initiate-address, no ability of speech-as-of-right against Him. The formula's machinery is grammatical denial of *milk* — ownership, control — over the addressing of God: at that Day, speech towards God is not a faculty creatures exercise at will but a concession granted case by case. The chatter-verse's counterword: on the street they asked and mocked (78:1); at the Day no one initiates speech to Him; the two worlds' speech-rules are absolutely reversed.

The sister-sūrah already drew the same scene with its distributaries: *“This is a Day they will not talk, nor will they be permitted to offer excuses”* (77:35-36) — ban on speaking, ban on excusing — and the hush-offices shown here are stricter still: not even a *khiṭāb*, an address-title. The majesty-theology crowns the whole sūrah's method: the God who debated nothing with the street-scorners in their season stands, at the Day, under no obligation to entertain petitions, cross-examination or manifesto: speech itself is locked, pending permission — and the next verse gives the basis of the exceptions.

**The Hush as the Court's First Furnishing**

The commentators read this verse as scene-setting of the whole Day of Decision: before any judgments are pronounced, the formal atmosphere is regulated — the silence of the great court, the awe-stricken condition of every creature assembled before sovereign Majesty. In that hush, rank means nothing: the boastful men of standing who commanded every Meccan assembly stand as speechless as their porters; speech-right was standing's counterfeit currency in the world, and the court accepts no such coin.

The hush also has a mercy-reading cherished by the tradition: that Day's silence belongs partly to *ease* — no one will be compelled to argue his own case through the atmosphere of wrath, permitted in the sūrah's earlier twin scenes (77:37-39); the intercessory permissions will simplify the procedure on the believers' behalf (78:38); and the ending of chatter is itself one of the consolations — as though even the ambient distress of unregulated talk in the plaza of the Day is mercifully shut off, except for words licensed as right.

**The Verse That Hands Over Its Own Second Half**

The speech-law's mechanism then arrives in the verse this one spills into: the standing of the Spirit and the angels in ranks — and the permission-rule stated in positive form: *none shall speak save one whom the Compassionate permits and who speaks aright* — where the Mercy-Name of 37 reappears as the permit's office (78:38). The two verses are in fact one sentence and one scene: the ban stated, then ranked audience, then the exceptions under the Name.

Together, verses 37-38 perform the sūrah's last court-regulation, and the reader who has followed the street's taunts, the interrogation, the inhabitants of both estates, now stands inside the court for the final law: the whole plaza of creation silent before al-Raḥmān, and only permitted speech proceeding, precisely true. It is the ultimate answer to the mocking-questioning parties with which the sūrah opened: they who used to ask one another sneeringly now stand in the ranks — while another group of voices, licensed by mercy, alone proceeds in words. The Day that settles the dispute settles first the speech-conditions under which it may proceed.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:38

> on the Day the ˹holy˺ spirit and the angels will stand in ranks. None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Standing of the Highest Rank**

The regulation-scene's personnel take their places: *yawma yaqūmu r-rūḥu wal-malāʾikatu ṣaffā* — the Day the Spirit and the angels stand in rows. The named Spirit heads the list, singled out ahead of the general angelic congregation — the same manner in which the Book elsewhere singles Gabriel from among the angels, as when enmity toward the whole heavenly order names *His angels and His messengers and Gabriel and Michael* (2:98). The singling marks supreme rank: at the Day's court, the chief of the heavenly messengers stands named while the hosts stand ranged behind.

The manner of the standing is *ṣaff* — row-formation: order, not crowd; rank, not throng. The parallel scene arrives with procession-plural: *“and your Lord comes ˹to judge˺ with angels, rank upon rank”* (89:22) — rank multiplied toward the horizon. The Day's traffic is marshalled throng: heaven has its procedures, hierarchies and formations, and the Day the dispute ends is run by the most ordered gathering in creation, drawn up below the Throne in rows from which no voice rises unbidden.

**Who Is the Spirit? The Register Stays Open**

The commentators have transmitted more than one reading of *al-rūḥ* here, in broad tiers. The dominant: Gabriel — the Trustworthy Spirit expressly so-called in the Book's account of the revelation: *“which the trustworthy spirit ˹Gabriel˺ brought down upon your heart”* (26:193-194) — and so on this Day he stands to the fore of the ranks, the chief of the messengers between God and the prophets. A second reading keeps the referent open: a created being distinguished among the first creations, angelic or beyond-the-angels; a third reads the many-rows structure as angels in ranks with the Spirit standing alone.

The Book itself legislates where the discussion must end: asked about the spirit generally, the Prophet was given only this to say: *“Its nature is known only to my Lord, and you ˹O humanity˺ have been given but little knowledge”* (17:85). The verse's majesty-psychology, not its personnel-mystery, is what the recitation intends: the *rank-web* of the heavenly order, attendancy completed, complete silence until command — that the Day is run at the level of formation, and no creature below the Throne shortens its assembly by a word.

**None Shall Speak: The Positive Form of the Ban**

Then the regulation's positive half: *lā yatakallamūna illā man adhina lahu r-raḥmānu* — they will not speak, except one whom the Most Compassionate grants permission. The speech-ban of verse 37 receives its procedural machinery: silence is default for every creature; voice is license, issued by name, from the office of the Mercy — which is why the mercy-name stands here a second time. Even the right-to-speak toward God arrives, at the Day, by bestowal — and the Raḥmân who gave the garden is the same who gives the word.

Which permission-classes the commentators recorded: the prophets, first — as Ibn Kathīr relates from the early authorities; and others add the martyrs, and the intercessors generally, since speech on another's behalf at that Day is itself the highest licensed transaction. One report-line holds that *who speaks aright* means those who say *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — testimony of God's unity remaining speech-licensed even there. The intercession-principle running under all of them is the Book's standing law of mediation before God: *“Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?”* (2:255) and *“No intercession will be of any benefit with Him, except by those granted permission by Him”* (34:23): permission, first and last.

**The Vastness of the Silence, Against the Manyness of the Angels**

The true scale of the silence impresses most when held against the crowd's multitude: the Book himself draws attention, elsewhere, to the countlessness of the heavenly hosts: *“Imagine how many ˹noble˺ angels are in the heavens! ˹Even˺ their intercession would be of no benefit whatsoever, until Allah gives permission to whoever He wills and ˹only for the people He˺ approves”* (53:26). Innumerable angels — and the plaza is quiet. Rank upon rank of obedient power — and no throat clears, no wing rustles argument, no case is opened, no exception pleaded. The atmosphere of the Day is manufactured by the speech-law into the most heavily attended silence in creation's records.

The microphones of the world are, by this verse, foreclosed: the rich man who paid for advocates, the powerful man who commanded the floor, the talk-clever man whose evasions always found an opening — all find the floor of the Day hears no unlicensed voice. Entry into speech is by the Mercy, and the measure of mercy's license is *speaking aright* — the fourth element, still to come in the verse.

**And Who Speaks Aright: The Only Speech-Specification**

The second condition attached: *wa-qāla ṣawābā* — and speaks rightly, speaks what is true. Permission alone is not the license's full grammar: even the licensed speak only the exact and the true — sound speech, the ṣawāb, the arrow of rightness. The two conditions, mercy-permission and truth-content, regulate the Day's entire discourse: nothing sounds there but authorized truth. No flattery, no distortion, no special-pleading: because on that Day, content is bound to the truth as tightly as voice is bound to permission.

The register of *ṣawāb* is the final correction, then, of all the child-speech and street-speech tracked by the sūrah from verse 1: the ikhtilāf is over at the level of licensing; the many tongues of Mecca and the many tongues of every generation are reduced at the Day to the licensed few, and what the licensed say is true. Speech having been so regulated, the court begins; and the court's next verse — the Day called Al-Ḥaqq — declares that speech-condition translated to events: the whole Day works to the standard of *true*: what is only-true, finally standing as the only-standing.

**After the Ranks and the Regulation, the Name of the Day**

The sentence that began at *your Lord* and continued through the cosmic title and the mercy-name and the speech-ban and the ranks and the exceptions finally exhales: the sūrah pivots from the court's procedures to the court's name — *That Day is the Truth* — the naming that explains why speech therein must be so regulated: a Day that is nothing-but-real admits of nothing-but-true inside it (78:39).

The believer's practical custody of the verse meanwhile forms around its two conditions: *authorized* and *true* — the training is simply to begin, in the world's unregulated plaza, to discipline one's own speech by the Day's standard in advance — until one's own words too become licensed and right in advance: seek permission of speech from God for the heart, before it speaks; bind speech by truth before it issues. The Day of ranks will not surprise those who learned its speech-conduct during the street-years; they have been standing in quiet formation already, among the few, in the midst of the chattering world.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:39

> That Day is the ˹ultimate˺ truth. So let whoever wills take the path leading back to their Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Yawmu l-Ḥaqq: The Day Is Named After Reality Itself**

After the court's regulation, the court's name: *dhālika l-yawmu l-ḥaqq* — that Day is the Truth, the Real Day. The definer *that* points across the sūrah's whole travelled distance: the day of the appointment (78:17), the trumpet, processions, opened sky, marching mountains, ambush and salvation and ranked angels — *that* Day — and its fundamental description is ḥaqq: the true one, the ultimately-real one, actuality as opposed to every provisional arrangement.

The naming has an edge against both destinations of the sūrah. To the world's sceptic, the Day seemed then *bāṭil-adjacent* — far-fetched, unlikely, un-real; the naming reverses the polarity: it is the *world* whose claims were illusory — mirage-mountains (78:20), little whiles, false enjoys — while the Day is ḥaqq precisely in that only what is real persists into it. Reality is the Day's title because at the Day nothing unreal remains standing — false fronts, false claims, false securities all stripped, and only true files, true speech, true estates remaining.

**The World as the Day's Anteroom of Illusions**

Qur'anic usage sharpens the contrast throughout: the world is called diversion and play-passes-time repeatedly; and the same naming-rhetoric is used for deeds — the good deed is the ḥaqq, the justified rank; for scriptures — the Book is ḥaqq, reality-utterance. What al-yawmu l-ḥaqq adds is epochal: one day sits across history as the *only day* in which experience and reality coincide completely. In the world, the senses report to mixtures — the mirage looks like water; the powerful look like winners; the delay looks like indefinite continuance. At the Day, looking = seeing = knowing.

The sūrah has been preparing this lemma throughout: the mockers asked *what* they asked about (78:1-2) — and the answer is, at the Day, suddenly perceptual-experiential: the momentous news *is present*. Their future-knowing (78:4-5) is the Day's own predicate: kallā sayaʿlamūn — the knowledge that arrives wholly because now every frame is true. The naming of the Day after Reality is therefore the naming of epistemology's final condition: that Day is ḥaqq in that the truth is *seen*, not merely told.

**Fa-Man Shāʾ: The Door Re-opens in the Middle of Doom-Description**

And then — with the Day named and everything already ranged in ranks — comes the sūrah's most startling act of mercy: *fa-man shāʾa ttakhadha ilā rabbihi maʾābā* — so whoever wills, let him take a way of return to his Lord. The hortatory imperative breaks into the eschatology-sentence — a door opened inside the description of the court: the description is not yet over before the invitation is issued to the still-undecided listeners. God — as it were — turns from the description of the ranked plaza in the future to the actual reader at *this* hour, and says: capacity to choose is what you *still* have — take the route home.

The *fa-* (so) connects the two clauses with strict consequence: the Day being the truth, the response due *now* is a return-way seized in the interval. The world's *little while* is hereby defined as *the interval in which one may take the maʾāb-road*: the only use the uncertain duration has, in eschatological economics, is to provide the choosing-window. The preacher's craft of the verse is the Book's standard mercy-figure: terror-verses repeatedly broken by hortatory doors — because the Qur'an's Day-descriptions never intend the audience to be stricken but redirected.

**Maʾāb: The Word the Hell-Block Already Reserved**

The choice-word of the destination is dovetailed with the sūrah's own architecture: *maʾāb* — the return-resort, the address of home-going — is the *identical noun* with which the Fire was named the home of the transgressors (78:22). God gives the same word in both contexts as the sūrah's most quietly devastating rhyme: both abodes are maʾāb — home-of-return — but one is taken in chains, the other taken by will — and the will-routed one is still available, from the world's side, to anyone reading the verse today. The Day's two maʾābs are both homeward; the only difference is whether the return was chosen and greeted, or awaited and ambushed.

Qur'anic parallels to the return-word support the wilful-path reading: the outpouring of the Book's instruction at this very verse-form rides the famous line of free counsel: *“This is the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve”* (18:29). Divine prerogative formulates human freedom: whoever wills, let him take. In our verse, God does not command; He ‌‌*licenses*: let the one who wills take — the mercified structure of the invitation.

**Your Will Inside His Will: The Guard of the Parallel Verse**

The will-counsel is crowned in the Book with its theological guard, stated in the twin-verse family: *“Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord. But you cannot will ˹to do so˺ unless Allah wills. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise”* (76:29-30). The practical lesson of the guard: the offered freedom is real but derivative — human willing is licensed within divine willing; the road cannot be commanded by the walker. The combination keeps two cures at hand: against despair (you may will), and against self-confident independence (you cannot will but by Him who names Himself al-Raḥmān in the adjacent verse).

The will-verse thus has a devotional edge very precious to the tradition: any person on the Day who sees the licensed speech of the saved's ranks knows that the will-road began with exactly the will that *this verse* inspires — the reading itself is the Call. One sitting with 78:39 recited in the interval has encountered, in the full formal character, the text's own offer to initiate that very will — *let the one who wills take* — issuing straight through the description of the Day of ranks into the heart of anyone not yet in the ranks.

**One Verse Left Between the Invitation and the Street**

The sūrah's final verse stands between the offer and its first audience: the warning's return to the near future — a punishment *near*, the Day of beholding what hands sent ahead, and the most regretful cry in all the Book: would that I were dust (78:40). The shape completed: description of the whole Day → name of the Day → invitation to the road → and warning while the road is still passable.

For the session's first reciters the order meant exactly what it means in every later reading: the revelation does not end in the ranks — it ends back at the hearer's door, open ajar. Verse 39 has already said the door and its latch; the last verse tells how soon the last bearing of the door shuts, and what is on the far side for those who never bothered to walk through it. The Day of Truth watches; the little wind of the street-reality blows through the room; the sūrah waits, with the path-choice stated in hortatory case, for whomever will take the maʾāb-road to his Lord.

## Sūrah an-Nabaʾ 78:40

> Indeed, We have warned you of an imminent punishment—the Day every person will see ˹the consequences of˺ what their hands have done, and the disbelievers will cry, “I wish I were dust.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Andharnākum: The Sūrah Names Itself Completed Delivery**

The sūrah ends by turning back upon itself, in the first person: *innā andharnākum ʿadhāban qarībā* — indeed, We have warned you of a punishment near. The verse is the certificate of delivery attached to the whole sūrah: everything from the street-scene's overheard question to the court's speech-law is here declared to have been one continuous act of *indhār* — warning issued. God signs the chapter as completed conveyance; whoever heard it has been warned, in full, of a punishment that is near.

The signature also closes the obligation-issue definitive of prophetic responsibility: nothing more need be proved after this, nothing more owed. The warners are discharged; the warned are filed. Verses such as this one mark the point in the Book where conveyance-stops and consequence begins — as the Prophet's own commission was regularly described in these terms: that his duty ran to delivery, and the accounting ran to God. The sūrah's last verse states its own discharge-receipt before the hearers' eyes.

**Qarīb: The Doctrine of Nearness**

The qualification is the shock: *qarīb* — near. The punishment whose day holds ages (78:23), escalations (78:30), and permanent estates is nonetheless *near* — the Book's two-scale calendar will not let either side be forgotten: what is vast in its interior measure is near in its approach. The Book's own announcement years earlier had put it with maximum plainness: *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺”* (54:1) — the event of the moon-splitting entered as the proof-marker that the Hour is already in pulling-distance.

Two nearnesses converge for every person: the Day's approach at the world's scale — in a universe whose whole course is one forenoon to God — and the personal near: that no individual's interval runs past his own death, and from death onward the punishment-era is entered without awaiting the public Day's parade. Between the two nearnesses, procrastination loses both its arguments: the public Day may not be seen by every hearer in this century, but the end of the warnable interval is one breath away for every hearer in every century.

**Qaddamat Yadāh: The Deeds That Went Ahead as Cargo**

The content of the Day arrives in the specialized formula: *yawma yanẓuru l-marʾu mā qaddamat yadāh* — the Day a man sees what his hands have sent ahead. *Qaddama* — to dispatch forward, to send ahead — is the Book's own terminal register for deeds: actions are cargo the self forwards to the Day, arriving there before the self does, awaiting inspection at arrival. What the hands forwarded is what the eyes will view: beholding the full accounted consignment of their own couriered conduct.

The parallel formulations frame the scene from both directions: *“˹then˺ each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind”* (82:5) — the sent and the abandoned both counted; and *“All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind”* (75:13). The scene's emotional mechanics are drawn in the Book's own crowning verse on the subject: at the sight of the fire, *“every ˹disbelieving˺ person will remember ˹their own sins˺. But what is the use of remembering then? They will cry, ‘I wish I had sent forth ˹something good˺ for my ˹true˺ life’”* (89:23-24). The seeing is not observational but conviction: at the Day, the seen cargo is understood — and it is precisely that understanding which regrets.

**Yā Laytanī Kuntu Turābā: The Cry of the Century of Wishes**

Then the speech one cannot permit oneself in the court unlicensed — and yet this cry is quoted as script of the Day: *wa-yaqūlu l-kāfiru yā laytanī kuntu turābā* — and the disbeliever says: would that I were dust. The commentators' layered readings of the wish run from the general to the scene-particular: (i) wish-of-nonexistence — would that I had never been created, or never proceeded beyond my grave-dust; (ii) wish-of-humiliation — would that my standing had been low instead of the pride-station from which I transgressed — trading traces of status for a lowliness that does not get summoned. And there are the reports carrying the scene-particular tradition, conveyed by many commentators: on the Day, after God will have dealt justice even among the animals — the hornless avenged upon the horned — the beasts are told *be dust*, and they are returned to dust; at that sight the disbeliever covets their exit: he would trade the whole standing of a man for the dismissal granted to beasts.

The attribution-tradition here is given in the registers of al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and others among the classical commentators to this verse; the critics of the later generation have treated the animal-scene report with markers of reserve in places — and the verse's meaning requires none of it: the wish of dust stands as the deepest self-victimage the Book quotes from the Day, rivalled only by the same wish's parallel: *“On that Day, those who denied ˹Allah˺ and disobeyed the Messenger will wish they were reduced to dust”* (4:42) — the two statements mirroring in the same cry.

**The Wish-Lineage of the Book's Regrets**

The cry joins the Book's growing catalogue of *yā laytanī* — the wish-form of the damned: *“Oh! I wish I had followed the Way along with the Messenger! Woe to me! I wish I had never taken so-and-so as a close friend”* (25:27-28) — wish-of-the-friend; *I wish I had sent forth something good* (89:24) — wish-of-the-lost-works; I wish I were dust — wish-of-unbeing. The quotations' function in recitation is phonetic pain: the particles of longing (*layta*) bordering each of the ends of a wasted chance, pronounced in the court from actual mouths — the shelf of second thoughts standing in the Book as permanent exhibits against every future squanderer of chance.

In the interval-world, *layta* has legitimate cousins: the wishes that arrive in time — if only I could repent — and those wishes, emitted before deadline, are what the Book calls turning and returning. The sūrah closes with the *layta* that is too late, in order that its sound may be captured early: the reciter who hears the wish-form memorizes its cadence, and the faithful tradition has long held that the cadence itself is one of the deterrences: do not let your tongue learn this phrase at the Day — learn, instead, its antidote-phrase in the interval: I turn back.

**A Sūrah Ends Where It Began: The Still-Deciding Street**

The closing verse returns the sūrah to present tense: warned — you've been warned. The circle completes from the street-scene of verse 1 (they asked one another) through the evidence parade, the appointment, the doors, the charters, the court's rules — and the reader who has travelled forty verses emerges at the last line standing exactly where the chapter began: in the world, with the warning delivered and the end still near. The content between gave the street-decision its full discovery; at the end, the street is still deciding.

And the verse's very motion seals the whole Book's posture toward the unpersuaded: no summary triumphant, no final taunt — God ends with a register of deliverance: warned *you*, that is, the still-deciding listener, the one still able to will (78:39). The wish of dust is the last sound the sūrah leaves in the ear, but beneath it the hortatory verb of one verse above is still standing open until the little while closes: *let whoever wills take, toward his Lord, a way home*. The street-end of the momentous news is thus the street's fork with the floor-signs still readable — punishment near, path homeward open — the sūrah of the News closing as the sūrah of the two addresses, beaconing in both.
