# Sūrah an-Nabaʾ (Chapter 78) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Great News and the Trumpet of the Gathering**

An-Nabaʾ, "The Great News" or "The Tiding"—also widely designated in classical exegesis as *ʿAmma* (from its opening interrogative) or *Sūrat al-Muʿṣirāt*—is the seventy-eighth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 40 verses of early Makkan revelation. It holds a monumental structural position in the sacred canon: it is the opening chapter of the thirtieth and final part (*Juz' ʿAmma*) of the Qur'an. It takes its name from verses 1–2: *“About what are they asking one another? About the Great News (*ʿani al-naba'i al-ʿaẓīm*)—that over which they are in disagreement!”* *Al-Naba' al-ʿAẓīm* is the monumental announcement of the Day of Resurrection, the prophethood of Muḥammad ﷺ, and the absolute reality of human accountability. With breathtaking majesty and relentless rhetorical power, Sūrah an-Nabaʾ takes the reader on a cosmic journey: from the stabilizing pegs of the mountain ranges and the solar furnace of the sun, to the blowing of the Trumpet, the opening of celestial portals, the boiling fluids of Hell, and the tragic cry of the condemned soul wishing it were mere dust.

**The Structural Architecture and the Pegs of the Earth**

The architecture of Sūrah an-Nabaʾ moves across three magnificent movements:

1. The Nine Cosmic Proofs of Divine Mastery (6–16): Challenges the human observer to contemplate the physical engineering of the planet: - Did We not make the earth a resting place / cradle (*mihādā*)? - And the mountains as pegs / stakes (*awtādā*) stabilizing the continental crust? - And created you in pairs (*azwājā*)? - And made your sleep for rest (*subātā*)? - And made the night as a cloak / covering (*libāsā*)? - And made the day for livelihood (*maʿāshā*)? - And constructed above you seven strong heavens? - And placed in it a blazing, radiant lamp (the sun, *sirājan wahhājā*)? - And sent down from rain-clouds water pouring in abundance (*mā'an thajjājā*), to bring forth grain, vegetation, and luxuriant gardens entwined (*jannātin alfāfā*)?

2. The Day of Decision and the Opening of the Portals (17–30): Declares that the Day of Decision is an appointed time (*mīqātā*): the Day the Horn is blown and humanity arrives in surging crowds (*afwājā*); the sky opened until it becomes portals, and the mountains moved until they become a mirage (*sarābā*). The Gardens of Triumph and the Cry of the Dust (31–40): Contrasts the doom of the wicked against the radiant triumph of the righteous (*al-muttaqīn*): an attainment of supreme success (*mafāzā*)—orchards and grapevines, youthful companions of matched age, and full brimming cups, hearing no vain speech or falsehood. Verse 38 stages the awe-inspiring silence of the celestial court: *“The Day that the Spirit [Gabriel] and the angels will stand in rows, they will not speak except for one whom the Most Compassionate has permitted. He will speak what is right.”* The chapter concludes with the chilling, immortal warning of verse 40: *“Indeed, We have warned you of a near punishment on the Day when a man will observe what his hands have put forward, and the disbeliever will cry: 'Oh, I wish that I were dust (*Yā laytanī kuntu turābā*)!'”*

The Qurayshi chieftains gathered in their clubs beside the Kaʿbah, whispering, debating, and laughing mockingly: *“Will we really be brought back to life after our bones are turned to dust? Is this man mad, or is he seeking prestige?”* Sūrah an-Nabaʾ descends to silence their frivolous chatter: *“Nay! They will soon know! Then, nay! They will soon know!”* (78:4–5).

## Verse 78:1

> What are they asking one another about

**The Sūrah That Opens by Eavesdropping**

The new sūrah begins not with oaths this time but with an overheard street-scene: *ʿamma yatasāʾalūn* — about what are they asking one another? The verb is in the reciprocal form — *tatasāʾalūn*, they question each other mutually — and it is priceless as a genre-marker. After the previous sūrah's solemn winds, here the discourse begins downstairs, in the Meccan alleys, where the deniers are already talking. God opens His address with a record of *their* address.

Something is being conceded by this structure, and cleverly: the news has achieved what all news wants. It is being discussed. The mission's success is silently indexed in the very first sentence — people do not trade back and forth over what leaves them indifferent. Before the sūrah has said one word in defense of its message, it has documented the message's grip on its opponents. The *they* is the Meccan opposition — the Qurayshite gatherings who had now heard, repeatedly, Muhammad reciting warnings of a Day that would sort mankind. Their mutual questioning, the commentators note, was of two registers: some mocking — asking each other mischievously about the *new* resurrection tale, as one trades over a curiosity — and some genuinely unsettled, probing what they had heard with the anxiety of people who suspect it might be true.

The revelation does not step down into the street to engage them directly — it overhears, and then it answers *over their heads*.

**A Question That Confesses an Itch**

Look closer at what their mutual questioning already concedes: the report has reached the center of their attention. A community that had gods, trade, poetry and pedigree now spends its assemblies on *this* — the resurrection claim. The sūrah's first verse by this gives a strange comfort to the bearer of any unwelcome message: that the mockery of the audience, the continual asking *about it*, is itself the symptom of penetration. Indifference would have been the burial.

The reciprocal grammar also suggests rehearsal: they were not asking *for* information so much as asking *at* each other — trading the report in tones that reassured the group. Doubt manufactured socially is weaker than doubt reasoned, but it is far more durable, because it is maintained by belonging rather than by argument. The sūrah will not try to out-argue this social production; it will simply predicted and then display its Day.

**The Door of the Sūrah Stands Open**

Structurally, 78:1–3 forms one complete phrase — the question, its object, the division over it — before the gavel of *kallā* drops in verse four. Those three opening verses proportion a listener for what follows: first the world's exhibits (78:6-16), then the world's terminus (78:17-20), then the day in both its doors (78:21-36), then the majesty that stamps the end of speech (78:37-38), then the name of that Day and a standing invitation (78:39). Finally the warning's return to the present tense (78:40).

## Verse 78:2

> About the momentous news

**The Qur'an Calls Itself by the Name**

The second reading — the news as the Qur'an — enjoys a startling proof: in Sūrah Ṣād, the Prophet is commanded to say, in the first person: *“Say, ‘This ˹Quran˺ is momentous news, from which you ˹pagans˺ are turning away’”* (38:67-68). There the identical doublet *nabaʾun ʿaẓīm* is worn by the recitation itself, and its possessors are described by their response — turning away. The two passages together establish both readings as one doctrine whenever the term appears: the Book and the Day are the one momentous news at two points of its itinerary.

Which is fitting, since the Qur'an's own account of itself is that its content is the Day. To ask dismissively about a Revelation whose theme is the resurrection is to ask dismissively about the resurrection; to turn away from the Day's warning is to turn away from the new-arriving Book. The identity protects the Prophet, too: their quarrel is not really with a man from Mecca but with the largest news in heaven and earth, which the man merely delivers.

**Too Big to Be True — the Skeptic's Arithmetic**

The Qur'an identifies this exact move everywhere — *“Who will revive ˹these˺ bones when they have decayed?”* (36:78) is the objection quoted with its own author silenced by His own origin-scene: the One who first composed man from nothing has no difficulty with composition from dust. The sermon of 78:6-16 will run the same correction at greater length: the evidence of divine capability is precisely what the doubters are living on, sleeping under, and standing on all along.

**A Report That Keeps Its Name in Both Theatres**

Track the word through the Book's two theatres. In the world, *nabaʾ* = the preached report, mocked at evening assemblies. On the Day, the same content arrives *in person* — and the sūrah at its far end will name that arrival *the Day of ultimate truth* (78:39), where what was news becomes what is seen. News and event keep one identity across the theatres; the bridge between them is nothing but time, and time is exactly what the sūrah says is appointed and short.

**Measured by the Response It Demands**

Finally, the weight of the news is a scale for the response it asks. A khabar of rain deserves an umbrella; a nabaʾ of the resurrection deserves examined life, repentance, the rearranged table of one's priorities. Greatness in the object makes smallness in the answer a kind of theft — withholding from the truth a response proportioned to its announced mass.

And there is the sūrah preparing its pivot: immediately after naming the object's great weight, the text will note the variety of answers the object received (78:3).

## Verse 78:3

> over which they disagree

**A Whole Future Compressed in Five Words**

The street-scene completes itself: *alladhī hum fīhi mukhtalifūn* — about which they are in disagreement. With that clause the sūrah prints Mecca's spiritual weather for posterity: not rejection alone, not acceptance alone, but *ikhtilāf* — a scatter of positions, arguments, moods and camps. The city was divided over the news; the revelation's first portrait of its audience is a people at variance.

**The Camps of the Ikhtilāf, Drawn From the Record**

The sūrah's own sequel will address each camp in turn without addressing anyone: the mockers receive the doubled arrest (78:4-5); the wavering are addressed by the evidence-parade of the world itself (78:6-16); and the believers find their portion in the garden-furniture of the Day (78:31-36). The one verb *mukhtalifūn* so quietly sets the chapter's entire rhetorical itinerary: a divided assembly is about to be argued with, warned by, and sorted.

**Meanwhile the Object Refuses to Wait on Its Reception**

The deepest lesson of the clause is asymmetry. The disagreeing is all on the human side; on the report's side there is no disagreement — the news announces a fixed event with an appointed time (78:17). The city can send its measure of the matter into as many camps as it pleases; the matter does not multiply to meet them. Truth, in this sūrah's physics, does not divide to accommodate its audience.

Which is why the sūrah can proceed so unbothered. Having registered the ikhtilāf, it never returns to referee between camps, quote party-platforms, or out-argue the disagreement in kind. Instead it reloads the interrogation: *have We not...?* follows *have We not...?* — the evidence parade of bed, pegs, pairs, sleep, night, day, heavens, sun, rain, orchards — as if to show that the camps are all camped on the same Earth-gift while they quarrel about the Giver's promise. The questioners will be shown what they had in common all along: the furnishings of a temporary lodging.

## Verse 78:4

> But no! They will come to know

**The Knowing Will Come to Everyone**

The Qur'an elsewhere counts the stages of that certainty with surgical terms: certainty by report, certainty by seeing, certainty by being in the event. The sūrah's next movement begins installing the first stage by evidence: *˹But˺ you will surely see the Hellfire* (102:6) illustrates, in a companion sūrah of this family, how the knowledge that arrives as grammar today arrives as spectacle tomorrow. *They will know* is so the briefest possible form of the whole doctrine of the Day of Decision compressed into two words.

**Kallā: The Gavel Falls on the Conversation**

The arrest-word arrives: *kallā* — by no means, no indeed, enough of that. In Arabic usage *kallā* is the particle of desistance: it halts a line of talk or a state of mind and orders it to stop.

**Why the Arrest Is Not Followed by Argument**

The refusal to argue is, in its way, the harshest sentence of the passage: the street's objections were not even worth retaining as objections. Nothing of the evening assembly's cleverness is preserved against them except the bare fact that they disagreed (78:3) — the content of the mockery is not even transcribed. The sūrah responds the way eternity responds to a season's chatter: not with a debating-society win, but with a calendar entry.

**Deferral as Both Terror and Mercy**

A deferred certainty has two spiritual effects, and the verse trades on both. On the terror side: a future knowing that is *promised by God* is more certain than any present knowing; the condemned may, in the interval, continue their trading and their assemblies, but they do so inside an appointment already entered in the register (78:17). The deferral does not relax the certainty; it suspends only the timing.

## Verse 78:5

> Again, no! They will come to know

**Two Strikes, Two Knowings?**

The commentators have long asked whether the repetition merely intensifies, or whether each *kallā sayaʿlamūn* is bound to its own moment of knowing. On the second reading — attributed broadly in the tradition's early layers — the first strike concerns the knowing that arrives at death, when the mockers meet the *barzakh*-state and the mocked-of report begins wearing personal address. The second concerns the Day of Decision itself, when the knowing becomes public conviction before the assembly.

The doubling by occasion has Qur'anic precedent: the companion-sūrah of Rivalry sounds the same doubled arrest — *“But no! You will soon come to know. Again, no! You will soon come to know”* (102:3-4) — and then maps the stages of certainty onto seeing (102:5-7). The two readings are not competitors: doubling for emphasis and doubling for two appointed knowings reinforce one another, since the doom arrives graded — first privately at death, then cosmically at the resurrection — and each grade deserved its own strike of the gavel.

**What the First Knowing Will Prove**

If the first knowing is at death, its content is the truth of the warner: the moment the veil of the visible world parts, the dying mocker knows, beyond argument, that the recitations he laughed at were divine speech — and knows it one hour too late for amendment. The Book's dread formula for this is everywhere: remembering comes exactly when remembering can no longer change anything: *“but what is the use of remembering then?”* (89:23).

This first knowing also explains a curiosity of Meccan persistence: the instigators watched companions die — some as believers, some as mockers — and nothing visible reported back from the grave. The second verse's doubling says: nothing reported back *to the gatherings*, but everything reported *to the dead*. The grave is the first courtroom, silent and private, and its knowing is already in session beneath the same earth the assemblies were standing on while they joked. The second knowing is the cosmic one — the Day the sūrah will call, from verse seventeen onward, the appointed time: when the trumpet is blown and the graves release their account-holders, and the mocked-of news stands assembled in crowds. At that stage the knowing is no longer private conviction but shared spectacle: the heavens opened into gates, the mountains passed off as mirage, and every soul present to what its hands sent ahead (78:40).

**After the Gavel, the Exhibits Are Rolled In**

The courtroom metaphor runs the whole sūrah. Charges registered (78:1-3); arrest and sentencing of the register (78:4-5); and now, at verse six, the evidence-parade begins: a long chain of questions — have We not — proceeding through the very floor underfoot. The transition itself is a lesson in the Book's pastoral method: silence the posture, then teach the evidence. Not evidence first, then silence — because posture defines hearability.

## Verse 78:6

> Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺

With the gavel struck twice, the sūrah opens its interrogation-exhibits: *a-lam najʿali l-arḍa mihādā* — did We not make the earth a resting-lay, a spread-out bed? The form is *a-lam* plus a past-tense fact: a question whose answer is too obvious to require an answer.

If the deniers can be brought to say *yes* — and the interrogative chain will corner that yes eleven times through earth, mountains, pairs, sleep, night, day, heavens, sun, rain, crops, orchards — then the Maker established by the agreed exhibits is the same Speaker of the mocked news.

**Smoothness as the Mercy Nobody Ordered**

The Qur'an returns to this exact exhibit with its purpose named elsewhere: *“˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for ˹all of˺ you, and set in it pathways for you, and sends down rain from the sky”* (20:53). Laid out for you — the purpose-clause is printed there, and assumed here. The mihād is not neutral property; it is *addressed* property, made *for* the creatures who will be questioned about what they did upon it.

**The Floor Where Their Own Bodies Were Founded**

There is a sharper point under the surface, and the sūrah's parallel-argument does not even need to state it aloud: the deniers of the resurrection disputed a God who could not reassemble dust — while standing on the very dust from which their assemblage came: *“From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you”* (20:55). The mihād is simultaneously their origin-material, their lodging, and their grave-in-waiting.

## Verse 78:7

> and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs

**Awtād: The Desert's Most Familiar Fastener**

Interrogation-exhibit two: *wal-jibāla awtādā* — and the mountains as pegs. *Watad* is the tent-stake: the short, sharp, deeply-driven pin that holds a dwelling's cords against every wind. The image was intimate to people whose architecture was canvas and guy-ropes — a stake looks small above ground, but everything depends on its buried length and angle.

What Meccan eyes read as the scenery of permanence — the grey hulks standing around the valley, older than every genealogy — is re-described as *installations*: placed, driven, purposed.

**The Purpose of Pegging Is Stated Elsewhere in Full**

The sūrah names the function by the image, and the Book states it explicitly in companion passages: *“He has placed into the earth firm mountains. It does not shake with you”* (16:15), and again, *“And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way”* (21:31). The pegs are *restraint upon movement*: the earth-gift would pitch and stagger without its anchors, and dwelling would be impossible on a rolling, unsecured crust.

**The Stakes Are Exhibit A for the Day Their Case Is Won**

But the mountain-image carries a secret verdict the sūrah will pronounce over itself within twenty verses: those pegs, at the appointed Day, are *plucked*. The same chapter that installs the mountains as anchors will say of them, at the resurrection, that they are set moving and pass *like a mirage* (78:20). Making-and-unmaking, in one sūrah, of the world's most permanent-looking furniture: the most silent, massive rebuttal possible of the sceptics' claim that resurrection outstrips divine capability.

The parallel passages then take over the courtroom: *“And ˹if˺ they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the mountains, ˹then˺ say: My Lord will wipe them out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen”* (20:105-107). Asked about resurrection's plausibility, the Book points at the mountains and says: those will be dust first. The pegs are not just mercies; they are dated exhibits — temporary stakes in a temporary tent of the world, whose withdrawal-time is already set by the Peg-driver.

**The Pegs and the Pathways of Life**

The Book's extended treatment adds a homely sequel to the mountain-exhibit: *“and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way”* (21:31) — the pegged landscape is also passable, valleyed, travelled. The stakes stabilize, and the passes between them connect; stability and mobility are the two conditions of human habitation, and the Book attributes both, clause by clause, to the mountain-furniture. Even the routes of commerce — the very caravans of the Quraysh — ran in the passes of God's driven stakes.

## Verse 78:8

> and created you in pairs

**Pairing as Mercy, Twice Over**

The Book's own extended reading of this exhibit names two embedded gifts: *“And one of His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them. And He has placed between you compassion and mercy. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect”* (30:21). Pairing addresses solitude first — *comfort*, *sukūn*, the settling of one life into another's presence — and continuation second: through the pair, the generations pass down the bed of the world.

**The Pair-Principle Stamped All Over Creation**

The sūrah's *azwāj* also rhymes with the principle the Book proclaims cosmically: *“Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know!”* (36:36). The created-pairs motif is not human-only: plants come in pairs; the not-yet-known orders come in pairs; the signature is on all the registers of made things. Humanity's sexual duality is one chapter of a book of pairing that fills the created world.

The pairing-exhibit by this swings directly into the topic of the news itself: continuation *by* the pair in the world; summons of *every one of the paired* at the Day.

**Duality Designed, or Duality Happened?**

The Qur'an's own inflection is reflection: *“in this are signs for people who reflect”* (30:21) — the signs withhold themselves only from the unreflecting. The verse's interrogative form (*have We not...?*) presumes the reflectors will answer *yes*. For Meccans listening under the night of revelation, each in a household made possible by this very pairing, it was an intimate cornering: the Giver of all of it stands in the recitation asking — did We not make *you* in pairs?

## Verse 78:9

> and made your sleep for rest

**Subāt: The Vocabulary of a Merciful Severance**

The chosen word, *subāt*, is built on the root of *s-b-t* — to cut, to cease, as the Sabbath (*sabt*) is the day of cutting work. Sleep, in this word, is the divinely-installed *severance*: the tensions of the daylight hours are cut; the nerves' files are closed; the mind's continuous self-torment is interrupted by an appointed command the creature cannot override. God's name for sleep is a mercy-word before it is a biology-word.

The Book's parallel proclamation makes the triplet coverage explicit: *“He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover, and ˹made˺ sleep for resting, and the Day for rising”* (25:47). Cover, rest, rising — the three terms of the night-cycle, each one a gift-item, each unearned. The pairing of the same phrase-cluster in two sūrahs marks this as standing doctrine: the daily cycle is itself a revelation, preached nightly whether recited or not.

**A Daily Rehearsal of Death and Rising**

Here the sūrah's argument comes to its sharpest edge, and the Book makes the connection openly: *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ calls back the souls ˹of people˺ upon their death as well as ˹the souls˺ of the living during their sleep. Then He keeps those for whom He has ordained death, and releases the others until ˹their˺ appointed time. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect”* (39:42). Every night, souls are *taken*; every morning, most are returned. The world's most common transaction is a miniature of the Day feared and denied.

The sceptic who says the dead cannot be brought back has spent every night of his existence inside a counter-example: taken, held, returned, and never by his own machinery. The One who manages this operation across all of mankind — hundreds of thousands simultaneously each night, soul-economies of infants and kings processed in the same dark hour — is being doubted over a single, one-time, mass-operation scheduled for a fixed morning (78:17-18). The evidence parades itself in bed each night; the doubter sleeps in the proof.

**The Economics of Unearned Rest**

Gratitude-economics again: sleep is the purest form of provision-mercy in the sequence, because it cannot be mistaken for something the creature produced. Sleep cannot be bought against its appointed onset, cannot be resisted by strength nor summoned by will; the emperor and the porter fall under it in the same helplessness. It arrives as hospitality — the covering and severance — and it departs as wakefulness, a *return* that no one remembers performing.

If He tends every soul nightly, He is tending the books daily (78:29 will claim exactly this). The bedroom ends up being the exhibit-room where a careless mankind's *unwatchedness* hypothesis is refuted first, nightly, personally.

**Four Exhibits Deep, and the Chain Grows a Spine**

The interrogation now has a shape: floors below (78:6), fixtures around (78:7), fellows beside (78:8), and the person's own nightly taking-up (78:9). Direction and set exhibit one consistent Speaker — one bedside manner across all scales. The sceptic's cumulative position has become this: a God who cannot raise the dead *made* the bed they are lying on, *drove* the stakes around it, *created* their marriage-partner, *takes* their soul nightly, and *returns* it every morning.

## Verse 78:10

> and made the night as a cover

**Libās: A Garment Spread Over the World**

Exhibit five pulls a covering over the sleeper: *wa-jaʿalnā l-layla libāsā* — and We made the night a clothing, a covering-wrap. *Libās* is the garment-word — what a body is wrapped in, worn next to the skin. The whole world's nightfall is by this described as God clothing His creation: darkness lowered not as absence of light but as *raiment* — deliberately fitted, deliberately lowered, for covered rest.

**A Garment-Word With a Household Echo**

The word's domestic resonance is one the Book develops elsewhere with unforgettable tenderness: *“Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them”* (2:187) — *hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna*. Husband and wife are each other's garments: covering, nearest-touching, warmth-keeping. The same noun for night's covering quietly binds the exhibits of this sequence (spouses in verse eight, cover in verse ten) into one household: the day's workers paired and covered, the world's family bedding down under its shared cloth.

**The Triplet Complete: Cover, Rest, Rising**

The Book restates this exact mercy in its full triple form: *“He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover. ˹made˺ sleep for resting, and the day for rising”* (25:47) — cover, then rest within it, then rising after it, all attributed to one Hand at each station. The two sūrahs together carve the circadian cycle into a standing proclamation: the cycle is not physics-that-happens but worship-window that happens *every* night — provision, wardrobe, resurrection-rehearsal in one dark shift.

**A Cover With an Expiry Print**

Nothing in this mercy-chain is unqualified, though; the garment also preaches its own coming withdrawal. A cover, by definition, is not the thing covered — the world is the thing, night is its temporary cloth; Day is coming in which every covering is lifted: graves open, records uncovered, skies split into gates (78:19), and the hidden things shone upon by the court's light. The circadian cover and the Day's uncovering stand at opposite ends of one doctrine: modesty first, exposure at the appointment.

## Verse 78:11

> and made the day for livelihood

**Maʿāsh: Daylight Hours as a Wage-Window**

Sixth exhibit: *wa-jaʿalnā n-nahāra maʿāshā* — and We made the day a livelihood, a time for making-a-living. *Maʿāsh* derives from *ʿāsha*, to live: the day is named after its appointed function — the opening in which sustenance is sought. The sequence’s household logic completes itself here: the night covers (78:10), the day commissions. Neither half is accidental; both are *made* — and made consecutively, in the order the creature actually experiences them.

**The Two Signs and Their Two Offices**

The Book's own fuller proclamation of this pair names the offices side by side: *“We made the day and night as two signs. So We made the sign of the night devoid of light, and We made the sign of the day ˹perfectly˺ bright, so that you may seek the bounty of your Lord and know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺”* (17:12). The day is for bounty-seeking; the night-day alternation, together, is also for *reckoning* — calendar-keeping, the numbering of years.

Two offices, one gift: livelihood and countability. The alternation that feeds the household also provides the clock by which all terms are measured — debts, journeys, ages, and, ultimately, the appointed term of the world itself. The Meccan merchants multiplying measurements by day were, without noticing, living inside the same *measure*-doctrine the sūrah preaches at the cosmic scale: a God of counted days has the mechanism of the Final Appointment built into every sunrise (78:17).

**The Commission Is Also the Test**

The wage-window carries its embedded hazard: employment. Hours ordained for seeking provision are, by density, the hours most prone to swallowing the seeker's attention — the daylight's roster filling until the Giver of daylight is forgotten in His own provisions. The Book's sting-lines about diversion locate themselves exactly here: *“Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺, until you end up in ˹your˺ graves”* (102:1-2). *Maʿāsh* occupied becomes *maʿāsh* idolized — the window loved for itself till it becomes the wall.

**Eleven Questions Later, One Corner**

Count the interrogation's holdings as this verse completes them: the earth-bed below, the mountain-pegs around, pairing beside, sleep within, the night-cover overhead, daylight for winning bread — and still to come, the heavens above everything, the lamp in the heavens, the clouds, the rain, the vegetation, the orchards.

## Verse 78:12

> and built above you seven mighty ˹heavens˺

**Seven Storeys, One Above Another**

The number is given exactness: *seven*. The heavenly storeys are not the-atmosphere-as-haze but enumerated levels — seven shidād, one above another, as the Book states through Noah's own preaching: *“Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other?”* (71:15). Storeyed creation implies Builder-order; count implies construction-daybook; the correspondence of levels declares the heavens a planned structure rather than a drift of vapor.

The number seven in this Book also carries the teaching that created realities have *definite measure* — bounded, numberable, known. The sky that stretches past all roads is still countable to its Maker: seven storeys, held, stacked, roof above roof. What appears limitless to the tenant lies under a definite inventory upstairs; the sūrah is slowly assembling the theology of a God who numbers what men call immeasurable — for the same God will soon number the deeds those men called unremembered (78:29).

**Shidād: The Word for the Roof's Strength**

The adjective *shidād* — mighty, firm, tightly-wrought — is the quality the interrogation fastens on. The heavens hold. No crack has appeared in the roof through the lifespans of all the nations; no sag has been reported in the rafters of the sky. The roof's strength is habitability's guard: the stores held aloft, the courses of the lights kept in their lanes, the upper order preserved against the chaos it would otherwise pour onto the floor.

**The Roof as Scale-Preacher**

The rhetorical second duty of *shidād* is to humble the scale-sense of the disputing tenants. Meccan assemblies settled their affairs inside a valley between hills; drilling one's gaze upward past the hills, past the weather-roof, one meets a seven-tiered firmness that renders the heralded Day proportionately ordinary. Against such Builder-scale, the promised unbuilding of mountains and splitting of the ceiling at the Day (78:19) loses its outlandishness: a seven-storeyd house is not astonished at the disclosure of its builder's further acts.

The interrogation returns them to evidence-status: did He not *build* these?

**Above the Storeys, the Unbuilt Throne**

The seven shidād are the highest *created* architecture; beyond them, the Book's own survey continues upward to what is not of their kind: the Seat that encompasses the heavens (2:255), the Throne borne above all boundaries. The sūrah's later verse will call the Day-hour *the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, the Most Compassionate* (78:37) — Lordship named over exactly these storeys here exhibited. The ceiling has a Landlord.

That direction of reference matters to the sūrah's destination: the Day of Decision will not occur in a void but *beneath* this very roof's opened gates, with the ranks of angels standing in the newly passed storeys (78:38). The house-architecture of verses six through sixteen is so the map of the place where judgment will convene: floor holding the procession, roof opened to pass the hosts. The evidence parade builds the courthouse at the same time as it proves the Builder.

## Verse 78:13

> and placed ˹in them˺ a shining lamp

**Sirājan Wahhājan: The Sun Reduced to Its Job Description**

Exhibit eight: *wa-jaʿalnā sirājan wahhājā* — and We set a glowing lamp. The sun — the most magnificent object in the visible experience of any human being — is here introduced not by name but *by function*: *sirāj*, a lamp. The naming is a deliberate demotion of the magnified and a promotion of the function over the idol. What men might call a god, the revelation calls household lighting: an appliance, hung in the house's roof, burning on fuel not its own, serving tenants not its own.

The parallel passage through Noah's preaching runs the same service-name across both sky-lights: *“placing the moon within them as a ˹reflected˺ light, and the sun as a ˹radiant˺ lamp”* (71:16). In God's catalogue, the greatest luminary reads as a fixture — sited, lit, maintained, and (the sūrah will later imply) extinguishable at the appointment.

**Wahhāj: The Adjective of Steady, Scorching Light**

The qualifying adjective *wahhāj* doubles the instruction: blazing, searing, shining with burning intensity. The sun's office is not decorative gleam; it burns — a lamp with heat in its light, warming as well as showing, continuous without flicker. The twin adjectives of the two lamps at 25:61 and 71:16 make the same praise steady: it does not fail, it does not gutter, it does not leak smoke; the world's one great lamp is also the one lamp that never needs trimming.

Steadiness is the sermon: the lamp has burned through all remembered mornings, through every civilization's record including the deniers' own genealogies, without once missing an appointment. If the lamp's Maker keeps such precision in a fixture, why would He be suspected of imprecision in a promised Day (78:17)? The sun is the standing alibi of the divine calendar — the most regular thing in sight attesting the Most Regular's own appointment.

**The Lamp's Payroll: Offices Named in Later Exhibits**

The verse stands bare of a benefits-list, but the chapel of offices is catalogued in the sequence itself. The lamp makes possible the wage-window (78:11) — daylight *is* the lamp at work; it feeds the production-chain of vv15-16, since no grain, plant or orchard repeats its annual miracle without the burning lamp's energy; and it paces the very calendar the mockers lived by, setting their fasts, months and pilgrimage-times.

Each office, traced back, lands on a Keeper: *“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him”* (2:255). A lamp that burns without cease across aeons is burning *on* conservation — its continued lit-ness is a continuous act of being held. The attribute *wahhāj* by this unleashes the whole doctrine of providence: the blazing is sustained, the sustaining is wakeful, and the Wakeful One is the being asked to be believed about a morning when sleepers rise.

**After the Lamp, the Rain-Service Descends**

The interrogation, having toured the house from floor to roof to lighting, now comes to the house's plumbing — the water-service: clouds pressed, water poured down, and by it the whole spread of provision set on the table below. The sun-lamp and the rain-service work in relay: light for growth, water for life.

The audience-locating remains constant: *above you* (78:12), *for you* implicitly — the house contains them; the exhibits are all features of their own lodging.

## Verse 78:14

> and sent down from rainclouds pouring water

**Thajjāj: Water Poured Past Measure**

Then the pouring-adjective: *māʾan thajjājā* — water cascading, gushing in abundance, outpoured beyond drop-economy. When the press releases, the release is generous: the rain-word here carries the sound of volume, of the roof-stores emptying lavishly over the fields. God's open hand in the sky, opened at *this* adjective's pressure, comes with no ration-book: thajjāj is the opposite of drizzle-tally.

The descriptive noun *al-muʿṣirāt*, from *ʿaṣara* — to press, to squeeze, as juice from the grape — images the rain-clouds as presses worked to yield.

The desert audience, for whom a year of drought could end a livelihood, lived closest to the theology carried in this word: mercy overhead, real and near. Issued at command — the same pattern the sūrah states for the Day: near, real, held, and scheduled (78:17) — pressed at no pleader's haste.

The extravagance of the rain-consignment sets the measure for the whole provision-theology of these verses: the Giver's custom is abundance against desert, generosity against scarcity. This matters against the terrified suspicion natural to survival-cultures — that heaven's stores are scarce, that every prayer is overheard counting — and the sūrah answers with an adjective: no, the custom overhead is *outpouring*. The later reward-furniture of the sūrah (78:32-36) will wear the same lavishness on another register.

**The Descent's Destination Is the Table**

Rain's telos arrives in the next two exhibits — grain and greenery raised *by it* — and the Book's own full statement of the chain is worth setting beside: *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”* (36:33). Dead land, pressed clouds, cascading water, life, grain, eating: the entire procession of provision is displayed as a single continuous dispensation from the same Hand — from the inert soil to the morsel.

**Water, the Element of Life and of Counter-Life**

The Book's water-doctrine is a pillar: the same voice that says life came from water *“˹and˺ made from water every living thing”* (21:30) teaches by the same element the pattern of revivification — dead earth quickened by descent, season upon season. The rain-consignment is, in God's curriculum of analogies, the resurrection rehearsed annually: what looks terminally dead drinks and stands green. *He who revives the land after its death* is the recurrent formula the Book attaches to this cycle.

The verse by this appoints the rain-service as the sūrah's transition-witness: a God certified by every wet season can conduct one greater wet season — the graves' opening — with no new machinery. The mockers burnt their objections under a lamp (78:13) and watered them down with rains (78:14) they could never create. The evidence now totals sky, roof, lamp, clouds — the upper storey fully audited — and the chain descends finally to the field, the garden and the mouth, in the verses that complete the house's inventory.

## Verse 78:15

> producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants

**Bi-hi: The Pronoun That Keeps God on the Controls**

Exhibit ten completes the chain: *li-nukhrija bi-hi ḥabban wa-nabātā* — to bring out by this grain and vegetation. The little instrument-pronoun, *bi-hi* — *by it* — carries the column of causation: the water is the instrument; the bringing-out belongs to *Us* — God. The revelation never lets its audience slip into instrument-worship: clouds do not farm; water does not germinate; the consignment-mechanism overhead is handled from beyond the mechanism.

The chain's placing is also its sermon. The verses have now run the full production-line — pressed clouds (78:14), poured water, grain and green — and the personnel at every grip-point of the line is the same divine We. Modern machinery-thinking would split the line into processes; the Book keeps the line unified under one Will, and draws the hearer's attention to the unity precisely by stringing the pronouns: *We* sent down... *by it* We bring out...

**Dead Earth Is the Film the Sceptics Watch Yearly**

The Book names the setting with precision: *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”* (36:33). Dead — that is the soil between seasons: cracked, grey, apparently finished; then the consignment falls, and the stand of life rises where death was the only analysis. The sceptics' entire homeland experiences this film continuously: wadis that bake in August run in February; hillsides that seemed mineral break green.

**The Kitchen as Mosque of Remembrance**

The sūrah will soon display the sharpest form of that inversion — the condemned receiving, from the same Provision-source, the counter-provision of scalding water (78:24-25) — the two tables of God in the two doors of the Day.

**One Item Remains Before the Question-Mark Falls**

After grain and generality, the inventory closes with the most luxurious item on the desert-hearers' list — the orchard, densely interplanted, green stacked upon green under its own woven shade (78:16). The approaching of the *jannāt alfāfā* is fitted to be the final exhibit, the superlative that seals the yes-chain before the chain's question-mark is pressed home.

---

## Verse 78:16

> and dense orchards

**Jannātin Alfāfā: Green Woven Upon Green**

The inventory's closing exhibit: *wa-jannātin alfāfā* — and gardens, orchards, densely-entwined. *Alfāf*, from the root of wrapping and binding, describes foliage grown so thick that its branches interlace — shade stacked over shade, green upon green until sunlight barely reaches the orchard floor. The sequence's superlative has been saved for last: not a sprout or a field but abundance at the level of *embarrassment*, vegetation overgrowing itself.

And the word *jannāt* is the sūrah's slyest choice. It is the ordinary word for orchards here — and the selfsame word for the Garden of reward in the verses ahead (78:31-32). The earth's interlaced orchards deliberately share the Garden's name: God's previews are distributed in the world's backyards before His main promise lands. The sceptic who strolled a vineyard at Ṭāʾif was strolling through the pledge's advance-gallery — *jannāt* here, *jannāt* there, and the difference precisely the one the sūrah is about to define.

**The Orchard-Evidence in Reach of Every Meccan**

The exhibition requires no imagination: the hearers had seen them. The date-plantations of Yathrib's basins, the grape-vines and fruit-trees of Ṭāʾif's walled gardens — green islands amid volcanic plains — were the peninsula's standing marvels. The Book's own exhibit-text runs parallel: *“And We placed in it gardens of date-palms and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth, so that they may eat of their fruit”* (36:34-35). Palm, vine, spring, eating: orchard theophany was a weekend's ride away.

**What the Interrogation Has Purchased**

The chain has also purchased the correlate mercy: no one in the audience can claim the case went unseen. The evidence was beneath them, around them, above them, in their marriages, sleep, bread and orchards — election-day notices issued from every dimension of the house. After this, the term *momentous news* (78:2) is no longer a claim about a strange report; it is a summary of a housebuilt world whose Owner visits.

**From the Orchard's Shade to the Appointed Day**

The orchard holds a quiet foretoken within its shade: *jannāt* again — the Garden-block (78:31-36) will open with the same word's plural promise for the righteous; the sūrah's closing third will so be seeded in the same noun as its evidence-block.

## Verse 78:17

> Indeed, the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is an appointed time—

**Faṣl: The Day Named After Separation**

The Day's name here deserves its full weight. *Faṣl* is decisive separation — the cutting between true and false, deserving and undeserving, claimant and claimant; also *settlement*, the closing of a case. The Book's family of occurrences is by this summoned into view: the day that settles what was disputed (44:40), the day the mocked communities first named at its dawn (37:20-21). This is the sūrah of the momentous news's own name-rise: the news, still today receivable as controversy, will on that day be unraveled without remainder — and every ikhtilāf of verse three will stand resolved by the event itself.

**Mīqāt: The Calendar-Word of Appointments**

The noun *mīqāt* is the everyday appointment-word: the ḥajj pilgrim's scheduled and located stations are called *mawāqīt*; a meeting-time fixed in advance is a *mīqāt*. God borrows the machinery of ordinary life for His greatest scheduling: the Day is not described as a vague eventuality but as a *fixed entry* — placed, dated, unchangeable. The audience's own calendar-instincts supply the meaning: an appointment from God has the same status as an appointment in trade or pilgrimage, but it is one that no courier can cancel and no negotiator renegotiate.

And the word implies the full content that appointment-words carry: an appointed time with an appointed Keeper, who will be exactly where He scheduled.

**Why a Set Time Preaches Both Directions**

Scheduling preaches mercy first: a set appointment implies an *interval* — the now between announcement and arrival — and the interval is exactly where the sūrah's audience still stands. The pinned date carries an open space before it in which the disagreeing parties may still revise their camps. Elsewhere the Book states the deferral-logic in a simile of utmost restraint: *“And We only delay it for a fixed term”* (11:104) — the waiting is the wait of justice so complete it will not arrive even one innocent lifetime early.

And the same scheduling preaches terror: postponed-only-until. A fixed mīqāt cannot drift away; it approaches the disagreeing city with the exact necessity that the city observed in the sun's morning arrival (78:13). What is set does not have to be hastened by the mockers or slowed by their treaties; it simply *faces* them, calendared, advancing one sunrise every sleep-interval they are granted. The appointment-language so transfers the argument from plausibility to mathematics: time-to-live between here and a fixed entry.

**The Sūrah's Hinge-Stone**

Note the verse's structural position: it stands precisely at the hinge. Everything before it (78:1-16) was world and talk: the street's question, the audit of the house. Everything after it (78:18-40) is the Day and its two doors. *Yawm al-faṣl* is named in the exact center, the axis on which the whole sūrah turns — the sentence to which the evidence points and from which the decree unfolds. Form and doctrine coincide with unusual purity: the settled statement at the sūrah's center announces the settlement of all unsettled things.

## Verse 78:18

> ˹it is˺ the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will ˹all˺ come forth in crowds

**Yunfakhu fi ṣ-Ṣūr: The First Sound of the Last Day**

The appointment now receives its program of events: *yawma yunfakhu fi ṣ-ṣūri fa-taʾtūna afwājā* — the Day the Trumpet is blown, and you come in companies. *Al-ṣūr* is the Horn — the Day's opening instrument, sounded at God's command to re-summon every sleeper and every grave-guest. The Book's own program-poster for those moments is one of its most staggering verses: *“The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead... Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺”* (39:68).

**Afwāj: Arrival in Sorted Companies**

The procession comes *afwājan* — crowds, companies, large successive droves. The word images the arriving of humanity as an immense caravan-season, generations and peoples in waves: nations filing with their prophets, cohorts arriving by era, the whole numbered species in ordered movement. Procession-vocabulary replaces individual biography: in the convoy, a person travels with his *kind* — the companies of the like, the file of his works and his opinions.

The Book's parallel uses mark the sorted character: the deniers *“driven to Hell in groups”* (39:71) wear the same procession-noun on the dark leg of the journey; and the company-formation serves a purpose beyond mere crowd-handling. Companionship at the arrival is itself a first disclosure of the file: the honest recognize who they marched beside, the mocked-of recognize who sneered beside them, and the sūrah has already named the lanes of the sorting — transgressors one way, the God-wary the other (78:21, 78:31).

**The Sound That Ends the World's Noise**

The trumpet-as-image preaches with special sharpness against the *ikhtilāf* that opened the surah: the markets and assemblies were full of voices — the voices of mutual questioning. One blast, and the whole distributed argument ends absolutely: buying stops mid-price, the sneer stops mid-word, the earth's noise yields to one unanswerable Voice. The Book marks that same finality: *“the Day all will hear the ˹mighty˺ Blast in ˹all˺ truth, that will be the Day of emergence ˹from the graves˺”* (50:42) — heard in truth, as against all the hearings in doubt that preceded.

The voice that asked *what are they asking one another about?* has, by verse eighteen, assumed the single register in which no questions stand: the trumpet.

**Above The Procession, the Sky Is Already Being Opened**

The program-poster continues with what stands over the marching: the ceiling of the house (78:12) — audited as the strongest architecture in existence — is at this hour *being opened into gates*, unbolted in all its storeys to let the Day's traffic pass. The raging of processions below and passages above runs simultaneous: the house is dismantled from above as the tenants are emptied from it below.

After the sky, the mountains: the pegs driven at verse seven pulled up in the blast-schedule, marched into mirage. The order of the appointed program is exactly the inverse of the interrogation's furnishing order — roof and stakes unmade first (78:19-20), then the house's two appointed occupancy-states (the ambush and the garden, 78:21ff). The tenancy audited is surrendered in the same order in which it was furnished; and the trumpet's blast is the key turning in that surrender.

---

## Verse 78:19

> The sky will be ˹split˺ open, becoming ˹many˺ gates

**The Strongest Thing in the World Is Unbolted**

The program of the appointment continues overhead: *wa-futiḥati s-samāʾu fa-kānat abwābā* — the heaven is opened, so it becomes gates. The reverse-witness here is exact: verse twelve's seven shidād — the mighty, tightly-wrought storeys — are now *opened*, and the roof of the whole house proves to have been a door or set of doors the whole time, closed until its hour. The most permanent-looking feature above the heads of every creature is reclassified, in half a verse, as *passage*.

*Kānat* — it *became* — performs the change without a process: there is no gradual architecture of damage, no siege of the firmament; the sky's nature at this hour is simply *being-opened*, as if opening had been its latent property awaiting command. Divine renovation is not construction-time; the storeys turn gateway by fiat. Every later reader of 78:12 must now re-read it in this light: the heavens' strength was always the strength of *doors kept shut by their Keeper*, never of a structure independent of His latch.

**Why the Sky Has Gates at All**

Gates exist for traffic. The Day's operations require passage between the upper and lower economies of the created order: the descending ranks of angels (78:38 will show them standing in rows below), the delivering of the records, the passage of command and authorization. The ceiling's opening routes the administration of the Day: the government above moves unimpeded into the courtroom below — not as raiders through a breach but as officers through opened gates.

The register of *abwāb* — plural, many gates — also unwrites the sky's inaccessibility in a second direction: what had always been the utmost *beyond* of the human gaze becomes traversable. Men asked their whole lives what lay above the ceiling of sight; at the Day the answer is delivered not by answer but by removal — the gate-many sky stands open, and the order beyond it stands at work in view. The Book holds another announcement of the same event brevity: *“When the sky bursts open”* (84:1) — the splitting registered in one line, like the final page of a ledger being turned.

**The Unmaking of the House Proves the Made-House Doctrine**

Here is the argument the sūrah has constructed so smoothly that it nearly goes unseen: the interrogation furnished the world as a house (floor, stakes, cover, roof, lamp), and the appointment's program un-furnishes it in the same vocabulary — sky opened, stakes marched off (78:20). The condition of being *made* is continuous between both states: built and dismantled by the one Landlord.

Which is why the gates-verse is not spectacle for spectacle's sake; it is the completion of the sermon begun at *a-lam najʿal*. The temple-that-was-a-house proves to be the way-station it always was. Every gift of the furnishing — the cover, the lamp, the peace of the roof — was a *tenancy-fixture*, not an endowment, held on the Landlord's warranty, and the warranty's term runs out exactly at the mīqāt (78:17). The architecture's opening is the lease's expiry. There is an argumentative undertone for the original audience: demands that the sky be made to fall were among the Meccan taunts — and here the sky behaves, at last, *even more* than the taunts imagined: not crashed but *opened*, administratively, into gates that serve the very judgment the taunters had demanded be hastened.

The verses speak as though the procession below — *you coming in crowds* (78:18) — has eyes lifted upward to this ceiling: the crowd's first sight at arrival is the world being unmade above it.

## Verse 78:20

> and the mountains will be blown away, becoming ˹like˺ a mirage

**Suyyirat: The Stakes Are Called In**

The last frame of the world's unmaking: *wa-suyyirati l-jibālu fa-kānat sarābā* — and the mountains are set in motion, so they become a mirage. *Suyyira* is the march-order verb: the most deeply-rooted objects in creation receive movement-orders and move. The verse is the sūrah's own back-reference executed: those *awtād* drilled into the earth-bed at verse seven — the pegs of the whole house — are here pulled up, and the bed they steadied ripples with their extraction.

God had argued from the mountains' emplaced firmness for His power of keeping; now He argues from their *removal* for the Day's certainty.

**From Bedrock to Heat-Shimmer: The Word Sarāb**

The endpoint-word is chosen with desert-eyes: *sarāb* — the mirage, the quavering false-water on hot plains that looks like substance and is air. Mountains become *that*: the most granite-certain feature of the visual world reclassed into the category of *things that never really had the substance they seemed to*. The metamorphosis named is more than pulverization; it is a verdict on appearances themselves — the solid was provisional; the shimmering departure is nearer its truth than its rootedness ever was.

The classical images arrive in dues order: the Book elsewhere says the mountains are moved off, crushed, carded like wool, flattened until the land lies bare — *“My Lord will wipe them out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen”* (20:105-107) — and in another register — *“when the earth is flattened out”* (84:3). The end-state is a plain without landmark: the muster-field requires flatness because nothing is left to hide behind, no highland for the high, no crag for the shelter of settlements. Geography's advantages are wound up before the judgment sits.

**At the Flattened World, Two Enclosures Await**

Now the sūrah's travel-sequence brings the two destinations within one horizon: with the mountains gone, the terrain visible from the muster-field has only two landmarks — the ambush-station of the Fire (78:21) and the enclosure of gardens (78:31-32). The levelling of the world is the removal of everything except what is *for* or *against* the assembled; the geography of what matters is all the geography left.

Notice that both destinations are described as *waiting*: hell lies *in ambush* — mirṣādā — and the gardens stand prepared under the same Giver; the two outcomes pre-exist the arrivals, as places must that were *made ready* before the making of mankind's last generation. The flattened plain so reads like the unveiled truth of the world's own moral geometry: all the while the sceptics traded talk among their hills, two enclosures stood beyond the hills, already built. The hills hid them; the hills are now mirage.

**The Mirage as the World's Self-Portrait, Mainly**

Everything, then, depends on whether anything in life was ever *not* mirage — and the sūrah's answer is ready and will occupy its remaining twenty verses: the works sent ahead (78:29-30, 78:36, 78:40), the God-wary's savings of taqwā, the Lord named Merciful and the Lord of all the storeys (78:37). Against a world whose mountains are shimmer, the only solidities are the ones the Book has been preaching since its first sentence: the Day, the news about it, and the path back to the Lord who fixed the appointment. The ambush-verse announces itself next from beyond the flattened plain.

## Verse 78:21

> Indeed, Hell is lying in ambush

**Kānat: The Ambush Was Already Posted**

The tense is past: *kānat* — Hell *has been* — is lying in wait. The ambush was posted before the summons, before the warning, before the first dispute in Mecca. This pre-existence is doctrine, reported also in the register of readiness: *“Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers”* (3:131) — *prepared*, *uʿiddat*, the same finished-state word that introduces the Garden (3:133). The two destinations stand built; the procession of the Day files into what is already standing.

The age-old question of *when* the two abodes were made finds here its sūrah-local answer: whenever — *they already await*. The sūrah is not interested in the theology-debate; its pastoral point is sharper: nothing remains to be *prepared* for the disbeliever's doom except the disbeliever's arrival. The ambush is already staffed — time is not building the case against them; time is only walking them toward it.

**Ambush-Theology: The Old Enmity Made Visible**

The hunter-image also completes a theme the Book has kept warming: hostility between the Fire and the sacred destiny of man. The enemy that waylaid Adam at the tree now has its own place of ambush; the plan that began with a whisper in a garden ends — for the transgressors — with a station-master's noose in an unlit ravine.

But ambush-theology preaches its own consolation, too: for the God-wary, the ambusher is posted *not against them*. The very people the mirṣād awaits — the *ṭāghīn*, the excess-runners — are named in the following verse; and for the other company, another prepared-place has its own verse coming (78:31).

**Waiting as Greater Dread Than Arrival**

The power of *mirṣād* over any mere statement of location is its tempo: waiting, watching, *for ages* (78:23). Horror amplified by foreknowledge is the conventional wisdom of all literature of doom, but here the foreknowledge belongs to the place, not the hunted: the Fire already knows its people; the only ignorant party is the traveller. The verse's mercy hides right inside its terror: a warning delivered *this early* can still reroute the file.

**The Tenancy's Last Landmark**

Within the sūrah's architecture, verse twenty-one is where the world's removal ends and the sorting begins: roof opened, mountains shimmered (78:19-20), and then — the first standing structure in the world-to-come is the ambush-station. The mirṣād is, starkly, the first durable landmark of the hereafter's landscape — the building that rises at the end of the flattened plain, before the garden-report.

Its positioning in the two-destination economy also has pedagogy: hell is described first (78:21-30) and the garden after (78:31-36). That the terror can do its work before the hope perfumes the ear; the sequence matches the sūrah's own earlier couplet — Fire described long (77:28-40 in the sister-sūrah), garden brief and bright. Preaching to the unpersuaded requires dread's full paragraph first. The flight from the ambush must outrun, in the hearer, the saunter toward the garden — and for that, the Fire needed ten full verses before the Garden could receive any.

---

## Verse 78:22

> as a home for the transgressors

**Why the Fire Has a Class-Name Already**

That hell receives *the ṭāghīn* by name is one of the sūrah's telling clues about the Day's operations: the filing at the destination is already fully categorized. The abode does not receive vague sinners, nor mixed crowds awaiting triage in its courts: it opens its mouth to the *class*, under their class-name. The judgment has, in practice, already happened by the time the reception begins — the procession companies (78:18) arrive at doors that know them.

Class-names are, in the Book's filing, only ever collections of *verified file-patterns*: transgressing is a repeat-offence category, not an incident-report. God does not classify by slip but by posture — the ṭāghī are those whose excess was their structure. The translation's phrase *for the transgressors* preserves the specialization: the abode is *ṭāghīn*-configured; its heat, its drink, its ages-provision all matched to the overflow-natures it houses.

**The Two Addresses of the Sūrah Stand Drawn**

With this verse, the sūrah's double-iconography is complete: *ṭāghīn* here (78:22), *muttaqīn* there (78:31) — transgressors toward one abode, the God-wary toward another. The Book's dyads always preach: the classes are not arbitrary circles but opposite postures, both available to every member of the audience for the length of the interval. The reader's every fresh day is a fresh enrollment opportunity — the class-names sit open even as the abodes stand ready.

The dyad also marks the sūrah's honesty: it does not only threaten. The same care that describes the ambush's lodging describes the other lodging — so the verse's real function in the reciter's ear is to present a fork with addresses posted at both ends. No denier of the momentous news can, after 78:22 and 78:31, claim lack of clarity about the deuxitous map. The ambush watches onward only for those who decide, knowing all this, to walk in its direction.

**Crossing Back: The Boundaries Were Mercy**

If transgression is boundary-crossing, then the revealed boundaries themselves were protective mercies — river-banks, not prison-bars: the banks that keep a river useful and alive. The ṭāghī treated mercy-banks as tyranny-walls; the final address so completes a symmetry: those who could not abide banks are now contained, ages-long, in the *unbankable* place (78:23) — in a flood with no land to overflow onto.

The pastoral word for all who hear: every limit God posts is a posting on someone's behalf; prayer-hours, appetite-banks, speech-banks, wealth-banks — all shoreline, not wall. The sūrah's parade of furnishings earlier taught the world's graced order; this verse teaches the congruent truth that overflow ruins: excess in any channel — profit, appetite, pride-of-state — is the early phase of the *ṭāghī*-trajectory, homeward-bound for a home fitted for exactly that condition, age upon age.

**A Lodging Prepared Before Its Tenants**

The furnishing-thread that began at the earth-bed ends, in the darker direction, here: *maʾāb* — a readied lodging — answers *mihād*, the readied earth-bed (78:6). Two prepared dwellings for man's two stays: the smooth lay for the brief sojourn, the resort-home for the long one. God prepared the first without consulting the tenant and the second *equally* without consulting him; and between the two preparations stands exactly one variable: the tenant's own works.

## Verse 78:23

> where they will remain for ˹endless˺ ages

**Aḥqāb: Ages Stacked, Not a Single Number**

The duration-noun the verse chooses is a strange and heavy one: *aḥqāb*, plural of *ḥuqb* or *ḥuqba* — long stretches, ages. Not *forever* stated bluntly, but *ages*, compounded and pluralized — durations stacked upon durations, eras upon eras. The early commentators are preserved explaining scale: some held each ḥuqba to be a great age — and some reports speak of each as eighty years, the year itself counted at a thousand — while others held the term open: ages with no final term, one after another endlessly.

**Ages Against Their Little While**

Elsewhere the Book states the closure explicitly: no death there to end it, no lightening of sentence — *“Neither will it be decreed for them to die, nor will its torment ever be lightened for them”* (35:36). And in yet another register, the wish that the punishment be revoked is granted only refusal — *“Our Lord! Remove us from it!”* — with only the sentence's own stability as answer. The ages-barrier is impassable; the settled residence has no forwarding address within it.

**The Endlessness Debate, Handled With Open Eyes**

The community of interpretation has always handled this verse inside a wider framework of God's freedom: for the people of pure denial, the consensus holds the ages endless. And yet the Book itself has seeded the hope-doctrine that God *increases what He wills* and pardons what He wills — and the authentic stream of tradition records exits: the Prophet's own intercession leading to the removal, from the very Fire, of whoever carries *faith equal to a mustard seed*, who is then thrown into the River of Life and grows again like a seed by a rainwater stream (Saḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6560).

**Duration as the Fire's Primary Instrument**

The worshipping imagination takes the same lesson in the other direction: the duration doctrine dignifies every hour of the warning-interval. Whatever repents, believes and works *during* the little while accumulates its wages across cycles that will themselves never end — for the other lodging has its own ages, with the direction reversed, a permanence no one will ever want revoked (78:31-36). The stacked ages of the maʾāb are the dark shadow of a duration-doctrine whose bright side — a Garden without exit — is promised in the same Book to the God-wary.

## Verse 78:24

> There they will not taste any coolness or drink

**Two Words, Two Lifelines Cut**

The interior tour reaches the senses: *lā yadhūqūna fīhā bardā wa-lā sharābā* — they taste in it neither coolness nor drink. Two negations, two lifelines: *bard* — cooling, the relief of heat, the touch of what soothes; and *sharāb* — drink-in-general, the elemental answer to thirst. For people whose land made these two words the condition of survival, the verse names the withholding of exactly what Arabia prayed for. The ambush's lodging offers neither.

The listing is a bare-minimum catalogue: not luxuries denied, but the *essentials* — a place whose climate is never cool and whose fluids never salve. Paradise's paired promises are precisely climate-governed and drink-governed (78:31-36); hell's mirroring negation is the same two dimensions, voided. The Book keeps its two houses in strict architectural rhyme: everything the Garden-household multiplies, the maʾāb strikes from inventory.

**The World Where Coolness Was Free**

The verse lands hardest on habitual thought: in the world, bard and sharāb arrived unearned: shade was free to the scoffer, water drunk without remembering. The sūrah's early exhibits — pressed clouds, pouring water (78:14) — are precisely the mercies now revoked in this verse; the same Book stating *We sent down water* now states *they will not taste drink*: ministry and austere withdrawal spoken by one Voice. The accounts of provision remain in the audit; the flowing has simply changed destination.

**The Garden's Climate Ban as the Counterlamp**

The opposite household's climate-terms are held nearby for comparison: its inhabitants recline *“never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold”* (76:13) — neither extreme admitted into the Garden's air; the two poisons of climate drawn off there, the two essences drawn off here. Paired, the verses preach the sūrah's kindness-doctrine precisely: peace lies in moderation and provision; torture lies in the negation of moderations and the extremes that rush in to fill them.

Every verse of the hell-block therefore keeps the garden-block glowing by echo ahead: *not taste coolness* is answered by shaded couches; *not drink* is answered by the full cup, diḥāq, diḥāq-filled to the brim (78:34). The Book's symmetry-architecture preaches with the reciter's own memory — the reciter who hears one room cannot but measure it against the other. The two destinations rhyme in every verse so that choosing can proceed by rhymes.

## Verse 78:25

> except boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus—

**The Exception Clause: A Beverage-List of Torture**

The negation now receives its exception: *illā ḥamīman wa-ghassāqā* — except scalding water and ghassāq. The lodging, after all, has its drinks: two of them, and both are instruments. *Al-ḥamīm* is the water brought to ultimate heat — the fluid that can no longer be called refreshment, because possessors of it find it functions as fire made pourable. The allowance of drink, in this exception, is only the delivery-form of further torment: thirst answered by the one liquid that re-ignites it.

The Book keeps this same menu consistent across its hell-passages, establishing the doctrine that the Fire's drinks are themselves from the fire: *“Let them then taste this: boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus”* (38:57) states the identical pairing in the sūrah of Ṣād. And another passage describes the mechanics of what such drink does to the drinker: *“made to drink boiling water that tears their bowels”* (47:15). The lodging's meal passes through, and the passage itself is described anatomically — its entrails make the proof of the menu. The verse yields the deepest architectural insight into the Fire yet: hell is not only hot — it is *extreme*, the place of the poles, where every moderated comfort has been drawn off and only the spectrum's ends remain. The commentators of the tradition, drawing the contrast, observe that this is exactly the inverse of the Garden's condition, whose every description is of *temperate* plenty: no sun-scorch there, no frost (76:13). And for its drink, *“a drink ˹of pure wine˺ from a flowing stream, that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* (56:18-19).

**The Cup of the Arrogant, Answered**

There is also mirror-retribution in the beverage-list. The transgressors' social signature included drink-pride — the wine-cups of the elite assemblies, the boasting over shared bowls; and the lodging sets its cups accordingly: ḥamīm and ghassāq, passed at the maʾāb's tables with the same hospitality-language, now filled from the cellar of hell. The drinker's culture of Mecca's clubs is answered by the drinker's culture of the abode: same gesture, same rituals of passing, new contents measured to the old company's file (78:26).

Nor is it merely social metaphor: the Book's own account of the transaction runs through 47:15 — the righteous in a scenery of rivers, including a *river* of wine delightful to its drinkers, beside the transgressor with his one tax-cup of scalding water that cuts through. The rivers and the cup stand at opposite ends of the same leaflet; the mockers mocked when the first household was described; at the lodging, the mockers drink. The parallel stands complete with the sūrah's own garden-block (78:34): the full cup, the pure cup — there, and the pole-poured cup here.

**Two Drinks as the Inventory's Lowest Point**

The tour of hell's interior has travelled duration (78:23), deprivation (78:24) and now the allowance-submenu (78:25) — and the exception-clause stands as the abysmal low-point of the description: it is the moment the hearers first saw that the doomed were being *kept alive to drink it*. The lodging is inhabited, ages-long; and the drinks of the lodging serve the habitation, delivering the torment *through* the lifeline by which life is maintained: what sustains them is also what punishes them, poured through the single opening of the throat.

## Verse 78:26

> a fitting reward

**Fit as the Whole Doctrine of Divine Justice**

The adjective confronts the perennial objection squarely: is any duration-ages punishment *fitting* for acts committed in a little while? The wifāq-word carries the standing answer of the Qur'anic jurisprudence of reward: recompense is computed against real measure — not surface-duration but full weight: the Warning ignored, the signs denied every day of the little while, the self's own confession signed at the end (77:35 in the sister-sūrah), the choices locked in at death.

The objection's premise is also silently exposed: men measure by spans what God measures by *files* — and His file on each transgressor is complete (78:29). The one who matches with full knowledge of every circumstance — mitigations counted in, aggravations counted in — is the only being whose decree of *fitting* can be absolute. Human recoil at the inventory is the recoil of partial files; God's wifāq runs on total ones. The identical formula-word binds the two destinations architecturally: the Garden, too, receives its *jazāʾ* and its full-measure gift (78:36), and the earlier sister-sūrah stated the statutes in matched grammatical sentences — so We deal with the wicked; so We reward the good-doers.

The bright side exposes the mercy-slope of the identical principle: for the God-wary, the reward explodes proportion upward — bounties beyond the file, gift folded into reward (78:36) — whereas for the transgressors, proportion is held exact: no gift, precisely the file.

**No Injustice: The Book's Centre-Held Claim**

The fitting-word anchors one of the Book's most dogmatic themes: at the Day, no one is wronged. *“So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:7-8) — the atom-floor under all accountability. *“We will set the scales of justice for the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least”* the Book states of the scales; and the verdict-scene's self-aware cry is: *“You will not be wronged at all”* (18:49) — patterns of the same proclamation.

The stamp on hell's inventory is so the stamp on the whole court: what is served at the lodging is not rage but case-law, not conquest but correspondence. *“So taste, for your deeds — Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation”* (3:182) is the same formula completed one clause further: taste *because* fitting, and fitting *because* just — with justice itself rooted finally in the defect-free file.

**Proportion Gives the Warning Its Latin**

Fittingness is not only defence; it is also the severest possible preaching: destiny as *self-matching*. The lodging is not arbitrarily horrible — its horrors correspond — that is, they are *generated from the files of the habits* of its inhabitants: drink-pride answered with the drinks (78:25), ages-planned answered with ages (78:23), no-expectation-of-audit answered with the completed audit (78:27-29). The lodging is the transgressors' own record, extended into place.

The mirror-structure of recompense, once grasped, converts every reflection on the inventory into practical instruction: read the lodging's features backward, and one is reading the file-patterns that generate them — pride, overflow, heedlessness, rejection. The wifāq-word is the key the sūrah gives its reciters for reading: punishment is biography rendered in architecture; examine the architecture and reverse-engineer the lives.

## Verse 78:27

> For they never expected any reckoning

**Rajāʾ: The Word of Hope Turned to the Sense of Awe**

*Rajāʾ* is ordinarily hope — the brightness of anticipated good. Here its absence means withdrawal of *awe*: never reckoning-on reckoning. Some of the early readers gloss the sense as fear — *never feared the reckoning* — and the double valence is the word's own richness: expectation and apprehension ride the same verb, because anticipating the audit is itself the hope-and-fear of the God-wary, working together.

**The Second Charge Follows: The Signs Themselves**

The sequence ends at the archive: within three verses, the lodging's charges read, the Fire has produced what no earthly defendant expects at his trial — complete documentation, *everything* numbered (78:29). The no-expectation charge and the archive verse stand deliberately back to front: they did not expect because they did not expect to be numbered. The verse of the numbered file exposes that their whole no-expectation strategy depended on being unnumbered — a hope that only ever held up in a world where no one was counting. God was counting all along.

## Verse 78:28

> and totally rejected Our signs

**The Outward Charge: Rejection Proclaimed by Grammar**

Charge two: *wa-kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā kidhdhābā* — and they rejected Our signs with utter rejection. Arabic nails the totality grammatically: the internal-object form (*kidhdhābā* as the absolute-object of the same root) is the language's instrument for intensifying a verb through itself — they denied, *denial-ly*, to the limit of the word. The translation's *totally* answers the form exactly: not a case of single acts of denial but of denial as their comprehensive mode toward the sign-economy of God.

The significance of the grammatical doubling is structural: God will not let the charge rest on one refusal or one bad week; He cites the *pattern*. Takdhīb — the very class-word the sister-sūrah tolled ten times over (the mukadhdhibīn) — is here stamped onto the charge-sheet with its accompanying intensifier: interior expectation had died (78:27); outwardly, every sign that arrived was met with a No.

**At the Lodging, the Denial Itself Is Fitted**

Wifāq reads this charge too: to the class who rejected every communication that God sent, the fitting abode is the place where all further addresses arrive only as verdicts. No call goes in there but the summons that executes: the taste-command of verse 30; the interrogating calls of its keepers (39:71); the plea-deflecting pronouncements of its management (43:77: *they will cry: O Mâlik! Let your Lord finish us! He will answer: You are here to stay*). Communications continue at the lodging, but their genre has become verdict-only — because the addressees had, in their kidhdhāb-season, communicated one total rejection to every genre of guidance.

Even the drink becomes confirmation: they rejected the tasting-gifts, the merciest mercies — the cool water, the shade — so the lodging's drinks answer with the mirror-fluids (78:25). Rejection's oceanic totality is mirrored in reply by the abode's totality: nothing in the lodging is un-torment; nothing there is un-after-their-denial. The kidhdhāb-object so haunts every room of the maʾāb, as though the place itself were the final shape of their rejections, standing on the ground they had earned for it.

## Verse 78:29

> And We have everything recorded precisely

**Kullu Shayʾ: Nothing Outside the Ledger**

Between charges and sentence, God produces the file: *wa-kulla shayʾin aḥṣaynāhu kitābā* — and everything, We have numbered it, recorded. *Kullu shayʾ* is the unbounded formula — every thing — the same phrase the Book reserves for the totality of divine knowledge and the bounds of creation; applied now to the deeds of creatures, it means: nothing done has fallen outside the record. The secret and the public, the word and the gesture, the intention matured and the glance half-formed: everything, encompassed in a count.

**Aḥṣaynāhu: Enumeration, Not Approximation**

The verb *aḥṣā* is counting-terminology — to number exhaustively, to tally until nothing remains outside the enumeration. God's record is not a narrative summary with the small items elided; it is a numbered inventory where every entry has its own place. The Book's own cry on the Day confirms the granularity: *“What is this record that leaves no deed, small or great, unaccounted for!”* (18:49) — the small included, the great included — with the observed content inducing the unbeliever's famous exclamation of dismay at the report.

Enumeration also dignifies the good half of the record with equal exactness: the iḥṣāʾ that totals the transgressor's whole file is the same iḥṣāʾ that keeps every secret kindness of the God-wary, every atom's weight of good (99:7-8). Counting is not the instrument of wrath; it is the instrument of *justice*, run in both directions, with no favorites and no forgettings. The verb's use in a hell-context is the specialized dark side of an universal promise: God counts *everything* — which means the overlooked good of the weakest worshipper is as permanently numbered as the loud sins of the proud.

**Kitābā: In Writing, Where Nothing Shifts**

The mode-noun *kitābā* — recorded, in writing, by the book — completes the machinery: the count is not held in memory's possible-flux but *written down*, fixed, in archive-form. The description of the Day's procedure elsewhere completes the scene: the angel-scribes who write in the world, the individual volume handed to each soul: *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺”* (39:69); then the reading-out addressed personally — *“Read your record! Your own soul is enough to take account of you this day”* (17:13-14) — the archive opened under the owner's own fingers.

## Verse 78:30

> ˹So the deniers will be told,˺ “Taste ˹the punishment˺, for all you will get from Us is more torment.”

**The Increase-Clause: The Day's Darkest Sentence**

The attached clause is the darkest sentence in the description of hell anywhere in the Book: *fa-lan nazīdakum illā ʿadhābā* — you will have no increase from Us except torment. Every other economy of God runs on increase of some bounty for its inhabitants: ordinary time brings new days; prayer brings increases of light; the Garden runs on increase upon increase — *“with Us is ˹even˺ more”* (50:35). Here, of being increase continues as God's rule does not change — *We will increase you* — but the only currency of the increase is the abode's own: more torment.

Commentators across the centuries have preserved, at this verse, a judgement of utter dread: as transmitted through the learned chain (Qatādah, from Abū Ayyūb al-Azdī, from Ibn ʿAmr), a report records: *no verse was revealed that is harder on the people of the Fire than this one*. Preserved in the tafsīrs of Ibn Kathīr and others, the judgement reflects what every reciter feels at the clause: it is hope's mathematical closure. A system in which the only increase is increase of penalty is a system with no gradient of escape remaining anywhere in it — not by diminution, not by abatement, not by plea.

**The Mechanics of Ever-Rising Torment**

How such increase stands the Book details elsewhere, in verses that read as schedules to this sentence. Abatement answered by flare: *“Hell will be their home. Whenever it dies down, We will flare it up for them”* (17:97) — the Fire's own capacity maintained by rekindling, the heat re-fed whenever its level begins to fall. Skin answered by skin: *“Whenever their skin is burnt completely, We will replace it so they will ˹constantly˺ taste the punishment”* (4:56) — endurance itself renewed, so that tasting does not diminish by callus or by neurology's mercy.

**Why Increase-Only Is the Finished Justice**

It is also the logical completion of the fitting-word (78:26): increase-only-torment fits the class whose only direction was increase-in-overflow — as they added transgression to transgression in the world's file, the abode increases torment to torment, age on age. The verse's finished justice is chilling precisely because it is *familiar* — the family-likeness of what they built for themselves in life, completed at last with infinite resources and no interference from the world's brakes.

**The Register of the Pronoun-Shock**

The pronoun-slip also performs the sūrah's narrow-door psychology: the audience that began as *they* (78:1) and entered the procession as *you* (78:18) now hears the lodging's execute-command as *you* again — the recitation training ears to hear all Day-speech as addressed-to-me, in advance, so that today's registration might prevent tomorrow's pronunciation. The verse is terrifying in recitation because it was *meant* to be terrifying in recitation; terror now is the appointed alternative to tasting then.

## Verse 78:31

> Indeed, the righteous will have salvation—

**The Mirror-Opening: The Other Inna**

Across the divide of one stroke the whole register changes: *inna lil-muttaqīna mafāzā* — indeed, for the God-wary, salvation. The particle that opened the hell-block — *inna* (78:21) — is struck again in the identical position, as though the text were deliberately rhyming the two announcements: one Indeed for the ambush, one Indeed for the winning. God's voice speaks both certainty-forms in one grammatical estate — the Book's habit of matching the two great truths at every formal level is nowhere cleaner.

And the rhyme reaches further back into the charge-file: the ṭāghīn-named inhabitants of the ambush are answered by the *muttaqīn* — transgressors matched with God-wary, the two class-names that have been sorting the whole chapter since the charge-sheet (78:22, 78:27-28). Between the two names ran the entire content of the world's probation: a person of the street could migrate camps while the street lasted; at the Day, the names govern two fully-built estates.

In this way, verse 31 is a sentence that defines before it describes.

**For Such Honour Let the Workers Work**

The Book sets the same word at the climax of its story of confrontation in Ṣāffāt: *“For such ˹honour˺ all should strive”* (37:61) — mafāz-grade victory named as the proper goal of all striving. The verse at our sūrah's garden-gate announces the same object in its hometown scene: for the God-wary of this contested world, the winning registered as arrival has an address, and it is about to be displayed. The worker's vindication verse and the garden-gate verse state the two ends of one doctrine: strive here; the winning is real, named, and standing.

**The Class-Name Finishes Its Work**

*Al-muttaqīn* — those who ware wary of God — completes the chapter's own definition-by-pairing exercise of vv27-31: the transgressors were they who expected no reckoning and rejected the signs (78:27-28). The wary are those who expected the reckoning and received the signs with watchful yeses — the two postures that were open to every Meccan ear from verse 1 onward. The sūrah's charge-sheet of one class and the prize-state of the other are written on one shared axis: expectation of the audit as the mark of taqwā.

The wary-class is generous in its inclusions: not saints only, not prophets only, but the simply-God-fearing — people whose lives were run by the sense that they would be asked. The Book delights in making honest people into the terminal class of salvation, pledging again and again, in the pattern of *“For Allah loves the good-doers”* (5:93), that belief joined to wariness is the whole entrance-requirement. The prerequisites for the mafāz were always the plainest possible disciplines, hewn daily, by plain people, in the plainest conditions.

## Verse 78:32

> Gardens, vineyards

**The Inventory of Joy Begins With Earthly Names**

The salvation's furniture arrives as a noun-list: *ḥadāʾiqa wa-aʿnābā* — gardens, grapevines. Notice the Book's chosen vocabulary here: not candle-dripping abstractions, but the best words the audience had for planted delight — ḥadāʾiq, walled lush gardens; aʿnāb, the vine and its clusters. The Garden of the Day speaks in the phrase of the world's gardens because God's kindness, at the description-stage, condescends to the categories of human longing: the hearer is told the winning in words whose worth he already knows in his mouth.

The choice is also an abortion of drab conceptions of the afterlife: what is coming is planted, tended, bearing — a working green, not a pearly waiting-hall of vagueness. The Book's the teaching about the Last Day is insistently horticultural: God sows reward the way a gardener plants, and waits: the believer's works were seeds in the world; the harvest is the garden of the Day. The imagery that told the interrogation's evidence (78:15-16) becomes the imagery of the reward — the same God-growing, now unending.

**Aʿnāb: The Specific Fruit That Crowned the Arab Table**

The Book's worldly promise-chain runs in one unbroken straight line from the nursery-name in the interrogation — *And We placed in it gardens of date-palms and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth* (36:34) — to the winning inventory of this verse: the same pairing, grapes and greenery, earthly and post-earthly. God plants in the Day the exact pairing with which He proved Himself in the world; the proof-item (36:33-35) is by this delivered as prize — continuity of mercy-items on both sides of the veil, durability the only difference.

**The Chemistry of the Worldly Vine, Perfected Upstairs**

The named grape also hooks one of the Qur'an's dearest arguments: the vine in the world is already a sermon — one wooden creeper producing, out of dust and water, a berry whose juice can be bread-softness or else forbidden-intoxication; and the Book several times names this doubling as deliberate sign-craft. In the winning-inventory, the whole debate withers: the grape of the Garden yields only its unambiguous face — 76-style cups share their wine with no headache in them, no blur at all (56:18-19).

## Verse 78:33

> and full-bosomed maidens of equal age

**Atrāb: The Equality That Makes Companionship**

The second adjective is the social one: *atrāb* — of one age, co-aged, matched in years (singular *tirb*. The promise is generational harmony: no companionship-strife of disparity between the saved person and their companions, no conversation lost across age-gaps, no watchfulness of one side ageing away from the other. Co-ageing in the world was the gift of the peer-years that one only keeps for a while; in the Garden it is made permanent and universal.

The parallel verse in al-Wāqiʿah, founding the same promise with open vocabulary, states the making itself: *“We have created ˹their companions˺ of noble character, making them virgins, loving and of matching age, for the people of the right”* (56:35-38). Note the two moral adjectives around the matched-age one there: *loving*, *devoted* — the companionship's beauty is not attributed to symmetry alone, but to love built into it, character made for accord. Co-aged + loving + full-formed = the Book's complete brief of the companionship-blessing.

**Who Are These Companions? The Two Answers Held Together**

The readers have kept two frames on the question, across the sūrah-tradition: companions understood as the pure consorts of the Garden — the ḥūr, created, as 56:35 states. The people of the right — and the wider frame within which the Garden receives as consorts the righteous spouses of the world too, joined to their believing mates in faith (52:21's promise of family-elevation, 13:23's embrace of righteous spouses, 40:8's prayer). The two frames do not compete: the Book promises both companionships newly-created and families reunited; the Garden's household accommodates every honourable bond.

Which answers, at last, the Meccan wives and mothers who believed in the early years, whose agonizing question about their own position in the promised place the revelation repeatedly takes up: the promises are general in scope — companions for the saved, of co-age and perfect grace — and the believer-family of the world enters within those promises. The verse's feminine adjectives describe the promise from the standard address-angle; the wider Qur'anic witness (recompense for every soul, women addressed throughout the Book) holds the promise open in full for all of the saved.

**Against Two Maladies of Reading This Verse**

The Book's sensuous-honest verses have always faced two maladies of reception. On one side, prudery flinches: can revelation speak this way? — an anxiety the Book itself answers by its composure: the God who created these forms does not need euphemism to mention them, and the reduction-of-embarrassment is itself part of the point — desire is creaturely, and the Garden's companions are promised to creatures. On the other side, vulgarity-commercialization converts the verse into the all-enough image of reward. This the Book answers architecturally: two adjectives in one clause, then the list moves on to the cups of pure wine — and to the far larger blessing of undistorted hearing (78:35). Finally to the only unpriceable item in this world or the next: a reward *from your Lord* (78:36), the Giver overshadowing every gift named.

**After the Companions, the Cup**

The inventory's next furnishing follows immediately — the beverage of the household: cups, and not sips in them but cups *full*, filled past ordinary measure — the Book will soon describe their effect-opposites (no headache, no intoxication) as part of the perfection (56:18-19).

## Verse 78:34

> and full cups ˹of pure wine˺

The verse's force doubles the moment one sets it beside the drinks of the lodged sinners three verses above: boiling water and ghassāq there — scorched and murk-fluid, the two poles — cup and cup; and here the brim-filled cup of pure wine.

The parallel-darkness extends to the servers: at the Garden the cup is circulated by attendants whose description is luminous from the companion-passages — *“They will be waited on by eternal youths with cups, pitchers. A drink ˹of pure wine˺ from a flowing stream, that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* (56:17-19).

**The Two Negative Adjectives Are the Wine's Crown**

The Book repeatedly finishes its wine-verses with those negations — no headache and no intoxication (56:18-19); no harm and no drunkenness (37:46-47) — because they are the religious edge of the promise: wine fully itself, with its penalty removed. The drink of the Garden keeps the oenophile's whole desire — hue, bouquet, coolness, lift — and disconnects the mortal surcharge that earthly wine has always exacted: blurred reason, damaged mornings, the slow corruption of the drinker by degrees.

**A Feast That Circulates, Never Monopolizes**

The verse's last social note: the cup is passing ware. The parallel feast-verses describe the drink in motion — *“They will pass around to each other a drink ˹of pure wine,˺ which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23) — circulated services, shared rounds, the banquet's classic gesture. The winning-household's joy is so designedly *table fellowship*, not private hoarding: the fellowship imagined in the shared cup is itself one of the finest furnishings.

In the world, such circulating vessels were the class-sized toys of the landed rich — the wine-halls of the well-born, guarded by rivalry and turned sour by boasting. At the winning household the same act is restored to its uncorrupted objectivity: shared delight with no proprietor smaller than God, no rivalry, no reckoning of who stood whom a round. The society of the Garden is the first society whose festive circulation carries no politics — its cups pass because its Host topped them, diḥāq, and no guest can ever recoil into the creditor-debtor thread of earthly feasting.

**The Cup as Sentence in the Legal File of the Transgressors**

A further sober thread: the same persistence-verb of the taunt is here run in delight — the Book's two imperatives-of-drinking (Taste there; Drink-deep here) keep their form because justice speaks to both files in qualified symmetry: to the class of the lodging, taste-of-boiling with increase; to the winning class, brim-cups with increase — the increase that at 78:30 was locked to torment in one household is at 78:36 unlocked into gift in the other. The cup-filling of diḥāq is the beverage-form of increase — and the lodging's flare-up of 17:97 is its dark visualizing form.

The verse stands next to one of the chapter's best-kept pivot-traditions: the company-of-youth who serve the cups immediately recall the service-throng of the Garden. The place where the wine streams from recalls the named springs the believers were shown earlier (76:17-18's Salsabīl). And every stream-source traces finally to the Grantor whose name comes trending up in verse 36 and arrives in verse 37 as *your Lord, the Lord of the heavens and the earth* — the feast-item and the Host-name delivering each other, as the Book keeps planning.

## Verse 78:35

> never to hear any idle talk or lying therein—

**Laghw: The Empty Wind of Words**

*Laghw* is the idle, the vain, the sound with no content under it — talk said only because silence might be thought-provoking, chatter whose whole meaning is that it fills time. The Book consistently classifies laghw as hallmark of heedlessness — of a life with too much noise in it to reflect — and then, with this verse, reports laghw as the first abolition of eternity. The saved will not merely avoid idle talk; they will not *hear* it — the world of laghw moved out of earshot altogether, placed beyond their experience.

The parallel of rescue from laghw is stated with characteristic economy elsewhere too: *“Only those are saved who turn away from pointless talk”* (23:3's formula); and of the Garden of the Elevated it states: *“where no idle talk will be heard”* (88:11) — the same rule in two sky-places. The pious reader hears in it a double-register promise: deliverance from one's own stray words, and deliverance from everyone's — the curse of empty talk that fills worldly public squares lifted from the saved forever.

**Kidhdhāb: The Word That Haunts the Sūrah**

The second banned acoustic is sharper: *kidhdhāb* — falsehood — the very noun-root worn by the hell-file of verse 28, where the ṭāghīn were charged with utter rejection (*kidhdhābā*) of the signs. The word appearing here as the banned acoustic of heaven is the same word the sinners were charged with in their lives — an interior rhyming of the sūrah's two estates: in the abode of the chargees, the sounding-list is torment-fluids. In the winning household, the world's old falsehood never once reaches the ears of the delivered.

**Peace as the Final Ambient Sound**

With the two noises abolished, what remains to be *heard*? The Book tells the acoustic content positively in its twin passage: *“There they will never hear any idle talk — only ˹greetings of˺ peace”* (19:62) — salām as the standing lawful noise of the household. And the inter-cursory cups of the parallel passage carry the same acoustic order: the drink passes *“which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23). The feast stays express, even intimate — but it never becomes noise, never slides into falsity; the banquet-conversation of the winning household remains salām-shaped, by construction.

## Verse 78:36

> a ˹fitting˺ reward as a generous gift from your Lord

**Min Rabbika: The Giver Steps Forward By Name-Relation**

The pronoun turns the charter intimate: *from your Lord* — the address aimed, over the feast described, at God's own Messenger, the hearer of the whole sūrah in His night of revelation. All the household's furnishings, the cups and the companions and the silence, are assigned as what proceeds *from your Lord*: the sender of the provision is named in the vocative-relation as though God were saying to the Prophet, alone in the recitation: it is I who prepared this — for them, and for you.

The comfort-purport for the reciting listener is the chain of the pronoun: a promise delivered to one man in Mecca becomes, by the text's own widening, the charter of all the saved of every people. The garment *your Lord* is worn first by Muhammad. It is the same Lord who names Himself at the next verse in His universal title: Lord of the heavens and the earth (78:37) — the personal pronoun and the cosmic title adjoining so that the two registers of God are never experienced separately: intimate Giver, universal Sovereign.

**Ḥisābā: Three Readings of the Amount-Word**

The charter's amount-word calls the commentators to their registers, and the tradition preserves three overlapping readings. First, *ḥisābā* as *enough* — an amount adequate against every wish, enough and unending: the household measured-out to the fullest extent of satisfaction. Second, ḥisābā as *abounding*, generous — generous abundance marked by the lavishness already previewed in diḥāq's overflowing cups (78:34). Third — as noted by commentators joining the two destination-blocks — *fitting*, full-account: in parallel with hell's wifāq (78:26), the believers received measured recompense, *as true account* — their earnings precisely honoured.

**The Jazāʾ-ʿAṭāʾ Pair Against the File-Model**

This pair also closes, definitively, any thought that the household is purchased by the works themselves. The model of the fire-block was: file produces home — sin for sin, drink for drink (78:22-26). The garden-block's model is declaredly richer: the works place you at the household. The household's content outstrips the works by the gift-clause — so that the saved, entering, do not survey the garden as contractors checking delivery of an order, but as guests overwhelmed by their host's generosity at a table they could never have commissioned.

**From the Feast's Charter to the Sovereign's Title**

That sentence-bridge is the garden-block's last architecture: the banquet's Giver is not a feaster-deity within the Day. He is the Ruler of all the territories of the Day — of the seven storeys that the sceptics saw opened (78:19), of the earth they saw levelled (78:20) — and yet the name by which He bestows is, before any title of might, the Mercy-Name. The gift-verse leads directly into the majesty-verse so that no one may imagine the gift issuing from smallness: the most lavish Householder is the Holder of everything, whose very next words regulate the speech of every creature at His Day — by permission alone, and in truth.

## Verse 78:37

> the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, the Most Compassionate. No one will dare speak to Him

**The Title Runs Over the Feast**

The sentence of verse 36 continues without pause, and the Giver of the garden is now named in full power: *rabbi s-samāwāti wal-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā r-raḥmāni lā yamlikūna minhu khaṭabā* — Lord of the heavens and the earth and all between them, the All-Merciful: they have no addressing of Him.

**Lord of the Storeys the Sūrah Audited**

The title cites exactly the territories the sūrah has been touring from verse 6: heavens — the seven storeys built above (78:12) — and earth — the mihād below (78:6) — and everything between: the lamp, the clouds, the rain, the orchards, the moving caravan-world of the listeners. The garden-charter's Giver is therefore not a stranger-saviour from outside the audited world but its own Landlord, re-named at the point of reward as Lord-of-the-furnished-cosmos. Everything between is in the formula — the transgressors and the God-wary alike included, for they too are *between* — which is itself the silent import of the Day: every inhabitant of the interval will now face the Landlord of the interval.

The parallel verse elsewhere crowns the title's breadth one stage further: *“The Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between. The Lord of all points of sunrise”* (37:5) — every east and all that rises — so the listener understands: not one territory, not one horizon; the Day's Ruler is Lord of every direction from which light comes and every realm the light visits. The garden promised is therefore mere property of the Owner of all; its being bestowed is the smallest imaginable exercise of His holding.

**Lā Yamlikūna: The Address-Right Withdrawn**

Then the speech-law of the Day: *lā yamlikūna minhu khaṭabā* — they will have no khiṭāb with Him, no start-address, no ability of speech-as-of-right against Him. The formula's machinery is grammatical denial of *milk* — ownership, control — over the addressing of God: at that Day, speech toward God is not a faculty creatures exercise at will but a concession granted case by case. The chatter-verse's counterword: on the street they asked and mocked (78:1); at the Day no one initiates speech to Him; the two worlds' speech-rules are absolutely reversed.

The sister-sūrah already drew the same scene with its distributaries: *“This is a Day they will not talk, nor will they be permitted to offer excuses”* (77:35-36) — ban on speaking, ban on excusing — and the hush-offices shown here are stricter still: not even a *khiṭāb*, an address-title. The majesty-theology crowns the whole sūrah's method: the God who debated nothing with the street-scorners in their season stands, at the Day, under no obligation to entertain petitions, cross-examination or manifesto: speech itself is locked, pending permission — and the next verse gives the basis of the exceptions.

**The Hush as the Court's First Furnishing**

The hush also has a mercy-reading cherished by the tradition: that Day's silence belongs partly to *ease* — no one will be compelled to argue his own case through the atmosphere of wrath, permitted in the sūrah's earlier twin scenes (77:37-39). The intercessory permissions will simplify the procedure on the believers' behalf (78:38); and the ending of chatter is itself one of the consolations — as though even the ambient distress of unregulated talk in the plaza of the Day is mercifully shut off, except for words licensed as right.

## Verse 78:38

> on the Day the ˹holy˺ spirit and the angels will stand in ranks. None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true

**The Standing of the Highest Rank**

The regulation-scene's personnel take their places: *yawma yaqūmu r-rūḥu wal-malāʾikatu ṣaffā* — the Day the Spirit and the angels stand in rows. The named Spirit heads the list, singled out ahead of the general angelic congregation — the same manner in which the Book elsewhere singles Gabriel from among the angels, as when enmity toward the whole heavenly order names *His angels and His messengers and Gabriel and Michael* (2:98). The singling marks supreme rank: at the Day's court, the chief of the heavenly messengers stands named while the hosts stand ranged behind.

The manner of the standing is *ṣaff* — row-formation: order, not crowd; rank, not throng. The parallel scene arrives with procession-plural: *“and your Lord comes ˹to judge˺ with angels, rank upon rank”* (89:22) — rank multiplied toward the horizon. The Day's traffic is marshalled throng: heaven has its procedures, hierarchies and formations, and the Day the dispute ends is run by the most ordered gathering in creation, drawn up below the Throne in rows from which no voice rises unbidden.

**Who Is the Spirit? The Register Stays Open**

The commentators have transmitted more than one reading of *al-rūḥ* here, in broad tiers. The dominant: Gabriel — the Trustworthy Spirit expressly so-called in the Book's account of the revelation: *“which the trustworthy spirit ˹Gabriel˺ brought down upon your heart”* (26:193-194) — and so on this Day he stands to the fore of the ranks, the chief of the messengers between God and the prophets. A second reading keeps the referent open: a created being distinguished among the first creations, angelic or beyond-the-angels; a third reads the many-rows structure as angels in ranks with the Spirit standing alone.

The Book itself legislates where the discussion must end: asked about the spirit generally, the Prophet was given only this to say: *“Its nature is known only to my Lord, and you ˹O humanity˺ have been given but little knowledge”* (17:85). The verse's majesty-psychology, not its personnel-mystery, is what the recitation intends: the *rank-web* of the heavenly order, attendancy completed, complete silence until command — that the Day is run at the level of formation, and no creature below the Throne shortens its assembly by a word.

**None Shall Speak: The Positive Form of the Ban**

Which permission-classes the commentators recorded: the prophets, first — as Ibn Kathīr relates from the early authorities; and others add the martyrs, and the intercessors generally, since speech on another's behalf at that Day is itself the highest licensed transaction. One report-line holds that *who speaks aright* means those who say *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — testimony of God's unity remaining speech-licensed even there. The intercession-principle running under all of them is the Book's standing law of mediation before God: *“Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?”* (2:255) and *“No intercession will be of any benefit with Him, except by those granted permission by Him”* (34:23): permission, first and last. The true scale of the silence impresses most when held against the crowd's multitude: the Book himself draws attention, elsewhere, to the countlessness of the heavenly hosts: *“Imagine how many ˹noble˺ angels are in the heavens! ˹Even˺ their intercession would be of no benefit whatsoever, until Allah gives permission to whoever He wills and ˹only for the people He˺ approves”* (53:26).

## Verse 78:39

> That Day is the ˹ultimate˺ truth. So let whoever wills take the path leading back to their Lord

**Al-Yawmu l-Ḥaqq: The Day Is Named After Reality Itself**

After the court's regulation, the court's name: *dhālika l-yawmu l-ḥaqq* — that Day is the Truth, the Real Day. The definer *that* points across the sūrah's whole travelled distance: the day of the appointment (78:17), the trumpet, processions, opened sky, marching mountains, ambush and salvation and ranked angels — *that* Day — and its fundamental description is ḥaqq: the true one, the ultimately-real one, actuality as opposed to every provisional arrangement.

The naming has an edge against both destinations of the sūrah. To the world's sceptic, the Day seemed then *bāṭil-adjacent* — far-fetched, unlikely, un-real; the naming reverses the polarity: it is the *world* whose claims were illusory — mirage-mountains (78:20), little whiles, false enjoys — while the Day is ḥaqq precisely in that only what is real persists into it. Reality is the Day's title because at the Day nothing unreal remains standing — false fronts, false claims, false securities all stripped, and only true files, true speech, true estates remaining. Qur'anic usage sharpens the contrast throughout: the world is called diversion and play-passes-time repeatedly; and the same naming-rhetoric is used for deeds — the good deed is the ḥaqq, the justified rank; for scriptures — the Book is ḥaqq, reality-utterance.

The sūrah has been preparing this lemma throughout: the mockers asked *what* they asked about (78:1-2) — and the answer is, at the Day, suddenly perceptual-experiential: the momentous news *is present*. Their future-knowing (78:4-5) is the Day's own predicate: kallā sayaʿlamūn — the knowledge that arrives wholly because now every frame is true.

**Maʾāb: The Word the Hell-Block Already Reserved**

The choice-word of the destination is dovetailed with the sūrah's own architecture: *maʾāb* — the return-resort, the address of home-going — is the *identical noun* with which the Fire was named the home of the transgressors (78:22). God gives the same word in both contexts as the sūrah's most quietly devastating rhyme: both abodes are maʾāb — home-of-return — but one is taken in chains, the other taken by will — and the will-routed one is still available, from the world's side, to anyone reading the verse today. The Day's two maʾābs are both homeward; the only difference is whether the return was chosen and greeted, or awaited and ambushed.

Qur'anic parallels to the return-word support the wilful-path reading: the outpouring of the Book's instruction at this very verse-form rides the famous line of free counsel: *“This is the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve”* (18:29). Divine prerogative formulates human freedom: whoever wills, let him take. In our verse, God does not command; He *licenses*: let the one who wills take — the mercified structure of the invitation.

**Your Will Inside His Will: The Guard of the Parallel Verse**

The will-counsel is crowned in the Book with its religious guard, stated in the twin-verse family: *“Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord. But you cannot will ˹to do so˺ unless Allah wills. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise”* (76:29-30). The practical lesson of the guard: the offered freedom is real but derivative — human willing is licensed within divine willing; the road cannot be commanded by the walker. The combination keeps two cures at hand: against despair (you may will), and against self-confident independence (you cannot will but by Him who names Himself al-Raḥmān in the adjacent verse).

The will-verse so has a devotional edge very precious to the tradition: any person on the Day who sees the licensed speech of the saved's ranks knows that the will-road began with exactly the will that *this verse* inspires — the reading itself is the Call. One sitting with 78:39 recited in the interval has encountered, in the full formal character, the text's own offer to start that very will — *let the one who wills take* — issuing straight through the description of the Day of ranks into the heart of anyone not yet in the ranks.

## Verse 78:40

> Indeed, We have warned you of an imminent punishment—the Day every person will see ˹the consequences of˺ what their hands have done, and the disbelievers will cry, “I wish I were dust.”

**Qarīb: The Doctrine of Nearness**

The qualification is the shock: *qarīb* — near. The punishment whose day holds ages (78:23), escalations (78:30), and permanent estates is still *near* — the Book's two-scale calendar will not let either side be forgotten: what is vast in its interior measure is near in its approach. The Book's own announcement years earlier had put it with maximum plainness: *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺”* (54:1) — the event of the moon-splitting entered as the proof-marker that the Hour is already in pulling-distance.

**Qaddamat Yadāh: The Deeds That Went Ahead as Cargo**

The content of the Day arrives in the specialized formula: *yawma yanẓuru l-marʾu mā qaddamat yadāh* — the Day a man sees what his hands have sent ahead. *Qaddama* — to dispatch forward, to send ahead — is the Book's own terminal register for deeds: actions are cargo the self forwards to the Day, arriving there before the self does, awaiting inspection at arrival. What the hands forwarded is what the eyes will view: beholding the full accounted consignment of their own couriered conduct.

The parallel formulations frame the scene from both directions: *“˹then˺ each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind”* (82:5) — the sent and the abandoned both counted; and *“All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind”* (75:13). The scene's emotional mechanics are drawn in the Book's own crowning verse on the subject: at the sight of the fire, *“every ˹disbelieving˺ person will remember ˹their own sins˺. But what is the use of remembering then? They will cry, ‘I wish I had sent forth ˹something good˺ for my ˹true˺ life’”* (89:23-24). The seeing is not observational but conviction: at the Day, the seen cargo is understood — and it is precisely that understanding which regrets.

**Yā Laytanī Kuntu Turābā: The Cry of the Century of Wishes**

The attribution-tradition here is given in the registers of al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and others among the classical commentators to this verse. The critics of the later generation have treated the animal-scene report with markers of reserve in places — and the verse's meaning requires none of it: the wish of dust stands as the deepest self-victimage the Book quotes from the Day, rivalled only by the same wish's parallel: *“On that Day, those who denied ˹Allah˺ and disobeyed the Messenger will wish they were reduced to dust”* (4:42) — the two statements mirroring in the same cry.

**The Wish-Lineage of the Book's Regrets**

The cry joins the Book's growing catalogue of *yā laytanī* — the wish-form of the damned: *“Oh! I wish I had followed the Way along with the Messenger! Woe to me! I wish I had never taken so-and-so as a close friend”* (25:27-28) — wish-of-the-friend; *I wish I had sent forth something good* (89:24) — wish-of-the-lost-works; I wish I were dust — wish-of-unbeing. The quotations' function in recitation is phonetic pain: the particles of longing (*layta*) bordering each of the ends of a wasted chance, pronounced in the court from actual mouths — the shelf of second thoughts standing in the Book as permanent exhibits against every future squanderer of chance.
