# Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq (Chapter 65) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

---

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:1
> O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women, then divorce them with concern for their waiting period, and count it accurately. And fear Allah, your Lord. Do not force them out of their homes ˹during the waiting period˺, nor should they leave—unless they commit a blatant misconduct. These are the limits set by Allah. And whoever transgresses Allah’s limits has truly wronged his own soul. You never know, perhaps Allah will bring about a change ˹of heart˺ later.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Man Addressed, A Whole Community Commanded**

The opening address uses the singular, yet the ruling binds every married man. Arabic often speaks to a leader when the law falls on his people, and the Qur'an does this in several places. The Prophet ﷺ receives the command, and the community hears its own duty inside his. Commentators call this double force normal, not strange, and they point to other singular addresses that carry general force. The wisdom is plain. A rule given to the head of a community reaches each member with greater weight than a rule given to a crowd. No husband can claim the law was meant for others. Early accounts link the occasion to a divorce in the Prophet's own household, after which he was told to restore the marriage. Whether that occasion fixes the meaning or only frames it, the law that follows is general. It sets timing, counting, housing, and limits for every divorce. The singular opening therefore does two jobs at once. It honours the Prophet ﷺ with direct speech, and it removes every excuse from those who follow him.

**Divorce Must Face The Waiting Period, Not Fight It**

The command ties every divorce to its waiting period from the first word. *Ṭalāq*, from a root that means release, ends a bond; *ʿiddah*, from counting, measures the bridge between marriage and full parting. The verse joins them: pronounce divorce in a way that respects that bridge. In the standard case the bridge is three monthly cycles, as the Book states elsewhere: “Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺” (2:228). The husband must therefore choose a moment that lets that count run cleanly, not a moment that confuses it. Jurists read the phrase in two close senses. One sense sets purpose: divorce them *for* the waiting period, aiming at it. The other sets time: divorce them *in* a state fit for it. Both senses push the same way. A divorce that starts the count clearly is valid and fair; a divorce that muddles the count harms the woman and breaks the aim of the law. Timing is thus not manners. It is part of the act itself.

**Menstruation Bars The Pronouncement Until Purity Returns**

A man may not pronounce divorce while his wife is menstruating. He must wait until she is pure, then let another cycle pass, and only then decide, before any new intimacy, whether to keep or to part. This is the ruling drawn from the well-known case in which a Companion divorced during menstruation and was ordered to take his wife back and restart the process correctly. The reason is protective, not formal. Blood makes the start of the count unclear, and desire or anger in that hour makes the decision unreliable. Purity gives a clean starting line and a cooler mind. The law also knows its own limits. Where no waiting period applies, timing rules do not apply. The Book states the exception plainly: “they will have no waiting period for you to count” (33:49) when marriage ends before any touch. Counting, purity, and restraint belong together. The husband who honours them keeps the door of return open; the husband who ignores them shuts a door the law meant to hold open.

**Her Home Remains Hers While The Count Runs**

Neither husband nor wife may end the housing bond before the count ends. The verse forbids the husband to force her out, and it forbids the wife to walk out on her own. Two rights stand behind the two bans. She keeps a claim to shelter and support from him, and he keeps a claim to the marriage bond until the term closes. Conjugal life stops, but the house does not split. Early jurists stressed the point because custom pulled the other way. Anger wants distance at once, and families often press for it. The law answers that distance now can destroy a return later. Staying under one roof, with proper bounds, lets tempers cool and lets affection return. It also guards the woman from sudden poverty and the man from sudden regret. The house named here is called theirs in this verse, which fixes her right to remain. Whoever holds the title deed, she may live within those walls until the term ends.

**Expulsion Needs Proven Misconduct, Not Suspicion**

Only one exception breaks the housing rule, and the verse narrows it hard. A woman may be removed only for a proven, open indecency, *fāḥishah mubayyinah*, plain conduct that leaves no doubt. Suspicion does not qualify. Anger does not qualify. A quarrel, a sharp tongue, or a report from a neighbour does not qualify. The classical reading links this bar to the law of proof elsewhere: “˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves” (4:15). Four upright witnesses must establish the charge before the right to housing falls. Some jurists widened the exception to cover persistent abuse or harm inside the home, but even they kept the test strict. The act must be clear, grave, and shown. The strictness guards two goods at once. It stops a husband from inventing a charge to evict a wife he no longer wants, and it stops a household from collapsing over rumour. The law prefers to bear a hard wait over a false expulsion.

**Limits That Protect Both Sides And Leave Room For Return**

The verse closes its first half by naming these rulings as limits set by Allah, *ḥudūd Allāh*, lines no one may cross. The same word marks the divorce law elsewhere: “Divorce may be retracted twice, then the husband must retain ˹his wife˺ with honour or separate ˹from her˺ with grace” (2:229). Twice the door stays open; a third time shuts it, as the ruling for the thrice-divorced wife confirms (2:230). Whoever crosses the line wrongs his own soul before he wrongs another, because he trades a path back for a moment of will. Then the verse opens a window: no one knows what Allah may bring after this. Hearts change. Anger lifts. Need and mercy return. The waiting period exists to give that change time to arrive. A divorce begun in the right way, counted with care, and housed with honour keeps the future open. A divorce begun in haste, hidden from the count, or hardened by eviction slams it shut. The law thus holds firmness and hope together. It fixes the line, and it leaves the heart its term.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:2
> Then when they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or separate from them honourably. And call two of your reliable men to witness ˹either way˺—and ˹let the witnesses˺ bear true testimony for ˹the sake of˺ Allah. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make a way out for them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The End Of The Term Forces A Clean Choice**

The verse speaks to the hour when the count is nearly done and delay is no longer lawful. Two paths stand open, and both carry the same guard: honour. A husband may hold his wife and restore full marriage, or he may release her and close the bond. What he may not do is drift, stall, or keep her hanging between marriage and divorce. The parallel ruling in the longer divorce passage uses the same pair: “either retain them honourably or let them go honourably” (2:231). Honour here means a clear will, a fair settlement, and speech that does not wound. The waiting period was given as a space for thought; its end demands a verdict. Holding without love and parting without fairness both fail the test. The law thus turns time into mercy. It grants months for anger to cool, then it requires a decision that lets both lives move forward.

**Taking Back Must Serve Marriage, Not Punishment**

Restoring a wife is lawful only when the aim is true marriage. A husband who takes her back days before the term ends, only to pronounce a fresh divorce and restart the count, uses the law against its purpose. The Book blocks that trick in plain words: “But do not retain them ˹only˺ to harm them ˹or˺ to take advantage ˹of them˺” (2:231). Harm includes every move that traps her: restoring without affection, restoring to block a new marriage, restoring to press her into giving up money. The same ban covers the wife's side, since she too must not use the return as a tool for strife. Genuine return needs three marks. The heart wants the marriage to work, the tongue speaks the return clearly, and conduct after it matches the claim. Where those marks are missing, the honest course is release. The verse therefore judges intentions, not only forms. A return that looks valid on paper but aims at injury wrongs the soul that planned it.

**Parting With Honour Means Paying Every Due**

Honourable parting is not a soft mood; it is full payment of rights. The wife keeps her due maintenance through the term, her housing without harassment, and any delayed bridal gift that remains unpaid. The husband may not trim those dues to punish her for accepting the divorce. Nor may he spread her faults after the split or turn the family against her. Equity governs the split of shared life: “Women have rights similar to those of men equitably, although men have a degree ˹of responsibility˺ above them” (2:228). That degree is a burden of care, not a licence to dominate. Parting well also means fixing the terms for any child with calm words, since the child did not choose the split. Witnesses, writing, and clear dates help both sides avoid later denial. A divorce that ends with dues paid and tongues held back leaves dignity on both sides. A divorce that ends with cheating and slander plants hatred that outlives the marriage.

**Two Just Witnesses Guard Both Decisions**

The verse orders two reliable men to witness the close of the process, whether the end is return or parting. Their task is to hear, to remember, and to speak the truth for Allah if dispute arises. Some jurists read the order as binding, others as strongly urged, but all agree on its benefit. Memory fades, papers vanish, and angry claims grow. Two upright witnesses cut through that fog. The phrase for their duty is heavy: they bear testimony for Allah, not for a friend, a tribe, or a fee. That tie to Allah lifts a domestic act into worship. False witness here is not a small lie; it steals housing, support, or lineage. The witnesses must therefore be just, adult, and free from clear bias. Their presence also slows the husband down. A man who must speak his choice before two just men is less likely to speak it in jest, in rage, or in doubt.

**Faith In Allah And The Last Day Binds The Rule**

The verse does not appeal to custom, pride, or family honour. It appeals to belief in Allah and the Last Day. That anchor matters because divorce often happens behind closed doors, where no court sees the pressure, the threats, or the quiet cruelty. Only faith reaches those rooms. A man who truly expects a Last Day knows that every unpaid due, every false excuse, and every harsh word will be weighed. A woman who shares that faith knows the same about her own claims and conduct. The Book often ties family rulings to this pair of beliefs, since family life tests them daily. Public prayer can be seen; private justice cannot. The exhortation therefore warns and comforts together. It warns the strong that no closed door hides them from account. It comforts the weak that no silent wrong will be lost. Belief turns a civil procedure into a covenant act.

**Mindfulness Opens A Way Where None Appears**

The verse ends with a promise that runs through the whole chapter: mindfulness of Allah brings a way out. *Taqwā*, guarding the heart against Allah's displeasure, is named here as the key to relief. In this setting the promise has a near sense. The husband who keeps the term, pays the dues, and speaks fairly will find his affair eased, whether through restored love or through a clean parting that frees both souls. The wife who keeps her own bounds will find the same. Beyond that near sense lies a wider rule. Patient obedience in a tight place brings openings that planning alone cannot force. The classical gloss extends the exit to the greatest strait of all, the passage from Fire to Garden. The two senses do not compete. The same God who eases a hard divorce for the mindful eases the final judgment for them. The next words will spell out how that provision reaches them.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:3
> and provide for them from sources they could never imagine. And whoever puts their trust in Allah, then He ˹alone˺ is sufficient for them. Certainly Allah achieves His Will. Allah has already set a destiny for everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provision Arrives From Unseen Doors**

The promise begins where the last verse stopped: a way out, then provision from a quarter never reckoned. *Rizq* in the Qur'an covers more than coins. It includes food, shelter, calm, health, a sound child, a kind spouse, and time used well. The verse stresses the unseen path. A divorced woman fears poverty after parting; a husband fears ruin after paying dues; a father fears want with many mouths to feed. Allah answers that fear with a fact about His own giving. Doors open from sides the mind did not watch. A job appears, a relative helps, a debt clears, a heart steadies. The point is not that effort stops, but that result does not rest on effort alone. The mindful must still work, count, pay, and house with honour. Yet the outcome they hope for may come through means they never planned. Hope therefore stays rational. It trusts a Lord whose stores no eye has mapped.

**Trust Means Acting Within The Measure, Not Against It**

Reliance on Allah, *tawakkul*, does not cancel the law; it completes it. In this passage trust sits beside waiting periods, witnesses, housing, and support. The link teaches the true shape of trust. A man trusts Allah by keeping the count, not by skipping it. A woman trusts Allah by guarding her term, not by fleeing it. The messengers voiced the same trust under threat: “And in Allah let the faithful put their trust” (14:12). Their trust did not stop them from planning, migrating, or enduring harm with patience. It fixed their hearts while their hands worked. False trust claims reliance and drops the duty. True trust takes the means Allah set, then rests the heart on Him for the end. Divorce offers a sharp test of the difference. The husband who pays fairly while fearing need practises trust. The husband who withholds dues while claiming trust practises greed dressed as faith.

**Sufficiency Is A Promise, Not A Metaphor**

The verse states the reward of trust in absolute terms: Allah alone suffices the one who relies on Him. *Ḥasb*, sufficiency, means the absence of need for another prop. The form is strong. It does not say Allah helps, supports, or nears; it says He suffices. That claim rests on two facts. Allah owns every store, and Allah never breaks a promise. Human props fail. Wealth thins, friends tire, strength fades, plans misfire. The Lord of those props does not fail. The Qur'an joins trust to love as well: “Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him” (3:159). Love here is not distant praise; it is care that covers the one who leans on Him. Sufficiency therefore includes both outward and inward gifts. Outwardly, needs find supply. Inwardly, fear gives way to steadiness. The trusting heart stops begging every creature and turns its full face to one Lord.

**Allah's Command Always Reaches Its End**

No plan blocks the divine will. The verse affirms that Allah achieves His purpose in full, *bāligh amrih*, reaching the end of what He decreed. Human plots may delay a hope, but they cannot void a decree. A hostile family may press for an unjust divorce; a rich husband may hide his means; a false witness may twist testimony. None of that outruns Allah. His command moves through causes and beyond them, through courts and outside them, until it lands where He set it. This truth steadies the wronged. A woman expelled without right, a child denied support, a poor father crushed by need—all stand under a will that cannot be cheated. It also warns the wrongdoer. Evasion may win a season, but it cannot win the end. The decree that fixed truth and justice will arrive. Trust therefore has a ground stronger than mood. It leans on a will that never falls short.

**Every Thing Has A Set Destiny**

The verse closes with measure: Allah has set for each thing a *qadar*, a fixed amount and term. Provision has a measure. Grief has a measure. Waiting has a measure. Even relief has its appointed hour. The doctrine of measure answers two errors at once. Despair claims the trial will never end; greed claims the gain can be forced beyond its term. Both deny the set amount. Faith accepts that Allah weighed all things with wisdom: how much, how long, and through which door. The early summary of faith lists this trust among its pillars, covering both welcome and unwelcome decrees. In divorce the measure appears clearly. Three cycles, three months, or delivery—each term is fixed, not stretched by anger nor cut by haste. The one who honours those measures honours the Measurer. Peace follows that honour, since the heart stops fighting the scale and starts walking within it.

**The Poor Father And The Lesson Of Patience**

Early commentaries attach this verse to a poor father of many children who sought help and was told to fear Allah and hold firm. Soon his captive son escaped and returned with flocks, and the Prophet ﷺ told the father the gain was lawful for him. Numbers vary in the reports, but the lesson stays steady. Patience with mindfulness opened a door no plan had drawn. The story fits the verse without limiting it. Many straits resemble that father's: divorce that threatens poverty, widowhood that threatens loneliness, debt that threatens shame. The counsel stays the same. Keep the duty, guard the tongue, pay what is owed, accept what is due, and wait on Allah. Relief has a shape the sufferer cannot sketch in advance. It may come as money, or as strength, or as a new bond better than the old. The verse guarantees the arrival, not the form. That is why it ends with destiny. What is measured will come; the task is to meet it mindful.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:4
> As for your women past the age of menstruation, in case you do not know, their waiting period is three months, and those who have not menstruated as well. As for those who are pregnant, their waiting period ends with delivery. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make their matters easy for them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Completed The Law**

The verse answers a gap the community itself raised. After the ruling of three cycles came down, a Companion asked about women outside that frame: the old who no longer bleed and the young who never started, along with those carrying a child. The question was honest, and revelation honoured it with a full answer. This pattern repeats through the law. Believers ask, Allah completes. The exchange shows how mercy works in legislation. Gaps are not left for guesswork or harsh custom; they are filled by clear words. It also shows the rank of asking well. A sincere question about women's dues brought ease to countless homes after it. The verse therefore opens with care for those easy to forget. Old age, youth, and pregnancy each bring need, and each finds its term fixed here with precision and kindness.

**Three Months Where Cycles Cannot Guide**

Where monthly courses run, they set the count: “Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺” (2:228). Where they never ran or will never return, months take their place. Three full months mark the term for the aged and the young alike. The number is not random. Three months mirror three cycles in length and give the same space for thought, calm, and possible return. Jurists treat the months as lunar, counted from the pronouncement to the same date three moons on, with care for short months. The phrase about doubt—“in case you do not know”—covers the unsure case. Where bleeding is absent and none knows whether it may return, the safe count is months. Certainty is not demanded before mercy. The law gives a clear clock where the body gives none, so neither husband nor wife must guess at the end.

**Pregnancy Ends Its Term With Delivery**

For the pregnant divorcee the term is not cycles or months but birth. Her waiting ends when she delivers, whether soon or after many months. The wisdom is twofold. Birth proves the womb is clear, which is one aim of every waiting period. Birth also marks the moment when support, housing, and nursing rules shift to their next stage. Some jurists debated rare cases—twins, miscarriage, unclear signs—but the core rule stayed firm. Delivery closes the count. The ruling protects mother and child together. She cannot be pressed into a new marriage while carrying, and the child cannot be left without a named father and due support. The verse thus joins biology to law without harshness. Nature sets the length; revelation sets the rights within it. A woman who might feel most exposed—divorced and expecting—finds her term defined by an event all can witness, not by dispute.

**Widowhood Follows A Different Count**

Divorce and death part in their counts, and the verse must not be mixed with the widow's term. A widow waits four months and ten days, as the Book fixes: “let them observe a waiting period of four months and ten days” (2:234). That longer term honours grief and proves the womb with fuller certainty. Divorce in this verse takes the shorter paths: cycles, three months, or delivery. The difference teaches a wider point. Law varies the clock with the cause. A living husband may return, so the term stays open to return. A husband who has died cannot return, so the term turns to mourning, clearing, and honour. Confusing the two harms women. Imposing the widow's wait on a divorcee stretches her bind without cause; imposing the divorcee's wait on a widow cuts her mourning short. Each term guards its own good. The believer keeps them distinct.

**Doubt Keeps Her Housed Until Certainty**

The clause about not knowing carries a housing rule with it. While doubt lasts about the end of the term, she stays. No husband may expel her on a guess that three months have passed or that bleeding will not return. The classical gloss links this caution to the first verse's ban on expulsion: keep her housed until the count is sure. Doubt favours the one at risk, and here she bears the risk of homelessness and haste. The principle spreads beyond this verse. Where a right turns on a date—support, inheritance, remarriage—uncertainty must not strip the weak. Records, witnesses, and fair reckoning settle the date; until then the duty stands. This guard also disciplines the husband's tongue. A man who knows that doubt keeps the duty alive will count with care from the first day, write the date down, and call witnesses. Precision at the start prevents quarrel at the end.

**Mindfulness Turns A Hard Wait Into Ease**

The verse closes with the chapter's refrain in a new form: mindfulness brings ease. *Taqwā* here means keeping these very counts—months, delivery, housing—without cheating or cruelty. The promise fits the strain. An old divorcee may fear loneliness; a young one may fear blame; a pregnant one may fear want. Allah promises to smooth their affairs when they and their husbands guard His bounds. Ease may come as restored marriage before the term ends, since return stays open throughout it. It may come as a clean parting with dues paid and hearts calm. It may come as strength to bear shared walls with an estranged spouse without sin. The Book states the wider law behind it: “So, surely with hardship comes ease” (94:5). Hardship in divorce is real, yet it carries ease within it for those who stay mindful. The count that feels like a cage becomes a path.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:5
> This is the commandment of Allah, which He has revealed to you. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will absolve them of their sins and reward them immensely.

**Expanded Commentary**

**These Rulings Are Revelation, Not Custom**

The verse lifts the divorce rulings above tribe, habit, and opinion. They are Allah's command, sent down, not men's advice worked out. The claim matters because family law always fights custom. Old Arab practice often divorced with harsh haste, expelled wives at once, and left the weak without support. New pressures in every age push the same way: quick splits, hidden dues, angry words. Against both, the verse fixes the source. A count set by Allah cannot be shortened by anger. A housing right given by Allah cannot be voided by family pride. Calling the rules revelation also binds the conscience where courts cannot reach. Judges see papers; Allah sees pressure behind closed doors. The believer who accepts the source obeys in private as in public. The verse thus seals the past four rulings and opens the next ones with one stamp: this is from above.

**Mindfulness Brings Cover For Past Faults**

The promise joins two gifts: cover for sins and a vast reward. *Takfīr*, absolution, means Allah wipes the fault from the record and shields the soul from its harm. The verse ties that cover to mindfulness in these very duties. A husband who counts fairly, houses kindly, and parts or returns with honour finds his past faults covered. A wife who guards her term, her tongue, and her trust finds the same. The Book repeats this link in many forms. Private charity earns it: good deeds done quietly “will absolve you of your sins” (2:271). Faith with mindfulness earns it for former deniers: “We would have certainly absolved them of their sins and admitted them into the Gardens of Bliss” (5:65). The pattern is steady. Allah meets guarded conduct with guarding mercy. Small obedience in a hard hour outweighs easy obedience in comfort, and He covers much for it.

**Great Reward Follows Small Obedience In Hard Hours**

The reward is named as immense, *ajran ʿaẓīmā*, a wage beyond measure. The size fits the strain. Keeping divorce law hurts. It costs money when funds are thin. It costs patience when hearts are sore. It costs pride when kin demand harshness. Allah prices that pain highly. The verse does not list coins or ranks; it leaves the reward vast and open. That openness itself comforts. No believer can picture the full return, so none can despair that the cost was wasted. The Book elsewhere joins the same pair—cover and best reward—in one breath: “Allah will absolve them of ˹even˺ the worst of what they did and reward them according to the best of what they used to do” (39:35). Worst deeds covered, best deeds priced: that is immense by any count. The husband who pays while fearing need and the wife who waits while fearing blame both trade a short loss for a lasting gain.

**Absolution Works Through Good Deeds That Wipe Evil**

Classical commentators read the cover here through the law of good deeds effacing bad ones. Fair counting, just witnessing, kind housing, and due support are themselves righteous acts. They do not merely avoid sin; they erase it. Prayer, charity, and patience work the same way across the Book. The point steadies those who enter divorce with a stained past. None need say the law is for the pure alone. The law itself purifies those who keep it. A man who wronged his wife with harsh words can cover those words by parting with full dues and a held tongue. A woman who wronged her husband with defiance can cover it by guarding her term and speaking fairly. The door does not ask for sinlessness first. It asks for mindfulness now. Present obedience becomes the eraser of past fault, by Allah's leave and measure.

**The Promise Repeats To Steady Weak Hearts**

This is the third time in five verses that mindfulness draws a promise: a way out, unseen provision, now cover and vast reward. Repetition here is mercy, not filler. Divorce shakes resolve. Each stage brings a fresh urge to cheat—at the count, at housing, at support, at parting. Each promise meets one stage with fresh hope. The refrain also teaches how Allah speaks to strained souls. He does not state the whole law and fall silent; He walks beside it with comfort at each step. The effect is cumulative. A believer who heard the first promise and held firm hears the second with stronger trust, and the third with settled calm. By the time support and nursing rules arrive, the heart is trained. It expects hardship, and it expects ease with it. The verse therefore does double work. It seals what came before, and it braces the soul for what comes next.

**Law And Mercy Arrive In One Sentence**

The verse joins command and comfort without a seam. First half: this is Allah's order sent down. Second half: the mindful gain cover and immense reward. Law without mercy would crush the divorcing pair; mercy without law would leave the weak unguarded. Together they form the chapter's core logic. Rights are fixed from above, and hearts are lifted from above to keep them. The order of the two halves matters. Command comes first, so none treats the promise as a prize for vague goodness. Mindfulness means keeping these counts, this housing, this fairness. Then mercy follows, so none treats the command as a cold burden. Every hard duty carries its cover and wage. That logic will carry into the housing and nursing verses ahead. They will detail money and shelter, yet each detail will rest on this base. What Allah sent down must be kept; what is kept with mindfulness will never be lost.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:6
> Let them live where you live ˹during their waiting period˺, according to your means. And do not harass them to make their stay unbearable. If they are pregnant, then maintain them until they deliver. And if they nurse your child, compensate them, and consult together courteously. But if you fail to reach an agreement, then another woman will nurse ˹the child˺ for the father.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Housing Must Match His Means, Not Her Fear**

The verse fixes shelter by his capacity. A wealthy man provides roomy lodging; a poor man provides what he can; neither may dump his divorcee into a shed while he keeps comfort. *Min wujdikum*, from what you find and afford, ties the duty to real means. Jurists differed on whether food and clothing join shelter in this line, but all agreed shelter itself is firm. The wife stays where he stays, in the same grade of life they shared. The rule blocks two cheats. The rich cannot plead poverty to squeeze her out. The poor cannot be crushed beyond their power. Fairness runs both ways. The lodging must be fit, safe, and private enough for decency, since she remains bound by the term. A house that meets his own standard for himself meets the verse. Anything less, chosen to pressure her, breaks it.

**Harm To Force Her Out Breaks The Law**

The verse bans a quiet crime: making her stay so hard that she flees and drops her rights. *Ḍirār*, harm, covers harsh words, spying, cutting water or heat, crowding her room, inviting hostile kin, or spreading shame. The aim is the same in each form: force her to leave or to pay for release. The Book blocks that aim across family law. Husbands must not hold wives to harm them, and all must treat them with fairness: “Treat them fairly” (4:19). The ban protects the housing right from the inside. Walls alone do not shelter; peace inside them does. A husband who keeps her under his roof but fills it with menace has broken the verse as surely as one who locks her out. The test is simple. Would a fair man call this stay bearable? If not, the husband owes change, not excuses. The waiting period must be lived in calm, not survived in fear.

**Pregnancy Extends Support Until Delivery**

Where the divorcee carries a child, support runs to birth without break. Food, shelter, and care continue through every month of the pregnancy, whether the divorce was revocable or final. The length follows nature, not wishes. A husband who hoped for a quick end must still provide for a long term. The duty guards two lives at once. The mother needs rest and food for a safe delivery; the child needs a sound start. Cutting support in those months risks both. Some jurists extend the same care through nursing, since the newborn's need does not stop at birth. Others treat nursing pay as a fresh duty after delivery. Either way the principle holds. The father bears the cost of the life he helped bring. His means set the level, but they never set it to zero. Need sets the floor; ability sets the ceiling.

**Nursing Earns Pay Agreed With Honour**

After birth the mother may nurse, and nursing earns its wage. The verse orders payment for that labour, fixed by kind consultation. *Maʿrūf*, what is known and fair, governs the sum. It must fit his means, her need, local custom, and the work itself. The Book elsewhere sets the full nursing term for those who complete it: “˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years” (2:233). Two years of nights broken, strength spent, and freedom paused deserve real pay, not a token. Jurists differed on whether the wage covers only the child's needs or the mother's own upkeep as well. The stronger reading joins them, since a nursing mother cannot feed a child while hungry herself. Payment must be prompt and full. Delay that starves mother or child breaks the honour the verse demands. Fair pay keeps milk, mercy, and dignity flowing together.

**Neither Parent May Use The Child As Leverage**

Consultation must be courteous, and the child must never become a tool. The mother may not withhold nursing to extort a sum beyond his means. The father may not withhold pay to force her into cheap labour or return. The Book states the guard in one line: “No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child” (2:233). Suffering includes threats to take the child, to cut access, or to shame the other before kin. Honourable speech is the verse's cure. Both sides name their needs plainly, hear the other fully, and fix a sum both can live with. Witnesses or writing help where trust is thin. The child's face must stay before both eyes through the talks. He did not choose the divorce, and his food must not wait on pride. Parents who guard him in this hour prove their care beyond words. Parents who use him wound him first.

**Another Nurse Guards The Child When Talks Fail**

Where parents cannot agree, the verse opens a lawful exit: another woman will nurse for the father. The mother need not be forced into labour she refused, and the father need not accept a price beyond his power. The child still eats. The Book allows the same hiring elsewhere, with fair pay as the guard. The exit teaches three lessons. First, the child's right to milk stands above the parents' quarrel. Second, no mother must be compelled to nurse; her body and will keep their honour. Third, the father's duty to pay does not lapse when the nurse changes; it moves to the new nurse at a fair rate. The verse thus ends a hard passage with mercy all round. Shelter by means, no harm, support to birth, paid nursing, kind talks, and a backup nurse—each rule holds one corner of the child's world. Together they keep it standing.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:7
> Let the man of wealth provide according to his means. As for the one with limited resources, let him provide according to whatever Allah has given him. Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wealth Sets The Level, Not The Excuse**

The verse opens with the rich. A man of means must spend from his plenty on the wife he divorced and the child they share. Plenty here means real surplus after needs, not boasts or show. His payment must match his life: the food he eats, the house he keeps, the ease he knows. The law blocks the rich man's favourite plea. He may not hide income, delay papers, or claim sudden ruin to pay a mite. Judges may look at his trade, his home, and his habits to set the sum. The point is honour, not ruin. Spending from plenty keeps the divorcee from falling while the man stands tall. It also cleans his wealth. Coins that guard a mother and child in a hard hour weigh more than coins spent on pride. Wealth in this verse is a trust with a bill attached.

**Strait Means Pay What Allah Gave, Not Less**

The verse then turns to the poor. One whose provision is tight must still pay from what Allah gave him. Tight does not mean free. A small wage still buys bread; a narrow room still shelters. The poor man must give his share with the same honesty as the rich, scaled to his lot. The law thus guards two sides. It shields the poor husband from demands beyond his wage. It shields the divorcee from a husband who pleads poverty to pay nothing. Both sides must speak truth about money. She must not demand a rich man's sum from a poor man's purse. He must not hide a day's wage to dodge a loaf's price. The Book states the wider guard: “No one will be charged with more than they can bear” (2:233). Bear here means true capacity, not comfort. Some strain is owed; crushing is not.

**No Soul Carries More Than Its Measure**

The middle line states a law that runs through all duties: Allah tasks no soul beyond what He gave it. *Wusʿ*, capacity, covers money, strength, time, and mind. The Giver knows each measure, so the Judge never overasks. In support cases the rule caps the claim. A jobless father is not ordered to pay a merchant's sum. A sick mother is not ordered to nurse beyond her health. In worship the same law eases prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage for the weak. The comfort is vast. No believer need despair that Allah asks the impossible. The warning is just as real. None may plead inability while holding ability. Allah gave the measure, so He knows it. Courts must seek it with proof, not guess. The verse thus sets both mercy and audit in one breath. Mercy caps the load; audit finds the true load.

**Need And Means Together Fix The Sum**

Jurists differed on how to price support. Some weighed both her need and his means. Others capped it at her bare need. The stronger path joins both without excess. Her need sets the floor: food, shelter, dress, and care fit for her state. His means set the ceiling: no higher than he can truly bear. Between floor and ceiling lies the fair sum, fixed by custom, prices, and the child's age. Custom, *ʿurf*, matters here. A town's normal food, rent, and dress guide the judge. The verse's phrase “according to his means” points straight at that custom. Fairness also asks for speed. Late pay starves; partial pay shames. A sum fixed fairly but paid slowly fails the verse. Both sides should write the terms, date them, and call witnesses. Clear sums paid on time keep the child fed and the quarrel dead.

**Fathers Bear The Child's Cost In Both States**

The verse is read as proof that fathers fund their children married or divorced. Marriage does not create the duty; fatherhood does. While wed, he feeds the house. After divorce, he feeds the mother through term and the child through need. The duty does not pass to her kin, to charity, or to the street while he lives and earns. Where he truly cannot pay, kin and community help, but his debt stands. The rule honours mothers. A woman who bore, bore again in nursing, and guards the child daily must not also beg for bread. It also honours children. A child who did not choose the split must not pay for it with hunger. The father's purse follows his seed. That link is not punishment; it is the shape of care. Provision is love made countable, month by month, loaf by loaf.

**After Hardship Allah Brings Ease**

The verse ends with the chapter's widest comfort: after hardship comes ease. The Book states it twice for stress: “So, surely with hardship comes ease” (94:5). “Surely with ˹that˺ hardship comes ˹more˺ ease” (94:6). Two eases for one hardship, as the early gloss loves to note. In this setting ease may arrive as wider provision, a better job, a kind kin, or a restored home. It may arrive as strength to bear a tight sum without sin. It may wait for the next life, where striving to pay while poor opens a smooth road to the Garden. The promise does not name the form, so none may despair when one door stays shut. It names the Lord, so all may hope while every door is tried. The husband who pays fairly in straits and the wife who accepts fairly in need both stand inside this promise. Hardship is real, but it is not last. Ease follows.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:8
> ˹Imagine˺ how many societies rebelled against the commandments of their Lord and His messengers, so We called each ˹society˺ to a severe account and subjected them to a horrible punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Many Towns Faced This Same Test Before**

The verse widens the lens from one house to many towns. Countless societies heard Allah's commands through His messengers, then chose pride over heed. The question form—how many—means the count is long. ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh's host, and others stand behind the words, each with its own prophet, proof, and respite. Their stories fill the Qur'an so that no age thinks itself first or safe. The link to divorce law is direct. Those towns scorned limits on wealth, sex, and power; some in Madinah scorned limits on divorce, housing, and support. The disease is one: hearing a limit from Allah and calling it small. The cure is one as well: heed before the account. History here is not tales for nights. It is court record. Each town's file closed the same way when defiance hardened.

**Rebelling Means Pride Against Lord And Messengers**

Rebellion in this verse joins two targets: the Lord and His messengers. Scorn for the messenger is scorn for the Sender, since the messenger carries no word of his own. *ʿAtat*, insolent defiance, pictures a camel that stiffens its neck against the rein. Towns stiffened the same way. They heard clear commands—free the weak, weigh fairly, guard the home—then tossed their heads. The verse names messengers in the plural, which widens the guilt. These towns did not slip once against one man; they stood against every warner sent to them. The same pride creeps into homes. A husband who hears the divorce count and calls it tedious stiffens his neck. A wife who hears the term and calls it unjust does the same. The verse names the act for what it is. Defying a revealed limit is not modern ease or manly pride. It is rebellion.

**A Severe Account Leaves Nothing Unweighed**

Allah called each defiant town to a severe reckoning. *Ḥisāban shadīdan*, a hard account, means full audit without discount. Small wrongs count, hidden wrongs surface, excuses fail. The Book fixes the term for every people: “For each community there is an appointed term” (7:34). When the term runs, the books open. Severity here is justice, not cruelty. Towns that mocked weak believers, seized widows' dues, and shamed prophets face a scale that forgets nothing. The same scale weighs family wrongs. Unpaid support, forced eviction, false charges, and witness lies all enter the book. The verse thus brings the courtroom into the living room. A man who cheats in divorce may dodge a judge; he cannot dodge this audit. A woman who cheats in claims faces the same. The severe account makes private cruelty public at last, before a Judge who needs no papers.

**Horrible Punishment Fits Persistent Defiance**

Beyond the account stands punishment described as horrible, *ʿadhāban nukrā*, strange and dreadful. The word *nukr* means unknown, shocking, beyond the usual. These towns did not meet a light slap; they met ruin that matched their pride—blast, flood, quake, or disgrace that songs still recall. The horror fits the crime. Light faults earn light correction; settled defiance earns uprooting. The verse pairs the two pains in order: account first, then torment. Knowledge comes before wrath, so none can claim surprise. Messengers warned, signs shone, respite stretched. Only after all that did the blow fall. The pattern warns every defiant heart now. Respite is not pardon. Delay in punishment is time to return, as the Book says of the defiant: “I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless” (7:183). The flawless plan gives room, then it closes the room.

**Family Law Stands Under The Same Lord**

These verses land right after divorce rulings for a reason. Family limits felt new and hard in Madinah, as inheritance shares for women had felt before. Some hearts whispered that home is private and small, too low for divine wrath. The verse answers that whisper. The Lord of towns is the Lord of homes. The messengers who warned peoples also fixed waiting periods, housing, and support. Scorn for those “small” rulings is scorn for the same throne. The link dignifies the weak. A divorcee's housing right carries the weight of a town's fate. A child's nursing wage stands under the same guard as a nation's treaty. Belittling them is not clever; it is perilous. The verse therefore lifts the whole chapter. Divorce law is not paperwork. It is command from the Lord who called towns to account and will call homes too.

**Warning Comes Before The Blow, Not After**

Mercy shapes the order. Warning precedes account, account precedes torment. The verse itself is part of that mercy. It tells the end before the end, so the living can turn. Towns that fell had heard the same shape of warning and shrugged. Believers now hear it while their books stay open and their hearts still beat. The practical answer is plain. Pay the due, house her calmly, count fairly, witness truly, and part or return with honour. Each act is a step back from the towns' road. The verse does not ask for perfection; it asks for heed. Turn while turning helps. The next verse will show what tasting the end feels like, so that none chooses it blind. Here the call is enough. Hear how many fell, fear the same fall, and keep the limits that guard the home from their fate. That heed turns history into mercy, and it keeps a small house from repeating the ruin of great towns that scorned their warners and fell. Keep that heed, and mercy will guard the home.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:9
> So they tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and the outcome of their doings was ˹total˺ loss.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tasting Means Pain That Cannot Be Denied**

The verse moves from warning to fact: they tasted. *Dhāqat*, tasting, means direct touch, not hearsay. News of torment can be doubted; its flavour cannot. The towns that laughed at messengers felt ruin on their tongues—fear at night, disgrace at dawn, hunger, quake, or sword. The word also fits the crime. They savoured defiance like sweet drink; Allah made them savour its dregs. Tasting comes in this life first. Crops fail, trade thins, homes split, hearts harden. Then the fuller cup waits beyond. The verse uses the past tense, since their end is fixed and known. For the living the tense is a mirror. Present ease while defying limits is not safety; it is the sweet sip before the bitter. Only return turns the cup away. Once the term closes, the flavour stays.

**Evil Consequences Grow From Evil Sowing**

The pain they tasted is named as the fruit of their own affair. *Wabāl amrihā*, the heavy evil of their course, means ruin grown from seed they sowed. Allah did not plant it for them; they planted it and He let it grow. False gods, rigged scales, seized dues, mocked prophets—each seed sprouted its own thorn. The law is steady across the Book. Longer life for the defiant only piles the harvest: “They are only given more time to increase in sin” (3:178). Time waters what the heart plants. Good seed grows shade; bad seed grows fire. Family wrongs follow the same soil. A husband who sows eviction reaps hatred. A wife who sows false claims reaps distrust. A witness who sows lies reaps shame. The verse thus roots torment in deed, not in fate's whim. None tastes but what his hands cooked. The cook may blame the fire, but the dish is his.

**Loss Covers Both Worlds, Not One**

Their end is named as loss, *khusrā*, utter failure. The word in trade means capital gone with no gain. These towns spent strength, wealth, and years, then gained ruin. Loss here spans two markets. In this world they lost peace, honour, and root; travellers pass their stones and learn. In the next they lose mercy, light, and return; no plea reopens the trade. The *total* in the translation marks the full sweep. Nothing salvaged, nothing carried over. The verse thus answers the whisper that defiance pays. It pays for a season, then it bankrupts. Contrast the mindful in this chapter, who gain a way out, unseen provision, cover, and immense reward. Two paths, two ends: gain upon gain, loss upon loss. The choice stands before every home that hears limits and weighs them. Keep them and profit; scorn them and spend all for nothing.

**Regret After The Term Helps No One**

Early glosses add the sting: they regretted when regret no longer helped. While messengers called, they mocked. When the account opened, they wept. Tears after the term do not reopen it. The Book shows that late cry in many towns: pleas to return, to warn kin, to do good at last—all denied. The point is not that Allah loves to refuse; it is that terms mean something. A waiting period that never ends is no law; a respite that never closes is no test. Mercy gave years; justice closes the door. The living must learn the timing of sorrow. Regret now opens doors. A husband who owns his harsh divorce can still pay, house, and speak fairly. A wife who owns her false claim can still drop it and seek pardon. Regret after death or after ruin is knowledge without power. The verse urges early tears. Weep now, change now, gain now.

**The Warning Fits The Divorce Law Before It**

These two verses guard the seven before them. Divorce limits cut against old pride: keep her housed, pay her due, count with care, witness truly. Hearts that loved quick, cheap parting heard those limits as insult. Allah answers with towns that called His limits insult and fell. The parallel is exact. They scorned prophets over idols and scales; some scorn messengers over women and children. The Lord is one, the scorn is one, the end warns one. The Book states the court rule behind it: “so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. And none will be wronged” (45:22). No wrong is too small for that court, no victim too weak for it. A divorcee's tear weighs there; a child's hunger weighs there. The verses therefore lift family justice to cosmic height. Guarding a waiting wife is standing with the warned towns' opposite: heed, not pride.

**Loss Warns The Living While Time Remains**

The verse ends the towns' tale but opens the living's chance. They tasted; you still choose. Loss described is mercy to those who have not yet lost. Parents can still teach sons that divorce has limits. Judges can still fix fair sums. Husbands can still house calmly. Wives can still claim justly. Each heedful act steps off the losers' road. The verse's brevity adds force. Two short lines close long histories: tasted evil, ended in loss. No detail of fire or flood is needed; the end is enough. Brevity also speeds the turn to hope. The next verse will name the Fire prepared, then call people of reason and faith to fear Allah and heed the Reminder. Warning and mercy walk together. Loss has spoken. Now the Reminder speaks. Blessed are those who hear the second before they taste the first. Their gain will be lasting, and their Lord will guard their homes and hearts. Walk that road, and loss will never claim you.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:10
> Allah has ˹also˺ prepared for them a severe punishment. So fear Allah, O people of reason and faith. Allah has indeed revealed to you a Reminder,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Fire Prepared While Mercy Still Calls**

The verse turns from past towns to the fire that waits their likes. Allah has prepared—*aʿadda*, made ready—a severe torment. The past tense matters. The fire is not a threat to be built later; it stands furnished now. Its fuel, keepers, and heat wait. Preparation proves resolve. A king who builds a prison has judged the crime worthy of it. Yet the verse speaks the warning while breath lasts, which proves mercy. None hearing it has yet entered what is prepared. The door of return stands open beside the pit. The Book elsewhere warns that delay is not pardon: “We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend” (68:44). Gradual drawing feels like ease—wealth grows, health holds—until the prepared place opens. The verse cuts that sleep. What is ready will be filled; wisdom fills Gardens instead through heed.

**Fear Allah, O Minds That Believe**

The call joins two titles: people of intellect and people of faith. *Ulū al-albāb*, possessors of hearts and minds, means those who grasp the core of things, not the shell. Belief adds trust to grasp. Mind without faith sees signs and calls them chance; faith without mind follows zeal without roots. Joined, they form the hearer this chapter wants. The order is fear—*ittaqū*, guard yourselves by guarding His bounds. Fear here is not panic but care: count fairly, house kindly, pay duly, witness truly. Each guard is a brick against the prepared fire. The call is plural and public, yet it lands on each chest alone. No tribe fears for a man; no scholar's mind covers a heedless heart. Reason must bow, and faith must work. Where both kneel, the severe torment stays a warning heard, never a home entered.

**Intellect Means Seeing The Inner Weight**

True intellect in the Qur'an is not quick wit or fine speech. It is sight of ends inside acts. The clever cheat sees gain in evicting a wife; the wise mind sees fire in the same act. The Book praises such sight across many chapters, where possessors of minds take heed from stories, scales, and ends. Their mark is transfer: they carry lessons from towns to homes, from ancients to selves. A ruined town is not old stones to them; it is a mirror. A divorce limit is not paperwork; it is a shield. The verse therefore tests every hearer. Does he hear “severe punishment” as poetry, or does he feel its heat on his own ledger? Intellect that stops at admiration is not intellect here. Intellect that moves the hand—to pay, to house, to forgive—is. Faith feeds that move with hope: the same Lord who prepared fire prepared Gardens for the mindful.

**The Reminder Has Already Come Down**

Hope arrives in the last line: Allah has sent down a Reminder. *Dhikr*, remembrance, means words that wake the heart to its Lord. The past tense comforts. Guidance is not awaited; it has landed. No one need plead ignorance while the Reminder lies open. The Reminder covers the whole Book, yet it points here at these very limits—counts, housing, support, witnesses. Each ruling is a line of the Reminder. Keeping it is remembering Allah; breaking it is forgetting Him. The verse thus pairs the two prepared things. Fire is prepared for scorners; Reminder is sent to seekers. Both stand ready; the soul chooses its store. The sending is also proof of care. A Lord bent on torment would spring it without word. Allah warns, details, repeats, and comforts before He judges. The Reminder in hand is mercy you can touch. Open it, heed it, live it.

**Quran Or Prophet, The Gift Is One Light**

Early readers differed on what the Reminder names. Most hear the Qur'an itself, the Book sent down as guidance and cure. Others, reading into the next verse, hear the Messenger who recites and clarifies. The two readings meet. The Book without a living reader stays ink; a reader without the Book brings no law. Together they form one mercy: clear words on a true tongue. The next verse will name the reciting messenger and his task of leading souls from darkness to light. Here the gift is announced; there the giver's agent is shown. The lesson for teachers follows. Carry both: the text exact and the life bright. Recite the limits as sent, without trimming; embody them as lived, without pride. Homes need that pair. Children learn the verse by ear and the virtue by eye. Where both shine, the Reminder does its full work.

**Reason And Faith Must Walk Together**

The verse closes its first half by binding mind to trust. People of reason hear proof; people of faith heed command. The chapter needs both arts. Divorce counts ask for care—dates, witnesses, sums. That is reason's work. Trust in unseen provision, cover, and reward asks for hope. That is faith's work. Split them and ruin follows. Reason without faith counts coldly and cheats warmly. Faith without reason loves loudly and counts poorly, harming by mistake. Joined, they guard the home. The husband counts exactly because he hopes truly. The wife waits patiently because she sees wisely. The Book seals the bond of term and trust elsewhere: “But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes” (63:11). Terms are fixed, so count them with mind; mercy is sure, so meet them with faith. That double walk leads past the prepared fire to the Reminder's Gardens. Blessed are the minds that believe and the hearts that heed in time.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:11
> ˹and sent˺ a messenger reciting to you Allah’s revelations, making things clear so that He may bring those who believe and do good out of darkness and into light. And whoever believes in Allah and does good will be admitted by Him into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. Allah will have indeed granted them an excellent provision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Messenger Who Reads The Signs Aloud**

The Reminder named just before arrives on a tongue. Allah sent a messenger who recites His signs to the people. *Yatlū*, reciting, means following the words closely and delivering them fresh, night and day. The messenger does not invent, trim, or hide. He voices divorce limits to husbands, housing rights to wives, and mercy to both, exactly as sent. His reciting also keeps the Book alive. Ink can be shut in a chest; a voice walks into markets, homes, and courts. The early community heard counts, terms, and promises from his own lips, asked its questions, and watched him live the answers. That living recital still runs through every teacher who voices the Book truly. The verse therefore honours both gift and bearer. The signs are Allah's; the reciting is His mercy through a man. Hear the voice, heed the verse, and the path clears.

**Clarity That Lifts Blame From The Hearers**

He recites revelations described as clear, *mubayyināt*, signs that show their own proof. Clarity here means plain speech about plain duties: wait this long, house her thus, pay this fairly, witness truly. None of it hides behind riddle. The Book elsewhere joins clear sending to rescue: “He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light” (57:9). Clear words cut excuses. A husband cannot claim he never knew the count. A wife cannot claim she never knew the term. A judge cannot claim the sum was guesswork. Clarity also softens hearts. Plain mercy—unseen provision, a way out, cover, vast reward—melts despair that vague hints would leave. The messenger's task is thus double: make the duty plain so none miss it, and make the promise plain so none doubt it. Plain law plus plain hope moves feet.

**From Darkness Into Light, Step By Step**

The goal of the recital is a journey: from darkness to light. Darkness here is plural in sense—doubt, pride, greed, custom, and fear—while light is one: Allah's guidance. The Book states the same passage as divine care: “so that He may bring you out of darkness and into light” (33:43). Steps mark the road in this chapter. Learning the count is a step. Housing her calmly is a step. Paying fairly is a step. Witnessing truly is a step. Each act moves the soul from the dark of whim into the light of law. Faith lights the lamp; good deeds carry it. The messenger walks ahead with the Book raised high, and believers follow lamp by lamp. None leaps the road at once. The verse promises passage, not teleport. Keep walking the plain path the recital shows, and the dark thins behind while light widens ahead.

**Faith Joined To Good Deeds Opens Gardens**

Entry to Gardens turns on a pair that never parts: belief and righteous work. The verse states it twice—once as the group led to light, once as the soul admitted to Gardens. Faith without deeds is a lamp without oil; deeds without faith are a body without breath. In this chapter the deeds are named: guard the term, house with honour, spend by means, consult kindly, part or return fairly. Small acts, yet they open vast doors. Allah pledges fair return for every deed, male or female alike: “I will never deny any of you—male or female—the reward of your deeds” (3:195). The pledge levels the field. A poor husband's fair count weighs with a rich man's large sum. A quiet wife's patient wait weighs with a scholar's long night. Gardens open not to rank but to truth. Believe deeply, act fairly, and the gate stands wide.

**Forever Means No Fear Of Eviction**

The Gardens are not a lease but a home: they stay there for ever and ever. *Abadan*, endless, ends the fear that haunts every earthly stay. Rented rooms expel, jobs end, health fades, marriages part. The Garden keeps its people with a keeping that never tires. Rivers run below their feet—cool, sweet, and near—while no bill, foe, or term intrudes. Eternity here answers the chapter's strain. A divorcee who kept a hard term for months gains a termless rest. A husband who paid in straits gains stores that never thin. The word “forever” also purifies motive. One who obeys for a quick payoff quits when payoff delays. One who obeys for an endless home holds through delay. The verse therefore stretches sight past the count of months to the count of never. Short patience, endless rest: that is the trade Allah offers.

**Excellent Provision Beyond All Wages**

The verse ends where the chapter's promise began, with provision—now raised to its peak. Allah has granted them excellent provision, *rizqan ḥasanā*, fair and lovely supply. On earth provision meant bread through unseen doors; here it means Gardens, rivers, nearness, and peace. Excellent marks both kind and manner. The gift is pure, without toil or taint, and it is given with honour, not doled with scorn. The past tense—has granted—speaks of a decree so sure it reads as done. Faithful souls may still walk through tight months, yet their excellent store stands booked. The phrase also answers fear of loss in divorce. Money spent on dues feels lost; the verse calls it stored. Housing kept in patience feels wasted; the verse calls it invested. Nothing fair is lost with Allah. Every loaf counted for Him returns as rivers. That is excellent by any measure.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq 65:12
> Allah is the One Who created seven heavens ˹in layers˺, and likewise for the earth. The ˹divine˺ command descends between them so you may know that Allah is Most Capable of everything and that Allah certainly encompasses all things in ˹His˺ knowledge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seven Heavens Proclaim A Single Maker**

The chapter closes with the sky. Allah made seven heavens in tiers, each above the next in perfect order. The Book names those tiers in many places, and the well-known verse of dominion counts them as stacked craft (67:3). Seven here means fullness and rank, not guess. Stars run without crash, rain falls in measure, night and day trade places without suit. Such order cannot rise from chance or from many gods pulling apart. One will holds all rungs. The point lands on divorce law with force. The Lord who stacks the sky without pillar can guard a woman's term without fail. The hand that wheels the stars can open unseen provision for a poor father. Vast craft proves near care. Lift eyes to the tiers, and the heart learns to trust the counts, the housing, and the promises below them. The same One rules both.

**Seven Earths Widen The Same Proof**

The verse adds earth's match: and likewise for the earth. This is the Book's plainest hint at seven earths beside seven skies. A well-known report warns that seizing a handspan of land unjustly will collar the seizer with seven earths on the Last Day. The warning fits this chapter's theme of dues and limits. Land grabbed, support withheld, housing denied—all meet a Judge whose earths are seven deep. Early readers split on the shape. Some hear seven lands stacked tier on tier, mirroring the skies. Others hear seven tracts spread wide—climes and continents across one globe. Both readings widen awe. Below our feet lie depths we never mapped; around us stretch lands we never saw. All are His craft. The divorcee's small room and the rich man's wide estate both sit on His ground. None of it escapes His eye.

**One Upon Another Or Spread Around, Both Glorify**

The debate over stacked versus spread need not divide hearts. Each view carries the same verdict: Allah's making is vaster than man's tread. Stacked earths picture depth—rock under rock, sea under sea, dark under dark—all held without prop. Spread lands picture breadth—desert, vale, isle, and ice—all fed without fail. The Book elsewhere bids travellers to walk the earth and see ends of deniers; this verse bids thinkers to picture earths they cannot walk and see the Maker. What matters is not the map but the moral. A Lord whose earth is sevenfold can find provision for the mindful from any fold. A Judge whose ground runs seven deep can weigh a hidden wrong on any layer. Maps may grow with knowledge; worship grows with wonder. Hold the wonder firm, and leave the map's edges to Allah.

**Strange Reports Teach Caution, Not Denial**

Two striking reports cling to this verse. One Companion is quoted as fearing that plain speech about it would meet denial. Another account pictures each earth with its own Adam and prophet like ours. Scholars handled these words with care. Some heard awe before a mystery too wide for crowds. Others heard a stress on Allah's power to repeat creation as He wills. None made them a creed to divide the faithful. The lesson is method. Where texts speak plainly—counts, housing, support—act plainly. Where hints open vastness—seven earths, descending command—believe firmly and picture humbly. Neither deny the hint to protect comfort, nor spin it into tales that split homes. The verse itself guides the response: know His power and know His knowledge. Wonder that ends in trust is true; wonder that ends in quarrel misses the point. Keep the heart wide and the tongue careful.

**The Command Descends Through Every Layer**

Between skies and earths the divine command comes down. *Amr*, command, covers decree, law, and measuring—rain fixed, terms set, fates written, rulings sent. Descent through all layers means no floor escapes rule. The same order that wheels stars fixes waiting periods. The same pen that writes lifespans writes support dues. Some read here the worldwide reach of revelation, since Allah sent a warner to every people (16:36). Law is not local custom; it falls from above on all. The picture steadies the wronged. A wife's cry in a locked room climbs through no closed ceiling; the command that answers descends through none. A poor father's need sits under no absent sky. Every layer carries the order down and every sigh carries the plea up. The cosmos is not empty space but a ruled house, from top tier to lowest soil.

**Power And Knowledge Close The Chapter**

The verse names the fruit of the whole proof: you may know His full power and full grasp. *Qadīr*, Most Capable, means nothing outruns His hand—provision, return, reckoning, or resurrection. *Iḥāṭah*, encompassing, means nothing escapes His ken—hidden income, secret harm, quiet good. The pair seals the chapter's every promise and threat. Unseen provision needs power to send and knowledge to place. Severe account needs knowledge to weigh and power to requite. Gardens need power to build and knowledge to assign. The Book joins term to trust in the same key: “But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes” (63:11). Fixed terms prove both names: power fixes them, knowledge keeps them. The chapter that began with counting days ends with the Counter of all. Count your term with care, house with honour, pay with truth—and rest on the One who holds seven skies, seven earths, and your affair between them. He counts all, knows all, and suffices all who trust Him.
