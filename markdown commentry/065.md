# Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq (Chapter 65) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Jurisprudence of Parting and the Open Door of Deliverance**

Aṭ-Ṭalāq, "The Divorce," is the sixty-fifth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 12 verses of Medinan revelation. In the Companion tradition, ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd famously designated this chapter as *Sūrat al-Nisā' al-Ṣughrā* (The Shorter Chapter of the Women), highlighting its legal continuity with the fourth chapter (al-Nisāʾ) in establishing the sacred rights, financial maintenance, and legal protections guaranteed to women during domestic crises. Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq is universally celebrated for containing some of the most radiant, famous verses of spiritual hope, divine deliverance, and reliance found anywhere in sacred scripture: *“And whoever fears Allah—He will make for him a way out, and will provide for him from where he does not expect. And whoever relies upon Allah—then He is enough for him. Indeed, Allah will accomplish His purpose”* (65:2–3), and *“Allah will bring about, after hardship, ease”* (65:7). In twelve concentrated, legislative verses, the sūrah transforms the painful, volatile reality of marital dissolution from a theater of vengeance and acrimony into an arena of spiritual mindfulness, justice, and transcendent trust in the providence of God.

**The Structural Fabric of Legal Compassion**

The architecture of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭalāq moves across two tightly integrated movements:

1. The Jurisprudence of Separation and Child Welfare (1–7): Codifies the precise timing of divorce, the calculation of the waiting period (*al-ʿiddah*), and the strict prohibition of evicting the wife from the marital residence, reminding humanity: *“You know not; perhaps Allah will bring about after that a [different] matter”* (65:1). It commands that when the waiting term nears its end, the couple must either reconcile honorably or part honorably, taking two just witnesses. It legislates the waiting periods for women who do not menstruate and pregnant women whose term ends upon giving birth (65:4 — *“As for those who are pregnant, their waiting period ends with delivery.”*). Verses 6–7 codify the economic obligations of the husband: he must house the divorced wife according to his means, never harass or pressure her to leave, provide full financial support if she is pregnant until delivery, pay her fair compensation if she nurses the child, and consult together with mutual kindness (*wa'tamirū baynakum bi-maʿrūf*). The movement closes with the golden rule of economic equity: *“Let a man of wealth spend from his wealth, and he whose provision is restricted—let him spend from what Allah has given him. Allah does not charge a soul except [according to] what He has given it. Allah will bring about, after hardship, ease (*sayajʿalu Allāhu baʿda ʿusrin yusrā*)”* (65:7).

2. The Warning of Past Ruins and the Descent of the Seven Heavens (8–12): Warns the community that societal laws are not disconnected from cosmic order: many an ancient town insolently rebelled against the command of its Lord and His messengers, so God held them to a severe account and punished them with an awful punishment, tasting the evil consequence of their conduct. It commands those of understanding who believe to be mindful of God, reminding them that God has sent down a Reminder and a Messenger who recites clear verses to lead humanity out of darkness into light. The sūrah concludes with the magnificent cosmic doxology of verse 12: *“It is Allah who has created seven heavens and of the earth, the like of them. [His] command descends between them so that you may know that Allah is over all things competent and that Allah has encompassed all things in knowledge.”*

## Verse 65:1
> O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women, then divorce them with concern for their waiting period, and count it accurately. And fear Allah, your Lord. Do not force them out of their homes, nor should they leave—unless they commit a blatant misconduct. These are the limits set by Allah. And whoever transgresses Allah’s limits has truly wronged his own soul. You never know, perhaps Allah will bring about a change ˹of heart˺ later

**Divorce Must Face The Waiting Period, Not Fight It**

The command ties every divorce to its waiting period from the first word. *Ṭalāq*, from a root that means release, ends a bond; *ʿiddah*, from counting, measures the bridge between marriage and full parting. The verse joins them: pronounce divorce in a way that respects that bridge. In the standard case the bridge is three monthly cycles, as the Book states elsewhere: “Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺” (2:228). The husband must therefore choose a moment that lets that count run cleanly, not a moment that confuses it. Jurists read the phrase in two close senses. One sense sets purpose: divorce them *for* the waiting period, aiming at it. The other sets time: divorce them *in* a state fit for it. Both senses push the same way. A divorce that starts the count clearly is valid and fair; a divorce that muddles the count harms the woman and breaks the aim of the law. Timing is so not manners. It is part of the act itself.

**Menstruation Bars The Pronouncement Until Purity Returns**

A man may not pronounce divorce while his wife is menstruating. He must wait until she is pure, then let another cycle pass, and only then decide, before any new intimacy, whether to keep or to part. This is the ruling drawn from the well-known case in which a Companion divorced during menstruation and was ordered to take his wife back and restart the process correctly. The reason is protective, not formal. Blood makes the start of the count unclear, and desire or anger in that hour makes the decision unreliable. The Book states the exception plainly: “they will have no waiting period for you to count” (33:49) when marriage ends before any touch.

The case that fixed the rule is named in the record. It was ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar who divorced his wife while she was menstruating, in the lifetime of the Messenger ﷺ, and ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb brought the matter to the Messenger ﷺ. The Messenger ﷺ became angry at it and commanded that the man take her back, keep her until she is clean, wait out the next period until she is clean again, and only then, if he still wishes to divorce, divorce her before any new intimacy. Al-Bukhārī recorded the report in several parts of his ṣaīḥ, and Muslim collected it as well, his version ending with the words that this is the ʿiddah for which Allah has ordered women to be divorced.

The phrase *fa-ṭalliqūhunna li-ʿiddatihinna* was explained as purity without intercourse by ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, and similar words are reported from Ibn ʿUmar, ʿAṭā, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, Ibn Sīrīn, Qatādah, Maymūn ibn Mihrān, Muqātil ibn Hayyān, ʿIkrimah, and ad-Dahhāk. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reported from Ibn ʿAbbās the full shape of the act: he does not divorce her while she is on her menses, nor while she is pure if he has had intercourse during that purity, but leaves her until the menses ends and then divorces her once. And the counting the verse commands has its own reason: know the beginning of the ʿiddah and its end, so that it does not drag on and leave the woman unable to marry again.

**Two Divorces, and a Third That Fits Neither**

The scholars drew the line the case draws. There is a divorce that conforms to the Sunnah, and there is a divorce that is an innovation. The divorce of the Sunnah is one where the husband pronounces a single divorce on a wife who is not menstruating and with whom he has had no intercourse since the menses ended, and a divorce pronounced when it is clear that she is pregnant is of the same kind. The innovated divorce is the one pronounced during the menses, or after intercourse in that purity, when the husband does not know whether she became pregnant or not. And there is a third kind that is neither, because the rule it would break is absent: a young wife who has not begun to menstruate, a wife past the age of menstruation, and a wife divorced before the marriage was consummated.

**Expulsion Needs Proven Misconduct, Not Suspicion**

Only one exception breaks the housing rule, and the verse narrows it hard. A woman may be removed only for a proven, open indecency, *fāḥishah mubayyinah*, plain conduct that leaves no doubt. Suspicion does not qualify. Anger does not qualify. The classical reading links this bar to the law of proof elsewhere: “˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves” (4:15).

The salaf fixed the range of *fāḥishah mubayyinah*. For most of them it means adultery, and the reading is attached to the names of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, Ash-Shaʿbī, al-Ḥasan, Ibn Sīrīn, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Abū Qilābah, Abū Sāliḥ, ad-Dahhāk, Zayd ibn Aslam, ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī, As-Suddī, and Saʿīd ibn Hilāl, among others. For Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrimah, and others, it covers open disobedience to the husband, or abusing his family in words and actions. The two readings keep the verse's bar: it is not suspicion that empties the house, but conduct that is open, proven, and beyond doubt.

**Limits That Protect Both Sides And Leave Room For Return**

The verse closes its first half by naming these rulings as limits set by Allah, *ḥudūd Allāh*, lines no one may cross. The same word marks the divorce law elsewhere: “Divorce may be retracted twice, then the husband must retain ˹his wife˺ with honour or separate ˹from her˺ with grace” (2:229). Twice the door stays open; a third time shuts it, as the ruling for the thrice-divorced wife confirms (2:230 — *“These are the limits set by Allah, which He makes clear for people of knowledge”*). Whoever crosses the line wrongs his own soul before he wrongs another, because he trades a path back for a moment of will. Then the verse opens a window: no one knows what Allah may bring after this. Hearts change. Anger lifts. Need and mercy return. The waiting period exists to give that change time to arrive. A divorce begun in the right way, counted with care, and housed with honour keeps the future open. A divorce begun in haste, hidden from the count, or hardened by eviction slams it shut. The law so holds firmness and hope together. It fixes the line, and it leaves the heart its term.

**Why She Stays in the House**

The reason the salaf give for keeping the divorcee in the husband's house is stated as a mercy on both sides: the husband might regret his action, and Allah might incline his heart to the continuation of the marriage, and the return would then be easier. Fāṭimah bint Qays was asked what the clause *you know not, it may be that Allah will bring a change later* means, and she said: taking her back, in the report az-Zuhrī carried from ʿUbaydullāh ibn ʿAbdullāh. Ash-Shaʿbī, ʿAṭā, Qatādah, ad-Dahhāk, Muqātil ibn Hayyān, and ath-Thawrī said the same. The house during the revocable ʿiddah is thus not a penalty kept on the woman. It is a door kept open for the man, and the law assumes he will use it to her benefit if he gets the chance.

**The Case of Fāṭimah bint Qays**

The case that settled the other side is hers. Fāṭimah bint Qays al-Fihrīyah was married to Abū ʿAmr ibn Ḥaf ibn al-Mughīrah, of Banū Makhzūm, and he divorced her the third and final time while he was away in Yemen with the army. He sent her the decision with his messenger and sent some barley as provision, but she was not pleased with the amount or the way it came, and he answered: by Allah, I am not obligated to spend on you. She went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and he said: there is no maintenance for you from him. Muslim added to his narration: nor housing. And he ordered her to finish her ʿiddah in the house of Umm Sharīk, and then said: she is a woman my Companions visit; spend your period in the house of Ibn Umm Maktum, for he is a blind man, and you may lower your garments in his presence. In the narration Imam Aḥmad recorded, the Messenger ﷺ put the rule in one sentence: look, daughter of the family of Qays — spending and housing belong to the wife only while her husband has the right to take her back; when he no longer has the right, there is no spending and no housing, so leave and go down to so-and-so. At-Ṭabarānī collected the report through ʿAmr ash-Shaʿbī, and An-Nasāʾī recorded it as well. The ruling the case carried is the one the sūrah applies: housing and maintenance belong to the revocable ʿiddah, and the irrevocable parting ends them.

## Verse 65:2
> Then when they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or separate from them honourably. And call two of your reliable men to witness ˹either way˺—and ˹let the witnesses˺ bear true testimony for ˹the sake of˺ Allah. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make a way out for them

**The End Of The Term Forces A Clean Choice**

The verse speaks to the hour when the count is nearly done and delay is no longer lawful. Two paths stand open, and both carry the same guard: honour. A husband may hold his wife and restore full marriage, or he may release her and close the bond. What he may not do is drift, stall, or keep her hanging between marriage and divorce. The parallel ruling in the longer divorce passage uses the same pair: “either retain them honourably or let them go honourably” (2:231). Honour here means a clear will, a fair settlement, and speech that does not wound. The waiting period was given as a space for thought; its end demands a verdict. Holding without love and parting without fairness both fail the test. The law so turns time into mercy. It grants months for anger to cool, then it requires a decision that lets both lives move forward.

**Parting With Honour Means Paying Every Due**

Honourable parting is not a soft mood; it is full payment of rights. The wife keeps her due maintenance through the term, her housing without harassment, and any delayed bridal gift that remains unpaid. The husband may not trim those dues to punish her for accepting the divorce. Nor may he spread her faults after the split or turn the family against her. Equity governs the split of shared life: “Women have rights similar to those of men equitably, although men have a degree ˹of responsibility˺ above them” (2:228). That degree is a burden of care, not a licence to dominate. Parting well also means fixing the terms for any child with calm words, since the child did not choose the split. Witnesses, writing, and clear dates help both sides avoid later denial. A divorce that ends with dues paid and tongues held back leaves dignity on both sides. A divorce that ends with cheating and slander plants hatred that outlives the marriage.

**What the Verse Commands and Why**

The verse speaks to the moment when the waiting period is nearly complete, and it gives the man two choices: keep his wife with honour, or separate from her with honour, and in either case, no return to a state of suspension. The sūrah has already told the men to count the period and to fear God (65:1 — *“O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women, then divorce them with concern for their waiting period, and count it accurately.”*), and the command here closes the door on the practice of keeping a woman in waiting without a decision, which the Book names in the Sūrah of the Cow as a harm (2:231 — *“When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or let them go honourably.”*). The English phrase that fits the ruling is that a woman is not to be made to wait indefinitely while a man makes up his mind.

**The Witnesses and the Ruling**

The verse requires two witnesses when the couple separate or reunite, and the jurists read that as a rule about the record rather than about the validity of the divorce itself. The schools differed on whether the witnessing is obligatory or recommended, and the arguments were drawn from this verse and from the practice of the community. The Book then gives the instruction that carries the passage to its end: whoever is mindful of God, He will make a way out for him, and He will provide for him from where he does not expect (65:3 — *“and provide for them from sources they could never imagine. And whoever puts their trust in Allah, then He ˹alone˺ is sufficient for them. Certainly Allah achieves His Will. Allah has already set a destiny for everything”*).

ʿImrān ibn Ḥusayn was asked, in the reports Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah collected, about a man who divorced his wife and then had intercourse with her, without notifying witnesses of the divorce or of the taking back. He answered: his divorce and his taking back went against the Sunnah; involve the presence of witnesses for the divorce and the taking back, and do not repeat your conduct. ʿAṭā gave the ruling its shape in the report Ibn Jurayj carried from him: it is not permissible to marry, divorce, or take back a divorced wife except with two just witnesses, as Allah stated, except where there is a valid excuse.

**The Promise in the Middle**

The sūrah places its promise of provision inside the laws of divorce, and the commentators noted the arrangement. A man who does the right thing at the hardest point of a marriage is told that his own case is in the hands of the One who makes the arrangements, and the same promise is repeated in the verse before the passage on the waiting period of the women who no longer expect to menstruate (65:5 — *“This is the commandment of Allah, which He has revealed to you. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will absolve them of their sins and reward them immensely”*). The Book's law of divorce is stated in this sūrah with a promise of ease, and the chapter's name in the community's use reflects the two sides of it: the divorce and the fear of God.

**The Verse of Relief, Named by Ibn Masʿud**

Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd naming the verses of the Book: the most comprehensive verse in the Qur'an is the one that begins (16:90 — *“Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, as well as courtesy to close relatives”*), and the greatest verse in the Qur'an that contains relief is *whoever fears Allah, He will make for him a way out*. And ʿIkrimah tied the relief to the law itself: whoever divorces as Allah commanded him, Allah will make a way out for him, and similar words are reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and ad-Dahhāk. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd and Masrūq explained the clause *from where he does not expect* as provision that comes from a source the person had not anticipated, in the knowledge that if Allah wills He gives and if He wills He withholds. The verse is thus a way out for the one doing the divorce as well, not only for the one being divorced.

## Verse 65:3
> and provide for them from sources they could never imagine. And whoever puts their trust in Allah, then He ˹alone˺ is sufficient for them. Certainly Allah achieves His Will. Allah has already set a destiny for everything

**Trust Means Acting Within The Measure, Not Against It**

Reliance on Allah, *tawakkul*, does not cancel the law; it completes it. In this passage trust sits beside waiting periods, witnesses, housing, and support. The link teaches the true shape of trust. A man trusts Allah by keeping the count, not by skipping it. A woman trusts Allah by guarding her term, not by fleeing it. The messengers voiced the same trust under threat: “And in Allah let the faithful put their trust” (14:12). Their trust did not stop them from planning, migrating, or enduring harm with patience. It fixed their hearts while their hands worked. False trust claims reliance and drops the duty. True trust takes the means Allah set, then rests the heart on Him for the end. Divorce offers a sharp test of the difference. The husband who pays fairly while fearing need practises trust. The husband who withholds dues while claiming trust practises greed dressed as faith.

**Sufficiency Is A Promise, Not A Metaphor**

The verse states the reward of trust in absolute terms: Allah alone suffices the one who relies on Him. *Ḥasb*, sufficiency, means the absence of need for another prop. The form is strong. It does not say Allah helps, supports, or nears; it says He suffices. That claim rests on two facts. Allah owns every store, and Allah never breaks a promise. Human props fail. Wealth thins, friends tire, strength fades, plans misfire. The Qur'an joins trust to love as well: “Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him” (3:159). Love here is not distant praise; it is care that covers the one who leans on Him. Sufficiency therefore includes both outward and inward gifts. Outwardly, needs find supply. Inwardly, fear gives way to steadiness. The trusting heart stops begging every creature and turns its full face to one Lord.

Imam Aḥmad recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās the scene that fixed the trust in one breath. He rode the Prophet's ﷺ camel sitting behind him, and the Messenger ﷺ said: o boy, I will teach you words — fear Allah and He will protect you; fear Allah and you will find Him facing you; if you ask, ask Allah, and if you seek help, seek it from Allah. And know that if the community gathered all its strength to bring you benefit, it could not bring you benefit except with what Allah has written for you, and if it gathered to harm you, it could not harm you except with what Allah has written upon you; the pens have been raised and the pages are dry. Al-Tirmidhī collected the report and called it ḥasan aḥī. And the measure that closes the verse has its parallel in the Book: with Allah everything is determined with precision (13:8 — *“And with Him everything is determined with precision”*). The verse ends where the law began: on the fixed, the known, and the One who holds both.

## Verse 65:4
> As for your women past the age of menstruation, in case you do not know, their waiting period is three months, and those who have not menstruated as well. As for those who are pregnant, their waiting period ends with delivery. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make their matters easy for them

**A Question That Completed The Law**

The verse answers a gap the community itself raised. After the ruling of three cycles came down, a Companion asked about women outside that frame: the old who no longer bleed and the young who never started, along with those carrying a child. The question was honest, and revelation honoured it with a full answer. This pattern repeats through the law. Believers ask, Allah completes. The exchange shows how mercy works in legislation. Gaps are not left for guesswork or harsh custom; they are filled by clear words. It also shows the rank of asking well.

The question is preserved with its questioner. Ubayy ibn Kaʿb said to the Messenger ﷺ: some women were not mentioned in the Qur'an — the young, the old, and the pregnant — and the answer came in the words of this verse. In the simpler narration Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded, after the verse of al-Baqarah prescribing the ʿiddah of divorce was revealed, some of the people of Madinah said: there are still women whose ʿiddah has not been mentioned in the Qur'an — the young, the old whose menstruation has stopped, and the pregnant — and this verse was revealed after that.

**Three Months Where Cycles Cannot Guide**

Where monthly courses run, they set the count: “Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺” (2:228). Where they never ran or will never return, months take their place. Three full months mark the term for the aged and the young alike. The number is not random. Three months mirror three cycles in length and give the same space for thought, calm, and possible return. Jurists treat the months as lunar, counted from the pronouncement to the same date three moons on, with care for short months. The phrase about doubt—“in case you do not know”—covers the unsure case. Where bleeding is absent and none knows whether it may return, the safe count is months. Certainty is not demanded before mercy.

The doubt the verse names has two readings in the salaf. Mujāhid, az-Zuhrī, and Ibn Zayd took it for the case where a woman of the doubtful age sees blood and it is not known whether it is menses or not. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr took it for the case where the person simply does not know the ruling, and the answer is: know that their ʿiddah is three months; Ibn Jarīr preferred that reading as the more obvious.

**Widowhood Follows A Different Count**

Divorce and death part in their counts, and the verse must not be mixed with the widow's term. A widow waits four months and ten days, as the Book fixes: “let them observe a waiting period of four months and ten days” (2:234). That longer term honours grief and proves the womb with fuller certainty. Divorce in this verse takes the shorter paths: cycles, three months, or delivery. The difference teaches a wider point. Law varies the clock with the cause. A living husband may return, so the term stays open to return. A husband who has died cannot return, so the term turns to mourning, clearing, and honour. Confusing the two harms women. Imposing the widow's wait on a divorcee stretches her bind without cause; imposing the divorcee's wait on a widow cuts her mourning short. Each term guards its own good. The believer keeps them distinct.

**The Pregnant Widow and the Count She Was Given**

The law of the pregnant woman's ʿiddah — it ends with the delivery, in divorce or in death — is fixed in the record by the case of Subayʿah al-Aslamīyah. Her husband was Saʿd ibn Khawlah, one of the men of Badr, and he died in the Farewell Pilgrimage while she was pregnant. She delivered a child soon after his death, and when her postpartum period ended she dressed herself for those who might propose. Abū As-Sanabīl ibn Baʿkak came to her and said: why do I see you have dressed yourself? Do you wish to remarry? By Allah, you cannot marry unless four months and ten days have passed. She went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ in the evening and asked for his verdict, and he ruled that she was allowed to marry after she had delivered, and that she could marry if she wished. Al-Bukhārī collected the short form of the report and Muslim the longer one, and Imam Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, An-Nasāʾī, and Ibn Mājah recorded it as well. And Abū Salāmah asked Ibn ʿAbbās for a verdict on a woman who delivered a baby forty days after her husband's death. Ibn ʿAbbās ruled that her ʿiddah lasts to the end of the longer of the two prescribed periods, and he recited this verse in proof; then he sent his slave Kurayb to ask Umm Salāmah, who told him the story of Subayʿah, and Abū Hurayrah, who was sitting with them, agreed with his cousin.

**Mindfulness Turns A Hard Wait Into Ease**

The verse closes with the chapter's refrain in a new form: mindfulness brings ease. *Taqwā* here means keeping these very counts—months, delivery, housing—without cheating or cruelty. The Book states the wider law behind it: “So, surely with hardship comes ease” (94:5).

## Verse 65:5
> This is the commandment of Allah, which He has revealed to you. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will absolve them of their sins and reward them immensely

**Mindfulness Brings Cover For Past Faults**

The promise joins two gifts: cover for sins and a vast reward. *Takfīr*, absolution, means Allah wipes the fault from the record and shields the soul from its harm. The verse ties that cover to mindfulness in these very duties. A husband who counts fairly, houses kindly, and parts or returns with honour finds his past faults covered. A wife who guards her term, her tongue, and her trust finds the same. The Book repeats this link in many forms. Private charity earns it: good deeds done quietly “will absolve you of your sins” (2:271). Faith with mindfulness earns it for former deniers: “We would have certainly absolved them of their sins and admitted them into the Gardens of Bliss” (5:65). The pattern is steady. Allah meets guarded conduct with guarding mercy. Small obedience in a hard hour outweighs easy obedience in comfort, and He covers much for it.

**Great Reward Follows Small Obedience In Hard Hours**

The reward is named as immense, *ajran ʿaẓīmā*, a wage beyond measure. The size fits the strain. Keeping divorce law hurts. It costs money when funds are thin. It costs patience when hearts are sore. It costs pride when kin demand harshness. Allah prices that pain highly. The verse does not list coins or ranks; it leaves the reward vast and open. That openness itself comforts. No believer can picture the full return, so none can despair that the cost was wasted. The Book elsewhere joins the same pair—cover and best reward—in one breath: “Allah will absolve them of ˹even˺ the worst of what they did and reward them according to the best of what they used to do” (39:35). Worst deeds covered, best deeds priced: that is immense by any count. The husband who pays while fearing need and the wife who waits while fearing blame both trade a short loss for a lasting gain.

## Verse 65:6
> Let them live where you live ˹during their waiting period˺, according to your means. And do not harass them to make their stay unbearable. If they are pregnant, then maintain them until they deliver. And if they nurse your child, compensate them, and consult together courteously. But if you fail to reach an agreement, then another woman will nurse ˹the child˺ for the father

**Housing Must Match His Means, Not Her Fear**

The verse fixes shelter by his capacity. A wealthy man provides roomy lodging; a poor man provides what he can; neither may dump his divorcee into a shed while he keeps comfort. *Min wujdikum*, from what you find and afford, ties the duty to real means. Jurists differed on whether food and clothing join shelter in this line, but all agreed shelter itself is firm. The wife stays where he stays, in the same grade of life they shared. The rule blocks two cheats. The rich cannot plead poverty to squeeze her out. The poor cannot be crushed beyond their power.

The measure the verse uses is the man's real capacity, and that is how the salaf read *min wujdikum*, from what you have: it is your ability, in the words of Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and several others. Qatādah gave the clause its floor: if you can only afford to accommodate her in a corner of your house, then do so. The duty scales with the man's means without breaking at either end.

**Harm To Force Her Out Breaks The Law**

The verse bans a quiet crime: making her stay so hard that she flees and drops her rights. *Ḍirār*, harm, covers harsh words, spying, cutting water or heat, crowding her room, inviting hostile kin, or spreading shame. The aim is the same in each form: force her to leave or to pay for release. The Book blocks that aim across family law. Husbands must not hold wives to harm them, and all must treat them with fairness: “Treat them fairly” (4:19). The ban protects the housing right from the inside. Walls alone do not shelter; peace inside them does. A husband who keeps her under his roof but fills it with menace has broken the verse as surely as one who locks her out. The test is simple. Would a fair man call this stay bearable? If not, the husband owes change, not excuses. The waiting period must be lived in calm, not survived in fear.

Muqātil ibn Hayyān stated the aim the clause forbids: do not annoy her to force her to pay her way out, nor expel her from your house. And ath-Thawrī carried from Manṣūr, from Abū Ad-Duhā, a description of the trick the clause is aimed at: he divorces her, and when a few days remain in the term, he takes her back, keeping her in a state that is neither marriage nor parting. Both readings point at the same harm: the husband using the position the law gives him to make the woman pay for the release the law was meant to keep open.

**Neither Parent May Use The Child As Leverage**

Consultation must be courteous, and the child must never become a tool. The mother may not withhold nursing to extort a sum beyond his means. The father may not withhold pay to force her into cheap labour or return. The Book states the guard in one line: “No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child” (2:233). Suffering includes threats to take the child, to cut access, or to shame the other before kin. Honourable speech is the verse's cure. Both sides name their needs plainly, hear the other fully, and fix a sum both can live with. Witnesses or writing help where trust is thin. The child's face must stay before both eyes through the talks. He did not choose the divorce, and his food must not wait on pride. Parents who guard him in this hour prove their care beyond words. Parents who use him wound him first.

The mother's wage for nursing is a contract, and the salaf made that explicit: she enters into it with the father or his representative for whatever payment they agree, and the agreed sum is her due. If the couple makes difficulty for one another — the mother asking for a fee the father will not pay, the father offering a sum she will not take — the father may find another woman to nurse the child, and if the mother then accepts the sum that was to be paid to the other woman, she has the greater right to nurse her own child. The clause thus prices the milk, and the price is set by agreement, not by pressure.

## Verse 65:7
> Let the man of wealth provide according to his means. As for the one with limited resources, let him provide according to whatever Allah has given him. Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease

**Strait Means Pay What Allah Gave, Not Less**

The verse then turns to the poor. One whose provision is tight must still pay from what Allah gave him. Tight does not mean free. A small wage still buys bread; a narrow room still shelters. The poor man must give his share with the same honesty as the rich, scaled to his lot. The law so guards two sides. It shields the poor husband from demands beyond his wage. It shields the divorcee from a husband who pleads poverty to pay nothing. Both sides must speak truth about money. She must not demand a rich man's sum from a poor man's purse. He must not hide a day's wage to dodge a loaf's price. The Book states the wider guard: “No one will be charged with more than they can bear” (2:233). Bear here means true capacity, not comfort. Some strain is owed; crushing is not. The middle line states a law that runs through all duties: Allah tasks no soul beyond what He gave it. *Wusʿ*, capacity, covers money, strength, time, and mind. The Giver knows each measure, so the Judge never overasks. In support cases the rule caps the claim. A jobless father is not ordered to pay a merchant's sum. A sick mother is not ordered to nurse beyond her health. In worship the same law eases prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage for the weak.

**After Hardship Allah Brings Ease**

The verse ends with the chapter's widest comfort: after hardship comes ease. The Book states it twice for stress: “So, surely with hardship comes ease” (94:5). Two eases for one hardship, as the early gloss loves to note. In this setting ease may arrive as wider provision, a better job, a kind kin, or a restored home. It may arrive as strength to bear a tight sum without sin. It may wait for the next life, where striving to pay while poor opens a smooth road to the Garden. The promise does not name the form, so none may despair when one door stays shut. It names the Lord, so all may hope while every door is tried. The husband who pays fairly in straits and the wife who accepts fairly in need both stand inside this promise. Hardship is real, but it is not last. Ease follows.

“Surely with ˹that˺ hardship comes ˹more˺ ease” (94:6).

**The Oven and the Millstone**

Imam Aḥmad recorded from Abū Hurayrah the story that carries the promise out of the law and into the unseen. A man and his wife from an earlier generation were poor, and once the man came back from a journey, hungry and fatigued, and asked his wife whether she had anything to eat. She said: yes, receive the good news of Allah's provision. When he pressed, she said: wait a little, and she was awaiting Allah's mercy. When the matter was prolonged, he said: get up and bring me whatever you have to eat, for I am truly hungry and fatigued. She said: I will; soon I will open the oven's cover, so do not be hasty. When he was busy and refrained from insisting, she looked in her oven and found it full of the meat of a lamb, and her mortar and pestle full of seed grains, the seed crushing on its own. She shook out the mortar and took what was in it, and took the meat from the oven. Abū Hurayrah added: by He in Whose Hand is my life, this is the same statement that Muhammad ﷺ made — had she taken what was in her two millstones and not emptied them fully by shaking, the seed would have kept crushing until the Day of Resurrection.

## Verse 65:8
> ˹Imagine˺ how many societies rebelled against the commandments of their Lord and His messengers, so We called each ˹society˺ to a severe account and subjected them to a horrible punishment

The verse widens the lens from one house to many towns. Countless societies heard Allah's commands through His messengers, then chose pride over heed. The question form—how many—means the count is long. ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh's host, and others stand behind the words, each with its own prophet, proof, and respite. Their stories fill the Qur'an so that no age thinks itself first or safe.

**A Severe Account Leaves Nothing Unweighed**

Allah called each defiant town to a severe reckoning. *Ḥisāban shadīdan*, a hard account, means full audit without discount. Small wrongs count, hidden wrongs surface, excuses fail. The Book fixes the term for every people: “For each community there is an appointed term” (7:34). When the term runs, the books open. Severity here is justice, not cruelty. Towns that mocked weak believers, seized widows' dues, and shamed prophets face a scale that forgets nothing. The same scale weighs family wrongs. Unpaid support, forced eviction, false charges, and witness lies all enter the book. The verse brings the courtroom into the living room. A man who cheats in divorce may dodge a judge; he cannot dodge this audit. A woman who cheats in claims faces the same. The severe account makes private cruelty public at last, before a Judge who needs no papers.

**Horrible Punishment Fits Persistent Defiance**

Beyond the account stands punishment described as horrible, *ʿadhāban nukrā*, strange and dreadful. The word *nukr* means unknown, shocking, beyond the usual. These towns did not meet a light slap; they met ruin that matched their pride—blast, flood, quake, or disgrace that songs still recall. The horror fits the crime. Light faults earn light correction; settled defiance earns uprooting. The verse pairs the two pains in order: account first, then torment. Knowledge comes before wrath, so none can claim surprise. Messengers warned, signs shone, respite stretched. Only after all that did the blow fall. The pattern warns every defiant heart now. Respite is not pardon. Delay in punishment is time to return, as the Book says of the defiant: “I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless” (7:183). The flawless plan gives room, then it closes the room.

## Verse 65:9
> So they tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and the outcome of their doings was ˹total˺ loss

**Evil Consequences Grow From Evil Sowing**

The pain they tasted is named as the fruit of their own affair. *Wabāl amrihā*, the heavy evil of their course, means ruin grown from seed they sowed. Allah did not plant it for them; they planted it and He let it grow. False gods, rigged scales, seized dues, mocked prophets—each seed sprouted its own thorn. The law is steady across the Book. Longer life for the defiant only piles the harvest: “They are only given more time to increase in sin” (3:178). Time waters what the heart plants. Good seed grows shade; bad seed grows fire. Family wrongs follow the same soil. A husband who sows eviction reaps hatred. A wife who sows false claims reaps distrust. A witness who sows lies reaps shame. The verse roots torment in deed, not in fate's whim. None tastes but what his hands cooked. The cook may blame the fire, but the dish is his.

The regret they felt came when it could no longer be undone. And the verses around it add the layer this one does not name: the severe torment prepared for them is in the Hereafter, on top of the torment that already fell on them in this life. They did not taste one punishment and be done. They tasted the world's verdict first, and the final one waits for them after it.

**The Warning Fits The Divorce Law Before It**

These two verses guard the seven before them. Divorce limits cut against old pride: keep her housed, pay her due, count with care, witness truly. Hearts that loved quick, cheap parting heard those limits as insult. Allah answers with towns that called His limits insult and fell. The parallel is exact. They scorned prophets over idols and scales; some scorn messengers over women and children. The Lord is one, the scorn is one, the end warns one. The Book states the court rule behind it: “so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. And none will be wronged” (45:22). No wrong is too small for that court, no victim too weak for it. A divorcee's tear weighs there; a child's hunger weighs there. The verses therefore lift family justice to cosmic height. Guarding a waiting wife is standing with the warned towns' opposite: heed, not pride.

## Verse 65:10
> Allah has ˹also˺ prepared for them a severe punishment. So fear Allah, O people of reason and faith. Allah has indeed revealed to you a Reminder

**A Fire Prepared While Mercy Still Calls**

The verse turns from past towns to the fire that waits their likes. Allah has prepared—*aʿadda*, made ready—a severe torment. The past tense matters. The fire is not a threat to be built later; it stands furnished now. Its fuel, keepers, and heat wait. Preparation proves resolve. A king who builds a prison has judged the crime worthy of it. Yet the verse speaks the warning while breath lasts, which proves mercy. None hearing it has yet entered what is prepared. The door of return stands open beside the pit. The Book elsewhere warns that delay is not pardon: “We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend” (68:44). Gradual drawing feels like ease—wealth grows, health holds—until the prepared place opens. The verse cuts that sleep. What is ready will be filled; wisdom fills Gardens instead through heed.

The reminder named in the clause is the Qur'an, and the Book carries the same name in its promise of itself: it is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it (15:9 — *“It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it”*). The warning and the reminder are thus held in the same breath: the fire is prepared for those who had the reminder and answered it with defiance, and the reminder is kept for those who are still hearing it.

**Reason And Faith Must Walk Together**

The verse closes its first half by binding mind to trust. People of reason hear proof; people of faith heed command. The chapter needs both arts. Divorce counts ask for care—dates, witnesses, sums. That is reason's work. Trust in unseen provision, cover, and reward asks for hope. That is faith's work. Split them and ruin follows. Reason without faith counts coldly and cheats warmly. Faith without reason loves loudly and counts poorly, harming by mistake. Joined, they guard the home. The husband counts exactly because he hopes truly. The wife waits patiently because she sees wisely. The Book seals the bond of term and trust elsewhere: “But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes” (63:11). Terms are fixed, so count them with mind; mercy is sure, so meet them with faith. That double walk leads past the prepared fire to the Reminder's Gardens. Blessed are the minds that believe and the hearts that heed in time.

## Verse 65:11
> ˹and sent˺ a messenger reciting to you Allah’s revelations, making things clear so that He may bring those who believe and do good out of darkness and into light. And whoever believes in Allah and does good will be admitted by Him into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. Allah will have indeed granted them an excellent provision

**Clarity That Lifts Blame From The Hearers**

He recites revelations described as clear, *mubayyināt*, signs that show their own proof. Clarity here means plain speech about plain duties: wait this long, house her so, pay this fairly, witness truly. None of it hides behind riddle. The Book elsewhere joins clear sending to rescue: “He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light” (57:9). Clear words cut excuses. A husband cannot claim he never knew the count. A judge cannot claim the sum was guesswork. Clarity also softens hearts. Plain mercy—unseen provision, a way out, cover, vast reward—melts despair that vague hints would leave. The messenger's task is so double: make the duty plain so none miss it, and make the promise plain so none doubt it. Plain law plus plain hope moves feet.

The description of the messenger as the one who recites the clear verses carries a reading the commentators preserved. Some said the messenger is the subject of what is sent, since the reminder is what is conveyed through him. Ibn Jarīr held that the correct reading is that the messenger explains the reminder, and that is why he is described as reciting its verses. The reminder is the Book, and the messenger is its reciter and its explainer, and the two are named together because the one needs the other.

**From Darkness Into Light, Step By Step**

The goal of the recital is a journey: from darkness to light. Darkness here is plural in sense—doubt, pride, greed, custom, and fear—while light is one: Allah's guidance. The Book states the same passage as divine care: “so that He may bring you out of darkness and into light” (33:43). Steps mark the road in this chapter. Learning the count is a step. Housing her calmly is a step. Paying fairly is a step. Witnessing truly is a step. Each act moves the soul from the dark of whim into the light of law. Faith lights the lamp; good deeds carry it. The messenger walks ahead with the Book raised high, and believers follow lamp by lamp. None leaps the road at once. The verse promises passage, not teleport. Keep walking the plain path the recital shows, and the dark thins behind while light widens ahead.

The Book gives the revelation two names that say the same thing from two sides: it is light, because it brings guidance, and it is a spirit, because it brings life to the heart (42:52 — *“But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants”*). And the passage this verse describes is the one the Book names as Allah's own guardianship: Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light (2:257 — *“Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light”*). The journey is thus not a private ascent. It is a transfer of guardianship, from the dark the person was under to the light the Lord gives.

**Faith Joined To Good Deeds Opens Gardens**

Entry to Gardens turns on a pair that never parts: belief and righteous work. The verse states it twice—once as the group led to light, once as the soul admitted to Gardens. Faith without deeds is a lamp without oil; deeds without faith are a body without breath. In this chapter the deeds are named: guard the term, house with honour, spend by means, consult kindly, part or return fairly. Small acts, yet they open vast doors. Allah pledges fair return for every deed, male or female alike: “I will never deny any of you—male or female—the reward of your deeds” (3:195). The pledge levels the field. A poor husband's fair count weighs with a rich man's large sum. A quiet wife's patient wait weighs with a scholar's long night.

## Verse 65:12
> Allah is the One Who created seven heavens ˹in layers˺, and likewise for the earth. The ˹divine˺ command descends between them so you may know that Allah is Most Capable of everything and that Allah certainly encompasses all things in ˹His˺ knowledge

**Seven Heavens Proclaim A Single Maker**

The chapter closes with the sky. Allah made seven heavens in tiers, each above the next in perfect order. The Book names those tiers in many places, and the well-known verse of dominion counts them as stacked craft (67:3 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other.”*). Seven here means fullness and rank, not guess. Stars run without crash, rain falls in measure, night and day trade places without suit. Such order cannot rise from chance or from many gods pulling apart. One will holds all rungs. The point lands on divorce law with force. The Lord who stacks the sky without pillar can guard a woman's term without fail. The hand that wheels the stars can open unseen provision for a poor father. Vast craft proves near care. Lift eyes to the tiers, and the heart learns to trust the counts, the housing, and the promises below them. The same One rules both.

The tiers are counted in the words the Book puts in Noah's mouth to his people (71:15 — *“Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other”*), and the same seven heavens are the first subject of the Book's standing account of the creation's glorification (17:44 — *“The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him”*). The sky is counted, and the sky praises, and the two facts are used together here: the number proves the maker's order, and the praise proves that the order is known by the made.

**The Seven Earths, and the Hand-Span**

*And of the earth the like of them* means seven earths, as the sky has seven heavens, and the hadiths of the two ṣaḥīḥs fix the weight of that earth. Whoever usurps the land of somebody unjustly, even if it was a mere hand-span, will have it encircled around his neck down to the seven earths; in the wording of ṣaīḥ al-Bukhārī, he will be sunk down to the seven earths. The various narrations of the report are collected at the beginning of *al-Bidāyah wa-n-Nihāyah*, in the account of the creation of the earth, and the reading that takes the seven earths to be the seven continents is rejected there as an explanation that contradicts the letter of the Qur'an and the hadith without proof. The earth is thus not a floor that merely holds the law. It is part of the measure the law serves.

**The Command Descends Through Every Layer**

Between skies and earths the divine command comes down. *Amr*, command, covers decree, law, and measuring—rain fixed, terms set, fates written, rulings sent. Descent through all layers means no floor escapes rule. The same order that wheels stars fixes waiting periods. The same pen that writes lifespans writes support dues. Some read here the worldwide reach of revelation, since Allah sent a warner to every people (16:36 — *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, “Worship Allah and shun false gods.”*). Law is not local custom; it falls from above on all. The picture steadies the wronged. A wife's cry in a locked room climbs through no closed ceiling; the command that answers descends through none.

**Power And Knowledge Close The Chapter**

The verse names the fruit of the whole proof: you may know His full power and full grasp. *Qadīr*, Most Capable, means nothing outruns His hand—provision, return, reckoning, or resurrection. *Iḥāṭah*, encompassing, means nothing escapes His ken—hidden income, secret harm, quiet good. The pair seals the chapter's every promise and threat. Unseen provision needs power to send and knowledge to place. Severe account needs knowledge to weigh and power to requite. Gardens need power to build and knowledge to assign. The Book joins term to trust in the same key: “But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes” (63:11). Fixed terms prove both names: power fixes them, knowledge keeps them. The chapter that began with counting days ends with the Counter of all. Count your term with care, house with honour, pay with truth—and rest on the One who holds seven skies, seven earths, and your affair between them. He counts all, knows all, and suffices all who trust Him.
