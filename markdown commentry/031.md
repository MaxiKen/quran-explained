# Sūrah Luqmān (Chapter 31) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Letters That Give No Translation**

Alif, lām, mīm. The line is not a sentence. It has no verb, it yields nothing when read as a word, and it is recited as three separate letters, each pronounced under its own name with its own pause. Arabic calls this class *al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the severed or disconnected letters, and twenty-nine of the Qur'an's 114 chapters open with a combination of this kind. The combinations are drawn from fourteen letters out of the twenty-eight-letter alphabet, and no authentic report from the Prophet ﷺ explains a single one of them. That absence is the first fact about these openings, and it is the fact the tradition has respected most consistently. Whatever function the letters serve, they serve it without a gloss from the one who received them. The material they are made of is equally plain: these are the ordinary components of Arabic, the same alif, lām and mīm a child learns to name before he can read a verse at all. Opening a book with its own alphabet states the terms of a challenge before the challenge is issued, since the words that follow are built entirely out of what every listener already possesses.

**Six Chapters Share This Exact Opening**

Alif-lām-mīm heads six chapters: al-Baqarah, Āl ʿImrān, al-ʿAnkabūt, al-Rūm, this one, and al-Sajdah. The spread is not scattered at random. Two clusters hold the six — chapters 2 and 3 at the front of the Book, and chapters 29 through 32 in an unbroken run of four. Luqmān is the third of that run, and the four chapters move through related ground: the signs lying in the world and in the history of earlier peoples, the counsel of a man given wisdom rather than revelation, then the resurrection of bodies and the settling of accounts. The shared opening marks the family resemblance without announcing it. A reader who has just finished al-Rūm hears the same three letters and knows that the argument continues rather than restarts, while the subject changes from the fortunes of empires to the instruction of a father in his own house. The scale contracts. What was true of Rome and Persia is now true of a single household, and the letters are the hinge on which the contraction turns.

**A Knock, and the Door It Knocks On**

In twenty-six of the twenty-nine chapters that begin with disconnected letters, a mention of revelation follows almost immediately; al-ʿAnkabūt, al-Rūm and al-Qalam are the exceptions. The pattern allows the letters to be described functionally without anyone having to claim a meaning for them. They are a knock. What the knock opens is named straight away, and in this chapter the naming happens in the very next line: *“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom”* (31:2). Nobody is left holding three sounds with nothing attached to them. The same structure governs chapters that open with a different set of letters: *“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom”* (10:1) follows Alif-Lãm-Ra, and *“These are the verses of the clear Book”* (12:1) follows the same three letters again. The knock is identical; the door differs. This is why the letters resist paraphrase and still do work. They perform an act of attention — stop, listen, a book is being opened — and that act needs no vocabulary.

**What the Exegetes Proposed, and Why It Will Not Hold**

Two families of interpretation compete here. The first holds that the meaning belongs to God alone and that these letters stand among the matters the text deliberately leaves unexplained; this is the majority position and rests on the absence of any Prophetic gloss. The second assigns each letter a divine name — alif for Allāh, lām for al-Laṭīf, the Subtle, mīm for al-Majīd, the Glorious — or treats the set as an abbreviation of a longer sentence. The second family fails for a structural reason rather than a pious one. Its expansions contradict each other, and each depends on picking one name out of several equally eligible candidates. Alif could stand for al-ʿAlīm as readily as for Allāh; mīm could stand for al-Malik or for Muḥammad. An interpretation satisfied by any of several mutually exclusive options, with no rule for choosing among them, is a preference dressed as a reading. The majority position is the stronger of the two because it is the only one that explains why the letters were preserved in recitation for fourteen centuries without anyone attaching a meaning to them. A gap that nobody filled is evidence about the gap.

**Knowing Where Knowledge Stops Is Part of Wisdom**

This opening trains a habit the rest of the chapter needs. Not everything in a book that gives reasons has to give one, and a text can mark the point where knowledge ends without losing any authority by doing so. The Qur'an names the category itself: some verses are precise and form the foundation of the Book, *“while others are elusive”* (3:7), and the chasing of the elusive ones is attributed there to hearts that deviate. Three letters at the head of a chapter about wisdom make that point in miniature. The first task of wisdom is to recognise what has not been given. The counsel that follows in this chapter is relentlessly practical — worship, the accounting of deeds, how to walk, how loud to speak — and it is placed behind an opening that declines to be practical at all. A reader who cannot tolerate an unanswered line will not manage the practical ones either, because those demand obedience before they deliver understanding.

**The Identical Opening of the Chapter That Follows**

Al-Sajdah begins with the same three letters and carries the same argument into harder ground: the resurrection of the body, the book of deeds placed in a hand, and the moment the wrongdoers ask to be sent back. The pairing shows what the letters are doing across chapters 29 to 32 as a block. They mark a sequence of four revelations that were received close together and that share a single question — what a human being does with the signs he has already been given. This chapter answers it through a wise man's instruction to his son.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:2

> These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥakīm Describes the Book Three Different Ways at Once**

The adjective is *ḥakīm*. Its most common sense is "wise", and that is how the translation renders it: the verses belong to a Book rich in wisdom. Two further senses are carried by the same letters, and the classical exegetes did not choose between them so much as stack them. *Ḥakīm* can be read as *muḥkam*, made firm or determined, which is the sense at work in the description of a Book *“whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained”* (11:1). It can also be read as *muḥkim*, the active form, that which makes firm or makes wise — a Book which confers the wisdom it is named for. The three readings are not rivals competing for one slot. A text can be wise in its content, settled in its wording, and productive of wisdom in its reader, and the Qur'an elsewhere claims all three: it is described as wise in an oath (*“By the Quran, rich in wisdom!”* (36:2)) and as wise in the heavenly record (*“And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom”* 43:4). One adjective doing three jobs is an economy the verse can afford because the subject is a book.

**Nothing Can Prove It False From Any Angle**

Reading *ḥakīm* as *muḥkam* gives the adjective an edge. A verse that has been made firm cannot be undone from inside or attacked from outside. The Qur'an states this directly of itself: *“It cannot be proven false from any angle. ˹It is˺ a revelation from the ˹One Who is˺ All-Wise, Praiseworthy”* (41:42). Ibn Kathīr connects the two claims: the firmness of the Book is what prevents falsehood from approaching it from before it or from behind it. The point is not that the text is beyond criticism but that it is beyond revision. A determined wording has no loose ends that a later hand can pull, and the doctrine of preservation in Islam rests on exactly that property. What was revealed was fixed at the moment of revelation, letter by letter, and the fixity is why a Muslim can recite in Jakarta what was recited in Madinah. Wisdom that could be edited would not be wisdom; it would be a draft.

**"These" Rather Than "Those"**

The demonstrative is near, not far. *Hādhihi āyāt* — these are the verses. The speaker is not pointing back at something finished or forward at something promised but at something present in the room, being recited as the line is heard. The near demonstrative turns an announcement into an exhibit. This matters for what follows in the chapter, because verse 6 describes people who prefer other talk and verse 7 describes people who turn away as though they could not hear. Before either group appears, the verses are placed in front of the listener as something already available. Nobody in this chapter is short of material; the failure described later is a refusal, not a deprivation. The same near demonstrative opens chapter 10 (*“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom”* (10:1)) and chapter 12 (*“These are the verses of the clear Book”* 12:1), which is enough to establish it as a habit of these openings rather than a local flourish.

**Wisdom Also Means That the Parts Agree**

A third implication of *ḥakīm* belongs to the structure of the text rather than to its content. Wisdom in ordinary speech is not only knowing the right thing but arranging things so that they fit, and a wise plan is one whose parts do not contradict each other. The Qur'an claims exactly this property for itself and ties it to the same adjective: God *“has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons”* (39:23). Consistency and repetition are named together because a book that repeats itself across decades of revelation without contradicting itself is doing something a human author working under changing circumstances does not manage. Chapter 11 makes the parallel claim in the language of firmness: a Book whose verses are perfected and then explained, from the One who is All-Wise and All-Aware (11:1). Perfection there means the wording is finished and settled; explanation means the meaning is unpacked rather than left obscure. The adjective in 31:2 gathers all of it. A Book that is *ḥakīm* is wise in what it says, firm in how it is worded, coherent in how its parts relate, and productive of wisdom in the person who follows it.

**Wisdom Belongs to the Book Before It Belongs to the Man**

The chapter takes its name from Luqmān, a man who is described in verse 12 as having been given *ḥikmah* — wisdom. The Book is called wise eleven verses earlier. That order is the chapter's argument in miniature. Human wisdom in this sūrah is borrowed, derivative, and addressed to a single son in a single household; the wisdom of the revelation is original and addressed to everyone. When the father's counsel finally arrives it agrees with the Book at every point, and the agreement is the evidence that his wisdom is real. Nothing here makes Luqmān a source of authority in his own right. He is a man whom God endowed, and his sayings are preserved in the Qur'an because they match the Qur'an, not because they preceded it. A reader who takes the chapter as a collection of a sage's maxims has inverted the sequence and lost the reason the maxims are there.

**Six Chapters Open on the Same Note**

The formula "these are the verses of the Book" runs through a family of openings: chapters 10, 12, 13, 15, 26, 27 and 28 all begin with disconnected letters followed by a declaration about the verses. Chapter 15 says, *“These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran”* (15:1); chapter 27 says, *“These are the verses of the Quran; the clear Book”* (27:1); chapter 13 adds the truth claim at once, *“What has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ from your Lord is the truth, but most people do not believe”* (13:1). The variation is small and instructive. Wisdom, clarity, and truth are three properties of one object, and different chapters announce different ones first. This chapter chooses wisdom, and the choice fits what follows: a book of signs, then a father's instruction, then the refutation of people who argue without evidence. Every one of those three needs judgement more than it needs information.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:3

> ˹It is˺ a guide and mercy for the good-doers—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance and Mercy Are Two Different Gifts**

The pair *hudan wa-raḥmatan* is joined by one conjunction, and the two words are not synonyms. Guidance is directional: it identifies the road and puts the traveller on it. Mercy is personal: it looks after the traveller who is on the road and cannot complete it alone. A map without mercy describes a journey no one could survive, since nobody follows a road without stumbling. Mercy without guidance is warmth with nowhere to go. The Qur'an pairs them constantly, and the pairing is a standing claim about revelation: it does not merely inform and leave the informed to manage. The same pair describes the scripture given to Moses — *“as a guide and a mercy, so perhaps they would be certain of the meeting with their Lord”* (6:154) — and the Book given to Muhammad ﷺ, *“a guide and mercy for those who believe”* (16:64). Wherever the two appear together the revelation is presented as doing something to its reader, not only saying something to him.

**The Sentence Is Left Hanging on Purpose**

The verse ends with a dash and no predicate completed. Grammatically the line is unfinished, and the next verse supplies what is missing: the good-doers are *"those who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter"* (31:4). The suspension is a deliberate device. It forces the reader forward, and it makes the definition arrive as a specification rather than as a compliment. If the two verses had stood separately, "guide and mercy for the good-doers" would read as praise of a virtuous class. Joined by an enjambment, it reads as a description with a checklist attached. The dash is the difference between a blessing and a condition, and the chapter wants the condition.

**Whether Guidance Is Restricted or Not**

The Arabic admits two readings of the case ending, and the exegetes divided on it. Read as a nominative, "a guide and mercy" describes the Book itself — the Book is guidance and mercy, full stop. Read as an accusative, it states the purpose of the revelation of its verses: they were sent down *as* guidance and mercy. The first reading is descriptive, the second teleological, and both are attested. A separate question sits underneath: does "for the good-doers" restrict the guidance, so that only they receive it? The stronger answer distinguishes two senses of the word. Guidance as direction is universal — the Book points the same way for everyone who opens it, and nobody is excluded from the pointing. Guidance as success in following is particular, because following requires the doer's own assent. Under that distinction the verse says something exact: the revelation is offered to all, and it functions as guidance and mercy for those who act in accord with it. The restriction is in the effect, not in the offer.

**Iḥsān Means Doing a Thing Beautifully**

The good-doers are *muḥsinūn*, from the root *ḥ-s-n*, which carries beauty and excellence rather than mere compliance. An *muḥsin* is not someone who has avoided wrong but someone who has done right well. The Qur'an attaches a promise to this quality: *“As for those who struggle in Our cause, We will surely guide them along Our Way. And Allah is certainly with the good-doers”* (29:69). The Prophet ﷺ defined the term in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8, as worshipping God as though you see Him, and if you do not reach that state, knowing that He sees you. That definition explains why the verse links iḥsān to prayer and to certainty about the Hereafter. Excellence in worship is the awareness of being watched, and the awareness is sustained by conviction that the watching has an end and a verdict.

**Mercy Follows the Struggle and Not the Wish**

There is a temptation to read "a guide and mercy for the good-doers" as a promise of comfort for people who already feel virtuous. The surrounding verses close that reading. The good-doers are specified as those who erect prayer, hand over alms, and hold certainty about a future reckoning (31:4) — three things that cost something, one of which costs wealth and one of which costs the ego five times a day. The chapter that contains this verse makes the cost explicit: *“As for those who struggle in Our cause, We will surely guide them along Our Way”* (29:69), and two verses earlier it states that whoever strives does so only for his own good, since God stands in need of none of His creation (29:6). Guidance is promised to the one who strives, and mercy arrives alongside the guidance. The sequence is not a reward for a feeling; it is support given to an effort already under way. This is also why the pair cannot be reversed. Mercy without the struggle produces a person who expects to be carried, and the Qur'an's account of such a person is unflattering: he is the one who disputes about God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating scripture, a description that appears twice in this chapter alone (31:20). The good-doer of verse 3 is defined against that figure, and the mercy he receives is the help he needs to keep going rather than an exemption from going.

**The Same Pair Was Given to Moses**

Two verses elsewhere describe an earlier revelation in the same two words. God gave Moses the scripture *“completing the favour upon those who do good, detailing everything, and as a guide and a mercy”* (6:154), and gave him the scripture again *“after destroying earlier nations—as an insight for the people, a guide, and mercy”* (28:43). The repetition establishes that guidance and mercy are properties of revelation as such, not privileges of one dispensation. What distinguishes this verse from those two is the audience named. Moses' book is described as guidance and mercy for those who do good in general terms; here the good-doers are defined within two lines, by prayer, alms and certainty. The mercy is the same. The specification of who receives it is sharper.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:4

> those who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Items, and Each Points a Different Direction**

The verse defines the good-doers with three things and no more. Prayer governs the relation to God and the discipline of the self. Alms govern property and therefore the relation to other people. Certainty about the Hereafter governs time, placing the whole of a life inside a horizon that extends past death. The three are not a list of duties chosen for their importance; they are three axes, and a person who holds all three is oriented in every direction at once. The identical formulation appears in the chapter of the Ant — *“those who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter”* (27:3) — which suggests a fixed triad rather than an ad hoc selection. Alms also appear in the description of the believers whose guardianship God accepts: *“who establish prayer and pay alms-tax with humility”* (5:55). The recurrence is the evidence that this is a defined category, not a rhetorical flourish.

**Establishing Prayer Is Not the Same as Praying**

The verb is *yuqīmūna*, from *qāma*, to stand or to set upright. The form is causative: they *erect* the prayer. Arabic could have used *ṣallā* — they pray — and the Qur'an uses that verb elsewhere. The choice of the causative form carries a distinct claim. A prayer that is erected is one that has been made to stand and kept standing: performed at its times, with its conditions met, its pillars complete, and its effect left standing in the person's behaviour afterwards. A prayer performed once and abandoned is not erected; it is dropped. The distinction is the one between an act and an institution the individual maintains inside himself. Translators who render *yuqīmūna* as "establish" are preserving a difference that "perform" would flatten.

**Zakāh Purifies Before It Transfers**

The word is *zakāh*, from a root meaning both to grow and to be pure. The alms-tax is named for what it does to the payer before it is named for what it does to the receiver. God commands the Prophet ﷺ to *“Take from their wealth ˹O Prophet˺ charity to purify and bless them”* (9:103). Purification comes first in that sentence, blessing second, and both are effects on the giver. This is why zakāh is an obligation with a fixed threshold and fixed shares rather than a discretionary act of generosity: it is not a favour extended downward but a cleaning performed on wealth that has begun to accumulate. The verse's placement of it beside prayer makes the point structurally. Prayer is the worship performed with the body; alms are the worship performed with property. Both are owed, and neither is optional.

**Prayer Is Measured in Time and Alms in Wealth**

The two acts named in this verse are governed by two different kinds of quantity, and the difference explains why they are paired so constantly across the Qur'an. Prayer is measured in hours. It is tied to the movement of the sun: *“Observe the prayer from the decline of the sun until the darkness of the night and the dawn prayer, for certainly the dawn prayer is witnessed ˹by angels˺”* (17:78), and it is described as *“a duty on the believers at the appointed times”* (4:103). Nothing about it depends on what a person owns. Alms are measured in wealth. They have a threshold below which nothing is owed, fixed categories of recipient, and a share that belongs to the poor as a right rather than as a favour: the alms-tax is assigned to eight named groups in a verse that closes by calling the assignment an obligation from God (9:60), and the righteous are described as those in whose wealth there is *“a rightful share ˹fulfilled˺ for the beggar and the poor”* (51:19). Between them the two cover a person completely. Prayer takes his time, which is the one resource he cannot recover; alms take his property, which is the resource he is most reluctant to release. A piety that offers neither has offered nothing, and a piety that offers only one has halved the demand. This is why the command *“Establish prayer, pay alms-tax”* appears as a single unit in the earliest Medinan legislation (2:43; 2:110) and why the guardianship of the believing community is defined by it (9:71).

**Certainty Is the Load-Bearing Member**

The third item is not another duty but a state: they *have sure faith* — *yūqinūn*, from *yaqīn*, certainty. The Qur'an elsewhere uses the same noun as a term for death itself, since death is the point at which certainty arrives whether or not it was held before: *“and worship your Lord until the inevitable comes your way”* (15:99). Naming the Hereafter rather than God as the object of this certainty is a precise choice. Belief in God can be held vaguely, as an inheritance or a habit, without altering a single decision. Belief that there is a return, an accounting, and a verdict is what converts prayer and alms from custom into investment. Without it the first two items lose their reason: a man who prays and gives alms to no final reckoning is performing etiquette. The triad is ordered so that the visible acts come first and the conviction that makes them meaningful comes last, holding them up.

**Why the Hereafter Is Named and Not God**

An obvious question sits in the verse's construction. Faith in God is assumed; the Qur'an's argument with its opponents is rarely about whether a God exists, since chapter 43 records that when they are asked who created the heavens and the earth they answer without hesitation (43:9). What is contested is the return. The Makkan objection was to resurrection, not to divinity, and the sūrahs of this period spend their energy there. Naming certainty about the Hereafter as the mark of the good-doer identifies the actual fault line of the audience. The same fault line runs through the rest of this chapter, which closes by listing the matters God alone knows, including the Hour and the place of every death (31:34).

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:5

> It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words That Close the Door**

The sentence is built to exclude. *Ulāʾika* — those — points back at the three-item description of the previous verse. *Hum* — they — repeats the subject, and in Arabic a repeated subject of this kind restricts the predicate to it. The effect is not "the guided include these people" but "these people are the guided, and nobody else is". The same device is used twice in the single line, once for guidance and once for success, which doubles the restriction. The verse is identical to the one that closes the opening description of the believers in al-Baqarah (2:5), where it also follows a list of qualities. That repetition across twenty-six years of revelation shows a fixed formula: the qualities are enumerated, then the group is sealed with a demonstrative and a pronoun. The seal is the point. A description of virtue without it would be an invitation; with it, it is a determination.

**Being Upon Guidance Is Not Walking Toward It**

The preposition matters. They are *ʿalā hudā* — upon guidance from their Lord. Guidance is treated as a surface or a mount, not as a destination. The commentators bring this out plainly: the people described ride upon guidance and are made firm by it, and they are upon a light from their Lord. The image is exact and it changes the sense of the sentence. Someone walking toward guidance does not yet have it; someone standing on it is carried by it and can see from it. The preposition also does something to agency. A rider is moving, but the mount is doing much of the work, and the verse attributes the guidance to their Lord rather than to themselves: *min rabbihim*, from their Lord. Guidance originates outside the guided. What the previous verse described — prayer, alms, certainty — is the response, not the source.

**Falaḥ Carries the Farmer's Meaning**

*Yufliḥūn* — they will be successful — comes from the root *f-l-ḥ*, which is agricultural before it is evaluative. To *falaḥa* is to cleave or plough the soil, and the *fallāḥ* is the ploughman, the word still used for a farmer across the Arabic-speaking world. Success in this idiom is not the arrival of good fortune but the harvest of ground that was opened up and sown. The etymology fits the verse's position perfectly. Verses 3 and 4 describe the labour: erecting prayer, paying alms, holding certainty. Verse 5 names the crop. Nothing in the sequence suggests that the harvest comes to someone who did not plough, and nothing suggests that the ploughing fails. The word *falāḥ* also gives Islam its call to prayer its final line, "come to success", which places the same agricultural promise at the threshold of every daily prayer.

**Why Success Comes Last and Not First**

The order of the three verses is deliberate: a description of the Book, then a description of the people, then a verdict on the people. The verdict is not stated at the outset because it depends on the description. A reader who wants the outcome has to pass through the conditions, and the conditions are specific enough to be checked. This is a general habit of Qur'anic rhetoric in these openings. Al-Baqarah spends three verses describing the righteous before it declares them successful (2:2–5). Here the sequence is compressed into the same shape: the good-doers are defined by what they do, and only then are they told what they will get. The arrangement protects the promise from being read as a general reassurance. It is a conclusion drawn from premises, and the premises are named.

**Guidance Is Present and Success Is Still Coming**

The grammar splits the sentence into two tenses, and the split is meaningful. They *are* upon guidance from their Lord — a present state, held now. They *will be* successful — a future outcome, not yet delivered. Nobody in this verse is described as having arrived. Guidance is what the good-doer possesses in the middle of his life, and success is what he receives at the end of it. The distinction protects the promise from two errors. It cannot be read as a claim that the virtuous prosper now, since the verse puts success in the future; and it cannot be read as a claim that virtue is unrewarded, since the future tense is a statement of what will happen rather than of what might. The Qur'an states the same structure elsewhere in plain terms: *“Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment”* (3:185). Triumph is located after death, and the world's enjoyment is called a delusion precisely because it arrives early and looks like the answer. The same word for success is spoken aloud five times a day in the call to prayer, "come to success", which places the future outcome at the beginning of every act of worship — the believer walks toward the prayer, and the prayer is a step toward something he has not yet reached.

**The Opening of al-Baqarah Ends the Same Way**

The parallel is exact. Al-Baqarah describes those who *“believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them”* (2:3), then those who believe in the revelation given to Muhammad ﷺ and before him and who *“have sure faith in the Hereafter”* (2:4), and closes with the identical line: *“It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful”* (2:5). The two chapters are separated by twenty-nine others and by the whole change of circumstance between Madinah and Makkah, yet the formula does not move. One item differs instructively. Al-Baqarah adds belief in the unseen and in previous revelation; Luqmān omits both and keeps prayer, alms, and certainty about the Hereafter. The shorter list is the harder one, because it contains nothing a person can hold passively. Every item on it is something done or something relied on under pressure.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:6

> But there are some who employ theatrics, only to lead others away from Allah’s Way—without any knowledge—and to make a mockery of it. They will suffer a humiliating punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lahw al-Ḥadīth Is Talk Whose Whole Job Is Distraction**

The Arabic behind "theatrics" is *lahw al-ḥadīth*. *Lahw* is diversion, amusement, the thing that occupies a person and takes him off something else; *ḥadīth* is talk, discourse, a story being told. Put together the phrase names a genre of speech defined not by its content but by its effect: talk that pulls attention away. The Qur'an has a term for the opposite, and it uses it of itself — the revelation is called *"the best message"* (39:23). The contrast is between two kinds of discourse competing for the same listener at the same hour. Nothing in the phrase restricts the talk to a particular subject. What condemns it is the direction it points: away from God's way, without knowledge, into mockery. Three qualifiers do the work, and each will be examined below, but the first thing to settle is that the verse is about a use of speech rather than a topic of speech.

**Three Candidates for What Is Being Bought, and Which Survives**

The exegetes proposed three referents. The first is the tale-telling of al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, who returned from his travels with the stories of the Persian kings — Rustam, Isfandiyār, Khosrow — and told the Quraysh that Muhammad ﷺ spoke to them of ʿĀd and Thamūd while he would speak of these; the audience found his tales more entertaining than the recitation. These occasion-reports are narrative *āthār* passed down through the commentators rather than graded prophetic traditions, and they carry the usual weakness of that genre. The second candidate is singing, and specifically the hiring of singing girls to amuse a client and keep him away from the message. The third is the broadest: any speech whatsoever that distracts from God. The broad reading is the strongest, and the reason is internal. The verse attaches a purpose (*to lead others away*), a condition (*without any knowledge*), and an attitude (*to make a mockery of it*). A genre cannot carry all three; a use of speech can. The narrow readings remain valid as instances, not as limits.

**The Verb Is Buying, and No Money Need Change Hands**

*Yashtarī* comes from purchase, but Arabic uses it for any act of taking one thing in preference to another, whether or not a price is paid. The same verb describes the people who *“trade guidance for misguidance”* (2:16), where the trade is explicitly called profitless. In this verse the purchase can be read literally — paying a singer, paying a storyteller, paying for an evening's amusement — or as the metaphor of preference, choosing idle talk over true talk and harmful discourse over beneficial discourse. The metaphorical reading explains a difficulty in the literal one: plenty of people are led away from God's way by talk that costs them nothing. The verse condemns the transaction, not the invoice. What is given up is named in chapter 2, and what is taken in exchange is named here, and the imbalance is the whole point.

**Three Qualifiers That Make the Condemnation Exact**

The verse does not condemn distraction in general. It names a man who leads others away from God's way, who does so without knowledge, and who takes the way in mockery. Each qualifier narrows the target. *Without any knowledge* removes the sincere enquirer from the description: a person arguing from error he believes is not the one being condemned here, and the Qur'an distinguishes the two elsewhere when it says that some dispute about God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating scripture (22:8). *To lead others away* makes the act social rather than private; the sin is proselytising, not merely drifting. *To make a mockery of it* supplies the tone, and mockery is the one attitude that cannot be corrected by argument, since it refuses to treat the object as a candidate for truth. A person who holds all three at once has closed the door that guidance opens.

**One Hadith, and How Much Weight It Can Bear**

The report most often brought to this verse is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5590, from Abū ʿĀmir or Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī, who heard the Prophet ﷺ say that there will be people among his followers who consider unlawful sexual intercourse, silk, wine and musical instruments to be lawful. The report's status needs stating honestly: al-Bukhārī records it under a chapter heading without giving the full chain of transmission attached to it, a form known as *muʿallaq*, suspended, and later scholars supplied the missing links through other routes. The report is therefore used as evidence about instruments, and it is also the reason the singing reading of *lahw al-ḥadīth* has never been abandoned. It does not, however, settle the scope of the verse. A hadith about a future tendency in the community is not a definition of a Qur'anic phrase, and the verse's own three qualifiers remain the controlling text.

**Humiliation Is the Punishment That Fits the Mockery**

The sentence ends on *ʿadhāb muhīn*, a humiliating punishment. The correspondence is exact and it is the verse's own logic. Mockery is an attempt to reduce the other party, to make the sacred look small in front of an audience; the punishment is reduction performed without an audience to appeal to. The same phrase appears where people learn something of the revelations and mock it: *“It is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment”* (45:9). Where the Qur'an promises the mocker humiliation rather than pain, it is answering the specific harm done. Pain is generic. Humiliation is the return of what was given, and the verse says so by choosing the word.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:7

> Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they turn away in arrogance as if they did not hear them, as if there is deafness in their ears. So give them good news ˹O Prophet˺ of a painful punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Similes, and the Second Explains the First**

The verse gives the same picture twice, and the doubling is not padding. The first simile is behavioural: he turns away *as if they did not hear them*. The second is physical: *as if there is deafness in their ears*. Both are governed by *ka-anna*, as though, which marks a comparison to something that is not the case. Nobody here is actually deaf and nobody actually fails to hear; the recitation reaches the ear and is understood well enough to be turned away from. What the two similes together describe is a decision dressed as an incapacity. The person behaves as though the sound never arrived, and the verse says the behaviour is a performance of deafness rather than deafness. This is a distinction the Qur'an makes repeatedly, and it is the reason the condemnation here is severe: an inability would call for help, while a refusal calls for a verdict.

**Turning Away Has a Direction and a Reason**

*Yuwalli mustakbiran* — he turns away in arrogance. The participle *mustakbir* comes from *kibr*, the self-magnification that the Prophet ﷺ defined in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 91 as disdaining the truth and holding people in contempt. Both halves are visible in this single verse. The disdain is toward the revelations; the contempt is toward whoever is reciting them. Chapter 22 uses almost the same construction and adds the purpose: *“turning away ˹in pride˺ to lead ˹others˺ astray from Allah's Way”* (22:9). The arrogance there is not a private mood but an instrument, and the same is implied here by the phrase "only to lead others away" in the preceding verse. Turning away in front of an audience does more than express a preference; it authorises the audience to turn away too.

**The Qur'an Has a Whole Vocabulary for Deafness of the Second Kind**

The theme recurs often enough to constitute a technical language. Some listen to the recitation while *“We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears”* (6:25). The disbelievers are compared to a flock that does not understand the calls of its shepherd, *“˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb and blind so they have no understanding”* (2:171). The worst creatures in God's sight are *“the ˹wilfully˺ deaf and dumb, who do not understand”* (8:22). The Prophet ﷺ is told directly that he cannot make the deaf hear the call when they have turned their backs and walked away (30:52), and that there is deafness in the ears of those who reject the Book, as though they were being called from a faraway place (41:44). The consistent marker in all of these is the adverb of will. The deafness is chosen, and it is chosen by turning the body around.

**One Reaction Among Many That the Qur'an Records**

This verse is not the only account of what happens when revelation is recited, and the alternatives show how specific the description is. Some hear the recitation and answer it with a challenge: *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they challenge ˹you˺, 'We have already heard ˹the recitation˺. If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar. This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!'”* (8:31). Some learn something of the revelations and mock it (45:9). Some pretend to listen while veils are cast over their hearts (6:25). The reaction described here is the quietest of the set: no counter-claim, no argument, no insult offered in reply — only a body turning away and a face arranging itself as though nothing had been said. That silence is what makes the gesture so serious. The man who argues can be answered; the man who claims he could produce something similar has at least entered the contest. The one who turns away has declined to appear, and the verse treats the refusal as the more revealing act, which is why the only thing left to give him is news of what he has refused.

**Good News Is Being Used Against Its Own Meaning**

*Bashshirhum* — give them good news — is the verb normally reserved for the announcement of salvation, mercy, and the gardens. It is used here, and in several other places, of a punishment. The inversion is rhetorical and deliberate, and it has two readings that are not mutually exclusive. The first is ironic: the man who took the way of God in mockery is answered in the register of mockery, and the news he is given matches the tone he set. The second is literal in a different sense: the punishment is announced as news because news is information about something certain and coming, and the certainty is the point. The same reversal runs through the Book — *“give them good news of a painful punishment”* (3:21; 84:24), and the command to the Prophet ﷺ to give good news to the disbelievers of a painful punishment (9:3). Chapter 45 pairs the mockery and the inverted news in two consecutive statements, which is the clearest evidence that the irony is intentional rather than incidental.

**The Singular Pronoun After a Plural Subject**

Verse 6 speaks of "some" in the plural; verse 7 in the Arabic turns to the singular, describing what happens when the revelations are recited *to him*. The English translation keeps the plural, and something is lost in doing so. The singular isolates one man in the act. It says that the description is not about a faction but about a person at a moment: someone sitting where a recitation is happening, and deciding. The shift also does a piece of moral work. A group that turns away shares responsibility diffusely; an individual who turns away owns it. The verse's final command to give the news confirms the individualisation, since news is delivered to a person.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:8

> Surely those who believe and do good will have the Gardens of Bliss

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jannāt al-Naʿīm Names the Place by What It Contains**

The phrase is *jannāt al-naʿīm* — gardens of bliss. The construction is a genitive of quality: the gardens are specified by delight as their defining content, not merely described as containing some of it. Both nouns are plural or unbounded. *Jannāt* is plural, which the exegetes read as indicating ranks and levels rather than a single plot, and *naʿīm* is a noun of intensive form, denoting abundance of ease. The word *jannah* itself is from a root meaning to cover or conceal, and it is the same root that gives *jinn* and *jannah* as an orchard whose floor is hidden by its canopy. The image is of shade and enclosure, of a place you are inside rather than looking at. Bliss here is not a mood added to a location; it is the substance the location is made of, which is why the verse can name the place and the promise in the same breath.

**The Answer to the Mockers Arrives Without Mentioning Them**

Verse 6 condemns those who employ theatrics to lead people away, and verse 7 tells them to expect a painful punishment. Verse 8 turns and states the reward of the believers without a word about the others. The juxtaposition does the argument's work. Two outcomes are placed side by side with no connective and no comparison drawn, and the reader is left to measure the distance. This is a habit of Qur'anic rhetoric in the Makkan chapters: the threat and the promise alternate, each sharpened by the other, and neither is explained by reference to the other. The structure also answers an objection that has not been voiced. The mocker of verse 6 was winning an audience, and an audience judges by results. Verse 8 states the result of the other course, and states it in the plural — gardens, not a garden — against the single humiliating punishment.

**Faith and Action Are Joined by One Letter**

*Āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believe and do good — is bound by a single conjunction, *wāw*, and the pair functions in the Qur'an as a compound term rather than as two conditions listed together. In this translation the formula appears forty-seven times across the chapters. Its frequency is the point: the Book does not treat belief as a state that can be complete without output, and it does not treat good deeds as valuable apart from the belief that gives them a direction. Where either member is missing the phrase is not used. The verse therefore names a category the reader can test himself against, and the test has two halves. Anyone who claims the first half while neglecting the second has not met the description in this line, and the reward attached to it is attached to the whole compound.

**Delight Supplied Rather Than Manufactured**

*Naʿīm* denotes ease that comes to a person from outside him, provision rather than achievement. The same noun is used for the luxuries of this world that are squandered and asked about: the question on the Day will concern the *naʿīm* people were given. The difference between the two uses is permanence, which the next verse supplies. Here the delight is what the gardens consist of; there it is what a life consisted of and what is now being accounted for. The word's neutrality is instructive. Ease is not condemned in the Qur'an and it is not praised; it is a gift with an address on it, and the address determines whether it becomes a blessing or a charge. The gardens are named for ease because ease, in that place, has no bill attached to it.

**Bliss Has a Counterpart in the Same Chapter, and It Is Not Bliss**

The Qur'an is careful to distinguish the delight of the gardens from the enjoyment of this world, and it does so in a single sentence elsewhere: *“As for the disbelievers, they enjoy themselves and feed like cattle. But the Fire will be their home”* (47:12). The comparison with cattle is the operative part. An animal eating is receiving pleasure; it is not receiving a gift, because a gift implies a giver recognised and a purpose understood. Enjoyment without recognition is what the verse calls the disbeliever's condition, and it ends when the body ends. *Naʿīm* in the gardens is the same substance under different conditions: delight that is known to be given, known to be deserved, and known to be uninterrupted. This chapter sets the two side by side within twenty verses, and the contrast explains why the reward is named as gardens rather than as pleasure. A garden is a place with structure, boundaries, water, shade and a resident — delight that has been built and settled rather than felt and gone. The mockery described in verses 6 and 7 is the behaviour of people who have chosen the feeding; the bliss promised in verse 8 belongs to people who have chosen the garden.

**Other Names for the Same Place**

The Qur'an varies the designation and each variation stresses a different property. Some have *“Gardens under which rivers flow”* (47:12), stressing the water and the shade. Some have *“the Gardens of Paradise as an accommodation”* (18:107), where the word *nuzul* is the provision set out for a guest on arrival, which makes the entry an act of hospitality. Some have a *“never-ending reward”* (41:8), which names the duration rather than the setting. And some have the same statement as here, that those who believe and do good will have gardens under which rivers flow, followed by the verdict: *“That is the greatest triumph”* (85:11). Read together the variations cover everything a promise needs: what the place is like, how the entrant is received, how long it lasts, and what the whole transaction amounts to.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:9

> staying there forever. Allah’s promise is true. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**Khālidūn Is the Participle of Staying**

The verb of permanence is a noun form here: *khālidūn fīhā*, staying there, abiding in them. The active participle denotes a continuous state rather than a completed event, and the preposition *fī* — in, not at — puts the inhabitants inside the place. The plural of the pronoun agrees with the plural of the gardens, so the abiding is in the gardens, and the gardens are the location of an unending condition. Arabic has other words for lasting, and the Qur'an uses them, but *khuld* and its derivatives carry the sense of remaining unchanged through time, which is why the same root names eternity itself. The word does real theological work: it excludes the possibility that the reward is a long sentence that eventually runs out, which was one of the claims the Qur'an disputes with earlier communities.

**The Promise Is Called True in the Same Breath**

*Waʿda Llāhi ḥaqqan* — God's promise in truth. The noun *waʿd* is a promise of good, as distinct from *waʿīd*, a threat; Arabic keeps the two apart, and the Qur'an uses *waʿd* for what believers are given and *waʿīd* for what deniers are warned of. Calling the promise true does two things. It asserts correspondence between the statement and the future event, so that what has been said will be found to have been accurate. And it asserts the character of the speaker: a promise is true when the promiser both intends it and can do it. Both halves are covered by the two names that follow. The verse is therefore not offering reassurance in the ordinary sense, which is comfort in the absence of evidence. It is offering a guarantee with the grounds of the guarantee stated in the next clause.

**Might and Wisdom Are the Two Guarantors**

The closing pair is *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*, the Almighty and the All-Wise. The commentators draw the logic out precisely. God is the Mighty whom nothing overcomes or thwarts, and so He is able to bestow what He has promised on whomsoever He wills; and He is the Wise, so the bestowal is not arbitrary but apportioned with justice and measure. Each name alone would leave a gap. Power without wisdom would mean a reward distributed at random, and a person could not rely on receiving his own share. Wisdom without power would mean a fair intention that circumstances might defeat. Together they close the promise from both sides. This is why the pair appears so often at the end of statements about reward and about legislation, and why it appears here rather than at the end of a statement about mercy alone.

**Why Not Mercy and Generosity at This Point**

The choice of attributes is not interchangeable, and the surrounding verses make the reason visible. The chapter has just described a humiliating punishment for people who mock the way of God (31:6–7), and it will describe the creation of the heavens, the mountains and the rain within the next verse (31:10). A reader moving through that sequence needs two assurances: that the reward will actually be delivered, and that it will be delivered to the right people. Mercy and generosity would answer a different anxiety. Might answers the question of delivery; wisdom answers the question of allotment. Where the Qur'an closes a statement of forgiveness it typically names the Forgiving and the Merciful; where it closes a statement of settled outcomes it names the Mighty and the Wise. The pattern holds here.

**ʿAzīz Is the Name That Guarantees Delivery**

Might in this verse is not a general attribute but a specific capacity: the capacity to do what has been said and to prevent anyone from undoing it. The Qur'an describes that capacity in operational terms. God *“keeps the sky from falling down on the earth except by His permission”* (22:65) — the structure of the world is being held, and the holding is continuous. He *“originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him”* (30:27), and chapter 85 makes the same claim in a single line: *“˹For˺ He is certainly the One Who originates and resurrects ˹all˺”* (85:13). A promise of eternal gardens requires exactly these two powers: the power to bring people back and the power to keep the place standing forever. Neither is within the reach of any created thing, which is why the promise is attached to the name that no creature shares. This is also why the verse does not hedge. There is no "perhaps", no "if God wills" attached to the statement that the promise is true, because the hedge would introduce a contingency and the whole purpose of naming the Mighty is to remove one.

**The Same Formula in Three Other Places**

The claim that God's promise is true recurs with the same confidence and the same note of complaint about those who miss it. *“Surely Allah's promise is ˹always˺ true, but most of them do not know”* (10:55). *“˹This is˺ the promise of Allah. ˹And˺ Allah never fails in His promise. But most people do not know”* (30:6). And in the address to all humanity near the end of chapter 35: *“Indeed, Allah's promise is true. So do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the Chief Deceiver deceive you about Allah”* (35:5). The repetition of "but most of them do not know" in the first two shows where the difficulty lies. The problem is not that the promise is doubtful but that people do not treat it as knowledge. This chapter will close on the same theme, listing the matters God alone knows (31:34), and the connection between the two is that a person who misplaces his certainty about the future misplaces his life.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:10

> He created the heavens without pillars—as you can see—and placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with you, and scattered throughout it all types of creatures. And We send down rain from the sky, causing every type of fine plant to grow on earth

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Ceiling Held Up by Nothing You Can Find**

The heavens are created *without pillars*, and the clause "as you can see" is where the exegetes divided. Read as qualifying the pillars, the phrase means that there are no visible supports: the structure stands, and if you look for what holds it up you will find nothing. Read as qualifying the heavens, it means that you see them in that condition — raised, standing, unsupported — and the seeing is continuous rather than something you must go and do. Both readings are grammatical and both are reported. The second is the stronger, for a reason the first cannot supply. The visible absence of pillars is a fact anyone can confirm by looking up, but the phrase *you can see* adds nothing to it that the negation has not already stated. Attaching it to the heavens makes it a claim about the observer's ordinary experience: the sight is available to every person at every moment, which is why the argument can be addressed to everyone without qualification. Chapter 13 uses the identical construction in its own account of the raising of the heavens (13:2).

**Mountains Placed So the Ground Holds Still**

*Rawāsiya* is the plural of a participle meaning fixed, settled, firmly planted, and it is used of mountains throughout the Qur'an. The verb with them is *jaʿala* or *alqā*, to place or to cast, which presents the mountains as installed rather than as merely existing. The purpose clause is explicit: *so it does not shake with you*. The preposition "with you" is worth pausing on, because it makes the stability a relation to the inhabitant rather than a property of the planet. The earth is not described as steady in itself; it is described as steady under its residents. The same statement appears twice more, once with the identical address — *“He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way”* (16:15) — and once with rivers and broad pathways again (21:31). Chapter 78 gives the mountains a different image, calling them *“˹its˺ pegs”* (78:7), which is the same function described as fastening rather than as ballast.

**Every Type of Creature Scattered, Not Concentrated**

The verb for the animals is *baththa*, to scatter or to spread out, and the object is *kull dābbah*, every creature that moves along the ground. *Dābbah* comes from a root meaning to creep or to move steadily, and it covers animals broadly rather than livestock alone. Scattering is the operative word. The creatures are not gathered in one region or produced in one event; they are distributed across the surface, which is what makes provision available wherever people settle. The commentators draw two conclusions from the distribution, and both follow from the verb. It shows mercy in the arrangement of nourishment, since no inhabited region is left without its share. And it shows the reach of knowledge and power, because a scattered arrangement cannot be maintained by accident. Chapter 2 lists the scattering of creatures among the signs in the creation of the heavens and the earth (2:164).

**Water From Above, and the Fine Kind From Below**

The verse's fourth movement reverses the first. Water comes down from the sky, and every *karīm* kind grows up out of the earth. *Karīm* means noble, generous, bountiful, and the exegetes read it here as a description of vegetation that gives abundantly without asking anything in return — the plant produces and hands over its produce. Others take it simply as excellent or fine, which is how this translation renders it. Both senses are carried by the word, and the ambiguity is productive: the plants are generous because they are excellent, and their excellence shows in what they give. The pairing of water from the sky with growth from the earth is one of the Qur'an's most repeated arguments, and it appears in the same form where the earth is described as producing types of fine plants in pairs (26:7).

**Four Movements Make One Argument**

The verse is organised spatially. Look up: heavens without pillars. Look down: mountains fixed into the earth. Look across: creatures scattered over its surface. Then look at the cycle that runs between the two: water sent down, growth brought up. The four are not four separate proofs but one tour, and the tour ends where the next verse begins, with a challenge: *“Now show Me what those ˹gods˺ other than Him have created”* (31:11). The argument's force depends on the ordering. Each of the four items is something the audience already accepts as real and unmade by any idol they worship. The verse does not ask them to accept a new fact; it asks them to notice who must have done the things they already know about.

**The Same Inventory in al-Baqarah**

Chapter 2 assembles the identical list in a single sentence: *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth; the alternation of the day and the night; the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death; the scattering of all kinds of creatures throughout; the shifting of the winds; and the clouds drifting between the heavens and the earth”* (2:164). Al-Baqarah names seven items and adds ships, winds and clouds; Luqmān names four and adds the mountains. Both conclude that these are signs for people who use their minds. The difference in the lists is a difference of setting. Al-Baqarah addresses a settled community with trade and seafaring in view; this chapter addresses an audience in the highlands of Arabia, for whom the stability of the ground under a tent and the arrival of rain are the immediate facts of survival.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:11

> This is Allah’s creation. Now show Me what those ˹gods˺ other than Him have created. In fact, the wrongdoers are clearly astray

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand to Produce, Not an Invitation to Debate**

The verse does not ask for a reason. It asks for an object: show Me what they have created. *Arūnī* is the imperative of showing, of making something visible to a witness, and it is addressed to people who claim that other beings share in divinity. The challenge is therefore empirical in the plainest sense. It does not require an argument about causation, a cosmology, or a tradition; it requires a produced thing. The move is devastating because it collapses the entire dispute into a question that can be answered by pointing. Either there is something the idols made — a mountain, a plant, a living creature, a drop of rain — or there is not. No amount of inherited custom, ancestral loyalty, or rhetorical skill can substitute for the pointing, and the verse's demand is phrased so that silence and confession are the only two available replies.

**God Speaks in the First Person Singular Here**

The pronoun attached to the imperative is the singular first person: show *Me*. Throughout this section of the chapter the speech alternates between the third person — "He created the heavens" — and the first person plural — "We send down rain" (31:10). The shift into the first person singular happens exactly at the challenge. The effect is to remove every intermediary from the exchange. The idols are not being compared with a deity at a distance; they are being compared with the speaker, who is present and asking. The same direct address appears in the parallel challenges: *"Show me what they have created on earth! Or do they have a share in ˹the creation of˺ the heavens?"* (35:40), and again, *"Bring me a scripture ˹revealed˺ before this ˹Quran˺ or a shred of knowledge, if what you say is true"* (46:4). In both cases the demand is issued in a voice that expects to be answered directly, and in both the answer never comes.

**The Parallel Challenges Share One Structure**

Chapter 52 puts the same question in three steps: *"Or were they created by nothing, or are they ˹their own˺ creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? In fact, they have no firm belief ˹in Allah˺"* (52:35–36). The three steps exhaust the possibilities — created without a creator, self-created, or creators of the cosmos — and the verse closes by denying that the opponents actually believe their own position. Chapter 7 makes the point from the other direction: the idols *"cannot create anything, but are in fact created"* (7:191), and chapter 16 repeats it almost word for word (16:20). Chapter 43 asks whether they were given a book to hold on to (43:21). Across all of these the structure is constant: a claim is made, the claim implies a power, the power is asked to demonstrate itself, and the demonstration is absent. This verse is the shortest version of the argument and the hardest to answer, because it asks for nothing but a thing that was made.

**Three Answers Exist and Each One Ends the Case**

Faced with the demand, an opponent has three moves. The first is to name something the idols created. Nobody in the Qur'an's audience ever did, and the reports about the Meccan shrines describe idols that were made by human hands, fed nothing, and asked for nothing but veneration. The second is to concede that the idols did not create but to claim they intercede — that they were created and yet hold influence with the Creator. The Qur'an answers this by pointing out that an intercessor who cannot create cannot be relied on, and by asking who would grant permission for the intercession at all. The third move is to abandon the claim, which is conversion. Every path out of the challenge leads either to silence or to monotheism, and the verse's final sentence records which one the wrongdoers chose.

**Ẓulm Means Putting a Thing Where It Does Not Belong**

The closing verdict names the wrongdoers: *al-ẓālimūn*. The root *ẓ-l-m* denotes putting something in other than its proper place, and Arabic uses it for both injustice and darkness, since a thing out of place cannot be seen clearly. Applied to worship, it describes an act of misallocation: giving to a stone what belongs to the One who made the stone. This is why the Qur'an calls associating partners with God a grave sin and says plainly that God does not forgive it while forgiving anything else to whom He wills (4:48). The unforgivable quality is not arbitrariness; it is that the wrong has no remainder to forgive. Every other sin is a failure inside a correct framework, and the framework can absorb the failure. Shirk dismantles the framework itself, and there is nothing left in which forgiveness could be recorded. The wrongdoers here are in error that is *mubīn* — manifest, obvious from outside, visible to anyone who looks at the case.

**The Verdict Follows the Failure to Answer, Not the Failure to Believe**

The order of the three sentences matters. First the exhibit: this is God's creation. Then the challenge: show me what they made. Then the verdict: they are clearly astray. The verdict is not an insult inserted into an argument; it is the conclusion of a procedure that has been completed. The wrongdoers are declared astray because they were asked a question they could not answer and did not withdraw the claim that produced the question. A person who holds a belief he cannot support, when the demand for support is clear and the cost of answering is low, is not in a state of innocent confusion. He is in a state of error he is maintaining. The verse's harshness is measured against that maintenance, and the word "manifest" tells the reader that the confusion was never genuine.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:12

> Indeed, We blessed Luqmân with wisdom, ˹saying˺, “Be grateful to Allah, for whoever is grateful, it is only for their own good. And whoever is ungrateful, then surely Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wisdom Is Given, and Its First Instruction Is Gratitude**

The verb is *ātaynā*, We gave. Wisdom arrives in this verse as a bestowal rather than an achievement, and the same verb is used of the gift given to anyone God chooses: *“Allah grants wisdom to whoever He wills. And whoever is granted wisdom is certainly blessed with a great privilege. But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason”* (2:269). The gift is then immediately paired with a command — be grateful to God — which tells the reader how a gift is held. It is held by acknowledging it. The verse makes gratitude the use of wisdom rather than a response to it, and the arrangement is the reason Luqmān's counsel in the verses that follow begins with the recognition of God and ends with manners at table and in the street. A gift that is not acknowledged becomes invisible to its holder, and an invisible gift stops shaping behaviour.

**Who Luqmān Was, According to the Reports**

The Qur'an gives no biography, and the exegetes filled the gap with reports of varying quality. Sufyān al-Thawrī, transmitting from al-Ashʿath from ʿIkrimah from Ibn ʿAbbās, said that Luqmān was an Ethiopian slave who worked as a carpenter. Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh, asked what he had heard about him, described a short man with a flat nose who came from Nubia. Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib told a black questioner not to be distressed by his colour, naming three of the best of people who were black: Bilāl, Mahjaʿ the freed slave of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, and Luqmān the Wise, a black Nubian with thick lips. Mujāhid said simply that Luqmān was a righteous servant and not a prophet. The reports converge on one point and diverge on the rest: a man of African origin, of low social standing, whose reputation rested on nothing but what he said. That convergence is worth more than the details, because it explains the chapter's choice of hero.

**Why the Majority Refuse Him Prophethood**

A minority held that Luqmān was a prophet, and the ground for the claim is that some reports place him giving religious rulings before David and then learning from David afterwards. The majority rejected the claim, and their reason is textual rather than historical. The Qur'an says he was given *ḥikmah*, and it uses different language when it means prophethood. David is described as receiving both kingship and wisdom together, and as being taught what God willed (2:251); a prophet is sent, given a book, and made a warner, and none of that vocabulary appears here. Wisdom in this verse is comprehension and sound judgement, the capacity to see what follows from what. That distinction is not a demotion. It is the reason the chapter can put a non-prophet's speech inside revelation without confusing the two authorities: Luqmān's words are preserved because they are true, and they are true because they agree with what the prophets brought.

**Gratitude Returns Only to the One Who Is Grateful**

The verse states the arithmetic of thanks twice, in both directions. Whoever is grateful is grateful for his own good; whoever is ungrateful harms nobody but himself. The same principle appears as a general rule: *“And whoever strives ˹in Allah's cause˺, only does so for their own good. Surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of˺ His creation”* (29:6), and it is repeated where disbelief is described as a loss carried by the disbeliever alone (35:39). The Qur'an's account of ingratitude is not that it offends God but that it disables the ingrate. Thanks is the faculty by which a gift continues to function as a gift; without it, provision becomes mere consumption and the consumer learns nothing from it. Chapter 2 phrases the same instruction as a direct command: *“remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful”* (2:152).

**Self-Sufficient and Yet Praised**

The two names closing the verse are usually separated in ordinary speech, and the verse joins them deliberately. *Ghanī* means free of need: God requires nothing from the gratitude of creatures, and their thanks adds nothing to Him. *Ḥamīd* is the intensive form of praised, and the exegetes read it actively — God is praised in Himself and by His nature, whether or not anyone praises Him. The combination removes both possible distortions. If God needed praise, worship would be a service rendered to a deficient being, and the ingrate would be withholding something God lacks. If God were not praised in Himself, the praise of creatures would be the only praise in existence and it would be contingent on creatures. Neither is the case. Participation in that praise is itself a gift, which is why the ingrate's loss is total: he has declined the one thing that would have made his other blessings intelligible.

**The Same Gift, Named in the Same Way, Given to Others**

Luqmān is not the only person in the Qur'an described as having been given wisdom, and the comparison sharpens his case. David receives kingship and wisdom together (2:251). Wisdom as such is granted to whom God wills, and the granting is called a great privilege (2:269). What distinguishes Luqmān is that nothing else accompanies his gift: no kingdom, no book, no nation, no miracle. He is given wisdom and he speaks to his son. The chapter that carries his name is the only place in the Qur'an where the speech of a man who is not a prophet is preserved at length as revelation, and the reason for the preservation is stated in the gift itself. Wisdom of this kind is available to a father in a household, and that is where the chapter places it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:13

> And ˹remember˺ when Luqmân said to his son, while advising him, “O my dear son! Never associate ˹anything˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺, for associating ˹others with Him˺ is truly the worst of all wrongs.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Wise Man's First Sentence to His Son Is a Prohibition**

The counsel does not open with an encouragement. It opens with a negative command: do not associate anything with God. Everything else the father has to say follows from that, and the ordering tells the reader how the Qur'an understands instruction. A person who gets the first item wrong has no use for the rest, since prayer offered to a rival and kindness performed for display are both directed elsewhere. The prohibition is stated absolutely, with no exception clause and no comparative — not "do not associate too much" or "do not associate the obvious idols". The Arabic construction is the imperative of warning, and it is delivered to a child, which means the father is treating his son as someone capable of the error. That assumption is itself part of the teaching: the danger is real for the person being addressed, not only for other people in other towns.

**Yā Bunayya Sets the Register of Everything That Follows**

The vocative is *yā bunayya*, the diminutive of son with a suffix of endearment, rendered here as "O my dear son". Arabic diminutives of this kind carry affection rather than belittlement, and the form is used in the Qur'an wherever a father speaks to a child he loves and fears for. Noah uses the identical address to the son who will not board the ark: *“O my dear son! Come aboard with us and do not be with the disbelievers”* (11:42). The parallel is instructive because the outcome differs. Noah's son refuses and drowns; Luqmān's son is not recorded as answering at all, and the silence leaves the counsel standing as instruction rather than as dialogue. The tenderness in the address does two things at once. It removes the possibility of reading the prohibition as a threat, and it makes the warning harder to dismiss, since it is delivered by someone whose affection is not in question.

**Associating Partners Is Called a Wrong, and the Wrong Is Called Tremendous**

The verse names shirk *ẓulm* — wrongdoing, injustice — and qualifies it as *ʿaẓīm*, tremendous, vast. The lexical sense of *ẓulm* is putting a thing in other than its place, and it is the precise word for what shirk does. Worship is a debt owed to the One who created, sustained and will judge; directing it to something created puts the debt in the wrong account. The Qur'an states the consequence in its sharpest form: *“Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. And whoever associates others with Allah has indeed committed a grave sin”* (4:48). Chapter 4 calls it a grave sin; Luqmān calls it the worst of wrongs. The two descriptions agree, and the agreement explains why the counsel starts here. Every other fault a son might commit is repairable inside the relationship with God. This one removes the relationship.

**Advising Is Not the Same Act as Commanding**

The verse sets the speech inside a frame: Luqmān said to his son *while advising him* — *yaʿiẓuhu*. The noun *waʿẓ* denotes speech that moves the heart by combining instruction with a warning and a softening; it is the register of exhortation rather than of legislation. The distinction is not merely stylistic. A commander issues a rule and enforces it; an advisor sets out a case and leaves the hearer free. Luqmān is in the second position. He has no legislative authority, no court, and no revelation to cite, and the Qur'an preserves his words in the voice of a man persuading someone he loves. That is why the counsel in this chapter reads as it does — concrete, sequential, addressed to behaviour — rather than as a code of law. It is also why the words can be placed inside revelation without confusion of authority: the frame tells the reader exactly what kind of speech this is.

**The Prophet ﷺ Put This Sin and the Next One Together**

The link between the two great prohibitions is established in a report of the highest grade. Abū Bakrah relates that the Prophet ﷺ asked three times whether he should tell the Companions the greatest of the great sins, and when they said yes he named joining others in worship with God and being undutiful to one's parents. He had been reclining and sat up as he said it, then went on to warn repeatedly against false testimony until the Companions thought he would not stop. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2654 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 87. Luqmān's counsel covers the first of the two in this verse, and the chapter turns to the second immediately afterwards. The physical detail in the report — the change of posture — indicates which of the sins the Prophet ﷺ considered the more commonly underestimated, and it is not the one people expect.

**Abraham Speaks Upward, Luqmān Downward**

The Qur'an records family instruction in both directions and the contrast is sharp. Abraham addresses his father with a vocative of tenderness and asks why he worships what can neither hear nor see nor benefit him (19:42), then says he fears a torment will touch him and you will become Satan's companion (19:45). Luqmān addresses his son. In both cases the speaker is the one who believes and the hearer is the one who does not, and in both cases the address is affectionate rather than denunciatory. The arrangement across chapters 19 and 31 covers the two situations a believer actually faces at home: an elder who will not follow, and a child who has not yet been taught. Neither situation licenses harshness in the Qur'an's account, and both are handled with the same diminutive of endearment.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:14

> And We have commanded people to ˹honour˺ their parents. Their mothers bore them through hardship upon hardship, and their weaning takes two years. So be grateful to Me and your parents. To Me is the final return

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Voice Shifts From the Father to God**

Verse 13 is Luqmān speaking to his son. Verse 14 breaks in with God's own voice in the first person: *We have commanded people*, *be grateful to Me*, *to Me is the final return*. The interruption is not a lapse in the narrative; it is the narrative's argument. The duty toward parents is not being presented as a father's opinion or as a piece of inherited wisdom, however sound. It is divine legislation, issued in the same register as the commands to pray and to give alms, and it is inserted into a sage's advice precisely so that the reader cannot file it under good counsel. The same formula appears twice more, in the same words and the same voice: *“We have commanded people to honour their parents”* (29:8), and again with a longer account of the mother's burden (46:15). Three chapters use the identical opening, which marks it as a fixed legislative statement rather than an exhortation.

**Hardship Upon Hardship Describes a Process, Not an Event**

The Arabic behind "through hardship upon hardship" is *wahnan ʿalā wahnin* — weakness upon weakness. The noun denotes a slackening, a loss of strength, and the doubling with *ʿalā* stacks one weakening on top of another. What is described is accumulation over time: conception, the months of carrying, the increasing weight, the labour, the recovery. The exegetes read it as the successive stages of childbearing and as exhaustion piled on exhaustion. Chapter 46 supplies the arithmetic and confirms the reading of a span rather than a moment: the mother *“bore them in hardship and delivered them in hardship”*, and *“Their ˹period of˺ bearing and weaning is thirty months”* (46:15). The two verses together give a total, and the total does the persuasive work. A command backed by thirty months of a specific person's suffering cannot be dismissed as a general exhortation to be nice.

**Two Years Is a Ruling With Consequences**

The mention of weaning is not incidental background. Two years is the period the Qur'an legislates elsewhere, and the legislation there carries obligations: *“˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years, for those who wish to complete the nursing ˹of their child˺. The child's father will provide reasonable maintenance and clothing for the mother ˹during that period˺. No one will be charged with more than they can bear”* (2:233). The verse in al-Baqarah ties the nursing period to the father's duty of support and to the mother's protection from harm, which is how Islamic law derives the entitlements of a nursing mother. Chapter 31 mentions the two years inside a sentence about gratitude, and the effect is to make the mother's claim visible to the child himself. He is told how long he was fed, and by whom, at the moment he is told to be grateful.

**Gratitude to Me and to Your Parents, in That Order**

The conjunction joins two objects of thanks without merging them. God comes first and parents second, and the order is not a politeness but a hierarchy. Gratitude to God is worship; gratitude to parents is duty, and it is a duty that verse 15 will qualify. The same hierarchy governs the legislation in other chapters: *“For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him. And honour your parents”* (17:23), where the command to worship precedes the command to honour in a single verse; and *“Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him. And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor”* (4:36), where parents head a list of social duties that follows the command of worship. In chapter 2 the kindness to parents is named as the first injunction of the covenant taken from the children of Israel, immediately after the command to worship none but God (2:83). The pattern is fixed across all four: God, then parents, then everyone else.

**The Return Clause Ends the Sentence Deliberately**

"To Me is the final return" closes a verse about family with a statement about the court. The clause does two jobs. It places the parent-child bond inside a larger frame, so that gratitude to parents is not the highest obligation and cannot be allowed to displace it. And it warns both parties: the parent who demanded worship of the child, and the child who neglected the parent, will both stand in the same place and be answered for the same relationship. The clause is also a hinge, because the very next verse states what happens when the return matters more than the family — when parents press a child toward associating partners with God. The reader has already been told where everyone is going, which is what makes the exception in verse 15 tolerable rather than harsh.

**Three Chapters Carry the Same Command**

The parallel verses are close enough to compare item by item. Chapter 29 is the shortest of the three: it opens with the identical statement, *“We have commanded people to honour their parents”* (29:8), names the exception in a single clause, and closes on the return and the reporting. Chapter 46 gives the fullest account of the mother's labour, the thirty months, and the child's prayer at the age of forty. Chapter 31 sits between them: it names the weakness upon weakness and the two years, and it alone places the command inside the mouth of a story, interrupting a father's advice to his son with the voice of God. The choice is telling. A law stated abstractly can be debated, and a law stated in three chapters in three different registers is harder to evade, because the reader meets it once as legislation, once as biography, and once as an interruption in a conversation he has been listening to. The chapter that carries Luqmān's name is the one that delivers it as an interruption, which is the register most likely to be remembered by a person who was once a child in a house.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:15

> But if they pressure you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, do not obey them. Still keep their company in this world courteously, and follow the way of those who turn to Me ˹in devotion˺. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and then I will inform you of what you used to do

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Command, One Limit, One Duty That Survives**

The verse has three parts and they must be kept apart. A prohibition: do not obey parents who press you to associate partners with God. A continuing duty: keep their company in this world courteously. An eschatological frame: the return is to God, and He will report what was done. The three do not qualify each other; they operate simultaneously. The believer who refuses his parents in the matter of worship does not thereby leave the relationship. He remains obliged to feed them, speak well to them, visit them, and protect them, while refusing the single thing they demand. Islamic ethics rarely produces a cleaner case of a duty being limited without being cancelled, and the limit is drawn at exactly the point where obedience would transfer worship from God to something else.

**Saʿd and the Mother Who Stopped Eating**

The occasion reports attach this verse and the one before it to Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqās, and the account is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1748, transmitted by his son Muṣʿab from Saʿd himself. Saʿd said that verses of the Qur'an were revealed concerning him: his mother swore by God that she would neither eat nor drink until he disbelieved in Muhammad ﷺ, arguing that God had commanded him to be good to his parents and that she was his mother commanding him. She held out until her cheeks were hollow, and the family forced milk into her mouth with a stick. The report names the verses revealed in connection with the event, including the command concerning parents and the phrase about weakness upon weakness. The details matter because they show what the prohibition was written against: not a parent's mild disapproval but a sustained attempt to use the child's conscience as a lever, backed by an appeal to the very duty the previous verse established.

**"What You Have No Knowledge Of" Does the Argumentative Work**

The object of the parents' pressure is described as something *you have no knowledge of*. The qualification is not a softening. Shirk is not excusable when it is known and inexcusable when it is unknown; it is inexcusable in both cases. The phrase instead identifies the groundlessness of the demand. There is no evidence for a partner of God, so a person urged toward that belief is urged toward something for which nothing can be cited. The Qur'an uses the same construction about argumentation in general: some dispute about God *“without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating scripture”* (22:8), and this chapter repeats the description (31:20). Placing it in the mouth of a son refusing his parents makes the point personal. The refusal is not disobedience to wisdom; it is a refusal to accept a claim that carries no support, however much emotional weight the person making it has.

**Courtesy Survives the Refusal of Obedience**

*Ṣāḥibhumā fī l-dunyā maʿrūfan* — keep their company in this world in a kindly manner. *Ṣāḥib* is the verb of companionship, from the same root as *ṣāḥib*, a companion; *maʿrūf* means what is recognised as good, the conduct a decent person acknowledges without needing it defined. Two features of the phrase deserve attention. It is limited to this world, *fī l-dunyā*, which quietly separates social obligation from religious agreement. And it is stated as companionship rather than as service or submission, so the refused demand does not dissolve the relationship into formality. A believer in Saʿd's position continues to sit with his mother, feed her, and speak gently to her while she is cursing him. The verse does not permit coldness, silence, or cutting ties as a response to religious pressure, and Islamic law has read it that way consistently.

**Whose Way Is the Way of Those Who Turn?**

The command to follow the way of those who turn to God in devotion — *man anāba* — has four readings in the tradition. Some take it as the way of the prophets, since they are the paradigm of turning. Some take it as the way of Muhammad ﷺ specifically. Some take it as the way of the believers generally. And some, reading the verse against the Saʿd reports, take it as the way of Abū Bakr, under whose influence Saʿd is said to have accepted Islam. The grounds differ in kind: the first three rest on the generality of the wording, the fourth on a historical occasion. The general reading is stronger. A command attached to a description rather than to a name is addressed to whoever fits the description, and the description here — those who turn back to God — is available in every generation. The Abū Bakr reading remains valuable as an illustration of the verse in its first application, not as its limit.

**The Return Clause Sharpens Between the Two Verses**

Verse 14 ended with "To Me is the final return." Verse 15 ends with "Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and then I will inform you of what you used to do." The addition is the reporting. The Qur'an uses this formula repeatedly to describe the Day: God will tell people what they used to do, and the telling is itself part of the reckoning. The clause lands differently here than anywhere else in the chapter, because it is addressed to a person whose family may be on the other side of the argument. The believer who disobeyed his parents in the matter of worship will be informed of what he did, and so will the parents who demanded it. The verse removes the possibility of settling the question at home. Nothing about this conflict is final until the reporting happens, and both parties are told that in the same breath.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:16

> ˹Luqmân added,˺ “O my dear son! ˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed—be it ˹hidden˺ in a rock or in the heavens or the earth—Allah will bring it forth. Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Smallest Weight in the Hardest Place**

The verse chooses its images with precision. A mustard seed is the proverbial smallest weight in Arabic usage, and the Qur'an uses the same measure elsewhere for the least quantity that can be counted. Then it names three hiding places in ascending order of difficulty: inside a rock, in the heavens, in the earth. The rock is the densest concealment available — a thing shut inside stone, out of sight, out of reach, with no opening. The heavens are the largest, and the earth is the ground a person walks on every day without knowing what lies under it. The three are not alternatives but a scale, moving from the sealed to the vast to the familiar. A deed that survives all three searches is a deed that cannot be lost, and the verse's whole purpose is to state that no deed is lost.

**Bringing It Forth Is Extraction, Not Remembering**

The verb is *ya'ti bihā* — God will bring it, produce it, deliver it. This is not the language of memory or of record-keeping alone. A record could be kept and never produced; the verse promises production. The same promise appears in the chapter of the prophets with nearly identical wording: *“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least. And ˹even˺ if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth”* (21:47). Chapter 17 describes the mechanism of delivery: every person's deed is bound to his neck, and on the Day a record is brought out and laid open (17:13). Chapter 99 states the outcome in two symmetrical lines, that whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7–8). Seeing is the common term across all three. The deed is not merely known to God; it is shown to the doer.

**Laṭīf and Khabīr Answer Two Different Problems**

The verse closes on two names and each covers a distinct difficulty. *Laṭīf*, the Most Subtle, is the name for a knowledge that penetrates: it reaches what is small, what is hidden, and what is inside other things. The subtlety answers the size problem — a mustard seed is beneath the threshold of attention. *Khabīr*, the All-Aware, is the name for knowledge that is comprehensive and current, aware of everything at every moment; it answers the place problem, since awareness does not depend on the object being exposed. Chapter 6 states the same coverage in an image: *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record”* (6:59). Chapter 50 puts it from the other side, that God created humankind and knows what their souls whisper, being closer to them than their jugular vein (50:16). Distance is not an obstacle in either direction.

**The Accounting Sits Between the Belief and the Practice**

The order of Luqmān's counsel is not incidental. Verse 13 establishes worship of one God. Verses 14 and 15 establish the duty to parents and its limit. Verse 16 establishes that nothing will be lost. Verses 17 to 19 establish what to do: pray, encourage good, forbid evil, be patient, control the face, the walk and the voice. The accounting is placed exactly at the hinge. Without it the practices that follow are burdens with no ledger, and the burden of enjoining good in a hostile town is considerable. With it, the small refusal, the small kindness and the small hour of patience all have a destination. The father does not tell his son to be brave; he tells him that nothing disappears. Courage is then a consequence rather than a command, which is a more economical way of teaching it.

**One Measure, Used for the Smallest Evil and the Smallest Faith**

The mustard seed is the Qur'an's and the Sunnah's standard unit of the negligible, and it is used in both directions. The same measure appears in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 91, where the Prophet ﷺ says that whoever has the weight of a mustard seed of pride in his heart will not enter Paradise, and in the parallel narration that whoever has a mustard seed of faith in his heart will not enter the Fire. The symmetry is exact and it is the reason the image works so well in Luqmān's mouth. Nothing is too small to save a person and nothing is too small to ruin him. A father teaching his son about the accounting is therefore teaching him about quantities he cannot ignore, because the threshold below which deeds stop mattering does not exist.

**The Atom's Weight in al-Zalzalah**

Chapter 99 devotes its second half to the same doctrine in its plainest form. The earth will bring out its burdens and every person will be shown his deeds, because the Lord has inspired it to do so (99:4–5), and then the two symmetrical statements about the atom's weight of good and the atom's weight of evil (99:7–8). The *dharrah*, translated as an atom or a mote, was in classical usage the tiniest visible particle — the specks seen moving in a shaft of sunlight. Luqmān uses the mustard seed, al-Zalzalah uses the mote, and the prophets' chapter uses the mustard seed again (21:47). Three images, one claim: the accounting has no lower limit.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:17

> “O my dear son! Establish prayer, encourage what is good and forbid what is evil, and endure patiently whatever befalls you. Surely this is a resolve to aspire to

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Items in a Fixed Order, and the Last One Exists Because of the Second**

The sentence names prayer, then enjoining good and forbidding evil, then patience under whatever strikes. The order is not a list of independent virtues; it is a sequence with a cause in it. Prayer establishes the person's own standing before God. Enjoining good and forbidding evil turns that person outward into a community, where he must say things other people do not want to hear. Patience is placed after the second item because the second item is what produces the need for patience. Nobody is told to be patient about prayer in this verse; the patience is specified as patience under what *befalls* him, and what befalls a person who forbids evil in public is opposition. The father is describing a career rather than a checklist, and he has arranged its stages in the order they will actually arrive.

**Encouraging Good and Forbidding Evil Is a Duty With a Stated Reason**

The formula *al-amr bi-l-maʿrūf wa-l-nahy ʿan al-munkar* — enjoining the recognised good and forbidding the disapproved evil — is one of the Qur'an's major ethical commands, and it is never presented as optional. A group is to exist for this purpose: *“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful”* (3:104). The community's rank depends on it: *“You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah”* (3:110), where the excellence is attributed to the practice rather than to lineage. The believers, men and women, are described as guardians of one another who do exactly this (9:71), and those established in the land are characterised by prayer, alms, and the same pair (22:41). The duty is communal, and it is what makes a community rather than a crowd.

**The Self Comes Before the Society**

One reading of the sequence deserves weight because it is drawn from the verse's own context. Luqmān has already told his son to give thanks and to associate nothing with God (31:12–13) and now tells him to pray before telling him to correct anyone. The reasoning is that a person who has set his own worship right is then in a position to help others set theirs right. This is not a claim that self-reform must be complete before public duty begins — the Qur'an nowhere requires perfection as a qualification for speaking — but it is a claim about credibility and about order. The one who forbids evil while practising it undermines the command he is delivering, and the community learns to treat the command as noise. The verse's placement of prayer first is a practical instruction about which work to do this morning.

**Patience Is Attached to What Strikes From Outside**

The object of patience is *mā aṣābaka* — whatever strikes you, befalls you, arrives at you. The verb *aṣāba* carries the sense of something reaching a target, and it is used in the Qur'an for calamities, injuries, and losses that come to a person rather than from him. The instruction is therefore about reception, not about action: the son is not told to be patient in performing the duties, but to be patient when the duties cost him. Chapter 25 makes the same point inside a statement about the prophets themselves, that every messenger ate food and walked in the markets, and that people were made a trial for one another: *“Will you ˹not then˺ be patient?”* (25:20). Chapter 3 states the cost concretely — believers will be tested in their wealth and their persons, and will hear much that hurts from those given the scripture before them and from the polytheists — and then closes with the identical verdict this verse uses (3:186).

**ʿAzm al-Umūr Has Two Readings and One Is Stronger**

The closing phrase is difficult and the commentators divided on it. Read one way, it means that these matters are among those requiring firm resolve: the son is being told that the course just described belongs to the class of undertakings a person must set his jaw for. Read the other way, *ʿazm* belongs to God and the phrase means these are among the matters God has determined and commanded, so the son is not choosing whether to adopt them. The grounds for each are real. The first fits the immediate context, which is advice from a father to a son and therefore addressed to the son's will. The second fits the Qur'an's habit of describing its commands as settled. The first reading is stronger here, because the sentence is inside a speech by a man who has no legislative authority, and because the same phrase is used elsewhere of human endurance: *“And whoever endures patiently and forgives— surely this is a resolve to aspire to”* (42:43). In that verse the resolve clearly belongs to the person who endures.

**The Same Closing in ʾĀl ʿImrān**

Chapter 3 sets out the same three elements and ends with the same words. Believers will be tested in wealth and persons and will hear hurtful speech from the People of the Book and the polytheists; if they are patient and mindful, *“surely this is a resolve to aspire to”* (3:186). The parallel is close enough to show that Luqmān's counsel and the Qur'an's instruction to the community cover one ground. Both place patience after public friction rather than after private hardship, and both describe the patience as something worth resolving upon rather than something merely to be endured. The Prophet ﷺ put the same estimate on the virtue in plain terms: no one is given a blessing better and greater than patience (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1469, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī).

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:18

> “And do not turn your nose up to people, nor walk pridefully upon the earth. Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Gestures, and Both Are Meant to Be Seen**

The verse forbids two movements: turning the cheek away from people in scorn, and walking on the earth with insolence. Neither is a private state. Both are performed in front of others and both communicate a ranking — the one who turns his face has decided the person in front of him is not worth looking at, and the one who walks proudly has decided the ground he walks on and the people he passes are beneath his notice. The choice of gestures rather than attitudes is characteristic of the whole passage. Luqmān is teaching his son how to behave, and behaviour is what other people encounter. An arrogance that never expressed itself in the face or the walk would not be visible to anyone, and the harm the verse is guarding against is the harm done to the person on the receiving end of the gesture.

**Ṣaʿar Comes From a Twist of the Neck**

The Arabic word translated as "turn your nose up" is from the root *ṣ-ʿ-r*. The lexical sense is a wryness or distortion in the cheek, the face, or the neck produced by pride, together with a turning of the face to one side; the same root is used of a camel afflicted by a disorder that causes it to twist its neck. The picture is therefore not merely of a rude expression but of a deformation. Pride is described in the language of a body that has lost its natural alignment, and the loss is presented as self-inflicted. This is a stronger claim than the verse appears to make on the surface. The arrogant man is not shown as large but as crooked, and the crookedness is in the part of the body that faces other people. Lane's Lexicon records both senses — the distortion from pride and the camel's twisted neck — and the double meaning is exactly what makes the word an insult rather than a description.

**The Adverb Qualifies the Manner, Not the Movement**

Nobody is forbidden from walking. The prohibition attaches to *maraḥan*, an adverb from a root meaning to move with insolence and self-admiring exultation. The manner is what is banned, and the Qur'an gives the reason for the ban elsewhere in terms that reduce the walker to his actual size: *“And do not walk on the earth arrogantly. Surely you can neither crack the earth nor stretch to the height of the mountains”* (17:37). The two images are physical and slightly absurd on purpose. A person stamping hard enough to split the ground, or standing tall enough to match a mountain, is a picture of what the proud walk implies about the walker, and the verse denies both capacities in one line. The body that cannot crack the earth has no business walking on it as though it could.

**The Prophet ﷺ Defined Pride in Two Halves, and Both Are Here**

The definition is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 91, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd. A man asked the Prophet ﷺ whether liking fine clothes and fine sandals was pride, and the answer separated the two things: God is beautiful and loves beauty, but pride is disdaining the truth and holding people in contempt. The same report states that whoever carries the weight of a mustard seed of pride in his heart will not enter Paradise. The two halves of the definition map onto this verse with unusual exactness. Contempt for people is the turned cheek and the insolent walk. Disdaining the truth is what the chapter described a few verses earlier, when it pictured a man turning away from the recitation in arrogance as though he could not hear it (31:7). Luqmān is addressing the outward half; the chapter has already addressed the inward one.

**Mukhtāl and Fakkhūr Name the State and Its Noise**

The closing pair is *kulla mukhtālin fakhūr*, everyone vainly self-admiring and boastful. The two words divide the same ground between inside and outside. *Mukhtāl* comes from *khuyalāʾ*, the self-admiration that makes a person imagine himself larger than he is; it describes a condition of the imagination. *Fakhūr* is the intensive form of the one who boasts, and it describes what the condition produces in speech. The pair therefore names a disease and its symptom, and the negation is attached to God's love rather than to a specific punishment: Allah does not like whoever is arrogant and boastful. The identical pair closes a list of social duties in chapter 4, immediately after kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, neighbours and travellers (4:36). In both places the dislike is directed at the person who has been given a great deal and responds with self-inflation.

**The Same Two Gestures in al-Isrāʾ**

Chapter 17 forbids the walk and supplies the argument this verse leaves implicit: a person walking arrogantly on the earth can neither crack it nor reach the height of the mountains (17:37). The two prohibitions sit inside a longer code in that chapter covering speech to parents, spending, honesty in measure, and the refusal to follow what one has no knowledge of, which places the walk among offences of a legal rather than merely social kind. The pairing of the two chapters settles a question about Luqmān's standing. His counsel is not a private family ethic running alongside the law, or a folk wisdom the Qur'an happened to preserve. It is the same law delivered in a father's voice to one child, and the father is the only teacher quoted at length in the Qur'an who is not a prophet. That is why the two passages agree word for word on the point of the insolent walk and differ only in the reason each gives for forbidding it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:19

> Be moderate in your pace. And lower your voice, for the ugliest of all voices is certainly the braying of donkeys.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Speed and Volume Are How a Body Announces Itself**

The two instructions cover the two channels by which a person makes himself noticeable before he says anything meaningful. Pace governs movement through space; voice governs sound. Between them they account for the impression a man gives when he enters a room, walks down a road, or answers a question. The previous verse dealt with the face and the walk as expressions of contempt; this one deals with the walk as a rate and the voice as a level. The shift is subtle and it matters. Arrogance is forbidden outright. Moderation is commanded in things that are not in themselves wrong — walking fast is not a sin and speaking loudly is not a sin — which places this verse in the territory of character formation rather than prohibition. The father is not telling his son what to avoid; he is telling him how to be present.

**Qaṣd Means Aimed, and Aimed Means Middle**

The verb is *iqṣid*, from the root *q-ṣ-d*, which carries intention, direction, and the middle course all at once. To *qaṣada* something is to head straight for it, and *qaṣd* as a noun denotes both purpose and moderation. The commentators split along that ambiguity. Some read the instruction as a command to be neither sluggish nor hasty in walking, taking the middle sense. Others read it as "direct your walking", taking the intentional sense, so that the command is to walk with a destination and a purpose rather than drift. The root supports both, and the two readings converge in practice. A person walking with an aim does not dawdle and does not rush; the aim sets the pace. The stronger reading is the intentional one, because it explains why the instruction is paired with the voice. Both are about deliberate self-presentation: the walk has a destination, and the sound has a level chosen by the speaker rather than imposed by his mood.

**The Donkey's Bray Sets the Measure for Ugly Sound**

*Ankara l-aṣwāti la-ṣawtu l-ḥamīr* — the ugliest of voices is certainly the braying of donkeys. The comparison is with a sound and not with the animal. Nothing in the verse calls a loud speaker a donkey; it calls his volume the worst sound that exists and then supplies the example. The bray is loud, harsh, unmusical, involuntary, and repeated — the donkey does not choose to bray at a certain level, and the level is fixed by its nature rather than by its judgement. That is the precise criticism. A raised human voice is not expressive; it is automatic, and the person using it has surrendered the choice of volume to his state. Arabic idiom made the donkey's bray a standing image of noise without content, and the verse uses it at its strongest: not among the ugly voices but the ugliest, with the superlative reinforced by *la-*, the particle of emphasis.

**The Tongue Is What Drags People Down**

Speech discipline is not etiquette in the Qur'an's estimation, and the Sunnah states the stakes plainly. When Muʿādh ibn Jabal asked the Prophet ﷺ whether people would be held accountable for what they said, the answer came back: may your mother be deprived of you — are people thrown into the Fire on their faces, or on their noses, except for what their tongues have produced? The report is in Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2616 and is graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. The lowering of the voice in Luqmān's counsel belongs to the same field of concern. The tongue is described in the ethical tradition as the translator of the heart, so a voice out of control indicates a heart out of control, and the control of the volume is the most accessible form of the control of speech. A man who cannot moderate his voice will not moderate his accusations, his promises, or his jokes.

**Lowering the Voice Is Reverence Before It Is Manners**

Chapter 49 turns the same instruction toward a specific setting and adds a theological claim: *“Indeed, those who lower their voices in the presence of Allah's Messenger are the ones whose hearts Allah has refined for righteousness. They will have forgiveness and a great reward”* (49:3). The voice is treated there as a diagnostic of the heart's condition, and the refinement is attributed to God's testing rather than to the person's training. Read alongside Luqmān's counsel, the two verses describe one practice with two objects. The voice is lowered before the Messenger out of reverence, and it is lowered before people out of wisdom. The first is a religious act; the second is a social one; and the chapter that legislates the first uses the language of the heart's refinement, which is the same language a father would use about his son's character.

**The Same Rule in the Chapter of the Rooms**

Al-Ḥujurāt opens by forbidding believers to put themselves forward ahead of God and His Messenger, then forbids raising the voice above the Prophet's voice or speaking loudly to him as they speak loudly to one another, on the grounds that the habit can render deeds worthless without the person noticing: *“O believers! Do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet, nor speak loudly to him as you do to one another, or your deeds will become void while you are unaware”* (49:2). The reasoning is identical in structure to Luqmān's. A level of sound is connected to a condition of the heart, and the condition determines whether the deed counts at all. Both passages treat volume as a moral datum rather than a matter of taste, and both attach a consequence to it that the speaker cannot observe from inside his own behaviour. The verse in al-Ḥujurāt adds the clause about unawareness, which is the sharpest form of the warning: the loss happens silently, and the loud person is the last to know.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:20

> Have you not seen that Allah has subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and has lavished His favours upon you, both seen and unseen? ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, or guidance, or an enlightening scripture

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alam Taraw Asks for Perception, Not for Sight**

The opening is the interrogative of attention: have you not seen. The things listed in the first half of the verse cannot be seen in the literal sense. The subjection of the heavens and the earth to human use is not a visible object; it is a relation, and relations are perceived by the mind rather than caught by the eye. Arabic uses *ra'ā* in both registers, and the Qur'an's habit of opening an argument with this phrase signals that what follows is a conclusion available to anyone who thinks about what he already knows. The form is a question expecting the answer "yes", which converts the rest of the verse from an assertion into a recollection. Nobody is being told something new. The favours are being named so that the dispute described in the second half can be seen against them.

**Taskhīr Means Put Into Service, Not Handed Over as Property**

The verb is *sakhkhara*, to make subservient, to press into service. It is used repeatedly for the arrangement by which creation serves human beings: God subjected the sun and the moon, both constantly orbiting, and subjected the day and night (14:33); He subjected what is on the earth and the ships that sail the sea by His command, holding the sky from falling except by His permission (22:65); and He subjected whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth, all by His grace (45:13). In each case the subjection is described as something God does and continues to do, and in each case it is traced to grace rather than to entitlement. The ground of the arrangement is stated at the beginning of the Qur'an's account of humanity: God told the angels He was placing a successive human authority on earth (2:30), and He placed people as successors on earth (35:39). A deputy uses what belongs to his principal. That is the limit the verb carries, and it is the limit the second half of this verse describes people crossing.

**Seen and Unseen Divide the Favours by the Faculty That Receives Them**

The blessings are poured out *ẓāhiratan wa-bāṭinatan*, outward and inward. The commentators draw the line two ways and both are useful. By faculty: the outward blessings are those reached through the senses, and the inward through the mind, the intellect and the heart. By content: the outward are health, provision, shelter and comfort; the inward are faith, certainty and knowledge. The second division explains why the inward blessings are the more dangerous to lose and the easier to overlook. A person can enumerate his food, his house and his health without difficulty, and the Qur'an says he cannot enumerate the total: *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, ˹totally˺ ungrateful”* (14:34). The inability to count is a statement about the inward half, since what can be counted is what arrives through the senses.

**Three Missing Grounds for a Dispute**

The people who argue about God are described as having none of the three things an argument requires. *Without knowledge* removes reason — they have no demonstration, no observation, no inference. *Without guidance* removes transmission — they have no inherited direction, no report from anyone who knew. *Without an enlightening scripture* removes revelation — they have no text that illuminates. The three are the standard sources of a claim in Islamic epistemology, and the verse denies all three at once. Chapter 22 uses almost the same wording of the same people (22:8), and the demand for one of the three is repeated wherever the dispute is challenged: show me what they have created on earth, or bring a scripture revealed before this one, or a shred of knowledge if you are truthful (46:4; 35:40); or have they been given a book they hold firm to (43:21); or do they have a scripture in which they read what they want (68:37–38). The challenge is always for one of the three, and no one produces any.

**A Blessing and a Betrayal in One Sentence**

The verse's structure is the argument. The first half lists what has been done for the disputant: everything in the heavens and the earth subjected to him, and favours poured out on him inside and out. The second half says that there are people who dispute about God. The two halves are joined without a connective that would soften the contrast, and the effect is to make the dispute an act of ingratitude rather than an intellectual position. This is the specific charge the Qur'an brings against its opponents: not that they lack information but that they use the gift to argue against the giver. Chapter 16 states the same condition in one line — *“They are aware of Allah's favours, but still deny them. And most of them are ˹truly˺ ungrateful”* (16:83). Awareness of the favour is present in that verse, which removes ignorance as an excuse and locates the fault in the will.

**The Same List in al-Ḥajj**

Chapter 22 gives the parallel statement and adds what Luqmān omits: the disputant argues about God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating scripture, *“turning away ˹in pride˺ to lead ˹others˺ astray from Allah's Way”*, with disgrace promised in this world and the torment of burning on the Day (22:8–9). The addition supplies the motive this verse leaves unstated. The dispute is not a search; it is a campaign, and it has a direction — away from the way of God, which is the same direction named in the opening verses of this chapter (31:6). The two chapters describe one phenomenon from two angles, and the agreement between them is the reason the description can be treated as a diagnosis rather than as a polemical slur.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:21

> When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.” ˹Would they still do so˺ even if Satan is inviting them to the torment of the Blaze

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Invitation Answered With a Family Tree**

The verse records a call and a reply, and the reply is not an argument. The call is to follow what God has revealed — a claim about evidence, open to examination. The answer is that the speakers follow what they found their forefathers practising. No statement is made about whether the ancestral practice is true, whether it works, or whether it can be defended. The only fact offered is that it was already in place when the speakers arrived. This is the shape of the response that makes the verse's final question necessary. A claim about truth can be met with a claim about truth; a claim about inheritance cannot be met that way, because inheritance is not a proposition and there is nothing in it to examine. The verse is diagnosing a specific failure of reasoning: substituting the age of a practice for the grounds of a practice.

**The Same Reply Appears in Three Other Places**

Chapter 2 records the identical exchange and adds the counter-question: when they are told to follow what Allah has revealed, they answer that they follow what they found their forefathers practising, and the verse asks whether they would do so *"even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance"* (2:170). Chapter 5 gives the same reply in a shorter form — what we found our forefathers practising is good enough for us — and the same rebuttal (5:104). Chapter 10 places it in the mouth of the people Moses and Aaron were sent to: *“Have you come to turn us away from the faith of our forefathers so that the two of you may become supreme in the land? We will never believe in you!”* (10:78). The recurrence across Makkan and Medinan chapters, and across communities separated by centuries, is what allows the exegetes to read these verses as a general rejection of blind adherence to earlier generations rather than as a report about one tribe.

**The Question at the End Supplies the Missing Premise**

The rhetorical question — would they still follow, even if Satan is calling them to the torment of the Blaze? — does more than express disbelief. It inserts the element the reply omitted. An inherited practice has a destination, and a destination implies a guide. If the practice leads to the Blaze, then somebody is leading it there, and the verse names that somebody. The logic runs from outcome to agent: you may not know who is directing your tradition, but you can know where it ends, and the ending identifies the director. This is why the question is posed as a conditional rather than as an assertion. The speakers are not being told they are following Satan; they are being asked whether they would continue if they were. The conditional forces them to consider the possibility, which is the only move available against a person who refuses to examine his own grounds.

**Tradition Itself Is Not the Thing Being Condemned**

The Qur'an does not treat inheritance as suspect. It commands the followers of Muhammad ﷺ to walk the way of Abraham, described as upright, and records that God chose Abraham as a close friend (4:125). It presents the prophets as confirming what came before them, and it faults communities for abandoning covenants their ancestors accepted (2:83). What is condemned in this verse is the use of ancestry as a substitute for evidence — the refusal to ask whether the inherited practice is right, on the grounds that it is inherited. The distinction is practical rather than theoretical. A tradition can be examined and kept; that is what the believers in this chapter are described as doing when they follow the way of those who turn to God (31:15). A tradition that is defended only by its age has been removed from examination, and the verse treats the removal as the offence.

**The Blaze Is Named as the Destination of the Invitation**

*Saʿīr* — the Blaze — is one of the Qur'an's names for the Fire, from a root meaning to be kindled and to blaze up. Naming it inside the conditional does specific work. The invitation attributed to Satan in the first half of the question is not an invitation to error in the abstract; it is an invitation that ends in a particular place. The Qur'an describes that invitation elsewhere in Satan's own voice after the judgement has been passed: *“Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me”* (14:22). The structure of the two verses matches. Satan calls, people respond, and the response is described in both cases as the reason for the outcome rather than as a fate imposed from outside.

**Moses and Aaron Received the Same Answer**

The parallel in chapter 10 is the fullest version of the exchange and it adds a motive the Quraysh do not state. The people of Moses accuse the two prophets of wanting supremacy in the land, and they declare that they will never believe (10:78). The suspicion is that the new teaching is a bid for power, and the ancestral practice is defended as the status quo against a political threat. Chapter 31 leaves that suspicion unstated, but verse 6 in this same chapter describes people leading others away from God's way and taking it in mockery (31:6), which is the same accusation turned around and applied to the tradition's defenders. Both verses are describing one contest in which each side claims the other is acting from interest rather than from truth, and the Qur'an resolves it by asking which side can produce evidence.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:22

> Whoever fully submits themselves to Allah and is a good-doer, they have certainly grasped the firmest hand-hold. And with Allah rests the outcome of ˹all˺ affairs

**Expanded Commentary**

**Submitting the Face Means Submitting the Person**

The Arabic says that whoever submits his *face* to God — and the face is used here as a metonym for the whole self. Arabic regularly names the part that faces the world in order to mean the person, and the Qur'an uses the same construction elsewhere: whoever submits themselves to God and does good will have their reward with their Lord (2:112); the Prophet ﷺ is told to say, *"I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers"* (3:20); and the best in faith are those who submit themselves fully to God, do good, and follow the way of Abraham (4:125). In each case the submission is described as an act performed by the person as a whole and not as an opinion held. The face is the part that cannot be hidden and that turns in one direction at a time, which is why it was chosen for the image: submission of this kind is visible and it is oriented.

**Islām and Iḥsān Are Joined, and Each Does Different Work**

The second condition is that he *is a good-doer* — a *muḥsin*, one who performs *iḥsān*. The two terms are not redundant. Submission is the acceptance of a direction and a law; iḥsān is the doing of a thing beautifully, with excellence, as though the doer were seen. The Prophet ﷺ defined iḥsān in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl as worshipping God as though you see Him, and if you do not reach that, knowing that He sees you (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8). The two conditions therefore govern the person from different sides. Submission disciplines the passions from outside, by setting limits and obligations. Iḥsān works from inside, by changing what the person wants. A believer who has both does not need to be restrained from much that the law restrains, because the desire has been reformed; and the Qur'an promises this pairing explicitly: those who struggle in God's cause will be guided along His way, and God is with the good-doers (29:69).

**Al-ʿUrwah al-Wuthqā Is a Handhold, and Handholds Are Used Off the Ground**

The image is precise. An *ʿurwah* is a handle, a loop, a handhold — the part of a vessel or a rope by which it is grasped; *wuthqā* is the superlative of firmness and reliability. Chapter 2 uses the same phrase with the addition that it does not break: whoever renounces false gods and believes in God *"has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold"* (2:256). The choice of a handhold rather than a foundation tells the reader something about the situation of the person holding it. A foundation is stood on; a handhold is gripped by someone whose weight is not otherwise supported. The commentators read the phrase as a metaphor for salvation, and the metaphor works because salvation is not something a person produces but something he holds on to while being held.

**ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām Dreamt the Handhold and Was Told What It Meant**

The image was given a concrete interpretation in the lifetime of the Prophet ﷺ. ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām reported that he saw himself in a dream in a garden with a pillar in the middle and a handhold at the top of the pillar. He was told to climb it and said he could not; a servant came and lifted his clothes, and he climbed and grasped the handhold, and he woke while still holding it. He told the Prophet ﷺ, who said that the garden is the garden of Islam and the handhold is the firm handhold of Islam, and that he would hold firmly to Islam until he died. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7014, with a shorter parallel version at 7010. Two features of the dream match the verse: the handhold is at a height, so it must be reached rather than found on level ground, and the climber could not manage it unaided.

**The Outcome of All Affairs Rests With God**

The closing clause shifts the register from the individual to the cosmos: *wa-ilā Llāhi ʿāqibatu l-umūr* — and to God belongs the outcome of affairs. *ʿĀqibah* is the end result, what a matter turns into after all its turns. The phrase does the work of reassurance that the handhold image begins. A person gripping a handhold is in a position of dependence, and dependence is only bearable if the one holding the other end is trustworthy. The same clause closes the description of the believers established in the land, who perform prayer, pay alms, encourage good and forbid evil (22:41) — a verse about public duty ending on the same statement about where things finally land. The pairing suggests that the community does its work without controlling the result, and that the arrangement is deliberate rather than a defect.

**The Same Handhold in al-Baqarah**

Chapter 2 states the condition in the negative and this verse states it in the positive, and together they cover both sides of the act. Al-Baqarah: no compulsion in religion, the truth clear from falsehood, and whoever renounces false gods and believes has grasped the firmest handhold (2:256). Luqmān: whoever submits his face to God and does good has grasped it. The first names the renunciation, the second names the turning, and both use the same object. The verse in al-Baqarah closes with God as All-Hearing, All-Knowing; this one closes with the outcome of affairs belonging to Him. The difference in the closing names corresponds to the difference in the subject — an act of belief requires a hearer, and an act of holding on through time requires a guarantee about the end.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:23

> But whoever disbelieves, do not let their disbelief grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. To Us is their return, and We will inform them of all they did. Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Speech Turns From the Father to the Messenger**

Luqmān's counsel ended at verse 19. Verses 20 and 21 returned to the argument with those who dispute and those who appeal to their forefathers. Verse 22 described the person who holds the firm handhold. Now the address is directed at the Prophet ﷺ personally, and the subject is his grief. The shift is not incidental. The chapter has described, in sequence, people who mock the revelation, people who turn away as though deaf, people who argue without knowledge, and people who follow what their ancestors did. Anyone carrying that message into such a town will be worn down by it, and the verse is written for him. The consolation offered is not that the situation will improve but that it will be adjudicated, and the difference between those two promises is the substance of the verse.

**Grief Is Not Forbidden; Being Consumed by It Is**

The prohibition is *lā yaḥzunka kufruhum* — let not their disbelief grieve you. The verb denotes the sadness that settles in and weighs a person down, not the passing sorrow of compassion. What is forbidden is the state in which another person's refusal takes up residence inside the messenger. The Qur'an repeats the instruction in several registers, and the escalation shows that the problem was real rather than hypothetical. The Prophet ﷺ is told to be patient, since his patience is only with God's help, and not to grieve over those who disbelieve or be distressed by their schemes (16:127). He is told not to grieve himself to death over those whose evil deeds were made appealing to them (35:8). And he is told not to grieve for those who race toward disbelief, since they will not harm God in the least (3:176). The last of these gives the reason in its plainest form: the grief implies a loss that has not in fact occurred.

**The Reason Given Is a Return, Not a Threat**

The verse justifies the command by pointing at procedure: to Us is their return, and We will inform them of all they did. Nothing here says they will be punished, though the punishment is implied elsewhere. What is stated is that the case will be heard and that the parties will be told what they did. This is the same clause that closed verse 15, where it was addressed to a believer whose parents were pressuring him. There it functioned as a warning to both sides of a family dispute; here it functions as relief for a messenger whose message is being refused. The two uses show what the clause is for. It transfers the burden of judgement from the person who is grieving to the One who will conduct the hearing, and the transfer is complete: the messenger's task is delivery, and the accounting is not his to perform.

**Breasts Are the Qur'an's Word for the Concealed Interior**

The closing statement is that God knows what is in the breasts — *dhāt al-ṣudūr*. The plural of ṣadr, the chest, is used throughout the Qur'an as the location of what a person does not show. The knowledge of interiors is stated in the same terms repeatedly: God knows best what is hidden in the heart (57:6); He knows what the breasts conceal (3:119, 39:7); He is aware of what is in the hearts of people and will respond to what is there (67:13). The connection to this verse is exact. The disbelief that grieves the messenger is largely invisible to him — he sees the turning away and hears the mockery, but he cannot see whether a given person is hardened, frightened, proud, or merely slow. The verse tells him that the interior is known, which is what makes the return something other than a formality.

**Why This Verse Sits Between the Handhold and the Respite**

The position of the verse in the chapter's argument is worth marking. Verse 22 describes the believer gripping the firmest handhold. Verse 24 states that the disbelievers are being allowed enjoyment for a little while before being driven into a harsh torment. Between them, verse 23 addresses the person watching both. He is not the one holding the handhold in that sentence, and he is not the one being granted the respite; he is the one who has to carry on working while the two outcomes unfold at different speeds. The grief forbidden in verse 23 is precisely the grief produced by that delay — the sense that the wrong side is being given time and the right side is being given hardship. The verse answers it by relocating the judgement rather than by shortening the wait.

**The Same Consolation in Fāṭir**

Chapter 35 makes the same argument with the same components and adds the theological ground. Are those whose evil-doing was made appealing to them, so that they deemed it good, like those rightly guided? God leaves astray whom He wills and guides whom He wills, so do not grieve yourself to death over them, for God knows best what they do (35:8). The addition is the knowledge clause, which in chapter 31 is placed at the end of the verse in the form of what lies in the breasts. Both verses end the consolation with an appeal to divine knowledge rather than to divine power. The reason is visible in the structure: a messenger who grieves is not doubting that God can act, but that God knows what is happening. The answer given is that He knows it better than the messenger does.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:24

> We allow them enjoyment for a little while, then ˹in time˺ We will force them into a harsh torment

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qalīlan Covers Both the Length and the Size**

The adverb *qalīlan* — a little — is doing double duty, and the ambiguity is deliberate. It can qualify time, so that the enjoyment is brief; and it can qualify amount, so that the enjoyment is small even while it lasts. Both readings are supported by the rest of the Qur'an's usage, and the two are not really separable. An enjoyment that ends is small in the only measure that finally counts, and an enjoyment that is measured out in portions rather than poured out is short by construction. The verse's force depends on holding both together: what is being given to these people is neither much nor lasting, and the harsh torment that follows is described without either qualifier. The comparison is therefore between a small, temporary good and a severe, fixed one, and the arithmetic is the argument.

**Istidrāj: The Respite That Looks Like Generosity**

The Qur'an has a technical name for what this verse describes. *Istidrāj* is the drawing-on of a person stage by stage, so that he is led further while believing he is being favoured. The command to the Prophet ﷺ is explicit: leave to Me those who reject this message, for We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend (68:44), and the next verse supplies the mechanism — *I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless* (68:45). The same statement appears in this chapter's earlier Meccan context: I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless (7:183). Chapter 3 states the purpose without the metaphor: those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them, since *“They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment”* (3:178). The delay is not a reprieve. It is an accumulation.

**The Verb of the Second Half Is Compulsion**

The first movement is permission — We allow them enjoyment. The second is *naḍṭarruhum*, from a root meaning to drive a person into a corner, to leave him no alternative, to compel. The same form appears where a person in starvation is driven to what is forbidden, and where the punishment is described as arriving without any way out. The contrast between the two verbs is the verse's structure: they are left free to enjoy, and then they are forced into the torment. Nobody is compelled into the enjoyment and nobody is free in the torment. The reversal is exact, and it answers the objection that the verse might otherwise invite — that a God who allows the enjoyment has approved it. The allowance is real and the freedom within it is real, and both end at the point where compulsion begins.

**Enjoyment Is Not a Verdict, and the Qur'an Says So Repeatedly**

The command to enjoy yourselves appears in the Qur'an as an ironic instruction rather than a permission, and its repetition is a warning against reading prosperity as approval. Let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by false hope, for they will soon know (15:3). Say: enjoy yourselves, for your destination is the Fire (14:30). They return Our favours with ingratitude, so enjoy yourselves, for you will soon know (16:55). Let them be ungrateful for all We have given them and enjoy themselves, for they will soon know (29:66). Eat and enjoy yourselves for a little while, for you are truly wicked (77:46). The formula is consistent: enjoyment, a short duration, and a knowing that is coming. The verse in this chapter states the same sequence without the irony, which makes it the plainest version of the claim.

**Ghalīẓ Describes a Punishment That Is Coarse and Heavy**

The torment is *ghalīẓ*, a word whose basic sense is thick, coarse, dense, and which is used of punishment that is severe and of oaths that are weighty. The choice of adjective matters because the verse is contrasting qualities rather than quantities. The enjoyment was *qalīl* — thin, slight, little. The torment is *ghalīẓ* — thick, heavy, dense. The two words belong to the same field of physical description, and the pairing is a deliberate reversal of texture. What was light becomes heavy; what was brief becomes what they are driven into. The verse asks nothing of the reader except that he notice which of the two he is currently inside, and the noticing is possible only because the first has been identified as temporary. A person who assumes the enjoyment will continue has no reason to compare it with anything, and the comparison is the entire content of the warning. This is also why the verse is stated in the present tense rather than as a prediction: the allowing is happening now, and the driving will happen at a point the person being driven cannot identify from inside the allowance.

**The Same Two Movements in al-Qalam**

Chapter 68 states the sequence with the mechanism made visible. The Prophet ﷺ is told to leave the rejecters to God, who will draw them on gradually in ways they cannot comprehend, and then that the delay is short and the planning flawless (68:44–45). The addition of "in ways they cannot comprehend" is the crucial difference from this verse. Here the respite is described from outside, as an arrangement God makes; there it is described from inside, as something the recipient cannot see happening. The two perspectives complete the doctrine. From outside, the delay is a decision with a purpose; from inside, it feels like good fortune. A person who cannot tell the difference between the two is being drawn on, and the only way to tell is the one this verse supplies: measure the enjoyment against its duration.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:25

> And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, “Allah!” Say, “Praise be to Allah!” In fact, most of them do not know

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Is Chosen Because the Answer Is Certain**

The verse opens with a conditional whose outcome is guaranteed: if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, Allah. The certainty is stated in the particle *la-* attached to the verb, and it is what makes the whole exchange useful. The Qur'an is not reporting an argument it hopes to win but a concession its opponents have already made. The method is diagnostic. By asking a question whose answer is agreed, the verse isolates the point of disagreement and shows that it is not about the creator at all. What is contested is not who made the world but who is to be worshipped in it, and the gap between the two positions is where the verse places its command to praise God.

**The Same Question Is Put in Six Other Places**

The formula recurs often enough to be recognisable as a fixed argument. Ask them who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon, and they will certainly say Allah — how then can they be deluded (29:61). Ask them who sends down rain from the sky, giving life to the earth after its death, and they will surely say Allah, and say: praise be to Allah, for most of them do not understand (29:63). Ask them who created them and they will certainly say Allah — how then can they be deluded (43:87); ask them who created the heavens and the earth and they will say the Almighty, the All-Knowing did (43:9). Chapter 10 puts the question in five parts: who provides for you from heaven and earth, who owns your hearing and sight, who brings the living from the dead, and who conducts every affair — and they will say, Allah (10:31). Chapter 23 asks to whom the earth and everyone on it belong, and the reply is to Allah, followed by the question why they are not then mindful (23:84–85).

**"Praise Be to Allah" Settles the Case Rather Than Arguing It**

The instruction to the Prophet ﷺ is to say *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*, praise belongs to God. The phrase is not a pious interjection here; it is the conclusion of the exchange. Once the concession has been made — that the creator is God — the question of praise answers itself, since praise follows creation and not merely power. The commentators draw out both implications: only God should be praised for the existence of the created order, since nothing else had any part in producing it; and only God should therefore be worshipped, since worship is the practical form of praise. The same command appears at the end of the parallel question in chapter 29 (29:63), which shows it is part of the fixed formula rather than a local addition. Saying the phrase aloud in the middle of a debate performs the deduction the opponent refuses to perform: if He made it, He is the one to be thanked for it.

**Most of Them Do Not Know, and What They Do Not Know Is Specified by Context**

The final clause is not a claim of total ignorance. These are people who answered the question correctly, so they know something. What they do not know is the consequence of what they know. The same verdict closes the parallel verse in chapter 29 with a slight variation — most of them do not understand (29:63) — and the two words between them describe the failure exactly. Knowledge that does not move a person is not knowledge in the Qur'an's sense; understanding, *yaʿqilūn*, is knowledge that grasps and holds. The verse distinguishes the two and denies the second. This is a claim about consistency rather than about information: the same people who say Allah when asked about creation call on other beings when they want help, and the inconsistency is what the phrase "most of them do not know" names.

**A Supreme Deity Is Not the Same as the Only Deity**

The historical shape of the Meccan creed explains why the concession was available. The audience of this chapter believed in a high god who created the world, and they associated other beings with him as intermediaries. Chapter 39 records the justification in their own words: *“We worship them only so they may bring us closer to Allah”* (39:3), and answers it by stating that sincere devotion is due to God alone and that He will judge between them regarding what they differed about. The verse is therefore not converting atheists. It is dismantling an addition. The strategy of asking about creation works precisely because the addition cannot be defended by the same route: the intermediaries are not claimed to have created anything, and the concession about the creator, once made, leaves them without a function that could justify worship. Chapter 29 makes the same point by asking who sends the rain, since provision is the function the intermediaries were invoked for.

**The Same Exchange in al-Zukhruf**

Chapter 43 gives both halves of the argument in two verses. Ask them who created the heavens and the earth and they will certainly say the Almighty, the All-Knowing did (43:9); ask them who created them and they will certainly say Allah, and then the verse asks how they can be deluded (43:87). The addition of the two divine names in the first reply is significant, because it shows the opponents attributing might and knowledge to the creator — the two attributes that make worship of intermediaries pointless. The second verse narrows the question from the cosmos to the questioner himself: you were created by the same being. Chapter 31 keeps the cosmic form and adds the command to praise, which is the step chapter 43 leaves to the rhetorical question about delusion.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:26

> To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. Allah is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership Is Stated Before the Names Are Given**

The verse has two movements and the order matters. First a claim about property: whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to God. Then two names: the Self-Sufficient and the Praised. The claim is comprehensive and admits no exceptions — the phrase covers everything located in either realm, which includes the idols being worshipped, the ground they stand on, and the people worshipping them. The same formulation appears in the middle of al-Baqarah in a verse that moves from ownership straight to accountability: *“To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it”* (2:284). In both cases the ownership statement does preparatory work. What follows depends on it, and nothing that follows could be said without it.

**Ghanī Returns, and Its Meaning Has Sharpened**

The name *Ghanī*, the Self-Sufficient, closed the verse in which Luqmān was given wisdom (31:12), where it was paired with gratitude: whoever is grateful is grateful for his own good, and whoever is ungrateful harms only himself, because God is free of need. Here the same name follows an argument about creation and ownership, and the content of the self-sufficiency has changed accordingly. In verse 12 God's independence was from the gratitude of creatures. Here it is independence from the creation itself — He owns everything and gains nothing by the ownership. The two uses together state a single doctrine in two applications: nothing a creature gives adds to God, and nothing God made supplies a lack in Him. Worship is therefore not a service rendered to a needy principal, which is the assumption every transactional religion carries.

**Ḥamīd Is Praised Whether or Not Anyone Praises**

The intensive form *faʿīl* can be read actively as well as passively, and the exegetes read this name actively: God is the Praised One by nature, praised in Himself, and the praise of creatures does not create the quality it acknowledges. The passive reading — the One who is praised by all — is also available and both are held together in the tradition. The active reading is the stronger here because of what it does to the argument. If praise were produced by creatures, then the refusal to praise would remove something from God, and the previous verse's command to say "praise be to Allah" would be an instruction to supply a deficiency. On the active reading the command is an invitation to join a praise already in progress. The ingrate's loss is then total, as verse 12 said, because the only thing he has forfeited is his own participation.

**The Three Claims Together Remove Any Transactional Reading of Worship**

Ownership, self-sufficiency and praise form a closed argument, and each step blocks an escape from the next. Because everything in the heavens and the earth is His, there is nothing a person can bring that is not already His. Because He is self-sufficient, nothing brought adds to Him. Because He is praised in Himself, the praise is not a payment for services and its withholding is not a loss to the one praised. What is left is a relation with no exchange in it, which is the Qur'an's consistent account of worship: it benefits the worshipper. The same conclusion was reached in verse 12 by a different route, through gratitude, and the chapter reaches it again here through creation. The two routes converge on the same statement, which is a structural feature of this sūrah rather than a repetition.

**The Two Names Also Answer an Objection About Worship**

A person asked why he should worship a God who owns everything and needs nothing has already framed worship as a transaction, and the verse removes the frame. The names do not merely describe God; they specify what can and cannot pass between Him and a creature. Nothing passes to Him that increases Him, because He is self-sufficient. Nothing passes from Him that He is deprived of, because everything already belongs to Him. What passes is praise, and the praise does not add to the one praised — it changes the one praising. That is the same conclusion Luqmān reached in his first instruction to his son, where gratitude was described as benefiting only the person who is grateful (31:12). The chapter states the doctrine twice, once in a household and once in a cosmological argument, and both statements land on the same asymmetry. The creature is the party with everything to gain, and the verse names the two attributes that make this so.

**The Verse Answers the Question the Previous Verse Raised**

Verse 25 ended with "most of them do not know". Verse 26 does not explain what they fail to know; it states the fact they are failing to draw. If God owns what is in the heavens and the earth, then the beings they invoke besides Him are owned too, and an owned thing cannot be approached as a patron. The Qur'an makes the inference explicit elsewhere when it asks whether those invoked besides God have created anything on earth or have a share in the heavens, and answers that the wrongdoers promise each other nothing but delusion (35:40). The names closing this verse supply the positive counterpart: a God who needs nothing and is praised regardless has no interest in intermediaries, and none were appointed. The ignorance named in verse 25 is the failure to make exactly this step.

**The Same Claim in al-Baqarah**

Chapter 2 states the ownership and then draws a conclusion about the interior life: whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, God will call you to account for it, forgiving whom He wills and punishing whom He wills, and He is Most Capable of everything (2:284). The parallel with chapter 31 is close. Both move from ownership to a divine capacity, and both close with names. Al-Baqarah names capability, which fits a verse about judgement; Luqmān names self-sufficiency and praise, which fit a verse following the command to praise God. The difference in the closing names is not arbitrary but follows from what each chapter has just been arguing, and the agreement on the opening claim is what allows both conclusions to stand.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:27

> If all the trees on earth were pens and the ocean ˹were ink˺, refilled by seven other oceans, the Words of Allah would not be exhausted. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Arithmetic Designed Never to Be Completed**

The verse sets up a calculation and then declares it impossible to finish. Take every tree on the earth and make them pens. Take the sea and add seven more seas to it as ink. Begin writing the words of God. The materials run out first. The image works because every term in it is finite and specified, and the quantity to be written is not. Trees can be counted, seas can be counted, and the addition of seven more is a way of saying that the supply has been multiplied beyond any conceivable need. The conclusion is stated as a fact about the materials rather than about the words: they would be exhausted, *qabla an tanfada kalimātu Llāh* — before the words of God were spent. The verse is not saying that God has a great deal to say. It is saying that the saying is not a quantity.

**Seven Is Not a Number in This Sentence**

The commentators agree that the seven additional seas are not literal. In Arabic usage seven, seventy, and seven hundred commonly denote an indefinite multiplicity rather than a precise count, and the Qur'an uses seven in that way elsewhere — the seven heavens describe a complete structure rather than an itemised list. The reasoning here is internal to the argument. If seven seas were a fixed total, the verse would be making a claim about a quantity, and a claim about a quantity could in principle be checked and found wanting. By naming a number that is obviously a figure, the verse forecloses the reading. The ink is unimaginably vast and it is still finite; the words are not vast but unbounded. Adding more seas does not change the outcome, which is why the number is left at seven rather than pushed higher.

**Kalimāt Allāh Are the Words That Make Things**

The words in question are not a book. The Qur'an uses the same phrase for the creative speech by which things come to exist: when God wills a thing, He says to it "Be!" and it is (36:82), and the same statement appears in three other chapters (2:117; 16:40). God's command is a single word, done in the blink of an eye (54:50). Read against that usage, the words of this verse are the acts of bringing-into-being, and the inexhaustibility claimed for them is the inexhaustibility of creation. Anything that could be made is within their scope, and the possibilities of what could be made are not countable. This is why the verse follows the statement that creation belongs to God and precedes the statement that the creation and resurrection of all humankind is as nothing but a single soul (31:28). The sequence is an argument about capacity, and this verse establishes that the capacity cannot be depleted.

**Two Reported Occasions, Neither of Them a Graded Report**

The exegetes transmit two accounts of what prompted the verse. In the first, a delegation of Jewish leaders in Madinah objected to the statement that humanity has been given but little knowledge (17:85), asking whether it referred to them and to their scripture, since they had been given a book clarifying all things; the Prophet ﷺ answered that in relation to God's knowledge what they had was little, and that what they had been given would suffice them if they acted on it. In the second, the objection was turned against the promise that whoever is granted wisdom has been granted much good (2:269) — how could little knowledge and much good be combined? A third report says the verse answered people who remarked that what Muhammad ﷺ transmits would soon be used up. These are occasion-narrations preserved by the commentators and the collectors of *asbāb al-nuzūl*, not graded prophetic traditions, and they should be read as explanations of context rather than as evidence. What they agree on is that the verse answers a claim about limits.

**Al-Kahf Runs the Same Calculation With One Ocean**

Chapter 18 puts the image in the Prophet's own mouth and simplifies it: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'If the ocean were ink for ˹writing˺ the Words of my Lord, it would certainly run out before the Words of my Lord were finished, even if We refilled it with its equal.'”* (18:109). One ocean, refilled once with the same amount, and the same conclusion. Chapter 31 multiplies the ink by eight and adds the trees as pens. The difference is one of emphasis. Al-Kahf's version is a statement about the Lord's words addressed to a questioner; this version is a statement about God's words embedded in an argument with people who doubt the reach of His knowledge. The multiplication in chapter 31 answers the doubt by making the impossibility of the task visible — the more ink you add, the clearer it becomes that no amount will do.

**Mighty and Wise Close an Argument About Capacity**

The two names are the same pair that closed the promise of the gardens (31:9), and they do similar work here. The Mighty is the one whose capacity cannot be overcome or exhausted; the Wise is the one whose action is measured rather than random. A verse claiming that God's words are inexhaustible needs the first name, and a reader might reasonably ask whether an inexhaustible speech implies an indiscriminate one. The second name answers that. What comes from these words is ordered, and the order is why the trees and the seas are the images chosen — both are structured, both are productive, and neither produces at random. The pairing also connects this verse to the one that follows, since the wisdom named here is what makes the ease of creation described there meaningful rather than merely impressive.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:28

> The creation and resurrection of you ˹all˺ is as simple ˹for Him˺ as that of a single soul. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison Runs Between a Multitude and a Single Person**

The verse states that the creation of all of you and your resurrection are, to God, as nothing but one soul. Two multitudes are being compared with a single unit — every human being who has ever lived, brought into existence, and then every one of them brought back, set against one person made once. The comparison is not about difficulty in a human sense, since no difficulty attaches to God in the Qur'an's account. It is about the absence of any scaling effect. Human making accumulates cost: the thousandth unit takes as much work as the first, and a crowd is harder to move than a man. The verse denies that the case is analogous. What God does, He does by command, and a command is not multiplied by the number of things it applies to.

**One Word and the Blinking of an Eye**

The mechanism is stated elsewhere without ambiguity. Our command is but a single word, done in the blink of an eye (54:50). All it takes, when God wills something to be, is to say to it "Be!" and it is (36:82); if We ever will something to exist, all We say is "Be!" and it is (16:40); when He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, "Be!" and it is (2:117). The resurrection is described in the same economy: it will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us (36:53), and it will take only one mighty Blast (79:13). A single word and a single sound accomplish what the verse is describing, and the reason the comparison with one soul works is that one soul is also made by one word. The quantity of the object does not change the quantity of the act.

**Greater Than the Recreation, and Most People Do Not Know**

Chapter 40 states the same conclusion from the larger term: *“The creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the recreation of humankind, but most people do not know”* (40:57). The argument there runs from the admitted to the disputed. The audience accepts that God made the cosmos; the cosmos is greater than the return of human beings; therefore the return is not beyond Him. This verse compresses the argument to its limit by comparing not the cosmos with humanity but humanity with one person. Chapter 30 makes the point in a single clause: He originates the creation then will resurrect it, *“which is even easier for Him”* (30:27). The three verses use three different scales — cosmos, species, individual — and arrive at the same place, which is evidence that the scales are rhetorical rather than substantive.

**A Single Soul Is Also the Origin of the Species**

The phrase "a single soul" is not chosen only for its smallness. The Qur'an describes the beginning of humanity in the same words: God created you from a single soul and from it created its mate, and through both spread countless men and women (4:1); He created you all from a single soul, then from it made its mate (39:6). The species did in fact begin as one, and the verse's comparison is therefore grounded in something the audience accepts about its own origin. What was done once at the beginning is what will be done at the end, and the difference between the two events is only the number of people involved — a number the verse has just declared irrelevant. This is a stronger form of the argument than a bare assertion of power, because it uses the hearer's own ancestry as the middle term.

**All-Hearing and All-Seeing Guarantee That Nobody Is Lost in the Crowd**

The names closing the verse answer an objection the comparison invites. If the making of everyone is like the making of one, is anyone's particularity preserved? The two names are the answer, and both are names of attention rather than of power. God hears and God sees, and hearing and seeing are exercised on individuals. The same pair closes the command to return trusts to their owners and to judge with fairness (4:58), where the names guarantee that the judging is attentive to each case. Chapter 22 uses the pair after stating that God causes the night to merge into the day (22:61), which is the same pairing of cosmic order with individual attention. The verse's logic is that a power capable of making a multitude as easily as one is a power that loses nothing by doing so, and the names are the confirmation.

**The Ease of the Act Does Not Make the Event Small**

One further implication follows from the comparison, and the verse's closing names carry it. If the making of everyone is like the making of one, the event is not thereby diminished; the opposite follows. The single word that creates one soul creates all of them, so the resurrection is not a mass operation in which individuals are processed in bulk. Each person is made by the same act that would make him alone, and the names of hearing and seeing confirm that the act is attentive. The Qur'an's account of the Day insists on the same point from the side of the record: every person will be brought with his own book, and the book will not leave out anything small or great. The ease stated in this verse is ease of power, not economy of attention, and confusing the two would produce exactly the error the verse is meant to prevent — the assumption that a multitude cannot be handled individually.

**The One Blast in Yā-Sīn**

Chapter 36 gives the event this verse describes in narrative form. There will be one Blast, and at once they will all be brought before Us (36:53). The word *ṣayḥah* is a shout or a cry, and the singular is emphatic: one, not one per person or one per generation. The verse in chapter 31 explains why a single sound can raise everyone, and the verse in chapter 36 shows what happens when it does. Read together they answer the two questions a Makkan audience actually asked about resurrection — how it could be done, and what it would look like. The first answer is arithmetic: the act does not scale. The second is temporal: there is no interval, since "at once" removes the possibility of a queue.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:29

> Do you not see that Allah causes the night to merge into the day and the day into the night, and has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term, and that Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yūlij Is the Verb of One Thing Entering Another**

The Arabic word for the merging is from the root *w-l-j*, to enter, to go inside, to penetrate. The form used here is causative and reciprocal in effect: God makes the night enter into the day and the day into the night. The image is of two quantities, each growing at the other's expense, one sliding inside the other rather than replacing it. That is a more precise description than "alternation", which suggests a swap. The same verb is used in three other chapters, and in each case the statement is followed by a claim about God's knowledge or authority: He merges the night into the day and the day into the night, and He knows best what is hidden in the heart (57:6); the same merging, followed by the assertion that Allah is your Lord and all authority belongs to Him (35:13); and the same again, followed by God as All-Hearing, All-Seeing (22:61). The verb belongs to a fixed formula.

**Two Readings: the Daily Exchange and the Seasonal One**

The commentators divide on what the merging describes. One reading takes it as the daily alternation of light and darkness — night giving way to morning and morning to night. The other takes it as the seasonal variation in the length of day and night across the year, in which the day's hours grow at the night's expense and then the reverse. The second reading is better served by the verb, because *yūlij* describes one quantity entering another and changing its extent, which is what happens to daylight across the seasons but not, strictly, at a single dawn. The first reading is better served by the frequency with which the phenomenon is invoked as a sign available to everyone every day. The two are not really in competition: the seasonal change is composed of daily changes, and a reader who observes the same sunrise across a year sees both. The verse's language covers the whole cycle without needing to choose.

**Musakhkharāt and the Appointed Term**

The sun and the moon are *musakhkharātān*, subjected, made subservient — the same verb used in verse 20 for everything in the heavens and the earth, and in 16:12 where the stars too are subjected by His command. The subjection is described as service rather than as compulsion: chapter 14 says God subjected the sun and the moon, both constantly orbiting, and subjected the day and night for your benefit (14:33). Each runs to an appointed term, *ajalin musammā*, and the phrase has two readings. One takes the term as the end of the world, when the celestial bodies cease; the other takes it as the fixed period of each cycle, the day, the month, the year. Chapter 36 supports the second: the sun travels for its fixed term, that is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing, and the moon has ordained phases until it ends up like an old curved palm stalk (36:38–39). Both readings can be held at once, since a cycle that repeats to a fixed term is also a system that will one day stop.

**The Awareness Clause Is Attached to Astronomy Deliberately**

The verse ends by joining a cosmic fact to a moral one: and that Allah is All-Aware of what you do. The conjunction makes the two items objects of the same act of seeing — have you not considered *this*, and *that*. The connection is not decorative. A system in which the night enters the day by measured amounts and the moon follows ordained phases is a system governed with precision, and precision of that kind is the same property required to keep a record of what people do. The verse argues from the observed regularity of the heavens to the reliability of the accounting. Chapter 22 makes the same join in one sentence, stating the merging of night and day and then that God is All-Hearing, All-Seeing (22:61). Chapter 57 states the merging and then that He knows best what is in the heart (57:6). Three chapters perform the same move, which makes it a method rather than a coincidence.

**The Question Form Puts the Sign Within Everyone's Reach**

*Alam tara* — have you not seen — is the same opening as verse 20, and it does the same work. The phenomena named here require no expertise, no travel and no instrument. Any person who has watched a sunset, noticed that the days lengthen and shorten, or followed the moon across a month has seen everything the verse refers to. The question form expects the answer yes, and by expecting it, the verse converts its claim into a recollection. This is the Qur'an's standard method with the natural world: it does not present new data but names old data as evidence. The signs in chapter 30 are of the same kind — the creation of spouses, the sending of winds as good news of rain, the ships sailing by God's command (30:21, 30:46) — and each is introduced as something already visible.

**The Same Two Signs in Fāṭir**

Chapter 35 combines the merging of night and day with the subjection of the sun and moon in a single verse and then draws the conclusion this verse leaves to the reader: *“That is Allah—your Lord! All authority belongs to Him. But those ˹idols˺ you ˹pagans˺ invoke besides Allah possess not even a date-stone's membrane”* (35:13). The addition is the measurement. An idol owns less than the thin skin on a date stone, and the comparison is placed immediately after the description of a cosmos run with precision. Chapter 31 supplies the moral clause instead of the measurement, and the two chapters between them give the full form of the argument: the system is ordered, therefore its orderer holds all authority, therefore what is invoked beside Him holds nothing, therefore what you do inside that system is known.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:30

> That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and ˹because˺ Allah ˹alone˺ is the Most High, All-Great

**Expanded Commentary**

**"That Is Because" Turns the Previous Verses Into a Proof**

The verse opens with *dhālika bi-anna* — that is because. The construction signals that what follows is the reason for what preceded, and the exegetes read the reference as covering everything just stated: the merging of night into day, the subjection of the sun and moon to an appointed term, and the awareness of what people do. Those facts hold because of what this verse asserts. An alternative reading treats the opening as an emphatic statement in its own right, close to "know that God is the Truth", which loses the backward reference. The first reading is stronger, because the verse supplies exactly what the previous one lacks. Chapter 29 describes an ordered system and ends with divine awareness, but it does not say why the order exists or whose order it is. This verse answers both.

**Al-Ḥaqq Means the True and the Real at Once**

The name *al-Ḥaqq* carries both senses in Arabic, and the Qur'an uses both. It is the true as opposed to the false, and it is the real as opposed to the apparent. The commentators draw out the second sense in a metaphysical direction that the word supports: God is the ultimate reality behind and beyond creation, and everything to which people ascribe independent power is entirely dependent on Him and possesses no reality of its own. In the vocabulary of the tradition, God is the only necessary being — the one whose existence is not derived — and every other reality is contingent, existing because it is given. The verse's claim is therefore stronger than a statement about truthfulness. It says that the distinction between what is real and what is derivative runs through everything a person might rely on, and that only one term of the distinction is underivable.

**Bāṭil Is Not Merely False but Void**

What is invoked besides God is described as *bāṭil*. The word covers falsehood and voidness together: something that fails, that has no substance, that perishes and leaves nothing. A false statement is one that does not correspond; a *bāṭil* thing is one that does not hold. Applied to the objects of worship, the term denies them a function rather than merely a correct description. They cannot create, as chapter 31 has already argued by demanding the production of something made (31:11). They cannot intercede without permission, and they own less than the membrane of a date stone (35:13). The word *bāṭil* gathers these negatives into a single verdict. This is why the verse can oppose *al-Ḥaqq* to *bāṭil* rather than to *kadhib* — lying. The opposition is between being and non-being, not between accurate speech and inaccurate speech.

**Al-ʿAlī and Al-Kabīr Complete the Metaphysics**

The two closing names are the Most High and the All-Great. *ʿAlī* denotes elevation — above everything, beyond the reach of what is below; *kabīr* denotes greatness of rank and magnitude, the opposite of the small. The pairing answers the two ways a creature can misplace divinity. Something invoked as a patron is being treated as elevated, and the verse states that elevation belongs to one term only. Something invoked as powerful is being treated as great, and greatness likewise belongs to one term only. The names also fit the argument from the preceding verses: a system in which the night enters the day and the celestial bodies run to appointed terms is being conducted from above, by someone greater than any part of it. Nothing inside the system can be its director, since everything inside is subjected.

**The Identical Verse in al-Ḥajj Is the Reason to Treat This as a Formula**

Chapter 22 contains the same statement almost word for word: *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great”* (22:62). In both chapters the sentence follows a run of cosmic signs — in al-Ḥajj, the merging of night and day and God as All-Hearing, All-Seeing (22:61); here, the same merging and the subjection of the sun and moon (31:29). The double occurrence marks the statement as a conclusion the Qur'an returns to when the signs have been listed. It is not an argument being developed differently in two places but one argument being closed twice, and the closure is identical because the premises are identical. A reader who has followed either sequence arrives here.

**The Distinction Explains Why Worship of Anything Else Is Void**

Set the two terms against each other and the conclusion is immediate. If one term of the distinction is underivable and every other term depends on it, then whatever a person invokes besides God is being invoked as though it stood on its own, and it does not. The invocation is therefore not merely misdirected but empty: there is nothing at the address given. This is why the Qur'an treats shirk as a failure of perception rather than as a moral preference. The one who associates a partner with God has made an error about what exists, and the error is correctable by looking at the same order the previous verse described. Chapter 31 has already made the point by demanding production from the supposed partners and receiving nothing (31:11), and chapter 35 measures their holdings at less than the membrane of a date stone (35:13). The verse states the metaphysical ground of both arguments in one sentence, and it is the shortest complete statement of tawḥīd in the chapter.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Before the Ships**

The position of the verse in the chapter's structure does work of its own. It closes the run of cosmic evidence — the heavens without pillars, the mountains, the scattered creatures, the rain, the challenge to the idols, the inexhaustible words, the ease of resurrection, the merging of night and day — and it states the doctrine those items support. Verse 31 then returns to a smaller and more human sign, the ship crossing the sea, and verse 32 shows what people do when that ship is in danger. The transition is from the conclusion about reality to the behaviour that contradicts it. The reader has just been told that everything invoked besides God is void, and is then shown people invoking God alone when the waves rise and returning to partial gratitude when the water is calm. The placement makes the behaviour legible as a violation of something already established rather than as a mere inconsistency.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:31

> Do you not see that the ships sail ˹smoothly˺ through the sea by the grace of Allah so that He may show you some of His signs? Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ship Is the Clearest Case of Creation in Service**

Of all the phenomena the Qur'an cites as signs, the ship is the one that most visibly combines human work with a medium no human controls. The verse names the sailing as something God does *for you*, and the same claim appears across the Book. God subjected the ships for your service, sailing through the sea by His command, and subjected the rivers for you (14:32). It is your Lord who steers the ships for you through the sea so that you may seek His bounty (17:66). God subjected the sea so that ships may sail upon it by His command, so that you may seek His bounty and perhaps give thanks (45:12). He made ships and animals to ride (43:12). The list is long enough to establish the ship as a standing exhibit rather than a local illustration, and verse 20 of this chapter has already laid the general principle underneath it: whatever is in the heavens and the earth has been subjected for you (31:20).

**By Grace, and Not by Skill Alone**

The verse specifies the cause: the ships sail *by the grace of Allah* — *bi-niʿmatin min Allāh*. The noun *niʿmah* is a favour conferred, a benefit given from above. Nothing in the statement denies the shipwright's craft, the navigator's knowledge, or the cargo's economics; all of those are real and the Qur'an describes the seeking of God's bounty through trade in the same contexts (45:12). What the preposition establishes is that the craft operates inside an arrangement it did not make. Wood floats, wind pushes, water supports, and no part of that was negotiated by the sailor. The grace named here is the standing permission of the medium, and it is why the same verse can move directly from the sailing to the showing of signs: the favour is being displayed, not merely consumed.

**"Some of His Signs" Specifies a Portion**

The purpose clause is that God may show you *some of* His signs — *min āyātihi*. The preposition is partitive, and it is doing deliberate work. What is shown from a ship's deck is a selection; the total remains unshown. Chapter 41 states the wider programme: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth”* (41:53). The signs are displayed in two locations, outside and inside, and the display has an endpoint — clarity about the Book. The partitive in this verse fits that programme. A sailor sees a portion of the evidence, and the portion is sufficient for its purpose, which is not to exhaust the case but to make the case visible. The verse does not promise a complete demonstration to anyone, and the earlier verse about the inexhaustible words of God explains why a complete one is not available (31:27).

**Ṣabbār and Shakūr Name the Two Responses to Two Conditions**

The signs are said to be for everyone who is *ṣabbār shakūr*. Both are intensive forms. *Ṣabbār* is not merely patient but habitually so, and *shakūr* is not merely thankful but abundant in thanks. The commentators draw the pairing out precisely: these are the people who are steadfast when affliction arrives and thankful when they are in ease. The two conditions are named because the sea produces both, sometimes in a single voyage. Chapter 42 uses the identical pair after describing ships becalmed on the water by a wind God withholds: surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful (42:33). The recurrence of the pair in a nautical context in two chapters indicates a defined category rather than a general compliment — the person who behaves correctly under both pressure and relief.

**Why the Sea Is the Setting for That Test**

The sea is the environment in which a person's control is most visibly partial. On land a traveller can stop, turn back, dig, build and store. Aboard a ship he is carried, and the carrying depends on a wind he cannot summon and a buoyancy he cannot increase. The two responses available to him are the two named in the verse. When the crossing goes well he can either recognise the favour or take credit for the navigation; when it goes badly he can either hold steady or panic. The setting strips away the ordinary distractions that make both errors easy, which is why the Qur'an returns to it so often and why the next verse describes what happens when the same people are in real danger. The ship is a moral instrument before it is a commercial one. A merchant who has made the crossing twenty times knows this in a way he cannot state, and the verse states it for him: the water does not negotiate, the wind does not answer to his schedule, and the two things he can control are his temper under pressure and his memory in relief. Everything else aboard is borrowed.

**The Same Verdict in al-Shūrā**

Chapter 42 gives the sequence in three verses. Among His signs are the ships like mountains in the sea (42:32); if He wills He can calm the wind and leave them motionless on the water, and in that are signs for whoever is steadfast and grateful (42:33); or He can wreck them for what people have committed, though He forgives much (42:34). The three cover the favourable crossing, the becalmed crossing, and the destroyed one, and each is called a sign. Chapter 31 names only the favourable crossing and reserves the danger for the verse that follows. Between them the two chapters describe the sea completely, and both end the description with the same two qualities — steadfastness and gratitude — as the equipment needed to read it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:32

> And as soon as they are overwhelmed by waves like mountains, they cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion. But when He delivers them ˹safely˺ to shore, only some become relatively grateful. And none rejects Our signs except whoever is deceitful, ungrateful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Waves Like Awnings, Rendered Here as Mountains**

The Arabic describes the waves as *ka-l-ẓulal*, and *ẓulal* is the plural of a shade or canopy — an awning, a covering stretched overhead, the shadow cast by something large. The image is of water rising over the travellers and hanging above them, so that the danger is not only around the ship but over it. This translation renders the phrase as "waves like mountains", which is the image used elsewhere in the Qur'an: the ark sailed with them through waves like mountains while Noah called to his son (11:42). The two pictures differ in what they stress. A mountain stresses size and immovability; an awning stresses enclosure and the loss of sky. Both convey the same fact — that the passengers have no room left and no view of anything but water — and the choice of the covering image here fits the verse's subject, which is what a person does when every exit is closed.

**Devotion Made Entirely His Is Named With a Technical Word**

The phrase is *mukhliṣīna lahu l-dīn* — making religion sincere, or exclusive, for Him. *Ikhlāṣ* is the verbal noun of a root meaning to purify and to make something unmixed, and in religious usage it denotes devotion with nothing else in it. The Qur'an treats the sea as the setting where this state arrives uninvited. If they happen to be aboard a ship caught in a storm, they cry out to Allah alone in sincere devotion (29:65). When those on board assume they are doomed, they cry out to Allah in sincere devotion, promising gratitude if they are saved (10:22). The command to the same state is given separately, apart from any danger: call upon Allah with sincere devotion, even to the dismay of the disbelievers (40:14); they were only commanded to worship Allah alone with sincere devotion in all uprightness (98:5). The verse in this chapter describes a sincerity produced by circumstances, and the next clause shows what happens to it when the circumstances change.

**"Only Some Become Relatively Grateful" Withholds More Than It Grants**

The deliverance arrives and the verse makes a qualification that is easy to miss: some of them take a middle course. The word is *muqtaṣid*, from a root meaning to be moderate, to keep to the middle, to hold a measured path. It is not a term of praise here. The person described does not return to open polytheism and does not become fully grateful; he occupies a reduced position, keeping part of what the danger taught him and dropping the rest. The verse's structure makes the reduction visible by contrast with the intensity of the prayer on the water, which was total. Chapter 17 states the worse outcome plainly: when hardship touches you at sea you forget every god you normally invoke except Him, but when He delivers you to shore you turn away — and humankind is ever ungrateful (17:67). Chapter 10 states the worst: as soon as He rescues them, they transgress in the land unjustly (10:23). Between full relapse and full gratitude, this verse places the middle course, and the placement is a criticism.

**Khaṭṭār and Kafūr Describe a Character, Not an Incident**

The final sentence names the kind of person who rejects the signs: *kull khaṭṭārin kafūr*. Both words are intensive forms. *Khaṭṭār* comes from a root meaning to break a promise repeatedly and to betray, and the exegetes gloss it as one who stabs in the back or who breaks every promise he makes. *Kafūr* is the intensive of the denier of favour, the one who covers over a benefit he has received. The pair therefore describes a settled disposition rather than a single lapse. The man who prayed sincerely on the water and forgot on the shore has not made one mistake; he has shown what he is. The word *khaṭṭār* fits the situation exactly, because the prayer made at sea was a promise — chapter 10 records it as a vow to be among the thankful (10:22) — and the promise was broken within hours of being kept.

**The Storm Is a Parable for Every Affliction**

The commentators read the scene generally, and the reading is supported by how often the Qur'an returns to it. A storm at sea is the one affliction that arrives without any possibility of preparation, that cannot be negotiated with, that removes every alternative patron at once, and that ends decisively in one of two outcomes. Those four properties belong to every severe trial in a human life, which is why the same scene is used in chapter 10, chapter 17, chapter 29 and chapter 31. The parable's lesson is about the reliability of a devotion produced by pressure. Sincerity that arrives when the alternatives have been stripped away proves something about the person's view of reality; the question the verse then asks is whether the view survives the return of the alternatives. For most of the people described, it does not.

**The Same Scene in Yūnus**

Chapter 10 gives the fullest version and adds the aftermath. He enables travel through land and sea; the ship sails with a favourable wind to the passengers' delight; suddenly a gale strikes and waves overwhelm them from every side and they assume they are doomed; they cry out to Allah, sincerely devoted, promising that if He saves them they will be among the thankful (10:22). Then the single verse of consequence: as soon as He rescues them, they transgress in the land unjustly, and they are told that their transgression is only against their own souls, that there is brief enjoyment in this life, and that the return is to God who will inform them of what they used to do (10:23). Chapter 31 stops before the transgression and reports the reduced gratitude instead, and the two accounts together cover the whole range of what people do on reaching the shore.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:33

> O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord, and beware of a Day when no parent will be of any benefit to their child, nor will a child be of any benefit to their parent. Surely Allah’s promise is true. So do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the Chief Deceiver deceive you about Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Widens to Everyone**

The chapter has moved through several audiences: the good-doers described in the opening verses, the mockers and the turners-away, a father and his son, the disputers and the traditionalists, and the Prophet ﷺ himself. This verse addresses *yā ayyuhā l-nās* — O humanity. The shift to the widest possible audience happens at the point where the subject becomes the Day, and the reason is that the Day applies without exception. Chapter 4 opens with the same address and the same ground, calling humanity to be mindful of the Lord who created them from a single soul (4:1), and chapter 82 asks what has emboldened humanity against its Lord, the Most Generous (82:6). Chapter 35 uses the identical formula for the identical warning: O humanity, Allah's promise is true, so do not let the life of this world deceive you (35:5). The vocative marks a statement nobody is exempt from.

**A Day on Which Neither Direction of Family Help Works**

The verse specifies two failures and they are symmetrical. No parent will be of any benefit to a child, and no child will be of any benefit to a parent. Both directions are named because both are relied upon in this life. A parent's protection, provision and standing normally cover a child; a grown child's support normally covers an ageing parent. The verse removes both, and it removes them in the relationship where the expectation is strongest. The Qur'an states the same fact without the family specification several times: guard yourselves against a Day on which no soul will be of help to another, no intercession accepted, no ransom taken, and no help given (2:48; 2:123). Chapter 26 puts it in terms of wealth and offspring: the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit (26:88). The underlying principle is stated as a rule of the moral order: no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another (6:164; 35:18).

**Two Reasons the Family Cannot Help, and They Are Different**

The commentators identify two distinct grounds, and both are supported by other verses. The first is incapacity: even if a parent wanted to take the child's place, the substitution would not be accepted, because burdens are not transferable. The second is preoccupation: everyone on that Day will be too absorbed in his own situation to attend to anyone else's. Chapter 80 states the second plainly — for then everyone will have enough concern of their own (80:37) — and chapter 70 describes a man who would ransom himself from the punishment with his sons, his wife, his brother, and his whole family, and would still not be saved (70:10–14). The two grounds are not alternatives but stages. The transfer is not permitted, and even if it were, the person who might make it is not in a condition to consider it. This is why the verse can be a warning rather than a description of cruelty: nobody will be withholding help.

**The Promise Is True for the Third Time in This Chapter**

Verse 9 closed the promise of the gardens with the statement that God's promise is true. Verse 33 repeats it after describing a Day on which family help fails. The repetition is not padding; the two contexts require the same guarantee for different reasons. In verse 9 the promise needed to be true so that the reward would arrive. Here it needs to be true so that the warning about the Day is not a rhetorical device. A person who accepts the first and discounts the second has accepted a promise selectively, and the verse forecloses that. The same statement appears twice more with the addition that most people do not know it (10:55; 30:6), which identifies the difficulty: the problem is not the reliability of the promise but the failure to treat it as knowledge.

**Al-Gharūr: The Deceiver Named by His Function**

The last clause warns against two deceivers and distinguishes them. The first is the life of this world. The second is *al-gharūr*, the chief deceiver, and the commentators identify him as Satan; the word can also be taken as delusion in general, which does not weaken the reference since the Qur'an elsewhere personifies the same activity. The description of the method is given directly: *“Satan only makes them ˹false˺ promises and deludes them with ˹empty˺ hopes. Truly Satan promises them nothing but delusion”* (4:120). The two instruments — promises and hopes — are both future-directed, which explains the pairing with the life of this world. The world deceives by what it currently offers; the deceiver deceives by what he says will happen. Chapter 35 joins the same two warnings in one sentence (35:5), and chapter 57 describes the outcome from the side of the deceived, who are told that the Chief Deceiver deceived them about Allah (57:14).

**Satan Explains the Delusion Afterwards**

Chapter 14 preserves the deceiver's own account, delivered after the judgement has been passed. Satan addresses his followers: Allah made you a true promise, I too made you a promise but I failed you, I had no authority over you, I only called you and you responded to me — so do not blame me, blame yourselves (14:22). The speech confirms three things this verse assumes. The deception works by promises, so the promise of God and the promise of the deceiver were competing in the same register. There is no compulsion involved, which is why the response is attributed to the deceived. And the exposure comes after the fact, which is why the warning has to be issued beforehand, to humanity in general, while the choice is still open.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:34

> Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and knows what is in the wombs. No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware

**Expanded Commentary**

**Five Items, and Each One Is a Matter People Plan Around**

The verse lists five things and the list is not assembled at random. Every item is something human beings need to know in order to plan, and every one of them is withheld. When the Hour will arrive determines the length of the runway available for repentance. When the rain will fall determines the harvest and therefore the year. What is in the womb determines the family, the labour, and the inheritance. What a soul will earn tomorrow determines the value of any undertaking begun today. In which land a person will die determines the meaning of every journey he takes. The five between them cover the eschatological, the agricultural, the domestic, the commercial and the personal. Nothing a person might want to schedule falls outside them, and the verse closes the scheduling by naming the knowledge as God's alone.

**The Keys of the Unseen, and the Report That Names Them**

The report comes from Ibn ʿUmar and stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4627. The Prophet ﷺ enumerated five locked matters — the Hour, the descent of rain, what a womb is carrying, what awaits a person tomorrow, and the ground in which he will die — and stated that God holds them alone and that no one else has been given access to any of them. The transmitters add that he then recited this verse, which is the clearest evidence that the report was understood as an explanation of it rather than as an independent statement. The same principle appears in chapter 6 in a single image: *“With Him are the keys of the unseen”* (6:59), and the verse continues that a leaf does not fall without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything green or dry, but it stands recorded. The metaphor of keys is exact. A locked room is not inaccessible because it does not exist; it is inaccessible because the means of opening it is somewhere else.

**Jibrīl Asked, and the Answer Refused a Date**

The most direct confirmation of the first item comes from the ḥadīth of Jibrīl. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reported that a man in white came and asked the Prophet ﷺ about Islam, then about faith, then about excellence, and then said: inform me about the Hour. The answer was that the one questioned knows no more about it than the one asking. The man then asked for its signs and received them. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8. The exchange is decisive because the questioner was an angel and the answer was still a refusal. The Qur'an repeats the refusal in the same terms: they ask you regarding the Hour, when will it be — say, that knowledge is only with my Lord, and it is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will come only by surprise (7:187); people ask you about the Hour — say, that knowledge is only with Allah, and you never know, perhaps the Hour is near (33:63). The last clause is the closest the revelation comes to a date, and it is not one.

**Two Reported Occasions, and Neither Is a Graded Tradition**

The exegetes transmit an account in which a man came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked when the Hour would be, adding that his land had dried up and he wanted to know when it would rain, that his wife was pregnant and he wanted to know when she would give birth, and that he knew the land of his birth but not the land of his death. Another account says the verse had already been revealed and that the Prophet ﷺ answered the last question by explaining that if God intends to take a servant's soul in a particular land, He places a need in that servant which draws him to it. These are occasion-narrations preserved in the collections of *asbāb al-nuzūl* and in the commentaries rather than graded prophetic reports, and they should be read as context. What is notable is that the four questions in the first account map onto four of the five items in the verse, which is why the report was attached to it.

**Rain and Wombs Have Signs and Still No Certainty**

Two of the five items have observable indicators, and the verse withholds knowledge anyway. Clouds, winds and seasons allow a person to expect rain; the Qur'an itself describes the winds sent as good news of rain (30:46). Pregnancy has stages and a term. Chapter 41 states the coverage in the same terms: no fruit comes out of its husk, and no female conceives or delivers, without His knowledge (41:47). The distinction being drawn is between prediction and knowledge. A prediction is a probability drawn from signs; knowledge is certainty about the particular event. The verse assigns the second to God alone and does not dispute the first, which is why the commentators note that rain and the womb were singled out precisely because people assume they can know them from customary signs. The assumption is what is being corrected, not the observation.

**The Last Two Items Are About Your Own Life, and That Is the Point**

The last two items turn inward. Nobody knows what tomorrow's ledger will hold for him, and nobody knows the piece of ground on which he will die. These two are addressed to the individual rather than to the world, and they work as an admonition rather than as information. The moral reading was stated by al-Tustarī: since a person cannot know what will be counted for him or against him out of what has been measured for him from the unseen, he should hold to the remembrance of God and call on Him for help until He takes charge of the affair — and God eliminates and confirms what He wills, and with Him is the Master Record (13:39). The uncertainty about tomorrow's earning and about the place of death produces a single practical conclusion: be ready, since the timing is not yours. Chapter 33 has just warned against being deceived by the life of this world (31:33), and this verse supplies the reason the deception works — the person deceived assumes he has time.

**The Keys in al-Anʿām**

Chapter 6 states the doctrine in its general form and then adds the detail that makes it comprehensive: with God are the keys of the unseen, none knows them except Him, and He knows what is in the land and the sea, and not a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything green or dry, but it is written in a perfect record (6:59). The verse in chapter 31 names five specific instances; the verse in chapter 6 names the principle and then sweeps in everything else. Read together they close the chapter on the same note the Qur'an closes its account of the unseen: the withholding is not a gap in divine knowledge but a limit on human knowledge, and the limit is what makes the remaining time consequential.
