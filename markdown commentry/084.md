# Sūrah al-Inshiqāq (Chapter 84) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:1

> When the sky bursts open

**Expanded Commentary**

**Opening on a Condition**

The sūrah begins with *idhā*, when, and the conditional particle governs the four verses that follow. Nothing in the opening states a consequence; the consequence arrives in verse 5, and even there it is left unexpressed in the Arabic and supplied by the reader. The structure is the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, which opens with four conditions and answers them in the fifth verse, and in Sūrat al-Takwīr, which builds thirteen conditions before reaching its answer. This sūrah uses four conditions and an implied answer, and the implication means that the reader's own participation is required to complete the sentence. The opening therefore places the listener inside the grammar of the chapter before telling him anything.

**Inshaqqat**

The verb is the fifth form of *sh-q-q*, to split, cleave, and burst open. The fifth form is intransitive and intensive: the sky splits itself, or is split thoroughly, and the splitting is complete. The root differs from the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār — *f-ṭ-r*, which gave *infaṭarat*, the sūrah's title — but the event described is the same. Two adjacent chapters open with the rupture of the sky, each using a different verb from a different root, and the doubling is characteristic of the Makkan descriptions of the end. The fact is fixed; the word varies. A reader who encounters the two chapters in sequence hears the same event twice in two vocabularies, and the repetition removes any suggestion that the description is figurative.

**Naming of the Sūrah**

Al-Inshiqāq, the Splitting Asunder, is taken from this verb, and the naming makes the rupture the chapter's identity. The title is a noun of the same root in the same form, so that the sūrah is named for what happens in its first word. Nothing else in the chapter is commemorated in the title — not the record in the right hand, not the oaths by twilight and moon, not the stages of verse 19. The selection of the sky's bursting as the chapter's name indicates what the revelation considered its defining image, and the image is the removal of the roof of the world. A chapter named for a rupture is a chapter about the failure of a boundary, and the failure is what the following verses describe in the earth as well as in the sky.

**Sky as a Boundary**

Throughout the Qur'an the sky is a structure: raised, roofed, adorned, protected, held from falling. It is described as a canopy over the earth and as a building whose raising is among the signs. The bursting is the failure of the structure in the most basic sense — a roof that no longer holds. Nothing in this verse describes what comes through the opening or what the pieces become, and the withholding is consistent with the chapter's method. Sūrat al-Infiṭār names the same silence, describing only that the sky splits and the stars scatter. The absence of an account of what follows the rupture means that the verse is describing the end of a limit rather than the beginning of an event, and the end of a limit is what makes everything that follows possible.

**Four Conditions, Two Subjects**

The four *idhā* clauses of this chapter are divided between two subjects: the sky in verses 1 and 2, and the earth in verses 3, 4, and 5. The division is even and the pairing is complete. Nothing in the created order above or below is left outside the description, and the two subjects each receive the same closing statement — obeying its Lord, as it must. The symmetry is what makes the implied answer of verse 5 general rather than particular. A change confined to the sky would leave the earth as a refuge; a change confined to the earth would leave the sky as an escape. Both are described, and the description of both is what produces the summons of verse 6, addressed to a humanity that has nowhere to stand while it listens.

**Absent Apodosis**

A conditional construction in Arabic requires an answer, and this one's answer is not given. The commentators note that the apodosis is deliberately omitted so that the reader supplies it, and that the supplied answer is the judgement announced in the bracketed gloss of the translation: surely you will all be judged. Other readings take the answer from verse 6 or from verse 19, where humanity is told that it will pass from stage to stage. The omission is therefore not a defect but a device — it makes the chapter's central claim something the listener states to himself rather than something he hears. A conclusion the listener supplies is held more firmly than one delivered to him, and the whole of the chapter's first movement is designed to produce that supplying.

**The Rupture and the Reader**

Nothing in the verse describes anyone watching the sky burst, and the absence of a witness is what makes the description immediate. The reader is not placed at a distance from the event as an observer of something happening to others; he is placed under it. The chapter's first word is *when*, and the *when* includes whoever hears it. The same technique governs the whole of the opening movement — the sky opens, the earth is flattened, the contents are cast out, and no one is described as surviving the description from outside it. The listener's position is inside the event, and the position is what makes the address of verse 6 land where it does.

**First Word of the Argument**

Everything that follows in this sūrah depends on the rupture described here. The record in the right hand and the record behind the back presuppose a Day; the Day presupposes the end of the present order; the end of the present order is the bursting of the sky and the flattening of the earth. The chapter therefore begins at the point of maximum scale and descends from it — from the sky to the earth, from the earth to humanity, from humanity to two classes, from the two classes to the oaths and the stages, and finally to belief and disbelief. The descent is continuous, and its first step is the opening of the roof. Nothing in the verse argues that the sky will burst; it states when, and the when is the whole of its content.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:2

> obeying its Lord as it must

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Statement About the Sky's Manner**

Verse 1 stated an event; this verse states how the event happens. The sky bursts open obeying its Lord, and the obedience is described with two elements — a verb of listening and a passive verb of fittingness. Nothing in the chapter explains why the manner matters, and the explanation is available from the structure: a rupture that occurs in obedience is not a catastrophe but an act of compliance. The sky is not failing; it is doing what it has been told. The distinction is what allows the chapter to move from a description of cosmic destruction to a summons to humanity without any change of register, since the destruction has been reclassified as obedience.

**Adhinat**

The verb is from *ʾ-dh-n*, the root of the ear, of hearing, and of permission. Its fourth form denotes giving ear, attending, hearkening, and by extension obeying — the same root gives *ādhana*, to announce, and *udhun*, an ear, and *idhn*, permission. Applied to the sky the verb describes an attention that issues in compliance. The sky has heard its Lord and has responded. Nothing in the verse describes a voice, a command, or a messenger, and the absence of a described command makes the obedience immediate: the sky listens and obeys in one act. The root's association with permission is also present — a thing that gives ear to its Lord has been granted the hearing, and the granting is what makes the response possible.

**Ḥuqqat**

The second verb is the passive of *ḥ-a-q-q*, a root whose field is truth, rightness, fitness, and obligation. *Ḥuqqat* means it was made rightful, it was entitled, it was bound, it was fitting that it should. The passive form removes any agent of the compulsion, and the removal is significant: nothing forces the sky, and nothing obliges it from outside. It was rightful for it to obey, and the rightness is internal to its being a created thing with a Lord. The translation renders the phrase as "as it must", which captures the necessity without attributing it to a compeller. The necessity is one of fitness rather than of force, and the distinction is what makes the obedience in this verse different in kind from the obedience of a subject under threat.

**An Obedience Without Speech**

The Qur'an describes the same compliance of the created order elsewhere in explicit terms. The heaven and the earth are told to submit, willingly or unwillingly, and they both respond that they submit willingly (41:11). Everything in the heavens and the earth prostrates to God, willingly or unwillingly. The sun and the moon and the stars are described as subjected by His command. This verse belongs to that account and states it in the shortest form available: the sky listens to its Lord and it was fitting that it should. Nothing here describes a dialogue, and the absence of dialogue means the obedience is not a response to a request but a property of the thing. The sky obeys in bursting as it obeyed in being raised.

**Its Lord**

The possessive is singular and specific: *li-rabbihā*, to its Lord. The sky has a Lord, and the Lord is the one it obeys. The statement is a claim about the created order rather than about human belief, and it is made in the middle of a description of the end of the world. The placement means that the rupture of the sky is being presented as an act of worship. A thing that bursts open in obedience to its Lord is performing the function for which it was made, and the performance is what the chapter offers as the model against which human conduct will be measured. Verse 6 turns to humanity and describes it as labouring toward its Lord, and the turn is prepared by this verse's statement that the sky already obeys its Lord.

**Stated Twice**

The same sentence appears again in verse 5, attached to the earth. The repetition is exact — the same two verbs, the same possessive, the same phrase of fittingness — and the exactness makes the two statements a formula. The formula is applied to both halves of the created order, and its application to both is what establishes the general claim. Nothing obeys its Lord except that it is fitting for it to obey, and nothing in the description of the sky or the earth deviates from the pattern. The repetition also produces a rhythm across the chapter's opening, in which two events are each closed by the same statement. The rhythm is what makes the implied answer of verse 5 possible: a description in which everything obeys can only lead to a summons addressed to the one thing that can refuse.

**Why the Manner Is Given**

A description of the end of the world could have been given without reference to obedience. The sky splits, the earth is flattened, the contents are cast out, and humanity is judged — the sequence would be complete. The chapter adds the manner, twice, and the addition changes the character of the whole passage. What is described is not a disaster but a compliance, and the compliance is universal except for one exception. The exception is the human being, who is addressed in verse 6 and who is described as labouring toward his Lord rather than as obeying him. The difference between labouring toward and obeying is the difference the chapter's remaining nineteen verses explore, and the exploration is opened by this verse's insistence that the sky does what it must.

**The Created Order as a Witness**

The obedience of the sky and the earth functions in the chapter as testimony against the only creature that can disobey. Nothing in these verses draws the comparison, and the drawing is unnecessary: a reader who has been told that the sky listens to its Lord as it must, and that the earth does the same, has been given the standard. Verse 6 then addresses humanity and describes its toil, and the description is measured against the standard already established. The chapter's argument therefore proceeds by placing the created order first and the human being second, and the ordering is what makes the summons in verse 6 an indictment rather than an invitation. The sky has already answered. The question is being put to the listener.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:3

> and when the earth is flattened out

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Subject**

The chapter's conditions move from the sky to the earth, and the movement completes the description of the created order. Nothing between them is mentioned — no mountains, no seas, no stars — and the omission is deliberate. The two subjects are the container and the contained, the roof and the floor, and the two are sufficient for the argument. The earth's condition is described in three verses where the sky's was described in two, and the additional verse is the one that states the casting out of its contents. The proportion indicates that the chapter is more concerned with what the earth holds than with what covers it, and the concern is what prepares verse 6's address to the people who live on it.

**Muddat**

The verb is from *m-d-d*, to stretch, extend, spread out, and prolong. The passive form here denotes the earth being stretched out, extended, levelled. The commentators explain it as the removal of everything that rises above the surface — the mountains, the buildings, the elevations — so that the earth becomes a single plane. The root's field of extension and duration is not exhausted by the physical sense; it also gives the words for a period of time and for a provision extended. The verse uses the root in its spatial sense, and the sense is exact: what had features has been made flat, and what had limits has been extended to the point where no feature marks it.

**The Earth Changed**

The Qur'an describes the same transformation in several passages and with several images. The earth and the mountains are lifted and crushed with a single blow (69:14). The mountains are wiped out completely, leaving the earth level and bare (20:105–106). They are blown away, becoming like a mirage (78:20). The earth is entirely crushed over and over (89:21). The Day on which the earth will be changed into a different earth, and the heavens as well, is the Day on which all appear before God, the One, the Supreme (14:48). The five descriptions agree on the removal of elevation and the production of a level surface, and this verse states the same outcome in a single passive verb.

**Flattening as the Removal of Distinction**

The earth's features are what organize human life: mountains mark boundaries, valleys hold water, elevations provide defence, and the variety of terrain produces the variety of settlement. The flattening removes all of it, and the removal is not merely physical but social. A level earth has no high ground and no low ground, no place of refuge and no place of advantage. Nothing in the verse states this consequence, and the stating is unnecessary for a reader who has followed the Qur'an's descriptions of the Day, in which the mountains are levelled, the land made an even plain, and every elevation brought down. The flattening is therefore the end of every position a person could occupy, and the end of position is what makes the standing of the Day universal.

**The Earth's Obedience**

Verse 3 states the event and verse 5 states the manner, with verse 4 between them describing the casting out. The arrangement means that the flattening is bracketed by two statements about what the earth contains and how it obeys. The obedience of the earth is the same as that of the sky — listening to its Lord, and it being fitting that it should — and the sameness is what unites the two descriptions. The chapter's picture of the end is therefore one of universal compliance: the roof opens because it must, the floor levels because it must, and the contents are ejected because the vessel has been emptied by command. Nothing in the description resists, and the absence of resistance is what makes the following address to humanity the chapter's only open question.

**A Plane Without Features**

The image produced by this verse and the next is of an empty level surface. The earth has been flattened and its contents cast out, and the emptying is described as complete — it becomes empty. What remains is a plane, and the plane is the setting for what verse 6 addresses. A humanity described as labouring toward its Lord has no ground to labour on except this one, and the ground has been stripped of everything that made labour meaningful. The chapter therefore removes the world before it summons the person who lived in it, and the removal is what makes the summons unanswerable by appeal to circumstance. There is no circumstance left. There is a level earth, an open sky, and a person who has been toiling and who will meet the result.

**Level Ground and the Standing of the Day**

The Qur'an describes the gathering of the Day as a standing, and a standing requires ground. The flattening of this verse supplies it, and the supplying is what makes the description functional rather than merely pictorial. Mountains removed, elevations levelled, and the surface extended produce a plain on which everyone can be seen and no one can be hidden. Sūrat Ṭā Hā states the same outcome in the language of the questioner and the answerer: the mountains will be wiped out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, so that no curve and no elevation will be seen in it (20:105–107). The absence of curve and elevation is the absence of concealment, and the absence of concealment is what the reckoning of verses 7 to 12 requires.

**The Pair Completed**

Verses 1 and 2 described the sky and its obedience; verses 3 to 5 describe the earth and its obedience. The two pairs are the chapter's first movement, and the movement is complete with the fifth verse. What follows is an address, and the address is possible only because the description has been finished. A chapter that described the sky alone would leave the listener somewhere to stand; a chapter that described both leaves him nowhere. The completeness of the pair is therefore a rhetorical achievement rather than a merely descriptive one, and the achievement is what the implied answer of verse 5 depends on. When everything has been described as obeying, the only thing left to ask about is the one who has not.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:4

> and ejects ˹all˺ its contents and becomes empty

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Acts in One Verse**

The verse describes the earth doing two things: casting out what is in it, and becoming empty. The two are not redundant. Ejection is an act; emptiness is a state. A vessel can be emptied partially and still contain something; a vessel that has cast out its contents and become empty has nothing left. The verse specifies both the act and the resulting condition, and the specification is what makes the description complete. Nothing in the Arabic leaves room for a remainder, and the absence of a remainder is the point. The earth's contents have been expelled and the earth itself has been emptied, and the two statements together exclude the possibility that anything of this world survives the transformation.

**Alqat**

The verb is from *l-q-y*, to throw, cast, hurl, and — in a different form of the same root — to meet. The fourth form is transitive and denotes a forcible expulsion: it threw out, it cast forth. The subject is the earth, and the object is *mā fīhā*, what is in it. The construction makes the earth an agent rather than a location, and the agency is consistent with the obedience described in verse 5: the earth acts, and it acts in compliance. Nothing in the verse describes an external force extracting the contents; the earth casts them out itself, in the same way that it is flattened and in the same way that it listens to its Lord. The agency of the created order is a persistent feature of the Qur'an's descriptions of the Day.

**What Is In It**

*Mā fīhā* is indefinite and comprehensive: whatever is in it. The commentators specify the contents as the dead, who are brought out, and the treasures, which are thrown forth. Sūrat al-Zalzalah describes the same event from the side of the earth's shaking: when the earth is shaken in its ultimate quaking and throws out all its contents (99:1–2). Sūrat al-Infiṭār describes the graves spilling out and being turned inside (82:4). Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt asks whether people do not know that the contents of the graves will be spilled out (100:9). The three passages agree that the contents include the dead, and the inclusion is what makes the ejection an eschatological event rather than a geological one.

**Takhallat**

The second verb is from *kh-l-w*, a root meaning to be empty, vacant, free of, and to pass away. The fifth form here denotes becoming emptied, clearing itself out. The verb's field includes the emptiness of a place after its occupants have left and the vacating of a vessel after its contents have been poured. Its application to the earth describes a total clearance, and the clearance is what distinguishes this verse from the parallel in Sūrat al-Zalzalah, which describes the throwing out without the emptying. The addition of the state to the act is this chapter's contribution to the description, and the contribution is significant: an earth that has thrown out its contents may still be full of holes, ruins, and remains; an earth that has become empty has none.

**The Emptying and the Meeting**

The verse prepares verse 6 in a way that is not stated. Humanity is described there as labouring toward its Lord and as meeting the result. A meeting requires two parties and a place, and the place described in verses 3 and 4 is a flattened, emptied earth. The chapter therefore constructs the setting for the encounter before announcing the encounter, and the construction is done in three verses of physical description. Nothing in those verses mentions people, judgement, or meeting, and the absence of mention is what makes verse 6's arrival a turn rather than a continuation. The reader has been shown an empty world, and he is then told that he is walking toward his Lord inside it.

**The Contents Cast Out and the Deeds Kept**

There is a contrast available between what the earth ejects and what the record preserves. The earth throws out everything it holds — the dead, the treasures, the structures — and becomes empty of all of it. Nothing material survives the emptying. The deeds, however, are in a book, and the book is described elsewhere as sealed, laid open, and given into a hand. The contrast is not drawn in this chapter, but the chapter's structure supplies it: verse 4 describes the total loss of the world's contents and verses 7 to 12 describe the total retention of the record. What is cast out is what a person had; what is kept is what a person did. The two verses are eleven apart, and the distance between them is the whole of the chapter's argument.

**An Earth That Holds Nothing**

The verse's two verbs produce a single result: a world without contents. The dead have come out, the treasures have been thrown forth, and the vessel is clear. Nothing in the chapter describes what becomes of the ejected contents, and the silence is consistent with the descriptions elsewhere — the people come forth from the graves swiftly, as if racing, and they are summoned to the standing. What is cast out is therefore not discarded but summoned, and the summoning is what verse 6 addresses. The emptied earth is not an end but a clearing, and the clearing is what allows the toil of humanity to reach its terminus without obstruction. Every structure that could have been relied on has been removed, and the person is left with his labour and his book.

**The Sequence Within the Conditions**

Verse 3 flattened the earth; this verse empties it; verse 5 states its obedience. The sequence is physical before it is theological, and the ordering means that the obedience is attributed to a completed act. The earth has been flattened and emptied, and it did so listening to its Lord and because it was fitting. Nothing in the verse describes reluctance, resistance, or delay, and the absence of all three is consistent with the chapter's account of the created order. The sky opens when it must; the earth levels and empties when it must. The compliance of both is stated in the same formula, and the formula's second occurrence in verse 5 closes a description that has removed everything a human being could stand on or possess.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:5

> obeying its Lord as it must, ˹surely you will all be judged˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Formula Repeated**

The sentence is identical to verse 2, word for word: it listened to its Lord, and it was fitting that it should. The repetition closes the description of the earth exactly as the earlier verse closed the description of the sky, and the closure produces a symmetrical first movement. Two subjects, two events, two identical statements of compliance. The symmetry is what allows the chapter to end its description here and turn to its address. Nothing further is said about the sky or the earth in the remaining twenty verses, and the reason nothing needs to be said is that both have been described as obeying. The formula's second occurrence is therefore a completion rather than an emphasis, and the completion is what frees the chapter to speak to people.

**Answer That Is Not Given**

A conditional construction requires an apodosis, and this one's is not expressed in the Arabic. Four *idhā* clauses have been given and no consequence has been stated. The commentators supply one, and the translation renders the supplied answer in brackets: surely you will all be judged. Other readings take the answer from verse 6, where humanity is addressed, or from verse 19, where it is told that it will pass from stage to stage. The omission is deliberate and its effect is precise. A listener who hears four conditions and no consequence is left holding an unfinished sentence, and the only way to finish it is to supply the consequence himself. The chapter's central claim is therefore made by the reader rather than to him, and the making is what gives it its force.

**Why the Answer Is Withheld**

Withholding the apodosis is not the same as withholding the information. The chapter states the judgement plainly in later verses — the record given in the right hand, the easy reckoning, the record behind the back, the cry for destruction, the burning. What is withheld at the opening is the grammatical connection between the cosmic events and the human outcome. The connection is left for the listener to establish, and the establishing is an act of assent. A person who supplies the answer to a conditional sentence has committed himself to it in a way that a person who hears it stated has not. The technique is one of the Qur'an's characteristic methods in the short Makkan chapters, and its use here at the hinge of the chapter's first movement makes it the structural centre of the argument.

**Bracketed Gloss**

The translation supplies the answer in brackets, and the bracketing is a faithful representation of the Arabic. What is bracketed is not in the text; it is what the text requires. The practice preserves the omission while making the sentence intelligible to a reader who cannot supply it from the grammar. The bracketed clause states a general judgement of all people, and the generality matches the universality of the description that precedes it — the sky and the earth, both obeying, nothing left standing. A judgement addressed to some people would not correspond to a transformation of everything; a judgement addressed to all does. The supplied answer is therefore not an arbitrary completion but the only one the description permits.

**Parallel in Sūrat al-Infiṭār**

The neighbouring chapter performs the same movement with an expressed answer. Four conditions — the sky splitting, the stars scattering, the seas bursting, the graves spilling out — are followed by the statement that each soul will know what it sent forward and left behind (82:1–5). The answer there is given; here it is withheld. The two chapters are therefore variants of one form, and the variation is instructive. Al-Infiṭār's answer is epistemic — the soul will know — while this chapter's supplied answer is judicial, that all will be judged. Both answers concern the individual person rather than the cosmos, and both make the cosmic events the occasion of a personal outcome. The correspondence between the two chapters is one of the clearest in the received order.

**Obedience as the Ground of Judgement**

The verse states the earth's compliance immediately before the chapter turns to humanity, and the placement makes the compliance the ground of what follows. If the created order obeys its Lord as it must, then the ordering of that creation toward a purpose is established, and a purpose implies a reckoning. Nothing in the verse argues this, and the argument is unnecessary. The listener has been shown a sky that opens on command and an earth that levels and empties on command, and the showing establishes that the world is not autonomous. A world that is not autonomous is a world that answers to someone, and the someone is named in the next verse as the Lord toward whom humanity is labouring.

**What the Supplied Answer Requires**

Any answer the listener supplies to the four conditions must have three features: it must concern people, it must follow the transformation of everything, and it must be final. The description leaves no room for an answer about the sky or the earth, since both have already been accounted for as obeying. Nothing remains to be said about them. What remains is the one thing the description has not mentioned — the beings who live between the opened sky and the emptied earth — and the listener's supplied answer necessarily concerns them. The withholding of the apodosis is therefore not an open invitation but a constrained one, and the constraint is what makes the supplied answer reliable.

**Turning to the Listener**

Verse 6 begins with a vocative: O humanity. The turn from description to address is unmarked by any connective, and the absence of a connective makes it abrupt. The chapter has spent five verses describing the end of the world and then speaks directly to everyone in it. The abruptness is characteristic of the Makkan style, which moves between registers without transition and expects the listener to feel the change of direction. The turn is also the completion of the withheld apodosis: what the listener was expected to supply is now addressed to him. He is not told that he will be judged; he is told that he is labouring toward his Lord and will meet the result. The second formulation is more intimate than the first, and the intimacy is what the chapter's second movement requires.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:6

> O humanity! Indeed, you are labouring restlessly towards your Lord, and will ˹eventually˺ meet the consequences

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address to the Species**

*Yā ayyuhā al-insān* — O human being. The vocative is addressed to the species rather than to a group, and it is the same address used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where humanity is asked what has emboldened it against its Lord (82:6). The two chapters are adjacent in the received order and both turn from a description of the sky's rupture to an address of this kind. The universality of the vocative excludes nobody: not the believer, not the disbeliever, not the Prophet, not the reader. Whatever follows is said to everyone, and the saying is what makes the chapter's second movement a summons rather than a report. Nothing in the address restricts its scope, and the unrestricted scope means that the description of labouring applies to whoever hears it.

**Kādiḥ**

The word is an active participle from *k-d-ḥ*, a root denoting strenuous labour, striving to the point of exhaustion, and the weariness that follows. The verb is used of a person who works hard for his livelihood, who strains at a task, who wears himself out. Its application here describes the human condition as one of continuous effort, and the effort is not qualified as good or bad. The verse does not say that humanity labours at anything in particular; it says that humanity labours toward its Lord. The toil is the constant, and its direction is what the verse supplies. Nothing in the word implies that the labour is chosen, and the absence of choice is what makes the description universal — every person is toiling, and the toiling has a destination.

**Toward Your Lord**

*Ilā rabbika* — toward your Lord. The preposition denotes direction and terminus, and the terminus is not a place but a person. The labour is not toward wealth, old age, death, or a goal of the labourer's choosing; it is toward the Lord. The statement reinterprets every human effort as movement in a direction that the efforter did not select. The possessive *your* is singular, matching the singular vocative, and the singular possessive makes the destination personal. Nothing in the verse says that the person is aware of the direction, and the unawareness is implied by the description of the toil as restless and continuous. A person who knew he was walking toward his Lord would not labour in the way described here.

**Meeting**

*Fa-mulāqīh* — and you will meet it, or meet Him. The word is from *l-q-y*, the same root used in the previous verse for the earth casting out its contents, and its fourth form denotes encountering, meeting, and facing. The noun of the root here is one who meets, and the object is a pronoun that can refer either to the Lord or to the labour. Both readings are transmitted and both are coherent. On the first, the person will meet his Lord; on the second, he will meet the result of his toil. The translation adopts the second and glosses it as meeting the consequences. The two readings are not alternatives in substance, since meeting the Lord is meeting the result of one's labour, and the ambiguity allows the verse to carry both at once.

**The Double Reference of the Object**

The commentators record both readings without preferring one decisively, and the undecided reference is a feature of the verse. A person who meets his Lord meets whatever his labour produced, and the two meetings are one event seen from two sides. The Qur'an describes the same meeting elsewhere in the language of return: to God is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to do. The informing and the meeting are the same occasion. This verse states the occasion in the shortest form — a toil, a direction, and an encounter — and the shortness is what makes it applicable to every reader without exception. Nothing in the verse distinguishes the labour of the believer from that of the disbeliever, and the distinction arrives in the verses that follow.

**The Human Condition as Toil**

The description is not a rebuke in itself. To labour strenuously toward a terminus is what a human being does, and the verse says so without condemnation. The condemnation in the following verses is directed at particular responses to the toil — the one who was joyful among his people, the one who thought he would never return, those who do not believe and do not bow when the Qur'an is recited. The toil is common to all of them and to the believers as well. What differs is not the labouring but what a person supposes about its end, and the supposition is what verse 14 identifies as the fault: he thought he would never return. The verse under discussion establishes the common condition so that the differing suppositions can be judged against it.

**The Verse as the Chapter's Hinge**

Five verses of cosmic description precede this one and nineteen verses of personal consequence follow it. The verse is the hinge, and the hinge is an address. The chapter moves from what happens to the world to what happens to a person, and the movement is made by speaking to the person directly. Nothing in the preceding verses mentioned humanity, and nothing in the following verses returns to the sky or the earth. The address therefore performs the whole of the transition, and it performs it in a single sentence: you are labouring toward your Lord and you will meet. The sentence is the chapter's thesis, and everything after it is the working out of the meeting in two classes, four oaths, and a summons to believe.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:7

> As for those who are given their record in their right hand

**Expanded Commentary**

**Division Begins**

Verse 6 addressed humanity as a whole and stated that everyone is labouring toward his Lord and will meet. This verse begins the division of that whole into two classes, and the division is introduced by the particle *fa-ammā*, as for — a topic marker that announces a case to be described. The second case follows in verse 10, and the two together exhaust the address of verse 6. Nothing in the chapter allows for a third class, and the absence of a third is consistent with its structure: a single toil, a single meeting, and two manners of receiving what the meeting produces. The verse under discussion states the first manner in a single clause and leaves its consequences for the two verses that follow.

**Record Given**

The verb is passive: *ūtiya kitābahu*, he was given his book. The passivity is significant and it matches the description of Paradise earlier in the neighbouring chapters, where the drink is given rather than obtained. Nothing in the verse describes the person reaching for the record, choosing it, or earning its delivery. It is handed to him, and the hand that receives it is specified. The record itself is the document described elsewhere as produced by noble angels who write and know whatever people do (82:10–12), as sealed in Sijjīn and in ʿIlliyūn (83:9, 83:20), and as laid open on the Day with the command to read it (17:13–14). This verse describes the moment of delivery and specifies the manner.

**Right Hand**

*Bi-yamīnih*, in his right hand. The right hand in Arabic usage is the hand of giving, of oaths, of honour, and of strength; the left is the hand of what is unclean or of secondary use. The specification of the right is therefore not arbitrary but carries the sense of a favourable reception. Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah describes the same moment and adds the recipient's cry: those given their records in their right hand call out for everyone to read their record, certain that they would face their reckoning (69:19–20). Sūrat al-Isrāʾ adds that whoever is given his record in his right hand will read it happily and will not be wronged by the width of a date-stone's thread (17:71). The three passages agree on the hand, the reading, and the joy.

**A Class Defined by What It Receives**

Nothing in this verse describes the people concerned by their conduct, their belief, or their character. They are defined solely by what is put into their hand. The definition is remarkable because it makes the outcome prior to the description, and the chapter then supplies the character of the outcome — an easy reckoning and a joyful return — without ever supplying the character of the people. The technique places the reader's attention on the delivery rather than on the recipient, and the placement is what allows the summons at the end of the chapter to apply to everyone listening. No one is excluded from the first class by a description of its members, because no description is given.

**Bracketed Left Hand**

The second case, in verse 10, contains a bracketed phrase in the translation: those who are given their record in their left hand from behind their backs. The bracket marks words understood rather than expressed, since the Arabic of that verse specifies only the behind-the-back delivery. The bracketing in verse 10 corresponds to the explicit right hand here, and the correspondence establishes the two manners of receipt as a matched pair. The reader is given the hand in one case and the position in the other, and the two details together produce the complete contrast: an honourable reception in front, and a shameful one from behind. Nothing in this verse anticipates the second manner, and the anticipation is unnecessary.

**Verse Continued in the Next**

The clause begun here is completed in verses 8 and 9, which give the easy reckoning and the joyful return. The three verses form a single statement about the first class, and the statement is symmetrical with the three verses that describe the second: the record behind the back, the cry for destruction, and the burning. Two cases of three verses each, following a single verse of address and preceding a verse of explanation about the second class's character. The symmetry is the chapter's architecture in its second movement, and the architecture means that neither class is described at greater length than the other. The balance of the description is what makes the difference between the two outcomes unmistakable.

**The Hand and the Honour**

Arab usage associates the right hand with what is given and received in honour, and the association is what makes the specification meaningful without explanation. A document taken in the right hand is taken as a thing of value; a document taken from behind the back is taken as a thing of shame. The chapter relies on the association and does not state it, and the reliance is possible because the association was common to the audience. Nothing in the verse describes the person's reaction to the delivery, and the reaction is given in the two verses that follow — an easy reckoning and a joyful return. The delivery itself is stated in six words, and the six words are sufficient because the hand carries the meaning.

**Meeting Realized**

Verse 6 stated that every person will meet — his Lord, or the result of his labour. This verse realizes the meeting for the first class in the form of a delivery. The meeting is not an encounter in a place but a receipt of a document, and the receipt is what makes the meeting concrete. Nothing in the verse describes a dialogue, a questioning, or a judgement pronounced from a throne. The person is given his book in his right hand, and the giving is the meeting. The economy of the description is consistent with the chapter's method throughout: state the fact in the fewest words, specify what is needed to make it intelligible, and leave the rest to the passages that describe the same scene at greater length.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:8

> they will have an easy reckoning

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Easy Reckoning**

*Ḥisāban yasīran* — a reckoning that is easy, gentle, light. The noun is from *ḥ-s-b*, the root of counting, calculating, and taking account, and it is the Qur'an's standard term for the reckoning of the Day. The adjective is from *y-s-r*, the root of ease, facility, and what is gentle rather than severe. The same root gives the Qur'anic statement that God intends ease for people and does not intend hardship, and it gives the verb used of making something easy. Its application to the reckoning specifies not that the reckoning is brief but that it is gentle in manner, and the specification of manner is what the verse's explanation in the tradition supplies.

**The Explanation Given in the Ḥadīth**

ʿĀʾishah reported that the Prophet said whoever is called to account will be punished, and she asked whether God had not said that he will receive an easy reckoning, citing this verse. He replied that this means only the presentation of the accounts, and that whoever is argued with about his account will be ruined (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 103). The explanation distinguishes two kinds of reckoning: a presentation, in which the deeds are shown and acknowledged, and a discussion, in which each item is examined and contested. The easy reckoning of this verse is the first, and the ruin belongs to the second. Nothing in the verse itself makes the distinction, and the distinction is what the report supplies — the ease is not in the quantity of what is counted but in the absence of argument about it.

**The Presentation of Accounts**

The notion of a presentation is consistent with the chapter's description of the record. A book is given in the right hand, and the person is told elsewhere to read his own record, being sufficient to take account of himself (17:14). The reading is the presentation: what was written is shown to the one who did it, and the showing is complete. Nothing in the presentation is hidden from the reader, and nothing in it is disputed by him. The ease consists in the absence of dispute rather than in the absence of content, and the absence of dispute is what distinguishes the first class from the second. A person whose record is argued with him is a person whose deeds are contested, and the contest is what produces ruin.

**The Contrast With What Follows**

Verses 10 to 12 describe the second class: the record given from behind the back, the cry for destruction, the burning in a blazing fire. The three verses contain no mention of a reckoning at all, and the omission is significant. The first class receives an easy reckoning; the second receives no description of one. What the second class receives is a document delivered shamefully and a fire entered immediately. The asymmetry suggests that the reckoning described here is a mercy granted to one group rather than a stage passed through by everyone, and the suggestion is confirmed by the report of ʿĀʾishah, in which the argument about the account is the ruin. The chapter therefore distinguishes not two kinds of reckoning but a reckoning and its absence.

**Ease as a Divine Attribute Applied**

The root of ease is used throughout the Qur'an of God's manner of dealing with people: He intends ease and not hardship; He has made the Qur'an easy to remember; He will make the easy path easy for the one who is given what is good. The application of the same root to the reckoning places the gentleness of the Day inside the character of the One who conducts it. Nothing in this verse says that the ease is earned, and the absence of the statement is consistent with the chapter's description of the record as given rather than obtained. The easy reckoning is a manner of being dealt with, and the manner belongs to the One described elsewhere as the Most Generous and as the Lord toward whom humanity is labouring.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that the deeds are few, that the faults are forgiven, or that the record is short. It says that the reckoning is easy, and the easiness is a property of the process rather than of the material. A person with a long record can be reckoned easily if nothing is contested, and a person with a short record can be ruined if every item is argued. The chapter's interest is therefore in the manner of the encounter rather than in the quantity of the deeds, and the interest is what makes the verse applicable to anyone. Nothing in the chapter suggests that the first class consists of people who did nothing wrong; the report supplies the opposite, since the presentation shows whatever is in the book without dispute.

**Reckoning as an Encounter Rather Than a Calculation**

The word *ḥisāb* denotes counting, and the counting described in the Qur'an's accounts of the Day is not arithmetic but encounter. A person is shown what he did and is required to acknowledge it, and the acknowledgement is the reckoning. The ease specified here is therefore an ease of encounter: nothing is withheld, nothing is contested, and nothing is argued. The person sees his deeds and the seeing is the whole of the process. Nothing in the verse describes a judge weighing, a scale tipping, or a sentence being pronounced, and the absence of these elements is what distinguishes the Qur'an's account from a courtroom scene. The reckoning is a disclosure, and its easiness consists in the disclosure being received without dispute.

**The Second of Three Blessings**

Verses 7, 8, and 9 describe the first class in three clauses: the record in the right hand, the easy reckoning, and the joyful return to his people. The three are distinct and they are sequential — a delivery, an accounting, and a homecoming. The verse under discussion is the middle term, and its position means that the reckoning is what stands between receiving the document and arriving at the destination. Nothing in the chapter describes the interval, and the absence of description allows the ease to be understood as the character of the interval itself. A person who passes through a gentle accounting arrives joyful, and the arrival is described in the next verse in a single word.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:9

> and will return to their people joyfully

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return**

*Yanqalibu* is from *q-l-b*, the root of turning over, inverting, and changing direction. Its eighth form denotes turning back, returning, and being transformed. The verse describes the person of the right hand turning back toward his people, and the turning is a movement with a destination. The same verb appears in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn for the mockers returning to their own households to recount their exploits with relish (83:31). The two returns are described with the same root and in opposite moral directions: one turns back to a household for the pleasure of derision, the other turns back to a people in joy. The recurrence of the verb across two adjacent chapters is one of the quietest contrasts in the received order.

**To His People**

*Ilā ahlihī* — to his people, his family, his household. The word *ahl* denotes those who belong to a person by kinship, marriage, or shared affiliation, and it is used in the Qur'an for a family, for the people of a town, and for the adherents of a religion. Its application here has been read in two ways. Some take it as the person's family who are also in Paradise, so that the return is a reunion; others take it as the people of his rank and company among the blessed, so that the return is an arrival among his own. Both readings agree that the destination is a company rather than a place, and the agreement is what makes the verse a statement about belonging rather than about location.

**Masrūr**

The word denotes one who is pleased, delighted, gladdened — a joy that is visible and complete. The same root gives *surūr*, delight, and it is used in the Qur'an for the state of the people of Paradise: faces on that Day will be bright, looking at their Lord (75:22–23), and the people of Paradise will be in joy and delight. The adjective here is singular, describing the person who returns, and the singularity is grammatically notable after the plural subject of verse 7. The construction treats the class as an individual: whoever is given his record in his right hand will return to his people joyful. The movement from plural to singular is the chapter's characteristic way of making a general statement personal, and it is the same movement made in verse 6's address.

**A Homecoming Rather Than an Arrival**

The verse describes the end of the journey announced in verse 6. Humanity is labouring toward its Lord, and the labour has a terminus; the terminus for the first class is a return to a company in a state of joy. The language of return rather than arrival is significant. A person who arrives somewhere has never been there; a person who returns has come back. The blessed are described as returning, and the returning implies that the company they rejoin is one they belonged to before. The Qur'an's account of the human being's origin supports the implication: a person came from God and returns to Him, and the return to his people is a return within that larger movement. Nothing in the verse elaborates the point, and the elaboration is unnecessary.

**The Contrast With the Second Class**

Verses 10 to 12 describe the other class receiving a document from behind the back, crying for destruction, and burning in a blazing fire. The contrast between the two descriptions is exact in form and opposite in content: three clauses each, in the same order of receipt, response, and destination. The first class receives honourably, is reckoned gently, and returns in joy; the second receives shamefully, cries for ruin, and enters fire. Nothing in the chapter compares them explicitly, and the comparison is produced by the parallel structure. The verse under discussion supplies the third term of the first case, and the third term of the second case is the fire. A homecoming against an entering.

**Joy and the People Who Mocked**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn describes the wicked as joyful among their people in this world — *masrūr*, the same word used here — and the chapter states that their joy was the joy of people who thought they would never return to God (83:13–14). The two uses of the word across adjacent chapters are the sharpest contrast available. One joy is described as belonging to a household of mockers in this life and as resting on the supposition that there is no return; the other is described as belonging to a household of the blessed and as following a return that has happened. The verse under discussion therefore contains, in one adjective, the answer to the supposition named in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn. There is a return, and it produces joy.

**Joy Made Visible**

The adjective describes an inner state, and the chapter's method elsewhere is to make inner states visible — the glow of delight recognized on faces, the humiliated faces of that Day, the cry uttered aloud. The joy of this verse is stated rather than shown, and the stating is possible because the return supplies the showing. A person returning to his people is a person whose arrival will be seen, and the seeing is what makes the joy public. Nothing in the verse describes the reception he receives, and the absence of description leaves the homecoming open. What is fixed is the state of the one who returns and the fact that he returns to a company rather than to a place.

**The Completion of the First Case**

After this verse the chapter turns to the second class and does not return to the first until the closing verse, where those who believe and do good are given a never-ending reward. The first case is therefore described in three verses and closed, and the closing is a state: the person has returned to his people and he is joyful. Nothing further is asked of him, and nothing further is described about him. The completeness of the case is what allows the chapter to move on to the explanation of the second class's character in verses 13 to 15, and then to the oaths and the stages, and finally to the question about belief. The joyful return is the chapter's picture of what the toil of verse 6 ends in for one group, and the picture is given in six words.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:10

> And as for those who are given their record ˹in their left hand˺ from behind their backs

**Expanded Commentary**

**Second Case**

The verse opens with the same topic marker used in verse 7 — *wa-ammā*, and as for — and the repetition establishes the two cases as a matched pair. Nothing between them intervenes except the two clauses describing the first, and the proximity means that the reader holds both manners of receipt in mind at once. The second case is described in the same three-verse pattern as the first: the delivery of the record, the response of the recipient, and the destination. The symmetry is exact, and the exactness is what makes the difference between the two descriptions severe rather than merely contrasted. Both classes receive the same document; what differs is how it is handed over and what follows.

**Left Hand**

The Arabic of this verse specifies the position — from behind his back — and leaves the hand to be understood, which the translation supplies in brackets. The understood left hand is required by the contrast with verse 7 and is confirmed by the parallel description in Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah, where the one given his record in his left hand cries out that he wishes he had not been given it and had not known his reckoning (69:25–26). The bracketing therefore marks a genuine ellipsis rather than an interpretive addition. The Qur'an's descriptions of the two classes consistently pair the right hand with the left, and the pairing is what makes the delivery in this verse intelligible without the word being spoken.

**From Behind the Back**

*Min warāʾi ẓahrihī* — from behind his back. The phrase is the verse's distinctive contribution, and no other description of the Day's records contains it. Its sense is twofold. The first is practical: a document received behind the back is received without being seen, and the receiving requires the hands to be brought around or the book to be taken in an unnatural posture. The second is social: in Arabic usage, to be given something behind the back is to be treated with contempt, and the phrase carries the sense of a thing disposed of rather than presented. The commentators take both senses, and the combination describes a delivery that is at once awkward and demeaning. Nothing in the verse explains the demeaning, and the explanation is unnecessary for a reader who has heard verse 7.

**Same Document**

The record given behind the back is the same kind of document as the one given in the right hand. Both were written by the keepers described in Sūrat al-Infiṭār — noble angels who record everything and know whatever people do (82:10–12). Both are sealed, as the two books of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn are sealed (83:9, 83:20). Both are produced on the Day and read by their owners. Nothing distinguishes the two documents except what is written in them and the manner in which they are delivered. The chapter therefore locates the whole of the difference in the conduct that filled the book and in the honour or shame of the handing over, and neither of these is a matter of the document's nature.

**Shame as a Feature of the Scene**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Day make shame visible. Faces on that Day will be humiliated; the wicked will see their record and cry out at what is in it (18:49); the one who receives his book in his left hand will wish he had not received it and that his death had been the end (69:25–27). The shame is not an inner state described by a narrator but a public condition shown in faces, cries, and postures. This verse contributes the posture: a person taking his document from behind his back. The posture is visible to everyone present, and the visibility is what makes it a feature of the scene rather than a private experience. The chapter's interest in what can be seen is consistent throughout its second movement.

**Verse Continued in the Next**

The clause begun here is completed in verses 11 and 12, which describe the cry for destruction and the burning in a blazing fire. The three verses form the second case and are followed by three verses of explanation — that they were prideful among their people, that they thought they would never return, and that their Lord was always seeing them. The explanation is attached to the second case only, and the attachment is significant. The chapter does not explain why the first class receives its record in the right hand; it explains why the second class receives its record behind the back. The explanation is a diagnosis of a supposition, and the diagnosis is what the chapter's remaining verses develop into a summons.

**Behind the Back and Before the Face**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Day repeatedly contrast what is seen and what is hidden, and this verse places the document of the second class in the hidden position. The first class reads its record and is told to read it; the second receives its record where it cannot be read at the moment of receipt. The distinction is between disclosure and concealment at the point of delivery, and it corresponds to the distinction made elsewhere between the record laid open and the person who wishes he had never been given it. Nothing in the verse describes the person turning the document around to read it, and the absence of a described reading is what makes the following cry intelligible.

**Two Manners of Receipt**

Set side by side, the two deliveries describe a single distinction: honour and shame. A record given into the right hand is presented; a record given from behind the back is disposed of. Nothing in the chapter says that the difference reflects a difference in the severity of the reckoning, and the report of ʿĀʾishah indicates that the reckoning of the second class is not easy but ruinous. The manner of the delivery is therefore a sign of the manner of the accounting, and both are determined by what is in the book. The verse states the sign and leaves the accounting to be inferred, and the inference is completed by the cry in the next verse.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:11

> they will cry for ˹instant˺ destruction

**Expanded Commentary**

**Calling Out**

*Yadʿū* — he calls, he cries out, he invokes. The verb is from *d-ʿ-w*, the root of calling, summoning, praying, and invoking, and it is used throughout the Qur'an for the prayer of a person in need. Its application here is to a call that is not a prayer in any ordinary sense: the person calls for ruin. The verb's range means that the cry is an invocation, and an invocation is addressed to someone. Nothing in the verse names the addressee, and the absence of a named addressee is what makes the cry a pure expression of desire rather than a request expected to be granted. The person wants destruction and he says so, and the saying is the verse's whole content.

**Thubūr**

The word denotes ruin, destruction, and perishing — the state of a thing that is undone. It is used in the Qur'an as the object of this cry in two places, and both describe people inside the Fire. The invocation is therefore not a wish to escape but a wish to cease, and the distinction is important. A person who calls for escape wants a different place; a person who calls for destruction wants an end to being. The verse describes the second, and the description is the chapter's account of what the record behind the back produces. Nothing in the cry asks for mercy, for a second chance, or for a lighter sentence. What is asked for is ruin, and the asking is what the following verse answers by describing the burning.

**Same Cry in al-Furqān**

Sūrat al-Furqān describes the scene in fuller terms. When the Fire sees the deniers from a distance they hear it fuming and growling, and when they are tossed into a narrow place inside it, chained together, they cry out for instant destruction (25:12–13). The cry there is answered: do not cry only once for destruction, but cry many times over (25:14). The answer indicates that the invocation is heard and that what is invoked cannot be granted, and the ungratability is the severity of the scene. This chapter gives the cry without the answer, and the absence of the answer leaves the invocation hanging. The reader is told what the person says and not what is said to him, and the withholding is consistent with the chapter's economy.

**Same Cry in al-Ḥāqqah**

Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah gives the speech of the one who receives his record in his left hand, and the speech is a wish rather than a call: he wishes he had not been given his record, that he had not known his reckoning, and that his death had been the end (69:25–27). The three wishes correspond to the three elements of this chapter's second case — the delivery, the reckoning, and the survival into the Fire. The parallel shows that the cry for destruction and the wish for a finished death are the same utterance in two forms. Nothing in either chapter describes the wish being granted, and the absence of granting is what makes the description of the second class complete: the document is received, the cry is made, and the fire is entered.

**Contrast With the Joyful Return**

Verse 9 described the person of the right hand returning to his people in joy. This verse describes the person of the left hand calling for ruin. The two responses are the second term of the two parallel cases, and their opposition is exact. One man is glad and goes home; the other calls for his own destruction. Nothing in the chapter compares them, and the comparison is produced by the structure. The reader encounters joy at verse 9 and ruin at verse 11, with two verses between them, and the proximity makes the difference unbearable rather than merely informative. The chapter's method throughout its second movement is to place the two cases adjacent so that neither can be considered apart from the other.

**An Invocation That Cannot Be Answered**

The verse describes a person calling for something, and the Qur'an's account of the Day is that certain calls are not answered there. No soul will avail another anything, and all authority on that Day belongs to God (82:19). No intercession benefits except by permission. The cry for destruction belongs to this category: it is uttered, it is heard by whoever is present, and it produces nothing. The futility of the invocation is what distinguishes it from the prayer of this world, which is described elsewhere as answered. The chapter does not state the futility, and the statement is unnecessary, because the following verse describes the burning. A person who is burning and calling for destruction is a person whose call has not been answered, and the non-answer is the description.

**Ruin Invoked by the One Being Ruined**

The cry has a peculiarity worth noting: the person calls for what is already happening. He is inside the fire when he calls for destruction, and the destruction he invokes is not an alternative to his condition but its completion. The invocation therefore expresses a wish for an end rather than a change, and the wish is the last form of agency left to him. Everything else has been taken — the honourable delivery, the gentle reckoning, the return to a company. What remains is a voice, and the voice asks for ruin. Nothing in the verse describes the request being refused, and the refusal is supplied by the following verse, which states that they will burn in a blazing fire.

**Verse Continued in the Next**

The clause begun in verse 10 is completed here and in verse 12, which states that they will burn in a blazing fire. The three verses describe the second case, and the case is closed by the fire. Nothing further happens to this class in the chapter except the explanation of verses 13 to 15, which is a diagnosis of what they were rather than a description of what becomes of them. The explanation turns the chapter's attention from the outcome back to the conduct, and the turning is what prepares the oaths of verses 16 to 18 and the statement of verse 19 that humanity will pass from stage to stage. The cry for destruction is therefore the last event described for the second class, and after it the chapter speaks only of causes.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:12

> and will burn in the blazing Fire

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Term of the Second Case**

Verses 10, 11, and 12 form a single statement about those given their record from behind their backs: the delivery, the cry, and the burning. The three correspond exactly to the three terms of the first case in verses 7 to 9: the delivery in the right hand, the easy reckoning, and the joyful return. The symmetry is complete and it is the chapter's central structural device. What differs is not the number or the order of the terms but their content, and the difference in the third term is the starkest of the three. One class returns to a company in joy; the other burns in a blazing fire. The verse states the second destination in six words and closes the case.

**The Verb of Entering Fire**

*Yaṣlā* is from *ṣ-l-y*, the root used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the wicked burning in the Fire on the Day of Judgement (82:15) and in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn for the same class burning in Hell (83:16). The root denotes exposure to fire, entering it, and undergoing its heat, and its form here is active: they burn. The active voice attributes the undergoing to the subjects rather than to an agent who applies it, and the attribution is consistent across the three chapters. Nothing in any of the verses describes someone casting these people in; each states that they burn, and the stating places them inside the fire as a condition rather than describing an action performed on them.

**Saʿīr**

The name of the fire here is *saʿīr*, a blazing or fiercely kindled fire, from a root meaning to flare, rage, and blaze up. The Qur'an uses the word as one of the names of the Fire and as a description of its state: God has prepared for the disbelievers a blazing Fire (33:64); those who consume the wealth of orphans unjustly consume nothing but fire into their bellies and will be burned in a blazing Hell (4:10). Sūrat al-Takwīr describes the moment of its kindling — when the Hellfire is fiercely flared up (81:12). The recurrence establishes *saʿīr* as a name for the Fire in its state of maximum burning, and its use here specifies the intensity rather than the location or the duration.

**Burning After the Cry**

The order of the two verses is significant. The person calls for destruction and then burns. The cry precedes the fire rather than arising from it, which means the invocation is uttered at the moment of arrival rather than in the middle of the suffering. Nothing in the verse states the order explicitly, and the sequence of the verses supplies it. The effect is to present the burning as the answer to the cry — the person asks for ruin and receives a blazing fire, though the ruin he receives is not the cessation he asked for. The chapter does not describe the request being refused, and the refusal is implied by the continuation of the burning after the cry has been made.

**The Completion of the Two Cases**

After this verse the chapter stops describing outcomes and begins explaining causes. Verses 13 to 15 diagnose the second class — joyful among their people, supposing there would be no return, and seen throughout by their Lord. Verses 16 to 18 swear three oaths, and verse 19 states what humanity will pass through. The description of the two destinations is therefore complete in six verses, and its completeness is what allows the chapter to turn to the person's suppositions. Nothing further will be said about the fire or about the return to a joyful company, and the absence of further description means that both destinations have been given in their final form.

**Fire as the Chapter's Last Image of the Wicked**

The chapter that opened with the sky bursting and the earth emptied closes its account of the second class with fire. The progression is from the largest scale to the smallest: the cosmos transformed, the individual toiling, the record delivered, the cry uttered, and the fire entered. Nothing in the progression is skipped, and nothing in it is elaborated. The economy is what makes the six verses of the two cases so severe. A longer description would have allowed the reader to dwell on one element; six clauses across six verses allow no dwelling at all. The fire is named, and the naming is the end of the matter.

**Fire and the Stages That Follow**

The chapter's third movement swears by the twilight, the night, and the full moon, and answers that humanity will pass from stage to stage. The fire described in this verse is one of those stages, and its position as the last term of the second case means that it is the stage at which the progression stops for this class. Nothing in the chapter describes a further stage beyond it, and the absence of a further stage is what makes the destination final. The progression of verse 19 is therefore not open-ended in every direction: it runs through weakness and strength, through life and death, through the record and the reckoning, and it arrives for one class at a blazing fire and for the other at a joyful return. The two arrivals are the termini of the stages, and this verse names one of them.

**The Fire and the Oaths That Follow**

Verses 16 to 18 swear by the twilight, the night, and the full moon, and the oath is answered in verse 19 by the statement that humanity will pass from stage to stage. The connection between the fire and the oaths is the transition. A blazing fire is a state of maximum intensity, and the twilight is a state of transition from light to dark. The chapter swears by things that change and answers with a statement about change, and the change includes the arrival at the fire described in this verse. Nothing in the oaths refers to the fire, and the absence of reference is what makes the answer of verse 19 general — it applies to everyone labouring toward his Lord, and the fire is one of the stages.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:13

> For they used to be prideful among their people

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Explanation Attached to the Second Case Only**

Verses 13 to 15 explain why the second class receives its record from behind its back, cries for destruction, and burns. No corresponding explanation is given for the first class. The chapter does not say why the record is given in the right hand, why the reckoning is easy, or why the return is joyful. The asymmetry is deliberate: the blessed state is described and left undescribed in its causes, while the ruined state is described and then diagnosed. The diagnosis occupies three verses, and the three verses turn the chapter's attention from outcome to character, which is where its summons operates. A reader cannot choose his outcome directly; he can choose his character, and the character is what the explanation addresses.

**Among His People**

*Fī ahlihī* — among his family, his household, his own people. The phrase is the same one used in verse 9 for the destination of the joyful return, and the recurrence is the chapter's sharpest irony. The person of the right hand returns to his people in joy; the person of the fire was among his people in a state the translation renders as prideful. The two uses of the same phrase describe the same social setting — a man among those who belong to him — and the difference between them is the difference between the two cases. Nothing in the verse says that the household is the cause of the fault, but the household is where the fault was exercised and where it was confirmed.

**The Word of Ease**

The Arabic word is *masrūr*, from the root of delight and pleasure, and it denotes a person who is pleased, gladdened, and at ease. The translation renders it as prideful, which carries the sense the context requires: a man at ease among his own, satisfied with his position, untroubled by anything beyond it. The commentators explain the word here as denoting a person who lived carefree and self-satisfied, taking pleasure in his circumstances without reckoning on what lies beyond them. The ease is therefore not joy in a good thing but the absence of concern, and the absence of concern is what the following verse identifies as a supposition about return.

**Pride as Security**

The state described is one of security rather than of arrogance in the ordinary sense. A man who is at ease among his people is a man who considers himself established — his position recognized, his company assured, his standing unquestioned. The security is social before it is personal, and the social character is what the phrase *among his people* supplies. Nothing in the verse describes cruelty, oppression, or violence. The fault named is a settled comfort in a position that the person takes to be permanent. The permanence is the supposition, and the supposition is what the next verse states explicitly. This verse describes the state in which the supposition is held, and the state is ordinary enough that any reader can recognize it.

**The Contrast With the Joyful Return**

Verse 9 describes a joy that follows a reckoning; this verse describes an ease that precedes a reckoning never expected. The two are distinguished by their relation to the Day rather than by their quality. A joy that comes after an easy accounting is a joy whose ground has been established; an ease that rests on the absence of any expectation of accounting is an ease whose ground is missing. The chapter places the two four verses apart and does not compare them, and the comparison is left to the reader. The absence of comparison is what makes the contrast effective: neither state is called good or bad in these verses, and the assessment is carried by the outcomes already described in verses 7 to 12.

**The Household as the Place of Confirmation**

A man among his people is a man whose self-assessment is echoed back to him. The household confirms his position, shares his assumptions, and does not contradict his suppositions. The Qur'an describes the same mechanism in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, where the mockers return to their own people and recount their exploits with relish (83:31). The recollection at home is a rehearsal of a shared view, and the rehearsal is what fixes it. Nothing in this verse describes the household as wicked, and the description is unnecessary. The fault named is the ease of a man among people who do not challenge him, and the absence of challenge is what allows the supposition of verse 14 to remain unexamined.

**A Diagnosis That Names No Sins**

The explanation of the second class contains no list of offences. There is no mention of fraud, of violence, of idolatry, of persecution, or of any particular transgression. What is named is a disposition — a man at ease among his own — and a supposition about the future. The absence of a list is what makes the diagnosis applicable beyond the chapter's original audience. A reader who has never defrauded anyone and never persecuted a believer can still recognize the state described here, and the recognition is the verse's purpose. Nothing in the chapter restricts the diagnosis to a historical group, and the unrestricted application means that the three verses of explanation function as a mirror rather than as an indictment of people who are absent.

**Why the Explanation Comes After the Fire**

The order of the chapter is outcome first, then character. The reader has already seen the record behind the back, the cry for destruction, and the burning before he is told what produced them. The ordering means that the diagnosis is received as an explanation of something already known rather than as a prediction of something to come. The technique is the same one used in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, where the covering of the heart is described after the dismissal has been quoted. The chapter shows before it explains, and the showing is what makes the explanation credible. A reader who has seen the fire is prepared to hear that the man inside it was at ease among his people, and the preparation is what makes the connection intelligible.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:14

> thinking they would never return ˹to Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Supposition Named**

Verse 13 described a state — a man at ease among his people. This verse names the thought that made the state possible: he supposed that he would never return. The supposition is the chapter's diagnosis of the second class, and the diagnosis is not about conduct but about expectation. Nothing in the verse accuses these people of specific sins; it accuses them of a belief about the future. The belief is negative — that a return will not happen — and its negativity is what allows the ease of the previous verse to continue unexamined. A man who expects no return has no reason to look at what he is doing, and the absence of looking is the whole of the fault.

**Ẓanna**

The verb is from *ẓ-n-n*, to think, suppose, reckon, and — depending on context — to know with certainty. Its range covers both conjecture and settled conviction, and the commentators differ on which is intended here. Some take it as a conjecture that these people acted upon as though it were certain; others take it as a settled belief. The verse does not decide between them, and the undetermined sense covers both cases. What is fixed is that the supposition governed their conduct. A person who supposes that he will not return behaves as though the supposition were knowledge, and the behaving is what the chapter is concerned with. The same verb was used in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn of the defrauders who do not reckon that they will be resurrected (83:4).

**The Return**

*Yaḥūr* is from *ḥ-w-r*, a root meaning to return, to turn back, to come back to a starting point. The same root gives *ḥawār*, the returning of a thing to its origin, and it is used in the Qur'an for the return of all things to God: to your Lord is the return, and to God is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to do. The verb's meaning of returning to a point of origin is what makes the negation significant. These people did not merely suppose that they would not be punished; they supposed that they would not go back to the one they came from. The supposition denies the origin as well as the destination, and the denial of the origin is what the following verse refutes.

**Never**

The negation is *lan*, a particle denoting a negation that extends into the future without limit. It is stronger than the simple negative: not that he will not return, but that he will never return. The particle's force removes the possibility of a later return entirely, and the removal is what makes the supposition a settled expectation rather than a passing doubt. Nothing in the verse says that the person argued for this position or examined evidence for it. The supposition is stated as a fact about his mind, and the fact is that he considered the matter closed. The closedness is what the next verse reopens with a single particle.

**The Thought That Made the Ease Possible**

Verses 13 and 14 are joined by *inna* in both, and the joining makes the second the ground of the first. He was at ease among his people because he supposed that he would never return. The causality is stated grammatically rather than argued, and the statement is the chapter's account of how a person comes to live without reckoning. Nothing in the ease described in verse 13 required a denial of God; it required only a denial of return. A person can acknowledge a Creator and still live as though he will not go back to Him, and the living is what the chapter identifies as the fault. The supposition is therefore practical rather than theoretical, and its practical character is what makes it available to anyone.

**The Same Supposition Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes this expectation in several places. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn asks whether the defrauders do not think that they will be resurrected for a tremendous Day (83:4). Sūrat al-Nabaʾ states that the disbelievers never expected any reckoning (78:27). Sūrat al-Kahf describes the man who enters his garden saying that he does not think the Hour will ever come (18:36). The three passages agree that the fault is an absent expectation rather than a reasoned denial, and the agreement makes this verse part of a settled diagnosis. Nothing in any of the passages describes the person as having investigated the question and reached a negative conclusion; each describes him as not expecting, not reckoning, not thinking.

**The Supposition and the Meeting**

Verse 6 announced a meeting that every person will reach, and this verse names the supposition that the meeting will not occur. The two statements are the chapter's central contradiction, and the contradiction is not between two claims about evidence but between a fact and an expectation. The fact is that humanity is labouring toward its Lord; the expectation is that there will be no return. Nothing in the expectation alters the fact, and the unalterability is what makes the supposition tragic rather than merely mistaken. A person who supposes wrongly about a matter he can influence may correct himself; a person who supposes wrongly about the direction of his own movement cannot, because the movement continues while the supposition is held.

**The Supposition Against the Labour**

Verse 6 stated that humanity is labouring toward its Lord and will meet. This verse states that the second class supposed it would never return. The two statements are in direct contradiction, and the contradiction is the chapter's central conflict. Every person is moving toward a meeting; some of them suppose there is no meeting. The supposition does not stop the movement, and the movement is what makes the supposition fatal. Nothing in the chapter suggests that the labouring can be redirected or halted by a belief about it; the toil continues toward its terminus regardless. The verse therefore describes a person walking toward something he has decided is not there, and the description is the whole of the chapter's warning.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:15

> Yes ˹they would˺! Surely their Lord has always been All-Seeing of them

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Particle That Reverses a Negative**

*Balā* is the particle used to affirm what a preceding negative statement has denied. Where *lā* negates and *kallā* refuses, *balā* answers a negation with a yes. Its function here is precise: the previous verse stated that they supposed they would never return, and this particle says that they will. One word reverses the supposition of the verse before it, and the reversal is not argued but asserted. The chapter's refutation of the second class's belief is therefore the shortest possible — a single particle followed by a statement about God's seeing. Nothing in the refutation engages with the reasons for the supposition, because the supposition had no reasons. It was an absence of expectation, and an absence is answered by an assertion.

**The Refutation in Two Clauses**

The verse contains the particle and one emphatic sentence: their Lord has always been all-seeing of them. The sentence does not state that they will return; it states that they have been seen. The substitution is significant. A direct assertion of the return would have answered the supposition with a prediction; an assertion of continuous seeing answers it with a fact already in force. The person who supposed he would never return was supposing something about the future, and the future cannot be shown to him. What can be shown is the present, and the present contains a seeing that has been operating throughout. The verse therefore refutes the supposition by describing something the person has already been inside.

**Baṣīr**

The word is from *b-ṣ-r*, the root of sight, insight, and perception. As a divine name *al-Baṣīr* denotes the one who sees completely — nothing is too small, too distant, too hidden, or too brief to be seen. The Qur'an pairs the name with *al-Samīʿ*, the all-hearing, in descriptions of God's knowledge of what people do and say. Its application here is to a class of people who considered themselves unobserved. The ease of verse 13 was the ease of a man among his own, in a setting where nothing challenged his position; the seeing of this verse states that the setting was never private. Nothing in the verse describes what was seen, and the absence of description makes the seeing comprehensive.

**Has Always Been**

The construction is *kāna bihī baṣīrā* — He was, and has continued to be, all-seeing of him. The auxiliary of continuity with the predicate produces a statement about a state extending through the whole of the past. The seeing is not something that will begin on the Day; it has been happening throughout the life being reckoned. The tense is therefore the verse's decisive feature. A person who supposes he will never return has supposed something about the future, and the verse answers him with the past and the present. The continuity of the seeing is what makes the record possible, and the record is what verse 7 to 12 have described as being delivered.

**Seeing Rather Than Knowing**

The verse uses the language of sight rather than of knowledge. God is described elsewhere as knowing what people hide, as aware of what is in the breasts, as not forgetting anything. The choice of seeing here is appropriate to the fault being refuted. A person who lives among his own in ease is living in a visual world — he sees his position, his company, his standing, and he does not see anything beyond them. The verse answers the limited field of vision with an unlimited one. Nothing in the verse says that God knows what these people thought; it says that He has been seeing them. The seeing is more immediate than the knowing would have been, and the immediacy is what the refutation requires.

**The Answer to the Supposition of Verse 14**

The supposition was that there would be no return. The answer is that there has never been an absence of observation. The two statements are not directly opposed in form, and the indirectness is the strength of the answer. A person can dispute a prediction; he cannot dispute a description of what has already been happening to him. The verse places the refutation in the past tense so that it cannot be argued with, and the placement is what makes the single particle *balā* sufficient. Yes, they will return — and the evidence is that they have been seen throughout, which is a fact about the present rather than a claim about the future.

**Seeing and the Record**

The continuous seeing of this verse is what makes the record of verses 7 to 10 possible. A document containing everything a person did requires a knowledge that misses nothing, and the verse supplies the knowledge in the form of sight extended across the whole of a life. Nothing in the chapter connects the seeing to the writing explicitly, and the connection is unnecessary: the record is delivered, its contents are complete, and the completeness presupposes an observer who was never absent. The seeing is therefore not a threat added to the diagnosis but the ground of the document, and the document is the ground of the two destinations. The chain runs from sight to writing to delivery to outcome, and this verse is its first link.

**The End of the Diagnosis**

Verses 13 to 15 form the chapter's diagnosis of the second class, and this verse closes it. Nothing further is said about these people's character, and the chapter turns to the oaths. The closure is complete: a state of ease, a supposition of no return, and a continuous seeing that refutes the supposition. Three verses, and the whole of the fault is accounted for. The account contains no reference to wealth, power, persecution, or specific sins, and the absence is what makes the diagnosis general. The fault is a manner of living without expectation, and the manner is described in terms that apply to anyone who has ever felt secure among his own.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:16

> So, I do swear by the twilight

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Introduced**

The chapter's third movement begins with an oath, and the oath is introduced by a formula: *fa-lā uqsimu*, so I swear, or no — I swear. The construction combines a negative particle with a verb of swearing, and the commentators explain it in two ways. Either the negative responds to an unspoken denial — as though refusing a claim that no oath is needed before affirming that one is being sworn — or it is an intensifying prefix that adds force to the swearing. Both readings produce the same result: a sworn statement of unusual emphasis. The translation adopts the second, rendering the phrase as "I do swear", with the emphatic auxiliary carrying the force that the particle contributes.

**The Twilight**

*Al-shafaq* is the redness that remains in the western sky after sunset — the last light of the day, which persists briefly and then goes. The word is specific rather than general: it does not denote evening or dusk in a loose sense but the visible red glow that marks the interval between day and night. The commentators identify it with this redness and note that its passing is the marker for the time of the evening prayer. The specificity is what makes the oath meaningful. A thing sworn by must be recognizable and recurrent, and the twilight is both — it occurs every day, it is visible to everyone, and it lasts only a short time.

**Why the Twilight Is Sworn By**

An oath in the Qur'an swears by something whose nature supports the statement being sworn to. The twilight is a transition: light passing into darkness, one state giving way to another, a boundary crossed every day without fail. The statement that the oath introduces in verse 19 is that humanity will pass from stage to stage. The correspondence is exact. A thing that changes its condition every evening is the appropriate witness to a claim about change, and the choice of the twilight rather than of the sun or the day indicates that the chapter is swearing by transition rather than by permanence. Nothing in the verse states the correspondence, and the statement of it arrives three verses later.

**The Last Light**

The twilight is what remains after the source of light has gone. The sun has set and the sky still shows its colour, and the showing is brief. The image carries a sense of what persists past its cause, and the persistence is temporary. Nothing in the verse draws a moral from the image, and the drawing is unnecessary; the oath's function is to invoke the thing as a witness, and the thing's character is available to anyone who has watched an evening sky. The chapter therefore swears by something the reader has seen many times and can see again, and the availability of the witness is what makes the oath binding rather than merely poetic.

**Oaths by Times in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an swears by times of day and night repeatedly: by the night when it covers (92:1), by the morning brightness (93:1), by the dawn (89:1), by the night as it departs and the morning as it brightens (74:33–34). The practice establishes the times as a recognized category of oath-object, and this chapter's three oaths belong to the category. What distinguishes them is that all three are celestial and sequential — the twilight, the night and what it gathers, the moon when it becomes full. The sequence describes a single evening from the last light through the darkness to the moon's fullness, and the sequencing is what makes the three oaths a single movement rather than three separate ones.

**The Particle of Consequence**

The verse opens with *fa-*, so, and the particle links the oath to what has preceded. The diagnosis of verses 13 to 15 has closed, and the oath follows as a consequence. Nothing in the particle specifies the connection, and the connection is supplied by the answer of verse 19: because a man has supposed that he would never return, and because his Lord has been seeing him throughout, I swear by the twilight that you will pass from stage to stage. The sequence from diagnosis to oath to statement is continuous, and the continuity means the oath is not an interlude but the introduction of the chapter's conclusion.

**Twilight as the Reader's Own Evening**

The object of the oath is something every reader has seen and will see again. The choice of a daily, universal, and verifiable phenomenon is what gives the oath its weight, and the weight does not depend on the reader's acceptance of anything beyond his own experience. Nothing in the chapter asks the listener to believe in the twilight; it asks him to notice that it passes. The noticing is the whole of the preparation for verse 19, and the preparation is available to anyone at any hour of the evening. The chapter therefore grounds its most emphatic claim in the most ordinary observation, and the grounding is a feature of the Qur'an's method throughout the short Makkan sūrahs, which swear by dawn, by night, by the sun, and by the things a person can see.

**A Sworn Statement About Change**

The oath's object is a claim about the human condition, and the claim is given in verse 19. What the chapter swears to is not the fire, not the record, and not the return, all of which have already been stated without an oath. What it swears to is the passing from stage to stage, and the swearing indicates that this claim is the one requiring emphasis. The reader has been told about outcomes and about the suppositions that lead to them; he is now told, under oath, that his condition is one of continuous transition. The claim is the ground of everything the chapter has said, and the oath marks it as such.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:17

> And by the night and whatever it envelops

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Oath**

The sequence continues with the night, and the night is sworn by together with what it contains. The construction joins two objects in a single oath: the night itself, and whatever it gathers or envelops. The joining expands the scope of the witness from a moment of transition to a period of containment. The twilight of the previous verse was brief and visible; the night is long and covers. Nothing in the verse describes the night's length or its darkness, and the description is unnecessary — the oath invokes a thing the reader knows, and the addition of what it envelops extends the invocation beyond the night itself.

**Wasaq**

The verb is from *w-s-q*, a root meaning to gather, collect, load, and pack together. It is used of gathering a load onto a beast, of collecting things into a container, and of the coming together of what was dispersed. Its application to the night describes a gathering: the night collects what the day scattered. The commentators specify what is gathered — the creatures returning to their shelters, the stars appearing, the deeds of the day concluded, everything that moves coming to rest. The translation renders the verb as envelops, which carries the sense of the gathering as a covering. The image is of a thing that gathers by enclosing, and the enclosing is what the night does.

**The Night as a Gatherer**

Sūrat al-Layl swears by the night when it covers (92:1), using a verb of covering rather than of gathering. The two descriptions are complementary: covering states the night's effect on what is beneath it, and gathering states its effect on what moves. Together they produce the picture of a period in which everything dispersed is collected and everything visible is hidden. Nothing in either description is moral, and the absence of moral characterization is what makes the night an appropriate object of an oath. It is a fact of the world, recurrent and universal, and its recurrence is what qualifies it as a witness to a claim about the recurrent stages of human life.

**What the Night Envelops**

The object of the gathering is indefinite — *mā wasaq*, whatever it gathers — and the indefiniteness makes it comprehensive. Everything the night contains is inside the oath: the sleeper and the watcher, the animal returning to its shelter and the person returning to his house, the deeds done in darkness and the things hidden from sight. The comprehensiveness is significant for the chapter's argument. Verse 13 described a man at ease among his people, and verse 15 stated that his Lord has always been seeing him. The oath by the night and whatever it gathers invokes the time in which concealment seems most complete, and the invocation is what makes the following statement about seeing and passing more forceful.

**Night and the Hidden**

The Qur'an associates the night with what is concealed and states that the concealment does not extend to God. He knows what is hidden in the darkness, and not a leaf falls without His knowing it. The night is the time in which human knowledge is most limited, and the limitation is what the oath invokes. A witness that gathers everything into darkness is a witness to the limits of what can be seen, and the swearing by it prepares the statement that follows about the stages a person passes through. Nothing in the verse says that God's seeing is unaffected by the night, and the statement is unnecessary because verse 15 has already made it.

**The Pair With the Twilight**

The two oaths describe successive moments of a single evening. The twilight is the interval; the night is what follows it. The sequence is temporal and it is universal — every day produces both, in that order, without exception. The chapter therefore swears by a succession, and the succession is the form of the statement sworn to: humanity passes from stage to stage. The correspondence between the oath's structure and the statement's content is the chapter's method here, and the method is completed by the third oath, which describes the moon becoming full. Three moments of one evening, and the third is a culmination.

**Gathering and the Deeds of the Day**

What the night gathers includes the day's doings, and the gathering is what the chapter's account of the record presupposes. A person's acts are dispersed across the hours of a day and are collected by the night that follows, in the sense that the day is closed and nothing further can be added to it. Nothing in the verse describes this closure, and the description is unnecessary for an audience accustomed to reckoning the day at nightfall. The oath by the night and whatever it envelops therefore invokes the daily closing of an account, and the account closed each evening is a rehearsal of the account sealed in the record. The correspondence is quiet, and the quietness is characteristic of the chapter's use of its oaths.

**Why the Night Is Included**

An oath by the twilight alone would have sufficed to invoke transition. The addition of the night extends the invocation to the period in which transition is invisible. The day changes into evening visibly; the evening changes into night by degrees that no one observes; the night changes into morning while everyone sleeps. The chapter's claim in verse 19 is about stages that a person passes through without necessarily perceiving the passing, and the night is the witness that best supports the claim. Nothing in the verse explains the choice, and the explanation is available from the oath's own content: a thing that gathers everything and hides what it has gathered is the appropriate witness to an unseen progression.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:18

> And by the moon when it waxes full

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Oath**

The sequence of oaths ends with the moon, and the moon is sworn by at a specific moment: when it becomes full. The specification is the verse's whole content. The moon is not invoked in its rising, its crescent, or its waning, but at the point of its completion. The choice completes the sequence begun in the previous two verses — a transition, a gathering, and a culmination — and the culmination is what the sworn statement of verse 19 requires. A person passing from stage to stage passes toward something, and the full moon is the image of arrival at a completed state.

**Ittasaq**

The verb is the eighth form of *w-s-q*, the same root used in the previous verse for the night's gathering. The eighth form denotes the coming together of a thing's parts into completeness, and its application to the moon describes the point at which the illuminated portion is whole. The word therefore carries both senses at once: the moon gathers its light, and the gathering is complete. The recurrence of the root across two successive oaths is significant — the night gathers whatever it envelops, and the moon gathers itself into fullness. Nothing in the verse explains the recurrence, and the explanation is available from the sequence: gathering is the process, and fullness is its term.

**The Moon in the Qur'an's Oaths**

The Qur'an swears by the moon in several places: by the moon in Sūrat al-Muddaththir, as a sign among the greatest (74:32); by the night as it departs and the morning as it brightens (74:33–34); and the moon is described elsewhere as a light and as one of the signs by which time is reckoned. Its use here at the moment of fullness is distinctive, because the full moon is the only phase that is complete. The crescent is a beginning, the waning is an ending, and the fullness is a state of arrival. The chapter swears by the arrival, and the swearing prepares a statement about the stages through which a person passes.

**Three Oaths as One Movement**

The twilight, the night with what it gathers, and the moon at full — the three describe a single evening from its last light through its darkness to its brightest point. The sequence is temporal and it is also qualitative: from a fading glow to an enveloping darkness to a completed illumination. Nothing in the chapter states that the three form a progression, and the progression is evident from the objects themselves. The effect on the reader is that the oath is not a list of witnesses but a description of a process, and the process is the same one that the sworn statement announces. The three verses of oath and the one verse of statement are therefore a single argument in four parts.

**Why the Full Moon**

The full moon is the point at which a thing that changes has reached the limit of its change. It cannot become fuller, and what follows is diminishment. The chapter's choice of this moment as the last of its witnesses is significant for the statement that follows. Humanity passes from stage to stage, and the stages include arrival at a limit. The full moon is the image of the limit reached, and its inclusion means that the progression described in verse 19 is not open-ended but has terms. Nothing in the verse says what the terms are for a human being, and the saying is unnecessary — the chapter has already described them in verses 7 to 12 as two destinations.

**The Celestial Against the Human**

The three oaths are all celestial, and the statement they support is about people. The correspondence between the two is the chapter's method: the sky and its lights pass through stages that no one can dispute, and the passage is used to establish that human beings pass through stages as well. Nothing in the verse draws the analogy, and the drawing is left to the reader who has heard all three oaths and then hears the statement. The analogy is one of the Qur'an's characteristic moves — swearing by the created order to establish a claim about the human condition — and its use here at the end of a chapter that began with the sky bursting open makes the frame complete.

**Fullness and the Limit of a Stage**

The full moon is a stage that cannot be extended. It is the point at which growth stops and diminishment begins, and the point is visible to everyone. The oath by it invokes the fact that every progression has a term, and the term is what verse 19's stages arrive at. Nothing in the verse draws the moral, and the drawing is left to the reader who has heard all three oaths and then hears the sworn statement. The chapter's witnesses are therefore not merely celestial objects but three demonstrations of a single principle: things change, the change has an order, and the order reaches a limit. The principle is what the statement about the stages of human life rests on.

**The Oath and the Sky of Verse 1**

The chapter opened with the sky bursting apart and closed its descriptive movement with three oaths by the sky's lights. The two are related: the same order that is invoked as a witness is the order that will fail. Nothing in the oaths suggests that the twilight, the night, or the full moon are permanent, and the absence of suggestion is consistent with verse 1. The chapter swears by things that change in order to establish a claim about change, and the things sworn by are themselves part of the created order that the opening verse described as obeying its Lord. The frame is therefore closed at both ends — a sky that opens, and a sky that testifies.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:19

> You will certainly pass from one state to another

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Three Oaths**

Verses 16 to 18 swore by the twilight, by the night and whatever it envelops, and by the moon at full. This verse is the statement sworn to, and it is the only statement the three oaths support. Nothing else in the chapter is placed under an oath, and the placement indicates that this claim is the one the chapter considers most in need of emphasis. The claim is short: you will pass from stage to stage. Everything the chapter has described — the bursting sky, the emptied earth, the toil, the two records, the two destinations — is contained within it, and the containment is what makes the verse the chapter's summary.

**You Will Mount**

The verb is *tarkabunna*, from *r-k-b*, the root of mounting, riding, boarding, and composing. The form here carries the emphatic *nūn*, which strengthens the statement into an assertion that cannot be qualified. The verb's basic sense is to mount something and be carried by it, and its application to stages describes a person boarding one condition and being carried into the next. The image is of a traveller who does not control the vehicle. Nothing in the verse describes the stages as chosen, and the absence of choice is consistent with the chapter's account of the toil in verse 6 — a person labouring toward a terminus he did not select.

**Stage Upon Stage**

*Ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq* — a layer after a layer, a stage following a stage, one condition giving way to the next. The word *ṭabaq* denotes a stratum, a level, a lid, and a stage of a progression, and the construction with *ʿan* describes succession: from one to the one after it. The phrase is general, and its generality has produced several readings of its reference. Some take it as the stages of the human life — weakness, strength, weakness and old age — as described elsewhere in the Qur'an (30:54, 40:67). Others take it as the stages from this world to the grave to the resurrection to the reckoning. Others take it as states of condition: poverty after wealth, sickness after health, death after life.

**Created in Stages**

The Qur'an uses the same vocabulary for the creation of the human being. Sūrat Nūḥ asks why people do not hope for dignity when He truly created them in stages of development (71:13–14). Sūrat Ghāfir describes the progression from dust to a sperm-drop to a clinging clot to infancy to prime to old age to an appointed term (40:67). Sūrat al-Rūm gives the same progression in three terms: weakness, then strength, then weakness and old age (30:54). The recurrence establishes staged progression as a recognized description of the human being, and this verse extends the description beyond the life of the body to the states that follow it.

**The Address**

The verb is second person plural, and the plural has been read in two ways. Either it addresses humanity generally, continuing the vocative of verse 6, or it addresses the Prophet specifically, in which case the plural is one of respect. The first reading is supported by the context — the chapter has been addressing humanity since verse 6, and the stages described apply to everyone. The second is transmitted and is coherent with the Qur'an's practice of addressing the Prophet in the plural at moments of consolation. The translation adopts the general reading, and the generality is what makes the verse applicable to whoever hears it. The choice between the two readings changes little in substance, since a statement about the stages of human existence is true of the Prophet as it is true of everyone else, and a statement addressed to him in the plural of respect would still be heard by the community praying behind him as a statement about itself. What the plural secures in either case is that no listener can treat the stages as belonging to somebody else.

**The Oaths and the Stages**

The three witnesses sworn by are themselves staged: the twilight fades into night, the night gathers what the day dispersed, and the moon becomes full. Each is a state passing into another, and the passing is observable every evening. The correspondence between the oath and the statement is therefore exact in form. The chapter does not argue that human beings pass through stages; it swears by things that visibly do so and then states that the listener will as well. The technique transfers the certainty of the observed to the certainty of the unobserved, and the transfer is what the emphatic verb and the three oaths together accomplish. No argument is offered, no evidence is cited, and no example from history is given; the witnesses are three features of a single evening, and the statement is about the listener.

**The Stages and the Two Destinations**

The chapter has already described the termini of the progression in verses 7 to 12, and the description means that the stages of this verse are not abstract. A person passes from weakness to strength, from life to death, from death to the standing, from the standing to the delivery of a record, and from the delivery to either a joyful return to his people or a blazing fire. Each transition is a stage, and each stage is described somewhere in the chapter or in its neighbours. Nothing in the verse enumerates the stages, and the enumeration would have made the statement a schedule rather than a warning. What the verse gives is the certainty of transition and the certainty that the transitions have ends, and the two certainties are what the following verses turn into a question about belief.

**The Verse as the Chapter's Centre**

Nineteen verses precede this one and six follow it. The first nineteen described the world's end, the human toil, the two destinations, and the diagnosis of the second class. The last six turn to belief, to prostration, to denial, to God's knowledge, to an ironic announcement, and to a never-ending reward. This verse stands between the two halves, and it is the statement that connects them. The stages described here include the arrival at the record, and the arrival is what makes belief a matter of urgency. Nothing in the verse names belief, and the naming arrives in the next, where the chapter asks what is the matter with those who do not believe.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:20

> So what is the matter with them that they do not believe

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question of Astonishment**

*Fa-mā lahum lā yuʾminūn* — what is the matter with them, that they do not believe? The construction is a standard Arabic form of astonished inquiry, and it does not request information. The speaker knows the answer, and the question is asked in order to mark the answer as inexplicable. Nothing could be more reasonable than belief in a situation where the sky will burst, the earth will be emptied, every person will meet his Lord, and a record will be delivered. The question expresses that the absence of belief in such a situation is not a position but a failure to respond to something obvious.

**Turning to the Third Person**

Verse 19 addressed the listener in the second person plural: you will pass from stage to stage. This verse turns to the third person: what is the matter with *them*. The shift is abrupt and unmarked, and it has a specific effect. The listener who has just been told that he will pass through stages is now placed beside the speaker, looking at a group that does not believe. The position is an invitation: the listener is being addressed as someone for whom the question is intelligible, and the intelligibility implies that he is not among those being asked about. Nothing in the verse names the group, and the naming arrives in verse 22 as the disbelievers.

**Particle of Consequence**

*Fa-*, so, links the question to the sworn statement of verse 19. The connection is a consequence: because you will pass from stage to stage, what is the matter with them that they do not believe? The stages include the meeting announced in verse 6 and the delivery described in verses 7 and 10, and the certainty of these things is what makes unbelief astonishing. No argument for the stages is repeated here. The argument has already been made under oath, and the question proceeds from it as from an established fact. The technique is one of the Qur'an's characteristic moves — establish a claim with maximum emphasis, then treat it as settled and ask why anyone disputes it.

**Belief as the Expected Response**

The question presupposes that belief is what the described situation calls for. The chapter has not asked for anything else — not for ritual, not for payment, not for a change of social position. The expectation is belief, and the expectation is presented as the natural response to what has been said. No hint appears that belief is difficult, costly, or unusual; the suggestion is the opposite, since the absence of belief is what occasions astonishment. The framing places the burden on the disbeliever rather than on the message, and the placement is the verse's rhetorical achievement. A person asked what is the matter with him is being told that the matter lies with him.

**Question Left Unanswered**

Nothing in the following verses answers the question directly. Verse 21 adds a second element to it — when the Qur'an is recited they do not bow down. Verse 22 states that the disbelievers persist in denial. Verse 23 says that God knows best what they hide. The three verses are responses to the question in the sense that they describe the people being asked about, and the descriptions supply the answer: there is nothing the matter with them that can be identified from outside, because the fault is inside and God knows it best. The withholding of a direct answer is what makes the question function as an expression of astonishment rather than as an inquiry.

**Two Elements in One Question**

Verses 20 and 21 form a single question with two parts: that they do not believe, and that they do not bow down when the Qur'an is recited. The first concerns an inward state and the second a bodily act, and the pairing is significant. Belief in the Qur'an's usage is not a private conviction separable from its expression; it is a state that produces a response. The absence of the response is evidence about the state, and the question treats the two together. The two are not separated here, nor are they separated in the following verse. The chapter's account of unbelief is therefore an account of a person who neither believes nor bows, and the two refusals are one.

**The Listener Beside the Question**

The shift from second person to third places the listener in a position he may not have chosen. He has just been told, directly, that he will pass from stage to stage; he is then asked, as though from outside the group, what is the matter with those who do not believe. The question invites him to join in the astonishment, and the joining is the invitation's mechanism. A person who wonders at another's unbelief has already separated himself from it, and the separation is what the chapter wants. Nothing here accuses the listener or reassures him, and the absence of both means that the question is doing the work of an examination rather than an assertion. He is being asked to consider whether the astonishment applies to him.

**Closing Movement of the Chapter**

This verse opens the chapter's last movement, which runs to verse 25. The movement contains the question about belief, the question about prostration, the statement of persistent denial, the statement of God's knowledge, the ironic command to announce a painful punishment, and the exception of those who believe and do good with a never-ending reward. Six verses, and the last is a promise. The movement therefore ends where the chapter began its account of the blessed — with those who believe and do good — and the ending is what makes the whole sūrah a summons rather than a description. The question asked here is the summons, and it is addressed to a group in the third person so that the listener can consider whether he is inside it.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:21

> and when the Quran is recited to them, they do not bow down ˹in submission˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Question**

This verse continues the question begun in the previous one, and the continuation adds a specific occasion. The first half asked what is the matter with them that they do not believe; this half asks what is the matter with them that they do not bow down when the Qur'an is recited to them. The addition converts a general question about belief into a question about a described situation: a recitation happening, people present, and no bowing. Nothing in the verse describes the recitation's content, its length, or its occasion, and the absence of description makes the situation general — any recitation, any audience, any occasion.

**They Do Not Bow Down**

*Lā yaskudūn* — they do not prostrate. The verb is from *s-j-d*, the root of prostration, which in the Qur'an denotes the act of placing the face on the ground in submission to God. The prostration is the most complete bodily expression of submission available in the Arabic practice of the revelation's audience, and its absence is therefore a significant fact rather than a minor omission. Nothing in the verse says that they refuse, mock, or leave; it says only that they do not bow. The understatement is what makes the question severe. A person who does not prostrate when the Qur'an is recited has not done something, and the not-doing is the whole of the accusation.

**Prostration as the Response to Recitation**

Sūrat al-Sajdah describes the response of belief in exactly these terms: the only true believers in Our revelation are those who, when it is recited to them, fall into prostration, glorify the praises of their Lord, and are not too proud (32:15). The three elements — prostration, glorification, and the absence of pride — are presented as the marks of belief, and their absence is the mark of its absence. This verse names the first element and its absence, and the naming relies on the reader's acquaintance with the description in Sūrat al-Sajdah. Nothing here explains why prostration is the expected response, and the explanation is unnecessary for an audience that has heard the earlier description.

**A Verse of Prostration**

This verse is traditionally counted among the Qur'an's verses of prostration, at which the reciter and the listener bow. The practice is attested in the earliest community: Abū Hurayrah reported that the companions prostrated along with the Messenger of God when Sūrat al-Inshiqāq and Sūrat al-ʿAlaq were recited (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1407). The report establishes that the prostration at this verse was performed by the Prophet and by those praying behind him, and the performance is what makes the verse's question a demand rather than a description. A verse that asks why people do not bow, at which the reader is expected to bow, places the question directly on whoever recites it.

**The Occasion of Recitation**

The chapter's parallel passages make the recitation the standard occasion for the rejection. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn describes the response to recitation as a dismissal: whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say ancient fables (83:13). Sūrat al-Qalam describes the same response in the singular (68:15). Sūrat al-Jāthiyah describes people who hear God's revelations recited and then persist arrogantly as though they had not heard them (45:8). The four passages agree that the recitation is public, that it is heard, and that the hearing produces either a dismissal or a silence. This verse describes the silence — no words, no bowing, only the absence of a response.

**The Body and Belief**

The question joins an inward state to a bodily act, and the joining is the Qur'an's consistent account of belief. Belief that does not produce prostration is described here as unbelief, and the description does not distinguish between a person who believes inwardly and refuses to bow and a person who does not believe at all. The absence of a distinction is significant. Nothing in the verse allows for a belief that is withheld from the body, and the refusal of that possibility is what makes the question a demand rather than an inquiry. A person who asks why they do not bow is asking why their belief has not reached their body.

**Bowing and the One Who Recites**

The verse describes people who hear a recitation and do not bow, and the recitation is an act performed by someone. The chapter's audience included the person reciting, and the tradition of prostrating at this verse places him inside the scene rather than outside it. A reciter who bows at the verse he is asking about has answered the question bodily, and the answering is what makes the verse's status among the Qur'an's prostration verses significant rather than merely liturgical. Nothing in the verse instructs the reciter to bow, and the absence of an instruction leaves the bowing to the practice of the community that received it. The practice is the answer, and the answer has been given since the earliest recitations.

**The Question Completed**

Verses 20 and 21 together form the chapter's longest interrogative, and the question mark falls at the end of this verse. The two halves — no belief, no bowing at the recitation — are held inside a single astonished inquiry. The completion means that the reader reaches the end of the question with both elements in mind, and the elements are what the following verses answer. Verse 22 states the persistent denial; verse 23 states that God knows best what they hold inside; verse 24 commands an ironic announcement. The three verses are the answer to a question that was not asked for information, and the answer describes the people rather than resolving the astonishment.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:22

> In fact, the disbelievers persist in denial

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn From Question to Statement**

The particle *bal* marks a turn: in fact, rather, on the contrary. What it turns from is the question of the two preceding verses, and what it turns to is a statement about the people the question was asked about. The turn indicates that the question is not to be pursued. There is nothing further to be discovered about why they do not believe and do not bow; the matter is settled, and the settlement is given in a single clause. The chapter therefore closes its inquiry after two verses and replaces it with a diagnosis, and the diagnosis is the shortest possible — they deny.

**Yukadhdhibūn**

The verb is the intensive form of calling something false, and it is in the imperfect, denoting a continuing activity. The same verb appears in Sūrat al-Infiṭār in the accusation that humanity denies the final Judgement (82:9) and in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn in the description of those who deny the Day of Judgement (83:11). The intensive form adds repetition and effort: not merely calling something false once but persisting in the calling. The translation renders the persistence explicitly, and the rendering is faithful to the form. Nothing in the verse describes a single act of denial or a particular claim being denied; the denial is a state, and the state is what the verb's aspect conveys.

**The Class Named**

*Alladhīna kafarū* — those who disbelieved, who covered over, who refused. The relative clause names the group that verse 20 referred to only as "them", and the naming is the first use of this term in the chapter. The earlier verses used *al-fujjār* for the wicked in verse 12's context, and the class receiving its record behind its back was described without a name. The introduction of the standard Qur'anic designation at this point marks a shift from description to classification, and the classification is what allows the following verses to speak of them as a group with a settled character.

**What Is Denied**

The object of the denial is not stated, and the unstated object is comprehensive. The chapter has described the bursting sky, the emptied earth, the toil toward the Lord, the meeting, the two records, the two destinations, the diagnosis of the second class, and the stages of human existence. The denial covers all of it. Nothing in the verse restricts the denial to the Day of Judgement as Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn does, or to the revelations as Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn's verse 13 does. The denial here is general, and the generality is consistent with the question of verses 20 and 21, which was about belief as such and about the response to recitation as such.

**Denial as the Answer to the Question**

The question asked what is the matter with them. The answer is that they deny. The answer does not explain the denial; it names it. Nothing in the chapter supplies a cause for the denial in the way that Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn supplied the covering of the heart or Sūrat al-Infiṭār supplied the deception of this world. The diagnosis here is the barest available: they are people who deny, and the denying is what they do. The bareness is significant, because it removes any suggestion that the denial has an intelligible ground. A fault with a cause could be addressed by removing the cause; a fault described as persistent denial has no such handle.

**Persistence Rather Than Doubt**

The verse does not describe uncertainty, hesitation, or inquiry. It describes persistence, and persistence is a matter of the will rather than of the intellect. Nothing in the chapter suggests that these people have examined the recitation and found it unconvincing; verse 21 described them as not bowing when it is recited, which is a failure to respond rather than a conclusion. The distinction is important for the chapter's closing movement, which addresses belief as something available rather than as something demonstrated. The people described here are not lacking evidence; they are persisting in a refusal, and the refusal is what the following verses treat.

**Denial Without an Object Named**

The verse states that they deny and does not say what they deny. The omission is unusual in a chapter that has been specific throughout, and the specificity of the earlier verses makes the generality here conspicuous. What has been described — the sky, the earth, the toil, the meeting, the records, the destinations, the stages — is all of it deniable, and the verse treats the denial as covering the whole. Nothing further is required, because a denial that extensive has no particular object to examine. The chapter therefore names the state and moves on, and the moving on is what allows the closing verses to proceed to God's knowledge, the ironic announcement, and the exception of the believers.

**The Statement and What Follows**

Verse 23 states that God knows best whatever they hide; verse 24 commands the Prophet to give them news of a painful punishment; verse 25 excepts those who believe and do good. The three verses proceed from the diagnosis in this one. Because they persist in denial, what they hold inside is known to God alone; because it is known to God alone, the announcement of punishment is made in irony; and because the announcement is made, the exception of the believers is required to complete the picture. The chain is continuous, and its first link is the naming of the denial. Nothing further is said about the disbelievers' reasons, their history, or their fate beyond the punishment announced, and the chapter moves to its close.

The diagnosis is also the last word the chapter says about these people as a group. After this verse they appear only as the recipients of an ironic announcement, and the announcement is addressed through the Prophet rather than to them. The movement from description to announcement marks the end of any attempt to persuade, and the end is what the persistence of the denial has produced. A person who keeps calling something false has placed himself beyond the reach of a further description, and the chapter acknowledges the placement by changing its mode of speech. What remains is not an argument but a notice, and the notice is given in the language of glad tidings.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:23

> But Allah knows best whatever they hide

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Knowledge That Surpasses**

*Aʿlam* is the elative of knowing — more knowing, best knowing, most fully aware. The construction *wa-llāhu aʿlamu bi-* is used throughout the Qur'an to state that God's knowledge of a matter exceeds everyone else's, including the knowledge of the person concerned. Nothing in the verse describes God as merely informed; the elative places His knowledge above every other knowledge of the same object. The object here is what these people hold inside, and the statement means that the denial described in the previous verse is known more fully by God than by anyone, including the deniers themselves.

**What They Contain**

The verb is *yūʿūn*, from *w-ʿ-y*, a root meaning to contain, to hold, to preserve, and to be conscious of. The same root gives *wāʿiyah*, a vessel that holds what is put in it, and it is used of a heart that holds and remembers. Applied to people the verb denotes what they contain within themselves — their intentions, their concealed beliefs, what they keep in their chests. The translation renders it as what they hide, which carries the sense of concealment; the Arabic also carries the sense of holding and preserving. Both are relevant: what a person contains is what he has kept, and the keeping may be deliberate or merely habitual.

**Concealment Against Seeing**

Verse 15 stated that their Lord has always been all-seeing of them, and this verse states that He knows best what they hold inside. The two statements are complementary and they belong to the same argument. The seeing of verse 15 answered the supposition of a man at ease among his people; the knowing of this verse answers the persistence in denial described in verse 22. A person who denies outwardly may suppose that his inward state is his own, and the verse removes the supposition. Nothing in it accuses these people of concealing something specific; the concealment is general, and the generality means that whatever is inside is inside God's knowledge.

**The Superlative as a Refutation**

The elative form is a refutation in itself. To say that God knows best what they contain is to say that no other knowledge of it competes — not theirs, not their companions', not the reader's. The refutation is directed at any account of these people that claims to explain them from outside. The chapter has just given such an account in verses 13 to 14 — joyful among his people, supposing he would never return — and the superlative indicates that even that account is partial. Nothing in the verse withdraws the earlier diagnosis, and the withdrawal is unnecessary. What is added is the statement that the diagnosis is not the whole of what is known, and that the whole is known to One.

**Why the Verse Is Needed**

The chapter has described the disbelievers' denial, their ease, their supposition, and their failure to bow at the recitation. It could have proceeded directly to the announcement of punishment. The intervening statement about God's knowledge serves a specific function: it establishes that the punishment announced in the next verse is not based on the visible alone. A person judged on appearances could appeal against the judgement; a person judged by one who knows best what he contains cannot. The verse therefore grounds the announcement that follows, and the grounding is what makes the irony of that announcement possible. An ironic command to give good news of a painful punishment requires that the punishment be just, and the justice requires the knowledge.

**Knowledge and the Record**

The chapter's account of the two records presupposes a complete knowledge of what people did. The document given in the right hand and the document given from behind the back were written by keepers who record everything and know whatever people do (82:10–12), and the writing was sealed (83:9, 83:20). This verse supplies the knowledge behind the record. Nothing in the descriptions of the writing says how the deeds are known; the verse states that God knows best what people contain, and the stating closes the gap. The record is therefore not a collection of observed events but a document produced by a knowledge that includes what was never observed by anyone else.

**Containing and Being Contained**

The root of the verb used here also gives the word for a vessel, and the image of the verse is of people as containers holding something inside. The image is the converse of the one used in verse 22 for the record and in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn for the sealed books: there, a document contains a person's deeds; here, a person contains his concealed state. Nothing in the verse says that the containing is deliberate, and the absence of deliberation makes the statement more comprehensive. A person holds inside him things he has chosen to hide and things he has never examined, and both are inside the knowledge described here. The knowledge is best, and the superlative covers both categories without distinction.

**The Verse in the Closing Movement**

Verses 20 to 25 form the chapter's close, and this verse is its fourth element. The movement has asked a question, described a refusal, named a class, and now states a knowledge. The remaining two verses give an announcement and an exception. The arrangement moves from inquiry to diagnosis to grounding to outcome, and the grounding is what this verse supplies. Nothing in it is addressed to the disbelievers or to the listener; it is a statement about God made in the third person, and the statement's placement between the diagnosis and the announcement is what connects them. The punishment is just because the knowledge is complete, and the knowledge is complete because it is God's.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:24

> So give them good news of a painful punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word Used in Irony**

*Bashshir* is the imperative of giving good news — the verb used throughout the Qur'an for announcing Paradise, forgiveness, and reward to the believers. Its application here to a painful punishment is ironic, and the irony is a recognized device of the revelation's style. The command asks the Prophet to announce punishment in the form used for announcing good, and the form's inappropriateness is what carries the meaning. Nothing in the verse marks the irony with a particle or an explanation, and the absence of marking means the reader must recognize it. The recognition is what makes the verse effective: an announcement of ruin delivered in the language of glad tidings is more severe than a plain statement of ruin.

**The Same Irony Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses the device repeatedly. Those who deny God's signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice are told: give them good news of a painful punishment (3:21). Those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend in God's cause are given the same announcement (9:34). The people who hear God's revelations recited and persist arrogantly as though they had not heard them are addressed with it (45:8). The recurrence establishes the construction as a settled form, and the settling means that a reader encountering it here recognizes it as irony rather than mistaking it for a promise. Three of the four occurrences concern people who have heard the revelation and refused it, which is the situation described in verse 21 of this chapter.

**ʿAdhāb Alīm**

The punishment is described as *alīm*, painful — an intensive adjective from the root of pain, denoting something that causes severe suffering. The Qur'an uses the phrase as a standing description of the punishment of the disbelievers, and its recurrence makes it a term of art rather than a fresh characterization. The combination of an intensive adjective with a noun of punishment is the strongest form available, and the strength is what the irony of the verb plays against. A command to give good news of a severe pain is a command to deliver the most severe announcement in the most pleasant form, and the mismatch is the verse's whole content.

**A Command to the Prophet**

The imperative is addressed in the singular, and the addressee is the Prophet. The chapter has moved from describing the disbelievers in the third person to instructing the one who recites to them. The instruction is not to argue, to warn, to persuade, or to leave them; it is to announce. The announcement is a form of speech with a specific function in the Qur'an — it delivers news to people who did not ask for it — and its use here means that the disbelievers are being treated as recipients of information rather than as opponents in a debate. Nothing in the verse describes their response to the announcement, and the absence of a described response is what makes the irony complete.

**The Reversal of the Word's Ordinary Use**

The ordinary use of *bashshir* in the Qur'an is directed at believers, and the announcements it introduces are the chapter's own closing content: those who believe and do good will have a never-ending reward. The two verses stand side by side, and the pairing shows the verb operating in both of its modes. The ironic use addresses those who persist in denial; the plain use addresses those who believe and do good. Nothing in the chapter explains the reversal, and the explanation is unnecessary. A word that announces good to one group and pain to another, in the same grammatical form, tells the reader that the difference lies entirely in the recipients rather than in the announcement.

**Why Irony at This Point**

The chapter has reached the end of its argument. The cosmic events have been described, the two destinations have been given, the diagnosis of the second class has been made, the oaths have been sworn, the stages have been stated, and the question about belief has been asked and answered. Nothing further can be added by way of demonstration. The ironic command is what remains when demonstration is exhausted: an announcement delivered in a form that marks the announcement as refused. The irony therefore expresses the chapter's conclusion about its opponents — that they have been told, that they have heard, and that what they have been told is ruin presented in the language of glad tidings.

**Glad Tidings of Ruin**

The irony of this verse depends on the ordinary function of its verb, and the ordinary function is established by the chapter's own closing verse. The same imperative of giving good news introduces, one verse later, the reward of those who believe and do good. The reader therefore encounters the verb in its ironic use and its plain use within two verses, and the juxtaposition is what makes the irony unmistakable. Nothing in the chapter explains the device, marks it, or apologizes for it. The device is simply used, and its use tells the reader that the announcement to the deniers is not good news and that the form in which it is delivered is itself part of the announcement.

**The Announcement and the Exception**

The verse that follows begins with *illā*, except, and the exception introduces those who believe and do good. The grammatical relation between the two verses is not one of exception from the command but of contrast in content: the announcement of pain is followed by the statement of a never-ending reward. The sequence means that the chapter's last two verses contain both outcomes, and the order places the punishment first. Nothing in the ordering suggests that the punishment is the chapter's main concern; the ordering is what allows the chapter to end on the reward. An announcement of pain followed by a promise of an uncut reward leaves the reader with the promise, and the promise is the chapter's final word.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:25

> But those who believe and do good will have a never-ending reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter's Last Verse**

Twenty-five verses, and the last is a promise. The chapter opened with the sky bursting and closes with a never-ending reward, and the distance between the two is the whole of its argument. Nothing in the closing verse refers back to the opening images, and the absence of reference is what makes the ending open rather than circular. The reader is not returned to the rupture of the sky; he is left with a statement about those who believe and do good. The finality of the statement is what gives the chapter its character as a summons — the last thing said is an offer, and the offer is made after everything that could make it urgent has been described.

**The Exception**

*Illā* — except, but. The particle follows the announcement of a painful punishment and introduces the group excluded from it. The exclusion is not from the command to announce but from the punishment announced, and the construction is the Qur'an's standard way of separating two outcomes after a statement about one of them. Nothing in the verse describes the exception as conditional or as earned; it states that a group has a reward, and the group is defined by two features. The definition is the shortest available in the Qur'an's vocabulary of salvation, and its shortness means that the verse is applicable to anyone who fits it.

**Belief and Good Deeds**

*Alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believed and did what is sound, right, and good. The pairing is the Qur'an's most frequently repeated formula, appearing in dozens of passages across Makkan and Medinan revelation. Its two terms are inseparable in the formula: belief without sound action does not satisfy it, and sound action without belief does not. The pairing answers the chapter's question of verses 20 and 21, which asked about belief and about bowing at the recitation. The answer is a class defined by belief and by doing, and the doing is what the bowing would have been an instance of. Nothing in the verse specifies the deeds, and the unspecified generality makes the formula comprehensive.

**A Never-Ending Reward**

*Ajr ghayr mamnūn* — a reward that is not cut off, not withheld, not diminished, not conferred with reproach. The participle is from *m-n-n*, a root with two related senses: to sever or weaken, and to confer a favour in a way that reminds the recipient of it. Both senses are relevant. A reward that is *mamnūn* would either be interrupted or be given with an upbraiding that diminishes it; a reward that is *ghayr mamnūn* is neither. The phrase therefore describes a reward that continues and that carries no reproach, and the two features are what distinguish it from every reward available in this life.

**The Same Phrase Elsewhere**

The formula appears in the closing verses of two other short Makkan chapters. Sūrat al-Tīn ends with the statement that those who believe and do good will have a never-ending reward (95:6). Sūrat Fuṣṣilat gives the same phrase: those who believe and do good will certainly have a never-ending reward (41:8). Sūrat Hūd describes the people destined to joy as being in Paradise forever, a generous giving without end (11:108). The recurrence across chapters establishes the phrase as a settled description of the reward, and the settling means that the reader receives it here as a known quantity rather than a new claim. The chapter's ending is therefore a quotation of the revelation's standard promise.

**The Answer to the Toil**

Verse 6 stated that humanity is labouring toward its Lord and will meet. This verse states what the meeting produces for one class. The toil is not described as wasted, and the meeting is not described as a loss. What the toil produces for those who believe and do good is a reward that is not cut off, and the absence of cutting is the answer to the labour's exhaustion. A person who toils toward a terminus and arrives at a reward that ends has laboured for a bounded thing; a person who arrives at a reward that does not end has arrived at the only terminus proportionate to the toil. The chapter's frame is therefore complete: a labour announced in verse 6 and a reward stated in verse 25, with everything between them describing how the two are connected.

**The Uncut Reward and the Short Measure**

The neighbouring chapter opened with woe to those who take a full measure and give less. This chapter closes with a reward that is not cut off. The two statements are the same subject at its two extremes: a quantity diminished in the taking, and a quantity undiminished in the giving. Nothing in the closing verse refers to the market, and the absence of reference is what makes the connection structural. A reader who has read the two chapters in sequence arrives at the last verse holding the first verse of the other chapter in mind, and the holding produces the recognition that the reward described here is the opposite of the shortfall described there. The reward is not measured out in fractions; it is given without diminishment and without reproach, and the giving is the chapter's final word.

**Why the Chapter Ends Here**

The last verse contains no image. No gardens, no couches, no records, no faces, no wine. It contains a class, a reward, and a negation of the reward's interruption. The absence of imagery is what makes the ending a promise rather than a description, and the promise is what the chapter requires. A description would have concluded the account of Paradise begun in the neighbouring chapters; a promise concludes a summons. The chapter has asked what is the matter with those who do not believe and do not bow, has announced a painful punishment in irony, and now states the alternative in the plainest terms available. The plainness is the final feature of a chapter whose method throughout has been to state the fact and leave the elaboration elsewhere.
