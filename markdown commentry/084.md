# Sūrah al-Inshiqāq (Chapter 84) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Cosmic Submission and the Toil of the Laboring Soul**

Al-Inshiqāq, "The Splitting Asunder," is the eighty-fourth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 25 verses of early Makkan revelation. It is the third chapter of the famous apocalyptic trilogy (alongside at-Takwīr and al-Infiṭār), taking its name from its opening verse: *“When the sky splits asunder (*Idhā al-samā'u inshaqqat*), and has hearkened to its Lord and was obligated [to do so]!”* (84:1–2). In prophetic liturgy, Sūrah al-Inshiqāq contains a mandatory prostration of recitation (*sajdat al-tilāwah*, verse 21), which Abū Hurayrah recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (1078) that he prayed behind the Prophet ﷺ and saw him prostrate at its recitation. In twenty-five dramatic, fluid verses, the sūrah portrays the universe in active, conscious obedience to the command of its Creator, captures the universal human journey as a relentless, laboring toil toward God (*Kadḥ*), and stages the terrifying distinction between the soul that receives its record from behind its back and the soul that receives it in its right hand.

**The Structural Movement and the Reckoning of Ease**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Inshiqāq moves across four distinct movements:

1. The Toil of Man and the Two Accounts (6–15): Declares human life to be an arduous labor toward God. It establishes the two recipients of the books: - *The Right Hand* (7–9): *“Then as for him who is given his record in his right hand, he will be judged with an easy account (*ḥisāban yasīrā*). Return to his people in joy!”* ʿĀ'ishah reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (103) that the Prophet explained: *“Whoever is audited strictly will be destroyed; the 'easy account' is merely the presentation of the record followed by divine pardon.”* - *Behind the Back* (10–15): As for him who is given his record behind his back, he will cry out for destruction (*daʿā thubūrā*) and burn in a blaze, because in his worldly life he was joyful among his family, assuming he would never return to God. Verse 15 shatters his delusion: *“Yes! Indeed, his Lord was ever, of him, Seeing!”*

3. The Oaths of Twilight and the Ascent from Stage to Stage (16–19): Delivers three exquisite cosmic oaths: by the twilight glow of evening (*al-shafaq*), by the night and what it encloses. By the moon when it becomes full (*al-qamari idhā ittasaq*), leading to the profound evolutionary and Last-Day law of verse 19: *“You will surely travel from stage to stage (*la-tarkabunna ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq*)!”* the commentators interpret this as moving from dust to embryo, from childhood to old age, from life to death, from the grave to resurrection, and from reckoning to eternity.

4. The Indictment of the Unyielding and the Prostration (20–25): Rebukes the deniers with astonishment: *“So what is [the matter] with them that they do not believe, and when the Qur'an is recited to them, they do not prostrate?”* It condemns their stubborn denial, promises the righteous an uninterrupted, unending reward (*ajrun ghayru mamnūn*), and commands the reader to prostrate before the Lord of the cosmos.

While human beings boast of their autonomy and defy their Creator, the physical universe—the vast celestial vault and the massive crust of the earth—listens with absolute, conscious submission to the divine command: *“And the earth when it is stretched out, and casts out what is in it and becomes empty, and has hearkened to its Lord and was obligated!”* (84:3–5).

## Verse 84:1

> When the sky bursts open

**Opening on a Condition**

The sūrah begins with *idhā*, when, and the conditional particle governs the four verses that follow. Nothing in the opening states a consequence; the consequence arrives in verse 5, and even there it is left unexpressed in the Arabic and supplied by the reader. The structure is the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, which opens with four conditions and answers them in the fifth verse, and in Sūrat al-Takwīr, which builds thirteen conditions before reaching its answer. This sūrah uses four conditions and an implied answer, and the implication means that the reader's own participation is required to complete the sentence. The opening therefore places the listener inside the grammar of the chapter before telling him anything.

**Inshaqqat**

The verb is the fifth form of *sh-q-q*, to split, cleave, and burst open. The fifth form is intransitive and intensive: the sky splits itself, or is split thoroughly, and the splitting is complete. The root differs from the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār — *f-ṭ-r*, which gave *infaṭarat*, the sūrah's title — but the event described is the same. Two adjacent chapters open with the rupture of the sky, each using a different verb from a different root, and the doubling is characteristic of the Makkan descriptions of the end. The fact is fixed; the word varies. A reader who encounters the two chapters in sequence hears the same event twice in two vocabularies, and the repetition removes any suggestion that the description is figurative.

## Verse 84:2

> obeying its Lord as it must

**Adhinat**

The verb is from *ʾ-dh-n*, the root of the ear, of hearing, and of permission. Its fourth form means giving ear, attending, hearkening, and by extension obeying — the same root gives *ādhana*, to announce, and *udhun*, an ear, and *idhn*, permission. Applied to the sky the verb describes an attention that issues in compliance. The sky has heard its Lord and has responded. Nothing in the verse describes a voice, a command, or a messenger, and the absence of a described command makes the obedience immediate: the sky listens and obeys in one act. The root's association with permission is also present — a thing that gives ear to its Lord has been granted the hearing, and the granting is what makes the response possible.

**An Obedience Without Speech**

The Qur'an describes the same compliance of the created order elsewhere in explicit terms. The heaven and the earth are told to submit, willingly or unwillingly, and they both respond that they submit willingly (41:11 — *“Then He turned towards the heaven when it was ˹still like˺ smoke, saying to it and to the earth, ‘Submit, willingly or unwillingly.’ They both responded, ‘We submit willingly.’”*). Everything in the heavens and the earth prostrates to God, willingly or unwillingly. The sun and the moon and the stars are described as subjected by His command. This verse belongs to that account and states it in the shortest form available: the sky listens to its Lord and it was fitting that it should. Nothing here describes a dialogue, and the absence of dialogue means the obedience is not a response to a request but a property of the thing. The sky obeys in bursting as it obeyed in being raised.

## Verse 84:3

> and when the earth is flattened out

**The Second Subject**

The chapter's conditions move from the sky to the earth, and the movement completes the description of the created order. Nothing between them is mentioned — no mountains, no seas, no stars — and the omission is deliberate. The two subjects are the container and the contained, the roof and the floor, and the two are enough for the argument. The earth's condition is described in three verses where the sky's was described in two, and the additional verse is the one that states the casting out of its contents. The proportion indicates that the chapter is more concerned with what the earth holds than with what covers it, and the concern is what prepares verse 6's address to the people who live on it.

**The Earth Changed**

The Qur'an describes the same transformation in several passages and with several images. The earth and the mountains are lifted and crushed with a single blow (69:14 — *“and the earth and mountains will be lifted up and crushed with one blow”*). The mountains are wiped out completely, leaving the earth level and bare (20:105–106 — *“leaving the earth level and bare”*). They are blown away, becoming like a mirage (78:20 — *“and the mountains will be blown away, becoming ˹like˺ a mirage”*). The earth is completely crushed over and over (89:21 — *“Enough! When the earth is entirely crushed over and over”*). The Day on which the earth will be changed into a different earth, and the heavens as well, is the Day on which all appear before God, the One, the Supreme (14:48 — *“˹Watch for˺ the Day ˹when˺ the earth will be changed into a different earth and the heavens as well, and all will appear before Allah—the One, the Supreme”*). The five descriptions agree on the removal of elevation and the production of a level surface, and this verse states the same outcome in a single passive verb.

**Level Ground and the Standing of the Day**

The Qur'an describes the gathering of the Day as a standing, and a standing requires ground. The flattening of this verse supplies it, and the supplying is what makes the description functional rather than merely pictorial. Mountains removed, elevations levelled, and the surface extended produce a plain on which everyone can be seen and no one can be hidden. Sūrat Ṭā Hā states the same outcome in the language of the questioner and the answerer: the mountains will be wiped out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, so that no curve and no elevation will be seen in it (20:105–107 — *“leaving the earth level and bare”*). The absence of curve and elevation is the absence of concealment, and the absence of concealment is what the reckoning of verses 7 to 12 requires.

## Verse 84:4

> and ejects ˹all˺ its contents and becomes empty

**Two Acts in One Verse**

The verse describes the earth doing two things: casting out what is in it, and becoming empty. The two are not redundant. Ejection is an act; emptiness is a state. A vessel can be emptied partially and still contain something; a vessel that has cast out its contents and become empty has nothing left. The verse specifies both the act and the resulting condition, and the specification is what makes the description complete.

**What Is In It**

*Mā fīhā* is indefinite and comprehensive: whatever is in it. The commentators specify the contents as the dead, who are brought out, and the treasures, which are thrown forth. Sūrat al-Zalzalah describes the same event from the side of the earth's shaking: when the earth is shaken in its ultimate quaking and throws out all its contents (99:1–2 — *“and when the earth throws out ˹all˺ its contents”*). Sūrat al-Infiṭār describes the graves spilling out and being turned inside (82:4 — *“and when the graves spill out”*). Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt asks whether people do not know that the contents of the graves will be spilled out (100:9 — *“Do they not know that when the contents of the graves will be spilled out”*). The three passages agree that the contents include the dead, and the inclusion is what makes the ejection a Last-Day event rather than a geological one.

## Verse 84:5

> obeying its Lord as it must, ˹surely you will all be judged˺

**Formula Repeated**

The sentence is identical to verse 2, word for word: it listened to its Lord, and it was fitting that it should. The repetition closes the description of the earth exactly as the earlier verse closed the description of the sky, and the closure produces a symmetrical first movement. Two subjects, two events, two identical statements of compliance. The symmetry is what allows the chapter to end its description here and turn to its address. Nothing further is said about the sky or the earth in the remaining twenty verses, and the reason nothing needs to be said is that both have been described as obeying. The formula's second occurrence is therefore a completion rather than an emphasis, and the completion is what frees the chapter to speak to people.

**Parallel in Sūrat al-Infiṭār**

The neighbouring chapter performs the same movement with an expressed answer. Four conditions — the sky splitting, the stars scattering, the seas bursting, the graves spilling out — are followed by the statement that each soul will know what it sent forward and left behind (82:1–5 — *“and when the graves spill out”*). The answer there is given; here it is withheld. The two chapters are therefore variants of one form, and the variation is instructive. Al-Infiṭār's answer is epistemic — the soul will know — while this chapter's supplied answer is judicial, that all will be judged. Both answers concern the individual person rather than the cosmos, and both make the cosmic events the occasion of a personal outcome. The correspondence between the two chapters is one of the clearest in the received order.

## Verse 84:6

> O humanity! Indeed, you are labouring restlessly towards your Lord, and will ˹eventually˺ meet the consequences

**The Address to the Species**

*Yā ayyuhā al-insān* — O human being. The vocative is addressed to the species rather than to a group, and it is the same address used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where humanity is asked what has emboldened it against its Lord (82:6 — *“O humanity! What has emboldened you against your Lord, the Most Generous”*). The two chapters are adjacent in the received order and both turn from a description of the sky's rupture to an address of this kind. The universality of the vocative excludes nobody: not the believer, not the disbeliever, not the Prophet, not the reader. Whatever follows is said to everyone, and the saying is what makes the chapter's second movement a summons rather than a report. Nothing in the address restricts its scope, and the unrestricted scope means that the description of labouring applies to whoever hears it.

**Meeting**

*Fa-mulāqīh* — and you will meet it, or meet Him. The word is from *l-q-y*, the same root used in the previous verse for the earth casting out its contents, and its fourth form means encountering, meeting, and facing. The noun of the root here is one who meets, and the object is a pronoun that can refer either to the Lord or to the labour. Both readings are transmitted and both are coherent. On the first, the person will meet his Lord; on the second, he will meet the result of his toil. The translation adopts the second and glosses it as meeting the consequences. The two readings are not alternatives in substance, since meeting the Lord is meeting the result of one's labour, and the ambiguity allows the verse to carry both at once.

## Verse 84:7

> As for those who are given their record in their right hand

**Right Hand**

*Bi-yamīnih*, in his right hand. The right hand in Arabic usage is the hand of giving, of oaths, of honour, and of strength; the left is the hand of what is unclean or of secondary use. The specification of the right is therefore not arbitrary but carries the sense of a favourable reception. Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah describes the same moment and adds the recipient's cry: those given their records in their right hand call out for everyone to read their record, certain that they would face their reckoning (69:19–20 — *“As for those given their records in their right hand, they will cry ˹happily˺, “Here ˹everyone˺! Read my record”*). Sūrat al-Isrāʾ adds that whoever is given his record in his right hand will read it happily and will not be wronged by the width of a date-stone's thread (17:71 — *“So whoever will be given their record in their right hand will read it ˹happily˺ and will not be wronged ˹even by the width of˺ the thread of a date stone”*). The three passages agree on the hand, the reading, and the joy.

**Division Begins**

Verse 6 addressed humanity as a whole and stated that everyone is labouring toward his Lord and will meet. This verse begins the division of that whole into two classes, and the division is introduced by the particle *fa-ammā*, as for — a topic marker that announces a case to be described. The second case follows in verse 10, and the two together exhaust the address of verse 6. Nothing in the chapter allows for a third class, and the absence of a third is consistent with its structure: a single toil, a single meeting, and two manners of receiving what the meeting produces. The verse under discussion states the first manner in a single clause and leaves its consequences for the two verses that follow.

**Record Given**

The verb is passive: *ūtiya kitābahu*, he was given his book. The passivity is significant and it matches the description of Paradise earlier in the neighbouring chapters, where the drink is given rather than obtained. Nothing in the verse describes the person reaching for the record, choosing it, or earning its delivery. It is handed to him, and the hand that receives it is specified. The record itself is the document described elsewhere as produced by noble angels who write and know whatever people do (82:10–12 — *“They know whatever you do”*), as sealed in Sijjīn and in ʿIlliyūn (83:9 — *“a fate ˹already˺ sealed”*, 83:20 — *“a fate ˹already˺ sealed”*), and as laid open on the Day with the command to read it (17:13–14 — *“We have bound every human’s destiny to their neck. And on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each ˹person˺ a record which they will find laid open”*). This verse describes the moment of delivery and specifies the manner.

**A Class Defined by What It Receives**

Nothing in this verse describes the people concerned by their conduct, their belief, or their character. They are defined solely by what is put into their hand. The definition is remarkable because it makes the outcome before the description, and the chapter then supplies the character of the outcome — an easy reckoning and a joyful return — without ever supplying the character of the people. The technique places the reader's attention on the delivery rather than on the recipient, and the placement is what allows the summons at the end of the chapter to apply to everyone listening.

## Verse 84:8

> they will have an easy reckoning

**The Explanation Given in the Ḥadīth**

ʿĀʾishah reported that the Prophet said whoever is called to account will be punished, and she asked whether God had not said that he will receive an easy reckoning, citing this verse. He replied that this means only the presentation of the accounts, and that whoever is argued with about his account will be ruined (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 103). The explanation distinguishes two kinds of reckoning: a presentation, in which the deeds are shown and acknowledged, and a discussion, in which each item is examined and contested. The easy reckoning of this verse is the first, and the ruin belongs to the second. Nothing in the verse itself makes the distinction, and the distinction is what the report supplies — the ease is not in the quantity of what is counted but in the absence of argument about it.

**The Presentation of Accounts**

The notion of a presentation is consistent with the chapter's description of the record. A book is given in the right hand, and the person is told elsewhere to read his own record, being enough to take account of himself (17:14 — *“˹And it will be said,˺ “Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself.”*). The reading is the presentation: what was written is shown to the one who did it, and the showing is complete. Nothing in the presentation is hidden from the reader, and nothing in it is disputed by him. The ease consists in the absence of dispute rather than in the absence of content, and the absence of dispute is what distinguishes the first class from the second. A person whose record is argued with him is a person whose deeds are contested, and the contest is what produces ruin.

## Verse 84:9

> and will return to their people joyfully

**The Return**

*Yanqalibu* is from *q-l-b*, the root of turning over, inverting, and changing direction. Its eighth form means turning back, returning, and being transformed. The verse describes the person of the right hand turning back toward his people, and the turning is a movement with a destination. The same verb appears in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn for the mockers returning to their own households to recount their exploits with relish (83:31 — *“and muse ˹over these exploits˺ upon returning to their own people”*). The two returns are described with the same root and in opposite moral directions: one turns back to a household for the pleasure of derision, the other turns back to a people in joy. The recurrence of the verb across two adjacent chapters is one of the quietest contrasts in the received order.

**Masrūr**

The word means one who is pleased, delighted, gladdened — a joy that is visible and complete. The same root gives *surūr*, delight, and it is used in the Qur'an for the state of the people of Paradise: faces on that Day will be bright, looking at their Lord (75:22–23 — *“looking at their Lord”*), and the people of Paradise will be in joy and delight. The adjective here is singular, describing the person who returns, and the singularity is grammatically notable after the plural subject of verse 7.

**Joy and the People Who Mocked**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn describes the wicked as joyful among their people in this world — *masrūr*, the same word used here — and the chapter states that their joy was the joy of people who thought they would never return to God (83:13–14 — *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, “Ancient fables!” But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit”*). The two uses of the word across adjacent chapters are the sharpest contrast available. One joy is described as belonging to a household of mockers in this life and as resting on the supposition that there is no return; the other is described as belonging to a household of the blessed and as following a return that has happened. The verse under discussion therefore contains, in one adjective, the answer to the supposition named in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn. There is a return, and it produces joy. After this verse the chapter turns to the second class and does not return to the first until the closing verse, where those who believe and do good are given a never-ending reward. The completeness of the case is what allows the chapter to move on to the explanation of the second class's character in verses 13 to 15, and then to the oaths and the stages, and finally to the question about belief.

## Verse 84:10

> And as for those who are given their record ˹in their left hand˺ from behind their backs

**Left Hand**

The Arabic of this verse specifies the position — from behind his back — and leaves the hand to be understood, which the translation supplies in brackets. The understood left hand is required by the contrast with verse 7 and is confirmed by the parallel description in Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah, where the one given his record in his left hand cries out that he wishes he had not been given it and had not known his reckoning (69:25–26 — *“And as for those given their record in their left hand, they will cry ˹bitterly˺, “I wish I had not been given my record nor known anything of my reckoning”*). The bracketing therefore marks a genuine ellipsis rather than an interpretive addition. The Qur'an's descriptions of the two classes consistently pair the right hand with the left, and the pairing is what makes the delivery in this verse intelligible without the word being spoken.

The verse opens with the same topic marker used in verse 7 — *wa-ammā*, and as for — and the repetition establishes the two cases as a matched pair. Nothing between them intervenes except the two clauses describing the first, and the proximity means that the reader holds both manners of receipt in mind at once.

**Same Document**

The record given behind the back is the same kind of document as the one given in the right hand. Both were written by the keepers described in Sūrat al-Infiṭār — noble angels who record everything and know whatever people do (82:10–12 — *“They know whatever you do”*). Both are sealed, as the two books of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn are sealed (83:9 — *“a fate ˹already˺ sealed”*, 83:20 — *“a fate ˹already˺ sealed”*). Both are produced on the Day and read by their owners. Nothing distinguishes the two documents except what is written in them and the manner in which they are delivered. The chapter therefore locates the whole of the difference in the conduct that filled the book and in the honour or shame of the handing over, and neither of these is a matter of the document's nature.

**Shame as a Feature of the Scene**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Day make shame visible. Faces on that Day will be humiliated; the wicked will see their record and cry out at what is in it (18:49 — *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it.”*); the one who receives his book in his left hand will wish he had not received it and that his death had been the end (69:25–27 — *“I wish death was the end”*). The shame is not an inner state described by a narrator but a public condition shown in faces, cries, and postures. This verse contributes the posture: a person taking his document from behind his back. The posture is visible to everyone present, and the visibility is what makes it a feature of the scene rather than a private experience. The chapter's interest in what can be seen is consistent throughout its second movement.

## Verse 84:11

> they will cry for ˹instant˺ destruction

**Same Cry in al-Furqān**

Sūrat al-Furqān describes the scene in fuller terms. When the Fire sees the deniers from a distance they hear it fuming and growling, and when they are tossed into a narrow place inside it, chained together, they cry out for instant destruction (25:12–13 — *“Once it sees them from a distance, they will hear it fuming and growling”*). The cry there is answered: do not cry only once for destruction, but cry many times over (25:14 — *“˹They will be told,˺ “Do not cry only once for destruction, but cry many times over!”*). The answer indicates that the invocation is heard and that what is invoked cannot be granted, and the ungratability is the severity of the scene. This chapter gives the cry without the answer, and the absence of the answer leaves the invocation hanging. The reader is told what the person says and not what is said to him, and the withholding is consistent with the chapter's economy.

**Same Cry in al-Ḥāqqah**

Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah gives the speech of the one who receives his record in his left hand, and the speech is a wish rather than a call: he wishes he had not been given his record, that he had not known his reckoning, and that his death had been the end (69:25–27 — *“I wish death was the end”*). The three wishes correspond to the three elements of this chapter's second case — the delivery, the reckoning, and the survival into the Fire. The parallel shows that the cry for destruction and the wish for a finished death are the same utterance in two forms. Nothing in either chapter describes the wish being granted, and the absence of granting is what makes the description of the second class complete: the document is received, the cry is made, and the fire is entered.

**An Invocation That Cannot Be Answered**

The verse describes a person calling for something, and the Qur'an's account of the Day is that certain calls are not answered there. No soul will avail another anything, and all authority on that Day belongs to God (82:19 — *“˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”*). No intercession benefits except by permission. The cry for destruction belongs to this category: it is uttered, it is heard by whoever is present, and it produces nothing. The futility of the invocation is what distinguishes it from the prayer of this world, which is described elsewhere as answered. The chapter does not state the futility, and the statement is unnecessary, because the following verse describes the burning. A person who is burning and calling for destruction is a person whose call has not been answered, and the non-answer is the description.

## Verse 84:12

> and will burn in the blazing Fire

**The Verb of Entering Fire**

*Yaṣlā* is from *ṣ-l-y*, the root used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the wicked burning in the Fire on the Day of Judgement (82:15 — *“burning in it on Judgment Day”*) and in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn for the same class burning in Hell (83:16 — *“Moreover, they will surely burn in Hell”*). The root means exposure to fire, entering it, and undergoing its heat, and its form here is active: they burn. The active voice attributes the undergoing to the subjects rather than to an agent who applies it, and the attribution is consistent across the three chapters. Nothing in any of the verses describes someone casting these people in; each states that they burn, and the stating places them inside the fire as a condition rather than describing an action performed on them.

**The Third Term of the Second Case**

Verses 10, 11, and 12 form a single statement about those given their record from behind their backs: the delivery, the cry, and the burning. The three correspond exactly to the three terms of the first case in verses 7 to 9: the delivery in the right hand, the easy reckoning, and the joyful return. The symmetry is complete and it is the chapter's central structural device.

**Saʿīr**

The name of the fire here is *saʿīr*, a blazing or fiercely kindled fire, from a root meaning to flare, rage, and blaze up. The Qur'an uses the word as one of the names of the Fire and as a description of its state: God has prepared for the disbelievers a blazing Fire (33:64 — *“Surely Allah condemns the disbelievers, and has prepared for them a blazing Fire”*). Those who consume the wealth of orphans unjustly consume nothing but fire into their bellies and will be burned in a blazing Hell (4:10 — *“Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell”*). Sūrat al-Takwīr describes the moment of its kindling — when the Hellfire is fiercely flared up (81:12 — *“and when the Hellfire is fiercely flared up”*). The recurrence establishes *saʿīr* as a name for the Fire in its state of maximum burning, and its use here specifies the intensity rather than the location or the duration.

**The Completion of the Two Cases**

After this verse the chapter stops describing outcomes and begins explaining causes. Verses 13 to 15 diagnose the second class — joyful among their people, supposing there would be no return, and seen throughout by their Lord. Verses 16 to 18 swear three oaths, and verse 19 states what humanity will pass through. The description of the two destinations is therefore complete in six verses, and its completeness is what allows the chapter to turn to the person's suppositions. Nothing further will be said about the fire or about the return to a joyful company, and the absence of further description means that both destinations have been given in their final form.

## Verse 84:13

> For they used to be prideful among their people

**The Explanation Attached to the Second Case Only**

Verses 13 to 15 explain why the second class receives its record from behind its back, cries for destruction, and burns. No corresponding explanation is given for the first class. The chapter does not say why the record is given in the right hand, why the reckoning is easy, or why the return is joyful. The asymmetry is deliberate: the blessed state is described and left undescribed in its causes, while the ruined state is described and then diagnosed. The diagnosis occupies three verses, and the three verses turn the chapter's attention from outcome to character, which is where its summons operates. A reader cannot choose his outcome directly; he can choose his character, and the character is what the explanation addresses.

**The Household as the Place of Confirmation**

A man among his people is a man whose self-assessment is echoed back to him. The household confirms his position, shares his assumptions, and does not contradict his suppositions. The Qur'an describes the same mechanism in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, where the mockers return to their own people and recount their exploits with relish (83:31 — *“and muse ˹over these exploits˺ upon returning to their own people”*). The recollection at home is a rehearsal of a shared view, and the rehearsal is what fixes it. Nothing in this verse describes the household as wicked, and the description is unnecessary. The fault named is the ease of a man among people who do not challenge him, and the absence of challenge is what allows the supposition of verse 14 to remain unexamined.

---

## Verse 84:14

> thinking they would never return ˹to Allah˺

**The Supposition Named**

Verse 13 described a state — a man at ease among his people. This verse names the thought that made the state possible: he supposed that he would never return. The supposition is the chapter's diagnosis of the second class, and the diagnosis is not about conduct but about expectation. Nothing in the verse accuses these people of specific sins; it accuses them of a belief about the future. The belief is negative — that a return will not happen — and its negativity is what allows the ease of the previous verse to continue unexamined. A man who expects no return has no reason to look at what he is doing, and the absence of looking is the whole of the fault.

**The Thought That Made the Ease Possible**

Verses 13 and 14 are joined by *inna* in both, and the joining makes the second the ground of the first. He was at ease among his people because he supposed that he would never return. The causality is stated grammatically rather than argued, and the statement is the chapter's account of how a person comes to live without reckoning. Nothing in the ease described in verse 13 required a denial of God; it required only a denial of return. A person can acknowledge a Creator and still live as though he will not go back to Him, and the living is what the chapter identifies as the fault. The supposition is therefore practical rather than theoretical, and its practical character is what makes it available to anyone.

**The Same Supposition Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes this expectation in several places. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn asks whether the defrauders do not think that they will be resurrected for a tremendous Day (83:4 — *“Do such people not think that they will be resurrected”*). Sūrat al-Nabaʾ states that the disbelievers never expected any reckoning (78:27 — *“For they never expected any reckoning”*). Sūrat al-Kahf describes the man who enters his garden saying that he does not think the Hour will ever come (18:36 — *“nor do I think the Hour will ˹ever˺ come. And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, I will definitely get a far better outcome than ˹all˺ this.”*). The three passages agree that the fault is an absent expectation rather than a reasoned denial, and the agreement makes this verse part of a settled diagnosis. Nothing in any of the passages describes the person as having investigated the question and reached a negative conclusion; each describes him as not expecting, not reckoning, not thinking.

---

## Verse 84:15

> Yes ˹they would˺! Surely their Lord has always been All-Seeing of them

**The Particle That Reverses a Negative**

*Balā* is the particle used to affirm what a preceding negative statement has denied. Where *lā* negates and *kallā* refuses, *balā* answers a negation with a yes. Its function here is precise: the previous verse stated that they supposed they would never return, and this particle says that they will. One word reverses the supposition of the verse before it, and the reversal is not argued but asserted. The chapter's refutation of the second class's belief is therefore the shortest possible — a single particle followed by a statement about God's seeing. Nothing in the refutation engages with the reasons for the supposition, because the supposition had no reasons. It was an absence of expectation, and an absence is answered by an assertion.

**Baṣīr**

The word is from *b-ṣ-r*, the root of sight, insight, and perception. As a divine name *al-Baṣīr* means the one who sees completely — nothing is too small, too distant, too hidden, or too brief to be seen. The Qur'an pairs the name with *al-Samīʿ*, the all-hearing, in descriptions of God's knowledge of what people do and say. Its application here is to a class of people who considered themselves unobserved. The ease of verse 13 was the ease of a man among his own, in a setting where nothing challenged his position; the seeing of this verse states that the setting was never private. Nothing in the verse describes what was seen, and the absence of description makes the seeing comprehensive.

---

## Verse 84:16

> So, I do swear by the twilight

**The Oath Introduced**

The chapter's third movement begins with an oath, and the oath is introduced by a formula: *fa-lā uqsimu*, so I swear, or no — I swear. The construction combines a negative particle with a verb of swearing, and the commentators explain it in two ways. Either the negative responds to an unspoken denial — as though refusing a claim that no oath is needed before affirming that one is being sworn — or it is an intensifying prefix that adds force to the swearing. Both readings produce the same result: a sworn statement of unusual emphasis. The translation adopts the second, rendering the phrase as "I do swear", with the emphatic auxiliary carrying the force that the particle contributes.

**Why the Twilight Is Sworn By**

An oath in the Qur'an swears by something whose nature supports the statement being sworn to. The twilight is a transition: light passing into darkness, one state giving way to another, a boundary crossed every day without fail. The statement that the oath introduces in verse 19 is that humanity will pass from stage to stage. The correspondence is exact. A thing that changes its condition every evening is the appropriate witness to a claim about change, and the choice of the twilight rather than of the sun or the day indicates that the chapter is swearing by transition rather than by permanence.

**Oaths by Times in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an swears by times of day and night repeatedly: by the night when it covers (92:1 — *“By the night when it covers”*), by the morning brightness (93:1 — *“By the morning sunlight”*), by the dawn (89:1 — *“By the dawn”*), by the night as it departs and the morning as it brightens (74:33–34 — *“and the night as it retreats and the day as it breaks”*). The practice establishes the times as a recognized category of oath-object, and this chapter's three oaths belong to the category. What distinguishes them is that all three are celestial and sequential — the twilight, the night and what it gathers, the moon when it becomes full. The sequence describes a single evening from the last light through the darkness to the moon's fullness, and the sequencing is what makes the three oaths a single movement rather than three separate ones.

---

## Verse 84:17

> And by the night and whatever it envelops

**The Night as a Gatherer**

Sūrat al-Layl swears by the night when it covers (92:1 — *“By the night when it covers”*), using a verb of covering rather than of gathering. The two descriptions are complementary: covering states the night's effect on what is beneath it, and gathering states its effect on what moves. Together they produce the picture of a period in which everything dispersed is collected and everything visible is hidden. Nothing in either description is moral, and the absence of moral characterization is what makes the night an appropriate object of an oath. It is a fact of the world, recurrent and universal, and its recurrence is what qualifies it as a witness to a claim about the recurrent stages of human life.

**What the Night Envelops**

The object of the gathering is indefinite — *mā wasaq*, whatever it gathers — and the indefiniteness makes it comprehensive. Everything the night contains is inside the oath: the sleeper and the watcher, the animal returning to its shelter and the person returning to his house, the deeds done in darkness and the things hidden from sight. The comprehensiveness is significant for the chapter's argument. Verse 13 described a man at ease among his people, and verse 15 stated that his Lord has always been seeing him. The oath by the night and whatever it gathers invokes the time in which concealment seems most complete, and the invocation is what makes the following statement about seeing and passing more forceful.

---

## Verse 84:18

> And by the moon when it waxes full

**The Third Oath**

The sequence of oaths ends with the moon, and the moon is sworn by at a specific moment: when it becomes full. The specification is the verse's whole content. The moon is not invoked in its rising, its crescent, or its waning, but at the point of its completion. The choice completes the sequence begun in the previous two verses — a transition, a gathering, and a culmination — and the culmination is what the sworn statement of verse 19 requires. A person passing from stage to stage passes toward something, and the full moon is the image of arrival at a completed state.

**The Moon in the Qur'an's Oaths**

The Qur'an swears by the moon in several places: by the moon in Sūrat al-Muddaththir, as a sign among the greatest (74:32 — *“But no! By the moon”*). By the night as it departs and the morning as it brightens (74:33–34 — *“and the night as it retreats and the day as it breaks”*); and the moon is described elsewhere as a light and as one of the signs by which time is reckoned. Its use here at the moment of fullness is distinctive, because the full moon is the only phase that is complete. The crescent is a beginning, the waning is an ending, and the fullness is a state of arrival. The chapter swears by the arrival, and the swearing prepares a statement about the stages through which a person passes.

---

## Verse 84:19

> You will certainly pass from one state to another

**The Answer to the Three Oaths**

Verses 16 to 18 swore by the twilight, by the night and whatever it envelops, and by the moon at full. This verse is the statement sworn to, and it is the only statement the three oaths support. Nothing else in the chapter is placed under an oath, and the placement indicates that this claim is the one the chapter considers most in need of emphasis. The claim is short: you will pass from stage to stage. Everything the chapter has described — the bursting sky, the emptied earth, the toil, the two records, the two destinations — is contained within it, and the containment is what makes the verse the chapter's summary.

**You Will Mount**

The verb is *tarkabunna*, from *r-k-b*, the root of mounting, riding, boarding, and composing. The form here carries the emphatic *nūn*, which strengthens the statement into an assertion that cannot be qualified. The verb's basic sense is to mount something and be carried by it, and its application to stages describes a person boarding one condition and being carried into the next. The image is of a traveller who does not control the vehicle.

**Stage Upon Stage**

*Ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq* — a layer after a layer, a stage following a stage, one condition giving way to the next. The word *ṭabaq* means a stratum, a level, a lid, and a stage of a progression, and the construction with *ʿan* describes succession: from one to the one after it. The phrase is general, and its generality has produced several readings of its reference. Some take it as the stages of the human life — weakness, strength, weakness and old age — as described elsewhere in the Qur'an (30:54 — *“It is Allah Who created you in a state of weakness, then developed ˹your˺ weakness into strength, then developed ˹your˺ strength into weakness and old age.”*, 40:67 — *“He is the One Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old—though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah’s power˺”*). Others take it as the stages from this world to the grave to the resurrection to the reckoning. Others take it as states of condition: poverty after wealth, sickness after health, death after life.

**Created in Stages**

The Qur'an uses the same vocabulary for the creation of the human being. Sūrat Nūḥ asks why people do not hope for dignity when He truly created them in stages of development (71:13–14 — *“when He truly created you in stages ˹of development˺”*). Sūrat Ghāfir describes the progression from dust to a sperm-drop to a clinging clot to infancy to prime to old age to an appointed term (40:67 — *“He is the One Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old—though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah’s power˺”*). Sūrat al-Rūm gives the same progression in three terms: weakness, then strength, then weakness and old age (30:54 — *“It is Allah Who created you in a state of weakness, then developed ˹your˺ weakness into strength, then developed ˹your˺ strength into weakness and old age.”*). The recurrence establishes staged progression as a recognized description of the human being, and this verse extends the description beyond the life of the body to the states that follow it.

---

## Verse 84:20

> So what is the matter with them that they do not believe

**Turning to the Third Person**

Verse 19 addressed the listener in the second person plural: you will pass from stage to stage. This verse turns to the third person: what is the matter with *them*. The shift is abrupt and unmarked, and it has a specific effect. The listener who has just been told that he will pass through stages is now placed beside the speaker, looking at a group that does not believe. The position is an invitation: the listener is being addressed as someone for whom the question is intelligible, and the intelligibility implies that he is not among those being asked about. Nothing in the verse names the group, and the naming arrives in verse 22 as the disbelievers.

**Question Left Unanswered**

Nothing in the following verses answers the question directly. Verse 21 adds a second element to it — when the Qur'an is recited they do not bow down. Verse 22 states that the disbelievers persist in denial. Verse 23 says that God knows best what they hide. The three verses are responses to the question in the sense that they describe the people being asked about, and the descriptions supply the answer: there is nothing the matter with them that can be identified from outside, because the fault is inside and God knows it best. The withholding of a direct answer is what makes the question function as an expression of astonishment rather than as an inquiry.

---

## Verse 84:21

> and when the Quran is recited to them, they do not bow down ˹in submission˺

**They Do Not Bow Down**

*Lā yaskudūn* — they do not prostrate. The verb is from *s-j-d*, the root of prostration, which in the Qur'an means the act of placing the face on the ground in submission to God. The prostration is the most complete bodily expression of submission available in the Arabic practice of the revelation's audience, and its absence is therefore a significant fact rather than a minor omission. Nothing in the verse says that they refuse, mock, or leave; it says only that they do not bow.

**Prostration as the Response to Recitation**

Sūrat al-Sajdah describes the response of belief in exactly these terms: the only true believers in Our revelation are those who, when it is recited to them, fall into prostration, glorify the praises of their Lord, and are not too proud (32:15 — *“The only ˹true˺ believers in Our revelation are those who—when it is recited to them—fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud”*). The three elements — prostration, glorification, and the absence of pride — are presented as the marks of belief, and their absence is the mark of its absence. This verse names the first element and its absence, and the naming relies on the reader's acquaintance with the description in Sūrat al-Sajdah. Nothing here explains why prostration is the expected response, and the explanation is unnecessary for an audience that has heard the earlier description.

**The Occasion of Recitation**

The chapter's parallel passages make the recitation the standard occasion for the rejection. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn describes the response to recitation as a dismissal: whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say ancient fables (83:13 — *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, “Ancient fables!”*). Sūrat al-Qalam describes the same response in the singular (68:15 — *“whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, “Ancient fables!”*). Sūrat al-Jāthiyah describes people who hear God's revelations recited and then persist arrogantly as though they had not heard them (45:8 — *“They hear Allah’s revelations recited to them, then persist ˹in denial˺ arrogantly as if they did not hear them. So give them good news of a painful punishment”*). The four passages agree that the recitation is public, that it is heard, and that the hearing produces either a dismissal or a silence. This verse describes the silence — no words, no bowing, only the absence of a response.

---

## Verse 84:22

> In fact, the disbelievers persist in denial

**Yukadhdhibūn**

The verb is the intensive form of calling something false, and it is in the imperfect, meaning a continuing activity. The same verb appears in Sūrat al-Infiṭār in the accusation that humanity denies the final Judgement (82:9 — *“But no! In fact, you deny the ˹final˺ Judgment”*) and in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn in the description of those who deny the Day of Judgement (83:11 — *“those who deny Judgment Day”*). The intensive form adds repetition and effort: not merely calling something false once but persisting in the calling. The translation renders the persistence explicitly, and the rendering is faithful to the form. Nothing in the verse describes a single act of denial or a particular claim being denied; the denial is a state, and the state is what the verb's aspect conveys.

**What Is Denied**

The object of the denial is not stated, and the unstated object is comprehensive. The chapter has described the bursting sky, the emptied earth, the toil toward the Lord, the meeting, the two records, the two destinations, the diagnosis of the second class, and the stages of human existence. The denial covers all of it. Nothing in the verse restricts the denial to the Day of Judgement as Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn does, or to the revelations as Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn's verse 13 does. The denial here is general, and the generality is consistent with the question of verses 20 and 21, which was about belief as such and about the response to recitation as such.

The diagnosis is also the last word the chapter says about these people as a group.

---

## Verse 84:23

> But Allah knows best whatever they hide

**Concealment Against Seeing**

Verse 15 stated that their Lord has always been all-seeing of them, and this verse states that He knows best what they hold inside. The two statements are complementary and they belong to the same argument. The seeing of verse 15 answered the supposition of a man at ease among his people; the knowing of this verse answers the persistence in denial described in verse 22. A person who denies outwardly may suppose that his inward state is his own, and the verse removes the supposition. Nothing in it accuses these people of concealing something specific; the concealment is general, and the generality means that whatever is inside is inside God's knowledge.

**Knowledge and the Record**

The chapter's account of the two records assumes a complete knowledge of what people did. The document given in the right hand and the document given from behind the back were written by keepers who record everything and know whatever people do (82:10–12 — *“They know whatever you do”*), and the writing was sealed (83:9 — *“a fate ˹already˺ sealed”*, 83:20 — *“a fate ˹already˺ sealed”*). This verse supplies the knowledge behind the record. Nothing in the descriptions of the writing says how the deeds are known; the verse states that God knows best what people contain, and the stating closes the gap. The record is therefore not a collection of observed events but a document produced by a knowledge that includes what was never observed by anyone else.

---

## Verse 84:24

> So give them good news of a painful punishment

**A Word Used in Irony**

*Bashshir* is the imperative of giving good news — the verb used throughout the Qur'an for announcing Paradise, forgiveness, and reward to the believers. Its application here to a painful punishment is ironic, and the irony is a recognized device of the revelation's style. The command asks the Prophet to announce punishment in the form used for announcing good, and the form's inappropriateness is what carries the meaning. Nothing in the verse marks the irony with a particle or an explanation, and the absence of marking means the reader must recognize it. The recognition is what makes the verse effective: an announcement of ruin delivered in the language of glad tidings is more severe than a plain statement of ruin.

**The Same Irony Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses the device repeatedly. Those who deny God's signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice are told: give them good news of a painful punishment (3:21 — *“Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment”*). Those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend in God's cause are given the same announcement (9:34 — *“Give good news of a painful torment to those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in Allah’s cause”*). The people who hear God's revelations recited and persist arrogantly as though they had not heard them are addressed with it (45:8 — *“They hear Allah’s revelations recited to them, then persist ˹in denial˺ arrogantly as if they did not hear them. So give them good news of a painful punishment”*). The recurrence establishes the construction as a settled form, and the settling means that a reader encountering it here recognizes it as irony rather than mistaking it for a promise. Three of the four occurrences concern people who have heard the revelation and refused it, which is the situation described in verse 21 of this chapter.

---

## Verse 84:25

> But those who believe and do good will have a never-ending reward

**The Exception**

*Illā* — except, but. The particle follows the announcement of a painful punishment and introduces the group excluded from it. The exclusion is not from the command to announce but from the punishment announced, and the construction is the Qur'an's standard way of separating two outcomes after a statement about one of them. Nothing in the verse describes the exception as conditional or as earned; it states that a group has a reward, and the group is defined by two features.

**A Never-Ending Reward**

*Ajr ghayr mamnūn* — a reward that is not cut off, not withheld, not diminished, not conferred with reproach. The participle is from *m-n-n*, a root with two related senses: to sever or weaken, and to confer a favour in a way that reminds the recipient of it. Both senses are relevant. A reward that is *mamnūn* would either be interrupted or be given with an upbraiding that diminishes it; a reward that is *ghayr mamnūn* is neither. The phrase therefore describes a reward that continues and that carries no reproach, and the two features are what distinguish it from every reward available in this life.

**The Same Phrase Elsewhere**

The formula appears in the closing verses of two other short Makkan chapters. Sūrat al-Tīn ends with the statement that those who believe and do good will have a never-ending reward (95:6 — *“except those who believe and do good—they will have a never-ending reward”*). Sūrat Fuṣṣilat gives the same phrase: those who believe and do good will certainly have a never-ending reward (41:8 — *“˹But˺ those who believe and do good will certainly have a never-ending reward”*). Sūrat Hūd describes the people destined to joy as being in Paradise forever, a generous giving without end (11:108 — *“And as for those destined to joy, they will be in Paradise, staying there forever, as long as the heavens and the earth will endure, except what your Lord wills—a ˹generous˺ giving, without end”*). The recurrence across chapters establishes the phrase as a settled description of the reward, and the settling means that the reader receives it here as a known quantity rather than a new claim. The chapter's ending is therefore a quotation of the revelation's standard promise.
