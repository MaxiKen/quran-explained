# Sūrah al-Qadr (Chapter 97) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Qadr 97:1

> Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ sent this ˹Quran˺ down on the Night of Glory

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Pronoun With Nothing Before It to Refer To**

The sūrah opens with *innā anzalnāhu* — indeed, We sent *it* down — and no noun has been mentioned. There is no antecedent for the pronoun, and the Arabic does not supply one. The listeners knew what "it" was, and the omission is a recognised device: a thing so present in the minds of speaker and hearer that naming it would be redundant, and its absence from the sentence increases rather than decreases its weight. The emphatic particle *inna* and the first-person plural add to the effect. The statement is not "the Qur'an was revealed"; it is "We — We sent it down," with the agent named and repeated. The verse establishes three facts in three words: that the descent was real, that it was God's own act, and that its object was already familiar to everyone listening.

**Laylat al-Qadr and the Three Meanings of the Name**

*Qadr* carries a cluster of related senses, and the commentators have drawn on all of them. It means decree or determination, from the verb *qadara*, to measure and to ordain; it means rank, honour and worth; and it means constraint or narrowness. The first gives the night its function: on it, matters for the coming year are determined, which is the reading associated with Mujāhid and supported by the parallel in Sūrat al-Dukhān: on that night every matter of wisdom is ordained (44:4). The second gives it its standing: a night of rank, honoured above other nights, which is what verse three states directly. The third is rarer, but some exegetes connect it to the descent of so many angels that the earth is constricted by them. The name is not a label with a single fixed sense; it is a word that gathers the night's decree, its honour and its density into one term.

**The Night Is in Ramadan, and the Qur'an Says So**

The sūrah does not name the month, but another verse does. Ramaḍān is the month in which the Qur'an was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard to distinguish between right and wrong (2:185). Since this verse places the descent of the Qur'an in Ramaḍān and al-Qadr places it on a night, the two together fix the event inside that month, and the Muslim tradition has never doubted the conclusion. The reports about the practice of the Prophet ﷺ narrow it further to the last ten nights, and more specifically to the odd-numbered nights among them: search for the Night of Decree in the odd nights among the last ten nights of Ramaḍān, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2017 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1169 from ʿĀʾishah. The concealment of the exact date is itself part of the arrangement, since a known date would concentrate the effort and end it.

**Two Stages of One Descent**

The exegetes read this verse together with the verses that describe the Qur'an's gradual delivery, and the combination produces the classical account of a two-stage revelation. The first stage is the descent as a whole, described here; the second is its delivery over roughly twenty-three years, which the Qur'an itself describes: it is a Qur'an We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace (17:106), and again in answer to the objection that it should have come all at once: We have sent it in stages so We may reassure your heart with it (25:32). The source from which it descended is described elsewhere as a Preserved Tablet (85:22). The two-stage account explains why the revelation arrived in fragments responding to events while remaining, in the tradition's understanding, a single book with a single origin.

**A Blessed Night in Another Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Dukhān opens with an oath by the clear Book and then states: indeed, We sent it down on a blessed night, for We always warn against evil (44:3). The parallel is close enough that the two verses are read as describing the same event, and the addition in al-Dukhān supplies a purpose — warning — that al-Qadr leaves implicit. The description "blessed" answers a question al-Qadr does not raise: what makes one night different from another. Nothing about a night in Ramaḍān distinguishes it astronomically. The blessing is conferred, not observed, and the conferral is an act of God described in both sūrahs in the same terms. Al-Qadr's contribution is the specificity: not merely a blessed night but a night of decree and rank, better than a thousand months.

**Why a Night and Not a Day**

Revelation in the Qur'an is consistently associated with night. The Prophet ﷺ received the first command in a cave during a period of withdrawal; the Night Journey is described in the opening words of Sūrat al-Isrāʾ; the sūrah of the night-comer swears by the night and by the star (86:1–3). The association is not arbitrary. Night is when the world's noise stops, when a person is alone, and when attention is not divided between worship and livelihood. The Qur'an describes the rising at night as more effective and more suitable for speech (73:6), and its description of the Prophet's practice during the last ten days of Ramaḍān shows the same preference. A night of decree is therefore consistent with the general pattern: the moment of greatest receptivity is the moment of least distraction, and the Qur'an was given at that moment rather than at noon.

**The Verse as the Ground of Everything After It**

Four verses follow, and all of them depend on this one. The greatness of the night, its comparison with a thousand months, the descent of the angels, the peace that lasts until dawn — none would be established without the initial statement that the Qur'an was sent down in it. The sūrah's structure is therefore a single argument: here is what happened on that night, and here is what follows from it. The verse is the shortest possible statement of the premise, and the sūrah does not elaborate on it. There is no account of the event, no description of the cave, no narrative. A fact is stated, and the remaining verses measure its consequence.

## Sūrah al-Qadr 97:2

> And what will make you realize what the Night of Glory is

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-Mā Adrāka: The Qur'an's Formula of Escalation**

The construction *wa-mā adrāka* is a set formula, and it does one thing: it announces that what follows exceeds what the listener can supply on his own. Literally, "and what will make you know," it is a question that does not seek information from the person asked. It marks a boundary. Up to this point the sūrah has been stating facts; from here it says that the facts it is about to state are of a kind the listener has no way of reaching by himself. The Qur'an uses the formula sparingly, which is why each use carries weight. Its appearance in the second verse of a five-verse sūrah tells the reader that the subject is not being introduced but being elevated, and that the description to come should be received as a disclosure rather than as an estimate.

**The Formula in Its Other Appearances**

The parallel uses show what the formula selects. And what will make you realize what the Inevitable Hour is? (69:3). And what will make you realize what the Striking Disaster is? (101:3). And what will make you realize what the Crusher is? (104:5). Every instance concerns something of ultimate consequence — the Hour, its calamities, its outcome. Not one is used for an ordinary subject. The formula is reserved for the Qur'an's largest themes, and its use for a night is significant: a single night is being placed in the same category as the Day of Judgment. The reason is not that the night is equivalent in kind but that its effect on a human life can be of that order, since what is decreed in it and what is granted in it reaches beyond the night itself.

**Mā Adrāka Against Mā Yudrīka**

The Qur'an distinguishes two similar constructions, and the distinction is exact. *Mā adrāka* — what will make you realize — is always followed by an answer in the text. *Mā yudrīka* — you never know — is followed by no answer at all: you never know, perhaps he may be purified (80:3); you never know, perhaps the Hour is near (33:63). The first says that knowledge will be given; the second says that it will not. Al-Qadr uses the first form, which means the sūrah intends to tell the listener something about the night rather than merely to impress him with its unknowability. The rhetorical question is not a refusal of information but a preface to it, and the following two verses deliver the information in the form of a comparison and a description. The construction promises disclosure and keeps the promise.

**The Question as a Measure of the Night's Greatness**

Rhetorical questions of this type work by implying that no answer is available. The listener is asked what will make him know, and he has nothing to offer. That silence is the point. The Qur'an could have said the night is exceedingly great, and the statement would have been received as a claim. By asking the question, it produces the recognition of greatness in the listener rather than asserting it, and the recognition is more durable than the assertion. Commentators note that the formula carries the sense that true knowledge of the night belongs to God alone, and that what the sūrah gives is as much as a human being needs. The verse therefore both magnifies the night and limits the inquiry, and it does both in seven Arabic words.

**What the Question Does to the Listener**

The effect is to stop the reader before he categorises the subject. A person who hears "the Night of Decree" may file it with other religious occasions and move on. A person who is asked what will make him realize what it is cannot file it, because the question implies that his existing categories will not contain it. The device is used in the Qur'an precisely where familiarity is the danger. The Hour is familiar as a word; the night is familiar as a name. The question breaks the familiarity and forces the listener to admit that he does not know. From that position the description that follows — better than a thousand months, angels descending, peace until dawn — lands as information rather than as decoration, because the listener has just conceded that he had none.

**Why Questions Rather Than Superlatives**

Arabic has abundant superlatives, and the Qur'an uses them. It also uses this interrogative form, and the choice between them is meaningful. A superlative tells the listener how to rank something; a question makes him discover that he cannot rank it. The second is stronger because it is not a claim about the thing but a statement about the listener's capacity. Al-Qadr uses both: the question in verse two and the comparison in verse three. The order matters. Had the comparison come first, it would have been evaluated against the listener's sense of what a thousand months means. Coming after the question, it arrives in a mind that has just admitted its own limits, and the comparison is received as a disclosure about a reality the listener cannot measure rather than as a figure of speech he can discount.

**The Answer Comes as a Comparison, Not a Definition**

The verse promises knowledge and the sūrah delivers it in the only form suited to the subject. No definition of the night is offered; no essence is described. Instead the listener is given a comparison with time he understands, a picture of descent, and a statement about peace. That is the shape of the disclosure the question promised. The method is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of everything beyond direct experience: it describes by relation rather than by essence, because the listener has no category into which the essence could be placed. A night that is better than a thousand months is not being defined but being located on a scale the listener already possesses, and the location is the knowledge the question offered to give.

## Sūrah al-Qadr 97:3

> The Night of Glory is better than a thousand months

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Arithmetic Behind the Comparison**

A thousand months is eighty-three years and four months. The figure is not round in years, and the oddness is informative: it is not a vague large number but a specific span, and the span is longer than the normal human life of the audience. The comparison therefore says that one night of worship exceeds the worship of an entire lifetime. Commentators draw this conclusion explicitly, and the arithmetic supports it without assistance. The verse does not say a thousand years, which would have been the obvious hyperbolic form; it says months, a unit a person counts in ordinary life. The choice of unit makes the comparison concrete rather than rhetorical, and it does so in a way the listener can verify for himself by simple multiplication.

**Better in What Respect**

The superiority is in reward, not in duration, and the commentators are clear on the point. Worship performed on this night carries a weight that the same worship performed across a thousand other months would not reach. The distinction matters because it prevents the verse from being read as a claim about time itself. The night is not longer than a lifetime; it is worth more than one. The mechanism is divine valuation rather than natural property — God assigns worth to acts, and the assignment is what the verse reports. This is why the promise is inseparable from the practice: the comparison applies to a night spent in worship, not to a night that merely passes while a person sleeps through it. The verse is a statement about value, and value attaches to what is done.

**A Thousand as a Number of Plenitude**

The Qur'an uses a thousand elsewhere without intending a precise count. They challenge you to hasten the torment, but a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting (22:47); and He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting (32:5). In both cases the number marks an unbridgeable difference in scale rather than a measurable interval. Some commentators take the thousand months of al-Qadr in the same sense, reading it as all time. The literal reading remains available and does no harm to the point, since a lifetime of worship is already the natural ceiling a human being can imagine. Whether the number is exact or plenitudinous, the comparison says the same thing: the night exceeds the maximum.

**What the Prophet ﷺ Said About Standing in This Night**

The promise has a verified prophetic statement attached to it. Abū Hurayrah reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: whoever establishes prayer on the Night of Decree out of sincere faith and hoping for reward from Allah, all his previous sins will be forgiven. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1901 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 760, and the wording ties the forgiveness to two conditions: faith and the expectation of reward, not display. The verse and the report together give the night its practical definition. It is not a night to be marked by celebration or by a special rite; it is a night to be stood in prayer. The report also explains why the sūrah's comparison is quantitative in form: forgiveness of everything preceding is the largest possible return on a single night, and it corresponds to the comparison with a lifetime.

**Why the Comparison Is Quantitative**

The Qur'an could have said the night is blessed, honoured, or great, and each would have been true. It says better than a thousand months, which is a comparison in the register human beings actually use when deciding how to spend their time. People weigh days against days and years against years; they calculate returns. The verse accepts that mode of reasoning and works inside it. A person who has spent a life in worship is being told that a single night, correctly used, outweighs it. The statement is addressed to the way people actually deliberate, and it is therefore actionable in a way that an abstract superlative would not be. Nothing in the verse requires mystical interpretation; it is a claim about value expressed in the units a working person understands.

**An Answer to the Shortness of Life**

The comparison has a pastoral edge that is easy to miss. A human life is short, and the Qur'an is unusually candid about the fact: it swears by the declining afternoon that humanity is in grave loss (103:1–2). A person who realises at forty that he has wasted twenty years cannot recover them. The verse offers a mechanism by which the loss can be offset — not by living longer but by encountering a night whose worth exceeds the years that are gone. This is why the concealment of the exact date matters. A known date could be prepared for like an appointment and then forgotten for another year; an uncertain date spread across ten nights produces sustained attention. The arrangement is designed for people with short lives and unreliable intentions, and it gives them a way to recover what shortness has taken.

**The Blessing Is Attached to a Time**

The verse locates the blessing in the night rather than in a person, a place or a lineage. Islamic teaching generally distributes excellence among people — the most noble of you is the most mindful of God (49:13) — and it refuses to rank human beings by ancestry. Here a period of time is ranked, and the ranking is absolute: better than a thousand months. The effect is to make the blessing universally available. Any believer in any century who stands in this night has access to what it offers, without ancestry, without status, without prior achievement, and without travel to a particular place. The night comes to the believer rather than the believer going to it, and the comparison in the verse is the measure of what arrives.

## Sūrah al-Qadr 97:4

> That night the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every ˹decreed˺ matter

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tanazzalu: A Descent That Happens in Stages**

The verb is *tanazzalu*, a form that denotes gradual or repeated descent rather than a single movement. The simple form would have said they descend; the form used says they come down in stages, or repeatedly, or in companies. Commentators read it both ways, and both are supported by the context. A descent in companies fits the picture of an assembly arriving; a repeated descent fits a night that lasts from sunset to dawn, in which the coming is continuous rather than instantaneous. The Qur'an uses related forms elsewhere for the delivery of revelation itself, which was given in stages over years (25:32). The choice of verb therefore describes a process, not an event, and it matches the character of the night: something is happening across its whole length, not at one moment inside it.

**Al-Rūḥ: Gabriel and the Alternative Readings**

The majority identify the spirit with Gabriel, the angel of revelation, and the Qur'an supports the identification in two places: the holy spirit has brought it down from your Lord with the truth (16:102), and the trustworthy spirit brought it down upon your heart (26:193–194). Mentioning him separately from the angels indicates his rank, since a distinguished member of a group is often named alongside it. Other readings exist. Some take the spirit to be a class of angels, the noblest of them; some to a specific group that descends only on this night; some to God's mercy itself. The first reading is the strongest, because the Qur'an already uses *al-rūḥ* for Gabriel in the same context of descent with revelation, and because the sūrah is about the descent of revelation. The alternatives are not arbitrary, but they require the word to change its established reference.

**By the Permission of Their Lord**

The qualification *bi-idhni rabbihim* is not decorative. It states that the descent occurs by authorisation rather than by the angels' own initiative, and the Qur'an puts the same statement in the angels' own mouth: we only descend by the command of your Lord; to Him belongs whatever is before us and whatever is behind us (19:64). The verse therefore closes off a reading that would make the angels independent actors arriving to bless the night on their own account. Everything in the Qur'an's account of the unseen operates under commission. The angels who record deeds, the angels who bring revelation, and the angels who descend on this night are all executing an order. The phrase also connects this night to the governance of the world: the descent is part of an administration, not an interruption of one.

**Min Kulli Amr: For Every Decreed Matter**

The final phrase is the verse's link to the meaning of *qadr*. The prevalent reading is that the angels descend with everything God has decreed for the coming year — life and death, provision and calamity, all of it determined and dispatched. The parallel is explicit in Sūrat al-Dukhān: on that night every matter of wisdom is ordained (44:4). Some commentators report an alternative vocalisation, *min kulli imriʾ*, meaning for every human being, which would make the descent individual rather than general. The first reading is the received one and fits the sūrah's title; the second is not impossible and would emphasise that the decree concerns persons rather than abstractions. Both readings agree on the substance: this is the night in which the year's determinations are settled.

**Why the Angels' Descent Is Mentioned at All**

The sūrah could have stated the night's superiority and stopped. It describes an arrival instead, and the description has a function. A night whose only distinction was a valuation would be an abstract fact; a night into which angels descend is an event with witnesses. The presence of angels is what makes the night's peace in the following verse intelligible, and it explains the traditional understanding that the worshippers of this night are greeted and accompanied. The Qur'an frequently marks the significance of a moment by describing who is present at it, and the description here is the sūrah's way of saying that the night is not empty. Something is happening in it whether or not the sleeper notices.

**The Night as the Annual Administration of the World**

Read with the parallel in al-Dukhān, the verse describes a recurring administrative event. Once a year, on a night in Ramaḍān, the determinations for the coming year are settled and dispatched, and the agents of that settlement descend. The picture is of a governance that runs on a calendar, and it gives the night its name in the strongest sense: *qadr* as decree. This reading does not conflict with God's eternal knowledge, which the Qur'an asserts without qualification; it describes the execution of that knowledge in time, which is the level at which creatures encounter it. The verse is therefore not a statement about divine foreknowledge but about a moment in the ordering of the world, and the moment is available to human beings in a specific way — by being awake in it.

**The Descent and the Human Being's Part**

The verse describes what the angels do and leaves the human contribution to the surrounding verses. Nothing here says the believer must be awake, yet the whole sūrah only makes sense to someone who is. A night of decree in which the year's matters are settled, into which angels descend by permission, is a night in which a person's presence is meaningful rather than incidental. The reports about the Prophet's practice confirm the expectation: he would tighten his waist belt in the last ten nights, pray through them, and wake his household. The verse states the divine side of the arrangement; the practice states the human side. Neither is complete without the other, and the sūrah keeps them in adjacent verses.

## Sūrah al-Qadr 97:5

> It is all peace until the break of dawn

**Expanded Commentary**

**Salāmun Hiya: A Nominal Sentence and What It Claims**

The Arabic is a nominal sentence — *salāmun hiya ḥattā maṭlaʿ al-fajr* — with no verb of occurrence. Arabic nominal sentences describe a state rather than an event, and the effect here is that the night is not doing something peaceful but is peace. The predicate is placed first for emphasis: peace, it is. The construction removes any suggestion that the peace is produced by the worshippers' efforts or that it depends on their number. It characterises the night itself. Commentators draw out the implication: there is no evil in this night, and what descends into it is mercy rather than harm. The verse is the sūrah's shortest statement and its calmest, and it follows immediately after the description of the angels' descent, which is what makes the calm credible.

**Peace From What**

Two readings of the peace are recorded, and both are supported. The first is that the angels greet the worshippers with peace throughout the night, so that the peace is something given to people. The second is that the night itself contains no evil, so that the peace is a property of the time. The first is the more widely reported; the second is the more comprehensive. They are not in competition, since a night in which angels descend to greet those at prayer is also a night in which harm does not occur. The exegetes add a third: that the peace extends to everything decreed for the coming year, which connects the verse to the decree described before it. On that reading the peace is not merely atmospheric but administrative — the year's determinations are dispatched in a condition of peace rather than of calamity.

**Until the Break of Dawn: A Night With a Boundary**

The verse gives the peace a terminus, and the terminus is precise. Not until morning in a loose sense, but until the rising of the dawn — *maṭlaʿ al-fajr*, the place and moment of dawn's emergence, which in Islamic law marks the beginning of the day and the start of the fast. The boundary does two things. It fixes the night's length, so the practice built on the verse has a defined window; and it distinguishes this night from the rest of the year, since the peace is not a permanent condition of the world but a temporary one with an end. The Qur'an regularly marks the limits of what it grants, and the marking is part of the grant. A blessing with a boundary is one a person can organise his life around, and this one is bounded to the hour.

**The Practice Built on the Verse**

The verse has produced more sustained devotional practice than almost any other in the Qur'an. The Prophet ﷺ would observe spiritual retreat in the mosque during the last ten nights of Ramaḍān until he died, and his wives continued after him. He told the community to search for the night in the odd nights of the last ten, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2017 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1169. When ʿĀʾishah asked what she should say if she recognised the night, he taught her: Allāhumma innaka ʿafuwwun tuḥibbu al-ʿafwa faʿfu ʿannī — O God, You are Most Pardoning and You love to pardon, so pardon me; the report is in Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3513, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī. The verse supplies the reason all of this exists: a night of peace with a known end is a night to be inside rather than asleep through.

**Peace as the Character of Revelation**

The sūrah is about the descent of the Qur'an, and its final statement is that the night of descent is peace. The connection is not incidental. The Qur'an describes its own function in similar terms: a healing and a mercy for the believers (17:82); and the sūrah of al-Anfāl describes the believers as those whose hearts find rest in the remembrance of God (13:28). A revelation that arrives in a night characterised by peace is being presented as a source of peace, and the presentation answers an objection the Meccan audience had already made — that the message brought division. The verse concedes the division and states the origin. What came down on this night was peace; what people did with it afterwards is another matter, and the sūrah that follows in the standard arrangement addresses exactly that.

**The Night That Ends With Light**

The boundary named in the verse is the moment darkness gives way to light, and the choice is fitting for a sūrah about revelation. The night begins in darkness and ends at the first light, which is the shape of the event it commemorates: a people in ignorance receiving a book. The Qur'an uses the same imagery throughout, most directly in the statement that God is the light of the heavens and the earth (24:35) and in the description of itself as a light by which God guides whom He wills (5:15). Ending the sūrah at dawn places the whole night inside that metaphor without stating it. The reader who has stayed awake until the break of dawn has enacted the movement the verse describes, which is presumably why the practice attached to the night takes the form it does.

**The Sūrah Closes on a Condition, Not a Promise**

The last word of substance is *al-fajr*, the dawn, and the sūrah ends there without a benediction. No reward is promised in this verse, no threat is issued, and no conclusion is drawn. The peace is simply stated as lasting until a fixed point. The effect is to leave the reader with a fact about the world rather than an assurance about himself: there is a night like this, it has a length, and it passes. What the reader does with the information is left entirely to him, which is consistent with the sūrah's method throughout. Every verse has stated a fact — the descent, the comparison, the angels, the peace — and no verse has told the reader what he should feel. The final verse states the last fact and stops.
