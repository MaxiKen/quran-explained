# Sūrah al-Mulk (Chapter 67) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Sovereign Dominion and the Shield of the Grave**

Al-Mulk, "The Dominion" or "The Kingdom," is the sixty-seventh chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 30 verses of early-to-mid Makkan revelation. It opens with the soaring, majestic benediction: *“Blessed is He in whose hand is the dominion (*Tabāraka al-ladhī bi-yadihi al-mulk*), and He is over all things competent”* (67:1). In the liturgical and spiritual life of Islam, Sūrah al-Mulk occupies an exceptionally revered station: universally designated in prophetic traditions as *Al-Māniʿah* (The Preventer) and *Al-Munjiyah* (The Deliverer), because it shields its reciter from the trial and torment of the grave. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd reported in Sunan al-Nasā'ī (3407) that *“Whoever recites Sūrah al-Mulk every night, Allah will protect him and so from the torment of the grave,”* and the Prophet ﷺ himself declared in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (2891): *“Indeed, there is a chapter in the Qur'an of thirty verses which intercedes for a man until he is forgiven: 'Blessed is He in whose hand is the dominion'!”* The chapter inaugurates the final, twenty-ninth *Juz'* of the Qur'an (often called *Juz' Tabārak*), setting forth the grand purpose of human creation—testing who is best in deed (*aḥsanu ʿamalā*)—and challenging the human eye to find a single flaw, rift, or imperfection in the cosmic architecture of the Almighty.

**The Structural Modulation and the Sinking Water**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Mulk moves across four tightly integrated movements:

1. The Sovereign Creator and the Flawless Cosmos (1–5): Proclaims God's unshared dominion, defines the creation of death and life as an active test of moral excellence (*li-yabluwakum ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalā*), challenges the human eye to detect any imperfection in the sky, and reveals the lowest heaven adorned with radiant lamps that serve as missiles against eavesdropping devils.

2. The Roar of the Abyss and the Confession of the Deniers (6–12): Stages the terrifying arrival of the disbelievers at the gates of Hell: when they are cast into it, they hear it gasping and inhaling with a terrifying boiling roar (*shahīqan wa-hiya tafūr*), almost bursting apart with fury. Its angelic keepers ask them: *“Did there not come to you a warner?”* They make the agonizing confession of verse 10: *“And they will say, 'If only we had been listening or reasoning, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze!'”* It contrasts this horror against the sublime promise of verse 12: *“Indeed, those who fear their Lord unseen will have forgiveness and great reward.”*

3. The Subjugation of the Earth and the Vulnerability of Man (13–22): Declares that God knows whether humanity speaks secretly or openly, asking the profound epistemic question: *“Does He who created not know, while He is the Subtle, the Acquainted?”* (67:14). It reminds humanity that God made the earth manageable, so walk through its paths and eat of His provision, but warns: are you secure that He who is in heaven will not cause the earth to swallow you while it convulses, or send upon you a storm of stones? It points to the birds above them, spreading their wings and folding them, held up exclusively by the Most Compassionate.

4. The Interrogations and the Vanishing Spring (23–30): Reminds humanity of the gifts of hearing, sight, and hearts, asks who has an army that can defend them besides the Most Compassionate, and who can provide for them if He withholds His provision. It presents the striking metaphor of guidance: *“Is he who walks fallen on his face more guided, or he who walks upright on a straight path?”* (67:22). The chapter closes with the chilling, haunting existential dilemma of verse 30: *“Say, 'Have you considered: if your water should become sunken into the earth [deep in the ground], then who could bring you flowing water?'”*

## Verse 67:1

> Blessed is the One in Whose Hands rests all authority. And He is Most Capable of everything

**Tabāraka: A Praise No Creature Can Hold**

The chapter opens with a verb, and the verb is one Arabic reserves for God. *Tabāraka* comes from the root *b-r-k*, which gives the language its word for blessing, *baraka*: increase that settles and does not drain away. The verbal form used here is not the one applied to creatures. A man is *mubārak*, a thing may be *bārakahū Allah*, and a place may be blessed; but *tabāraka* is never said of anyone except God, because it means that all good is rooted in Him, has no outside source, and cannot be exhausted. The Qur'an uses it that way in other places: *“Blessed is the Name of your Lord, full of Majesty and Honour.”* (55:78), and at the completion of the creation of man, *“So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators.”* (23:14). Both verses attach the word to what God is and does, never to what a creature has.

**Mulk and Malakūt: Two Words for One Rule**

*Mulk* and *malakūt* share the root *m-l-k*, and the difference between them is the difference between a surface and its depth. *Mulk* is rule as it appears: the throne, the tax, the army, the law that can be enforced. *Malakūt* is the same rule as it operates beyond sight, the ordering that runs under every visible thing. The Qur'an uses both, and it uses the phrase in this verse for the second: *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is the authority over all things, and to Whom ˹alone˺ you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (36:83). The pairing of the two verses is deliberate: what 67:1 calls authority, 36:83 — *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is the authority over all things, and to Whom ˹alone˺ you will ˹all˺ be returned”* calls authority over all things, and both are held in the hand that no one can pry open.

The same root reaches further. *Mālik* is master, *malik* is king, *malāʾikah* — angels — is formed from the same letters, as though their very name described a delegated power that never becomes independent. The Qur'an states the relation directly in more than one place: *“To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And He is Most Capable of everything.”* (57:2). Ownership in this vocabulary is never a title held at a distance from the thing owned.

**The Last Clause Is Not Only a Comfort**

*And He is Most Capable of everything* reads like reassurance, and in many verses it is exactly that. The same clause, however, closes verses of warning, and its force depends on its context. In the verse about what hearts conceal: *“He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”* (2:284). In the verse about the revival of dead land: *“See then the impact of Allah's mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead. For He is Most Capable of everything.”* (30:50). Capability in the Qur'an is never a harmless attribute. It is the guarantee that announcements about the Last Day, the reckoning, and the raising of the dead will be carried out by the One who made the announcement.

The next verse of the Qur'an's own vocabulary of kingship makes the point sharper — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please...’”* (3:26) — the hand gives and the hand takes away.

The best-attested of these is Abū Hurayrah's narration that the Prophet ﷺ said: *“There is a sūrah in the Qur'an of thirty verses that will intercede for its reciter until he is forgiven: Tabārak alladhī bi-yadihi al-mulk”* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1400; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2891, graded ḥasan).

## Verse 67:2

> ˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life in order to test which of you is best in deeds. And He is the Almighty, All-Forgiving

**Death Is Something God Made, Not a Gap Where Life Stops**

The verse says that death was created. That one grammatical fact carries a whole doctrine: death is not the simple failure of a mechanism or the fading of a flame, but a creature with a purpose, brought into being the way life was. The commentators noted the consequence. If both are created, then the boundary between them belongs to the One who set it, and passing through it is an event He arranged rather than an accident that escaped Him. The Qur'an returns to this often, and always in the same register: *“Every soul will taste death. And We test you ˹O humanity˺ with good and evil as a trial, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (21:35); *“Every soul will taste death, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (29:57). The taste is universal, the return is universal, and neither is left to chance.

Calling death a creation also explains why the Qur'an can speak of it as an experience rather than a void. Since it was made, it can be measured, faced, drunk, tasted; it has an owner and it comes at a fixed appointment.

**"Best in Deeds" Is a Question of Quality**

That reading takes the verse far past a general call to be good. It puts intention and form on the same scale, and it makes the small deed done properly heavier than the large deed done for display. The same phrase governs other statements of purpose in the Qur'an: *“We have indeed made whatever is on earth as an adornment for it, to test which of them is best in deeds.”* (18:7).

**The Test Is Not a Search for Information**

An obvious question follows: if God knows everything, what does the test add? The verse answers by describing the purpose, not the need. God does not examine to learn; the examination is the process by which a capacity that exists in a person is turned into an act that belongs to him. Knowledge of what someone would do is not the same thing as the deed he does, and the difference is the entire basis of responsibility. The Qur'an states the principle in the verse that closes a long passage of laws: *“O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.”* (35:15). Nothing in the arrangement serves Him; the arrangement serves the tested.

The Prophet ﷺ described the intensity of that testing in a report from Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, who asked which people suffer the most severe trials. He answered: the prophets, then those nearest to them, then those nearest to them; a man is tested according to his faith, so that if his faith is firm his trial is increased, and if it is frail, it is lightened (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2398; Sunan Ibn Mājah 4023). Difficulty, on this account, tracks closeness rather than distance.

The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same priority in a report kept by his companions, that the servant's feet will not move on the Day of Judgement until he is asked about his life and what he did with it, and about his body and how he wore it out (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2417).

## Verse 67:3

> ˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other. You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate. So look again: do you see any flaws

**A Claim That Invites Inspection**

*So look again: do you see any flaws?* is one of the Qur'an's most exposed sentences. It hands the sceptic a test and stakes the argument on the result. Nothing in the verse confines the search to what a believer may look at, or the searcher to a friendly observer; the command is addressed to anyone willing to raise his eyes. Compare this with the claims of the idol cults of the city, in which the object of worship stood in a fixed place and answered nothing, and no one was invited to examine it. The Qur'an's method repeatedly takes the opposite posture: *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5); *“Do they not ever reflect on camels—how they were ˹masterfully˺ created”* (88:17); *“There are ˹countless˺ signs on earth for those with sure faith”* (51:20). Revelation presents itself as compatible with investigation, and then dares the investigator to finish.

**Tafāwut: No Part Out of Joint**

*Tafāwut*, translated here as imperfection, comes from a root meaning to slip past or to be missed. A thing is *mutafāwit* when its parts fail to meet: a joint that does not close, a measurement that does not line up, a gap where a connection should be. So the verse is not claiming that the world contains nothing unpleasant, or that no one suffers; it is claiming that the construction has no break in it. The same claim is made in the interrogation of the sky: *“Have they not then looked at the sky above them: how We built it and adorned it ˹with stars˺, leaving it flawless?”* (50:6).

Other statements in the Book supply the reason. The heavens and the earth were not made as an amusement: *“We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport.”* (44:38). *“We have not created the heavens and the earth and everything in between except for a purpose.”* (15:85); and the purpose is answered for in the same breath, *“For Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose, so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. And none will be wronged.”* (45:22). A work built for a purpose and paid for in justice will not show a fault at the seams.

**fāṭir and fuṭūr: The Originator Whose Work Has No Split**

A lexical link holds this verse together and is easy to miss in translation. *Fuṭūr*, the word for a flaw, comes from *f-ṭ-r*, to split lengthwise; and *fāṭir*, the active participle of the same root, is a name of God: *“˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth.”* (42:11). The opener of things is also the one whose handiwork cannot be opened by searching. The root is the one behind *ifṭār*, the breaking of the fast, and behind the day when the sky will finally be cracked apart — the very event the verse implies cannot be found now.

The same wordplay appears where the Book compares the cosmos to itself: *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies.”* (4:82). One test is applied to both books, the written and the made, and the claim is that neither search ends in a fracture. Anyone looking for a way to dismiss either will have to do without the discovery of a defect.

The number seven appears elsewhere in the Book with the same ordering: *“He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned toward the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.”* (2:29), and *“So He formed the heaven into seven heavens in two Days, assigning to each its mandate.”* (41:12).

## Verse 67:4

> Then look again and again—your sight will return frustrated and weary

**A Command Built to Be Repeated**

The previous verse closed with an order to look. This verse repeats it and doubles it: *thumma rjiʿi al-baṣara karratayn* — then bring the sight back, twice over. The construction gives the command the shape of procedure. A single glance can be dismissed as inattention; two looks, and then a third, remove that excuse, and the reader who obeys finds himself running an experiment rather than listening to a sermon. The Qur'an often works this way when it argues from the natural world: it does not ask for assent at the first impression but demands that attention stay long enough for a conclusion to be earned.

**The Limit Belongs to the Looker**

There is a principle here about knowledge that the sūrah states as an image rather than a proposition. Every claim to have surveyed existence completely has to account for the surveyor, and the surveyor is a creature with a lifespan, a field of vision, and a memory that forgets. The Qur'an raises that fact against the confident verdicts of the deniers: *“Do they not ever reflect on camels—how they were ˹masterfully˺ created”* (88:17), and, in a verse that reads like a companion to this one, *“Now you see the mountains, thinking they are firmly fixed, but they are travelling ˹just˺ like clouds. ˹That is˺ the design of Allah, Who has perfected everything. Surely He is All-Aware of what you do.”* (27:88).

**When the Signs Are Everywhere and Refusal Is Still a Choice**

The sūrah never treats unbelief as a shortage of evidence. It treats it as a decision made in the presence of evidence, and it says so in the bluntest available words: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Consider all that is in the heavens and the earth!” Yet neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe.”* (10:101). A search that ends in exhaustion can be honest or dishonest, and the difference lies in what the searcher does with the limit he has reached. The honest answer is to admit that his instrument is finite and that the order he examined is not; the dishonest answer is to announce that the absence of his own verdict is a verdict.

**Scrutiny That Ends in Humility**

The earlier chapters of this Book make the first path a command, not a concession: *“There are ˹countless˺ signs on earth for those with sure faith”* (51:20), a verse that places the signs and the faith in the same sentence. The sūrah under way will soon name what the eye cannot see and the ear must carry, and this verse has already established why the transfer of trust is reasonable rather than timid.

## Verse 67:5

> And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps, and made them ˹as missiles˺ for stoning ˹eavesdropping˺ devils, for whom We have also prepared the torment of the Blaze

**Lamps in the Nearest Sky**

The verse assigns the stars two tasks, and the first is beauty. The Arabic word translated *lamps* is *maṣābīḥ*, the plural of a word for the flame of a lamp, chosen because of what a lamp does in a dark place: it makes the surroundings visible and turns a roof into a ceiling. The Book describes this adornment elsewhere in almost identical words: *“Indeed, We have placed constellations in the sky. Adorned it for all to see.”* (15:16); *“Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration”* (37:6); and in the verse that gives each heaven its duty, *“And We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps ˹for beauty˺ and for protection. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (41:12). The root of the word rendered *lowest* is the same one used for the near and the worldly: this is the sky closest to human habitation, the one a person sees with the naked eye.

That identification matters for the second task. Nothing in the verse descends to the level of astronomy; it is speaking of the sky the reader can look at, and of a function inside a world that includes beings the reader cannot see.

**The Second Task: Blocking the Eavesdroppers**

The same lights serve as missiles. The Qur'an returns to this operation in several places and always in the same setting, the boundary between the visible world and the angelic assembly: *“and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil. They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side, ˹fiercely˺ driven away. And they will suffer an everlasting torment.”* (37:7–9); *“And We protected it from every accursed devil, except the one eavesdropping, who is then pursued by a visible flare.”* (15:17–18). The jinn themselves describe the change in the sūrah devoted to them: *“˹Earlier˺ we tried to reach heaven ˹for news˺, only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars. We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping, but whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for them.”* (72:8–9).

**The Blaze Prepared for Those Who Try**

The verse ends by naming the destination of the devils: the *saʿīr*, the Blaze, whose fire is described elsewhere as a torment reserved for them. The word is one of the several names the Book gives the fire, and each name describes a manner of burning; this one is built from the root for igniting. Its placement here, in the middle of the passage about access to heaven, discloses the stakes. An try on the boundary is not mischief but rebellion, and rebellion has a prepared end.

## Verse 67:6

> Those who disbelieve in their Lord will suffer the punishment of Hell. What an evil destination

**From the Devils of the Sky to the Deniers on Earth**

The previous verse ended with a prepared torment for the jinn who tried to cross the boundary. This verse pronounces the same end for human beings who deny their Lord, and the join between the two is not accidental. Both parties are guilty of the same practical error: they treated knowledge of the unseen as an open commodity, and they looked for it anywhere except in the revelation sent to them. A person who consults a fortune-teller has accepted the trade the jinn once ran at the edge of the sky, and the Book keeps the two forms of trafficking side by side.

The reports on the practice are explicit about the gravity. The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever goes to a fortune-teller and asks him about a matter, his prayer will not be accepted for forty days (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2230). And the narration in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3904 goes further, that whoever goes to such a person and believes what he says has disbelieved in what was revealed to Muḥammad. The verse's audience in Makkah lived inside that trade, and the sūrah answers it by describing where the traffic in hidden knowledge ends.

**"In Their Lord": the Denial of an Ownership They Admitted**

The verse says disbelievers *in their Lord*, not merely disbelievers in a messenger, and the phrase carries its own charge. *Rabb* in Arabic is not only creator but owner, provider, and the one who manages what he owns. And the Qur'an records that the Makkans conceded the Creator's existence without difficulty: *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, “Allah!” How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?”* (29:61). Their fault was not a theory about origins. It was the refusal of a master's claim on their conduct after admitting his authorship of things.

## Verse 67:7

> When they are tossed into it, they will hear its roaring as it boils over

**The First Contact Is Sound**

Before the deniers see anything, they hear. The Qur'an describes the fire's voice with the word *shahīq*, the sound of a creature drawing a long breath, and then adds that it seethes as it does so: *tafūr*, the boiling of a liquid heated past its limit. Other verses complete the impression from a distance: *“Once it sees them from a distance, they will hear it fuming and growling.”* (25:12). Fury and noise are what the place does at the sight of them, and the Book's sequence is deliberate. Sound reaches the person who has not yet arrived, and it arrives before the eyes have anything to work with.

**The Verb That Strips Away All Ceremony**

They are *tossed* — *ulqū*, thrown, and the passive voice removes them from the sentence as agents. Nobody walks in, nobody is conducted, nobody is announced. Compare the entry of the blessed in the same Book: *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups. When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, “Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever.””* (39:73). In one case the gates are opened in advance, keepers speak a greeting, and the enterers are escorted. In the other, a group of people is picked up and thrown, and the sentence has no subject performing the act because the drama is about the object, not the hand.

**A Creature That Answers Its Maker**

Nothing in the sūrah's treatment of the fire suggests that it is a passive instrument. It sees, it boils, it rages, it will speak. The Qur'an describes it as a created thing among created things, kept by angels appointed for the work, and its behaviour is the behaviour of something set to a task and doing it completely. The Book's wider account of the fire belongs to the same view of nature that runs through this chapter: winds, seas, birds, stars — all of them carry out what they were made for. *“In fact, they deny the Hour. And for the deniers of the Hour, We have prepared a blazing Fire.”* (25:11). That preparation is not an emergency measure but a provision, made in advance for the case at hand.

**Why the Warning Chooses Physical Horror**

The verse that follows in another sūrah states the reason for the urgency with unusual directness: the repentance that arrives at the last moment does not count, *“nor from those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.”* (4:18).

## Verse 67:8

> almost bursting in fury. Every time a group is cast into it, its keepers will ask them, “Did a warner not come to you?”

Another sūrah supplies the number, and it supplies the reason in the same breath: *“It is overseen by nineteen ˹keepers˺.”* (74:30), followed immediately by the statement that *“We have appointed only ˹stern˺ angels as wardens of the Fire. And We have made their number only as a test for the disbelievers...”* (74:31). The count itself strains the reader — and that is what is being said.

**Mālik and the Plea That Is Refused**

The Book names the chief keeper once. The people in the fire will call out to him, and the exchange is recorded with terrible economy: *“They will cry, “O Mâlik! Let your Lord finish us off.” He will answer, “You are definitely here to stay.” We certainly brought the truth to you, but most of you were resentful of the truth.””* (43:77–78). Their request is not for release but for annihilation — the last retreat available to a person who has lost every other hope.

The refusal answers without cruelty and without hope. And the sentence joined to the refusal identifies the crime: the truth came, and they resented it. Nothing in the fire's administration is arbitrary; the resentment recorded on the last day is the same aversion that operated in Makkah while the Prophet ﷺ was reciting in the street.

**A Question That Is Not Seeking an Answer**

*Did a warner not come to you?* No keeper needs to ask this for his own information. The question exists to establish the state of the case, and the Book has already explained why it can be asked universally: *“We have surely sent you with the truth as a deliverer of good news and a warner. There is no community that has not had a warner.”* (35:24), and again in the sūrah of the bee, *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, “Worship Allah and shun false gods.””* (16:36).

The doctrine built on this is stated with the same consistency: responsibility follows delivery. A person is not judged for a message he never received. The keepers' question, therefore, is the last page of a file that was opened in the person's own lifetime, in his own language, by someone he could see and argue with.

**The Justice That Makes the Sentence Safe**

The Qur'an states the principle in legal form: *“Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺.”* (17:15). The keepers' question is the enforcement of that clause, and the Book says the jinn and the humans together will answer it in their own words: *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?” They will say, “We confess against ourselves!”* (6:130).

The Book names them plainly when it instructs the believers in self-protection: *“O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded.”* (66:6).

## Verse 67:9

> They will reply, “Yes, a warner did come to us, but we denied and said, ‘Allah has revealed nothing. You are extremely astray.’”

**"Allah Has Revealed Nothing": the Naked Claim**

The sentence the deniers attribute to themselves is absolute. They did not say that the messenger had misunderstood or exaggerated; they said that nothing had been sent down at all. That is the simplest form of denial and the least costly to maintain, because a partial criticism invites a discussion of texts while a total denial asks the audience to believe that the entire phenomenon of revelation is empty. The sūrah that follows this one in the Book answers the claim from another direction: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth. Is it not enough that your Lord is a Witness over all things?”* (41:53).

**"You Are Extremely Astray": the Accusation Turned on the Warner**

Later revelation tells us how far their own confidence went. One verse asks the assembled jinn and humans whether messengers came to them, and records the answer: *“They will say, “We confess against ourselves!” For they have been deluded by ˹their˺ worldly life.”* (6:130). The same voices that called a prophet astray will call themselves lost, and the Book keeps both sentences on the same page.

**The Confession That Arrives Too Late**

An identical exchange in another sūrah shows how this ends. The keepers ask, the disbelievers answer, and the two-word answer is an admission: *“Yes ˹indeed˺! But the decree of torment has come to pass against the disbelievers.”* (39:71). The pattern is fixed in the Book: the truth is not withheld from anyone in this world, and it is admitted by everyone in the next. What changes is the consequence of the admission. In this life, a confession can still be a turning point; in the fire it is only a statement of fact.

**No Punishment Without a Delivered Warning**

The doctrine this verse supports is stated without ambiguity: *“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺.”* (17:15). The Book goes further and says that the messenger speaks in the language of his people, so that the warning is intelligible rather than merely delivered: *“We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them.”* (14:4). The answer in the fire is therefore not only a confession; it is the closing of a case in which every procedural condition was met. The keepers asked, the record answered, and no one in the scene can claim that the road was hidden.

## Verse 67:10

> And they will lament, “If only we had listened and reasoned, we would not be among the residents of the Blaze!”

**Listening in the Qur'an Is More Than Hearing**

The Arabic *samʿ* covers both the physical act and compliance, and the Book slides between the two senses because it treats the ear as a gate to the will rather than a microphone. To have listened, in this vocabulary, means to have taken something in with the intention of acting on it. That is why the opposite of listening in the Qur'an is not deafness but turning away, and why the Book describes a whole class of people as hearing the signs recited and continuing as though they had never been present. The reported convention of the Makkans — *“When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.””* (2:170) — is the perfect illustration. The recitation reached them; they made their ancestors the deciding voice instead.

**Why the Two Faculties Are Named in This Order**

Hearing comes first in the sentence because it comes first in the process. Revelation reaches a human being through the ear, and the Book's normal sequence is: something is recited, it is listened to, and then it is weighed. The opposite order is what the Book calls following what one has no knowledge of, and it names the faculties that will be questioned about it: *“Do not follow what you have no ˹sure˺ knowledge of. Indeed, all will be called to account for ˹their˺ hearing, sight, and intellect.”* (17:36). The account is taken because the equipment was issued.

The positive version of the same ordering appears where the Book describes those who profit: *“those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it. These are the ones ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah, and these are ˹truly˺ the people of reason.”* (39:18). Listening and reasoning appear there together, and the result is not only an opinion but a path. The verse of regret reverses the picture without changing the vocabulary: the same two faculties, unused, become the subjects of a lament that can no longer change anything.

## Verse 67:11

> And so they will confess their sins. So away with the residents of the Blaze

**Confession in a Place Where It Cannot Be Used**

The verse reports that the people of the fire acknowledge their sins. The Arabic verb is one of acknowledgement between parties — *iʿtarafa* — the language of a man admitting a debt, not of a defendant resisting a charge. Nothing is denied, nothing is minimised, and no excuse is offered. And the response to the confession is not acceptance but expulsion: *away with the residents of the Blaze*.

That combination is the teaching of the verse. Confession is worth what access to the forgiver is worth, and the access closes with death. The Book states this limit elsewhere with the same bluntness: repentance is not accepted from a person who persists until death arrives and then says that he repents now, *“nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.”* (4:18). The acknowledgement still functions in the fire, but only as testimony.

**The Door That Stays Open Until the Last Breath**

Against the finality of this sentence, the Book sets the widest possible opening in the world before it. Repentance is accepted while a person lives; the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said that God accepts the repentance of His servant as long as the soul has not reached the throat (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3537). Two things are taught by that report together. The first is mercy, since the door is not closed to anyone still breathing. The second is urgency, since the door is not open indefinitely, and no one is told when his own breath will end.

**What Confession Without Repentance Cannot Do**

The Book distinguishes carefully between the two forms of agreement. The hypocrites of Madinah are described as believing on the surface while concealing denial, *“When they come to you ˹believers˺ they say, “We believe.” But they are committed to disbelief when they enter and when they leave. And Allah knows what they hide.”* (5:61). They are not confused; they are hiding, and the hiding is the offence. The residents of the fire have no hiding left, and their acknowledgement is complete.

**The Difference Between the Two Confessions**

The Book contains another confession from the people of the fire, and comparing the two sharpens this verse. When the keepers ask whether warners came to them, the answer in the sūrah of the Groups is an admission followed by a sentence about the decree: *“The disbelievers will cry, “Yes ˹indeed˺! But the decree of torment has come to pass against the disbelievers.””* (39:71). Here the acknowledgment is described as an admission of sin, which is a fuller statement: the condemned accept not only that the warning was delivered but that their own conduct deserved what followed.

Against that finality the Book sets the repentance it will still accept: *“Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after—Allah will pardon them. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”* (4:17). The phrase *soon after* is the hinge. The confession in the fire is the same statement made after the window closed, and anyone still holding that window is being addressed by it.

## Verse 67:12

> Indeed, those in awe of their Lord without seeing Him will have forgiveness and a mighty reward

**The Fear That Works With No Witness Present**

The phrase *without seeing Him* — *bil-ghayb* — has been read in two ways, and the difference between them is a difference in depth. On the first reading, a person fears God because he fears the punishment, which he has not seen and which no one can prove to him in advance; he acts on a warning he cannot verify by eye. On the second, he worships God while unseen and by himself, in a room where no one is watching and no reputation is at stake. The second reading is the stricter and the better, because it names the situation in which fear has nothing to feed on: no audience, no shame, no reward from people. It also includes the first, since such a person also has not seen his Lord.

The Book supports both directions. It says of the messenger's work that only some can be warned: *“You can only warn those who follow the Reminder and are in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him. So give them good news of forgiveness and a honourable reward.”* (36:11), and it describes the reward of such people in the sūrah of Qāf: *“who were in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing ˹Him˺, and have come with a heart turning ˹only to Him˺.”* (50:33).

**The Hidden Devotion Among the Seven**

The Prophet ﷺ left a list that has become one of the most memorised passages in Islamic teaching: seven categories of people whom God will shelter on a Day when there is no shade but His. Among them are the just ruler, the young man who grew up in worship, the man whose heart is attached to the mosques, two who love each other for God's sake, the man who refuses temptation saying that he fears Allah, the man who gives charity so secretly that his left hand does not know what his right hand gives. Finally *a man who remembers Allah in solitude and his eyes overflow with tears* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The last entry is precisely the person this verse describes, and the entry sits beside the secret giver for a reason: both belong to the category of actions performed where no one can be credited but God.

Read together with 67:12, the list answers a practical question about how to live out the verse. The believers of Makkah had almost no public sphere in which their religion could be displayed honourably. What they did have was privacy, and the report tells them that privacy is not the absence of the arena; it is the arena in which the real contest occurs.

## Verse 67:13

> Whether you speak secretly or openly—He surely knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**A Verse About People Who Whispered**

The occasion reported for this verse explains its first clause. It is said that the idolaters of Makkah, when they wanted to slander the Prophet ﷺ among themselves, would warn one another to keep their voices down, in case the God of Muhammad should overhear them — as though a deity were a neighbour behind a wall. The verse answers by removing the wall completely: secret speech and public speech stand in the same relation to Him, and behind both stands the heart, which neither whisper nor shout can reach but which He knows. The report is transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and matches the verse's wording so closely that the words themselves preserve the joke the whisperers made.

The incident exposes a confusion about what a god is. A deity of the pagan sanctuary could be addressed, ignored, or avoided; he was located, and therefore he could be circumvented. The verse quietly abolishes that geography. There is no room the whisperers can enter to speak where God is not, and the reason is that He is not a being whose hearing is a sense like theirs.

**The Plane Where Words Are Still Being Formed**

The Qur'an's treatment of intention is more practical than philosophical. It says that God knows what the heart conceals, and it uses that knowledge both as a warning and as a mercy. Human courts deal with acts because acts are what can be observed; the divine court deals with the intention behind the act, which is the reason the Book places such weight on sincerity. The same principle appears in a verse that answers the whisperers of a different kind: *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺.”* (43:80). Nothing a person thinks fails to reach the One who is judging him.

What the knowledge produces, in the Book's own account, is not a paranoid policing of thoughts but an orientation of the inner life. A person who knows he is transparent stops pretending, first to God and eventually to himself.

**The Report About Speech Between Two Options**

The Prophet ﷺ gave his community a rule for the tongue that is short enough to memorise: whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should speak well or stay silent (recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The order is significant. Speech is permitted by default and silence is the alternative when nothing good is available, which places the day's ordinary conversation under a mild discipline rather than a prohibition. A conversation that neither tells a truth worth telling nor helps anyone can simply be ended.

The rule also served the community this verse addresses. Most of the harm done to the early believers in Makkah travelled by word of mouth — the invented accusation, the joke passed around the market, the whisper between two elders. The verse denies the whisperers their privacy, and the report gives the believers a rule they can practise in the same streets.

## Verse 67:14

> How could He not know His Own creation? For He ˹alone˺ is the Most Subtle, All-Aware

**An Argument From the Act of Making**

The verse reasons from craftsmanship, and the reasoning is available to anyone who has made anything. Whoever builds an object knows its parts, its weaknesses, and its capacity, because knowledge of the thing is contained in the knowledge that produced it. A potter knows where his clay will crack; a mason knows which stone will hold. The question, phrased as an incredulous *how could He not know*, asks the whisperers of Makkah to consider what they had conceded. They believed that God had made them — and then spoke as though He could not hear them.

**al-Laṭīf: the Subtle Who Reaches the Innermost Layer**

The first name is *al-Laṭīf*, formed from a root describing fineness and penetration, and it carries two related senses that Arabic keeps together. It means the One whose knowledge reaches what is subtle and small, past the surface to the interior — and it also means the Kind, the One whose care extends to details no one notices. The Qur'an uses the second sense in a verse describing His provision: *“Allah is Ever Kind to His servants. He provides ˹abundantly˺ to whoever He wills. And He is the All-Powerful, Almighty.”* (42:19). The two senses belong to one another, because knowledge of a detail is what makes care for that detail possible.

**Knowing What He Made, He Asks Only What Is Possible**

The final implication of the verse runs through the whole of the Book's moral teaching. A maker who knows his material does not issue instructions that the material cannot carry. The Qur'an states this explicitly elsewhere, *“And your Lord will never wrong anyone.”* (18:49), and applies it by limiting obligation to capacity. The verse's knowledge of the inner life is therefore not a trap; it is the guarantee of fair assessment, since a judge who knows the difficulty of the terrain will not demand that a man walk further than he can. The Book states the limit as a rule: *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford.”* (2:286), and restates it where the record of deeds is described, adding that the account itself is truthful: *“We never require of any soul more than what it can afford. And with Us is a record which speaks the truth. None will be wronged.”* (23:62). A maker who measures the load to the strength of what he made is the same maker who knows exactly what is in the chest. The same knowledge that exposes the whisperer is the reason no one is asked to be other than human.

## Verse 67:15

> He is the One Who smoothed out the earth for you, so move about in its regions and eat from His provisions. And to Him is the resurrection ˹of all˺

**An Earth Made Tractable, Like a Broken Mount**

The verb behind the translation is *dhallala*, and the picture inside it is a camel trained to carry a rider. The same root gives Arabic the word for a docile animal, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly for the way the created world has been arranged to serve human purposes — animals, winds, seas, and here the earth itself. The planet is described as a mount that has been broken to use: it holds a road, it bears a load, it yields a harvest.

Noah is reported making the same point to his people with the same imagery: *“And Allah ˹alone˺ spread out the earth for you to walk along its spacious pathways.”* (71:19–20).

**"So Move About in Its Regions"**

The command is phrased as permission with an edge. The Arabic is *famshū fī manākibihā*: walk in its shoulder-blades, its broad tracts. The metaphor of the mount surfaces again, since the shoulders are where the rider sits. So the earth is not a place to be endured but a territory to be crossed, and the Book attaches a whole method of knowledge to that command. Travel in the Qur'an is an instrument. It is prescribed as a way of learning history — *“Say, “Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers.””* (6:11); *“Similar situations came to pass before you. Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers.”* (3:137) — and equally as a way of studying the beginning of things, *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Travel throughout the land and see how He originated the creation, then Allah will bring it into being one more time.””* (29:20). Ruins teach the moral lesson; habitats teach the design.

**The Furnished Workshop, Not a Shrine**

A pattern runs through the Qur'an's treatment of the land: it is prepared for human use rather than for human worship. Paths are set in mountains *“so they may find their way”* (21:31), mountains are placed as pegs to stop the earth shaking (15:19 — *“As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and caused everything to grow there in perfect balance”*; 78:7 — *“and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs”*), fruits are made in pairs (13:3 — *“And He is the One Who spread out the earth and placed firm mountains and rivers upon it, and created fruits of every kind in pairs.”*), and rivers run by design. None of this is offered as an object of devotion. The creation is the workshop and the messenger; the One who fitted it out is the one to be served.

**Two Lessons the Traveler Is Meant to Learn**

Put the two objects of Qur'anic travel side by side and a curriculum appears. The first is the fate of those who denied: cities reduced to ruins, civilisations remembered only by their monuments, and the Book's repeated challenge to walk through them and read what happened. The second is the origin of the human being and the fitting-out of a planet that could sustain him. One teaches consequence and the other teaches dependence, and the traveller who learns only one of them has completed half the journey.

The Book marks the failure of the other half with a sentence about perception: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind.”* (22:46). Journeys are not counted by distance covered but by what they open.

## Verse 67:16

> Do you feel secure that the One Who is in heaven will not cause the earth to swallow you up as it quakes violently

**A Question Aimed at a Feeling**

The verse does not begin by asserting a fact about God; it asks about a state of mind. *A-amintum* — do you feel safe — targets the assumption of impunity that had settled over Makkah after years of unpunished mockery. The recitation had warned them, the boycott had come and gone, the years had passed, and nothing had fallen on their heads. From that evidence they drew a conclusion, and the verse names the conclusion for what it is: a feeling, not a guarantee. The distinction matters in ordinary life as much as in theology.

**"The One Who Is in Heaven"**

The phrase translated *the One Who is in heaven* is an Arabic phrase for God as the One who is above the created order, sovereign over it, and not one of its residents in the way a creature is. It appears in the Book alongside statements of His encompassing presence, and the two kinds of language are held together without embarrassment: God is nearer to a person than his own jugular vein (50:16 — *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein”*), and He is also the One above. What the phrase does in this verse is locate the power that decides outcomes. The earth cannot be commanded by its inhabitants, and the One who can command it is not standing on it.

**Security as a Symptom of Comfort**

Years of unanswered provocation had done something to the elite of Makkah, and the Book names the diagnosis elsewhere with clinical brevity. When a people ignore warnings, the Qur'an says, *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!”* (6:44). And again, addressed to those who mistook longevity for approval: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment.”* (3:178).

**A Warning That Is Still Mercy**

Everything about the verse is designed to be heard before the event. If the earth's obedience to God were only visible in the moment of punishment, the warning would be useless; the verse's value lies in its being recited while the ground is still—while decisions can still be changed. The Book states the principle of conditional provision in a formula the Prophet's community recited often: *“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord proclaimed, ‘If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe.’”* (14:7). A warning is not a threat made for its own sake; it is the last instrument available to a person who is being given time.

## Verse 67:17

> Or do you feel secure that the One Who is in heaven will not unleash upon you a storm of stones. Only then would you know how ˹serious˺ My warning was

**Punishments the Audience Had Seen With Its Own Eyes**

The Book's warnings are not drawn from a distant archive. The punishment of Lot's people by stones is one of the most frequently cited disasters in the Qur'an, and the towns involved lay on the caravan road that the merchants of Makkah travelled twice a year. The ruins were not a report to them but a landscape they passed, as the Book reminds them elsewhere: *“And indeed, you pass by them in the morning, and at night. Will you not then understand?”* (37:137–138). A storm of stones was not a hypothetical. It was a documented method of divine response to a town that had exhausted its warnings.

**The Companions of the Elephant**

One event stood even closer to the first audience. Forty years before the beginning of the mission, an army from Yemen marched on Makkah with the intention of destroying the sanctuary. It was destroyed by flights of stones carried by birds — a story every Quraysh elder had heard from his fathers, and a story the Book preserves in a short sūrah of its own. The sūrah that preserves the story ends its account with the same sky-borne image: *“For He sent against them flocks of birds, that pelted them with stones of baked clay, leaving them like chewed up straw.”* (105:3–5). The lesson the Makkans had drawn from it was that God defended the House. The lesson this verse draws is less comfortable: the same power that defended the sanctuary against a foreign army can be turned on the men who were using the sanctuary as cover for denying the messenger sent to it.

**The Interval and What It Is For**

There is a difference between a warning and a sentence, and the difference is time. Every word of this verse is addressed to people who are still alive, still trading, still mocking, and still able to change their position. The Book keeps the door of return wide in the middle of its harshest passages, and it states the reason for the delay in a sentence that has the character of an invitation: *“When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one’s prayer when they call upon Me. So let them respond ˹with obedience˺ to Me and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided ˹to the Right Way˺.”* (2:186).

## Verse 67:18

> And certainly those before them denied ˹as well˺, then how severe was My response

**"Not Even One-Tenth of What We Had Given Them"**

One sūrah sharpens the comparison into a measurement. Addressing the Makkans, the Book says that the peoples destroyed before them had been given far more than they had, and that the disproportion did not protect anyone: *“Those ˹destroyed˺ before them denied as well—and these ˹Meccans˺ have not attained even one-tenth of what We had given their predecessors. Yet ˹when˺ they denied My messengers, how severe was My response!”* (34:45). The same sentence appears in the sūrah of the Pilgrimage, where the catalogue of destroyed nations closes with Moses being denied and the identical verdict: *“and the residents of Midian. And Moses was denied ˹too˺. But I delayed ˹the fate of˺ the disbelievers ˹until their appointed time˺ then seized them. And how severe was My response!”* (22:44).

**"Those Before Them Denied As Well"**

The pattern stated here is developed at length in the Book's historical passages, and one of them supplies the comparison in detail: *“Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them? We had made them more established in the land than you. We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet. Then We destroyed them for their sins and replaced them with other peoples.”* (6:6). The comparative clause is the one that stings. Those nations had greater resources — more rain, more rivers, more time — and their power did not delay anything. The Makkans were being measured against a field in which they were the weakest entrants.

The verse also quietly refutes a common defence: that the message is being rejected on the merits of the messenger. The earlier generations rejected their messengers on the merits of the same instincts, and the Book treats the recurring phenomenon as evidence about human beings rather than evidence about God.

**Ruins as a Textbook**

The Book's habit of sending its readers to look at the sites of earlier judgement gives this verse a practical edge. *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might; they cultivated the land and developed it more than these ˹Meccans˺ ever have.”* (30:9); and again, with the same emphasis on strength, *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those before them? Allah annihilated them, and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers.”* (47:10). The ruins in question lay within reach of the caravan routes, and the merchants of Makkah passed them twice a year in the ordinary course of business.

## Verse 67:19

> Have they not seen the birds above them, spreading and folding their wings? None holds them up except the Most Compassionate. Indeed, He is All-Seeing of everything

**Wings Spread and Folded**

The Arabic describes the two halves of flight: the wings outspread and the wings drawn in. A bird climbing, gliding, or turning adjusts the surface it presents to the air, and the adjustment is continuous. The Book does not explain aerodynamics, and it does not need to; it points at the fact and asks who is responsible for the arrangement. Other sūrahs put the same observation in the same form: *“Have they not seen the birds glide in the open sky? None holds them up except Allah. Surely in this are signs for those who believe.”* (16:79).

**The Report About Trust and Daily Bread**

The Prophet ﷺ used the same image for a different purpose when he described reliance on God. He said that if the community were to place its trust in God as He deserves, He would provide for them the way He provides for the birds, which leave in the morning hungry and return in the evening full (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2344). The report is often quoted as an argument for a quiet, passive trust, and the image itself corrects that reading: the bird leaves the nest. Effort and provision are paired in the same sentence, and the trust being described is what a person carries while doing the work rather than a substitute for it.

For the sūrah's audience the report also carried a practical reassurance. The community that had been boycotted and driven from the caravan trade was being told that the provision of the Most Compassionate does not depend on the men who control the market.

## Verse 67:20

> Also, which ˹powerless˺ force will come to your help instead of the Most Compassionate? Indeed, the disbelievers are only ˹lost˺ in delusion

**The Question of the Host**

The word translated *force* is *nāṣir* — a helper, and specifically the kind of helper who drives an attacker away. Armies are hired for this purpose, and so are alliances, patrons, and gods. The question asks who among that list is available to the men being addressed. It is put in the form of a challenge rather than a statement, and the modifier *powerless* that accompanies the translation makes the answer explicit before the sentence has closed: the forces in question are being asked to appear, and the sūrah's audience knows that none of them can.

**Fear, and Where It Should Be Placed**

Reliance on patrons is a strategy for managing fear, and the Book addresses it directly by relocating the fear rather than by suppressing it: *“That ˹warning˺ was only ˹from˺ Satan, trying to prompt you to fear his followers. So do not fear them; fear Me if you are ˹true˺ believers.”* (3:175). The instruction is not to become fearless; it is to transfer a fear that has been attached to the wrong object. A person who fears his tribe's displeasure more than he fears God's is already being governed by an alliance, whether he admits the arrangement or not.

**When the Deluders Are Asked About the Deluded**

The Book supplies the ending of the alliance the verse describes, and the ending is spoken by the being who encouraged it. On the day the matter is settled, Satan will address the people who relied on him and disclaim every part of the arrangement: *“Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, nor can you save me.”* (14:22). The sentence about authority is the most cutting part of the speech. No compulsion was ever applied, which is why the deception worked through desire rather than force.

## Verse 67:21

> Or who is it that will provide for you if He withholds His provision? In fact, they persist in arrogance and aversion ˹to the truth˺

**The Idols That Cannot Afford Anything**

The Book states the negative form of this argument elsewhere with the same instruments of rhetoric: *“Yet they worship besides Allah those ˹idols˺ who do not afford them any provision from the heavens and the earth, nor do they have the power to.”* (16:73). The heavens and the earth are named because those are the two sources the Makkans themselves tracked: rain from above, crops from below. The idols had no access to either, and the men who served them knew it; the incense and offerings went up to beings who could not answer a drought.

**Do the Provision and the Test Look Alike?**

A hard question follows from the verse, and the Qur'an does not avoid it. If God provides, why do the generous and the selfish both receive? The answer the Book gives is that provision is a test rather than a certificate: *“Do they think, since We provide them with wealth and children, that We hasten to ˹honour˺ them ˹with˺ all kinds of good? No! They are not aware.”* (23:55–56). The verse is addressed to people who had made exactly the mistake this sūrah's opponent made — reading the flow of goods as evidence about their standing with God.

**Provision and the Obligation Attached to It**

The Book never treats provision as a private possession, and the duties attached to it run through the sūrah's concerns. Gratitude is the first, since the gift arrives before there is anything to give in return. The second is distribution: the Book commands that the due of the harvest be paid when it is gathered, *“Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.”* (6:141). Provision that is received without gratitude and hoarded without obligation is stopped at the point where it was meant to flow onward.

**The Question in the Specific Terms of Makkah**

The Book uses the same reasoning when it challenges the audience to consider the origin of their food: who causes the seed to grow, and who could turn the whole harvest to chaff in one night (56:63–67 — *“If We willed, We could simply reduce this ˹harvest˺ to chaff, leaving you to lament”*). The men of this sūrah were being asked to name the agent responsible for the thing they spent their lives pursuing. There is no other candidate available to them, and the refusal to say so out loud is what the verse calls insolence.

## Verse 67:22

> Who is ˹rightly˺ guided: the one who crawls facedown or the one who walks upright on the Straight Path

**The Posture Completed in the Hereafter**

The Book elsewhere joins the posture of this life to the manner of the gathering to come. It does so with a sentence that reads like the second half of this verse: *“Those who will be dragged into Hell on their faces will be in the worst place, and are ˹now˺ farthest from the ˹Right˺ Way.”* (25:34). The verse is a reminder that the Qur'an's physical descriptions of the next world are often the visible completion of a spiritual condition already present in this one. A person who spent his life with no view of the road will be produced for judgement in the same attitude.

**The Traveller Who Turns Back**

The Book's travel imagery has a darker variant, and it appears in the same context of hearing and walking: *“You certainly cannot make the dead hear ˹the truth˺. Nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and walk away.”* (27:80). The figure is a man walking, upright, on two sound legs — and moving in the wrong direction, away from a voice he can no longer hear. The posture is fine; the destination is not. This is the reason the sūrah's question asks about the guided traveller and not merely about the able-bodied one, and why guidance in the Qur'an is never a matter of capability alone.

## Verse 67:23

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He is the One Who brought you into being and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks.”

**Three Faculties, One Giver**

The verse names an inseparable trio that the Qur'an repeats whenever it wants to describe what a human being has been equipped with: hearing, sight, and the faculty of understanding. The pattern appears in several sūrahs with almost identical wording. *“And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect so perhaps you would be thankful.”* (16:78); *“then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks.”* (32:9). The recurrence is a teaching device. A reader who encounters the same three gifts under the same complaint learns to treat them as the inventory of his own equipment.

**The Two Outer Senses and the One Inner One**

A verse in the Children of Israel's sūrah states the accountability as a formal matter: *“Do not follow what you have no ˹sure˺ knowledge of. Indeed, all will be called to account for ˹their˺ hearing, sight, and intellect.”* (17:36).

**What the Faculties Can Do Without**

Two verses answer the people who trust the equipment and nothing else. The first is a reminder of who could withdraw it: *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Imagine if Allah were to take away your hearing or sight, or seal your hearts—who else other than Allah could restore it?” See ˹O Prophet˺ how We vary the signs, yet they still turn away.”* (6:46). Hearing, sight, and understanding can be removed one at a time, and every adult has met people in whom one of them is gone.

The second concerns nations that had the equipment in working order and were destroyed anyway: *“And We gave them hearing, sight, and intellect. But neither their hearing, sight, nor intellect were of any benefit to them whatsoever, since they persisted in denying Allah's signs. And ˹so˺ they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule.”* (46:26). The faculties are not a protection against the thing they were designed to detect. A working eye that refuses to look at the road is no advantage over a blind one.

**Gratitude as the Use of What Was Given**

The Book always treats thanks as a practice rather than a sentiment, and it attaches a promise to the practice: *“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord proclaimed, ‘If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe.’”* (14:7). The mechanism is not a reward for flattery. A person who acknowledges where his faculties came from will use them in the way they were designed, and a person who uses his hearing to listen, his sight to observe, and his mind to conclude has already begun to act on the truth the verse is pressing.

The Book repeats the trade in another form: *“remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful.”* (2:152). The verse before us closes with the same accusation that other sūrahs make — *you hardly give any thanks* — and the accusation is not that the Makkans failed to say a formula. It is that they were conducting their whole lives on borrowed equipment while treating the equipment as their own.

## Verse 67:24

> ˹Also˺ say, “He is the One Who has dispersed you ˹all˺ over the earth, and to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered.”

**Diversity Treated as a Sign Rather Than a Problem**

The Book does not treat human variety as an embarrassment to be smoothed over; it lists difference among the evidence of design: *“And one of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages and colours. Surely in this are signs for those of ˹sound˺ knowledge.”* (30:22). The same principle governs the verse that explains why peoples and tribes exist at all: *“O humanity! Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another. Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you.”* (49:13). Distinction of nation is an instrument of acquaintance, and the ranking that human beings invent from it is replaced by a single measure.

**One Origin Beneath All the Differences**

The Book's account of human beginnings makes the dispersal a family matter. Humanity was once a single community: *“Humanity had once been one community ˹of believers before they lost faith˺. Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners.”* (2:213), and the disagreements that later divided them are the reason messengers were sent. Division is therefore not the original state of the human family; it is a development, and revelation is the response to it.

**The Test Inside the Differences**

Variation in resources and conditions is assigned a purpose, and the purpose is examination: *“If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you. So compete with one another in doing good. To Allah you will all return, then He will inform you ˹of the truth˺ about your differences.”* (5:48). The Book makes the same point about rank and wealth: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you.”* (6:165).

**"To Him You Will All Be Gathered"**

Against a scattering that looks permanent, the verse sets a gathering that is certain. The Book gives the day a name drawn from this event — the Day of Gathering — and describes its purpose: *“˹Consider˺ the Day He will gather you ˹all˺ for the Day of Gathering—that will be the Day of mutual loss and gain.”* (64:9). The same claim appears in a verse that answers anyone who might treat death as the end of the matter: *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ gives you life, then causes you to die, then will gather you ˹all˺ on the Day of Judgment, about which there is no doubt. But most people do not know.”* (45:26).

The two words of this verse's ending — dispersed, gathered — describe the entire span of human history in one line, and the sūrah places them in the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ as an announcement rather than a threat.

The Book uses the same verb of the first human pair, describing the spread of populations from one origin: *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women.”* (4:1).

## Verse 67:25

> ˹Still˺ they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”

**The Demand for a Date**

The sūrah now reports what the audience had been saying all along. The question is not whether the message can be understood, but when its warning will be executed; the demand is for a schedule, and the conditional clause attached to it — *if what you say is true* — frames the whole issue as a wager about timing. The verse's first word, translated *still*, marks the repetition: the question had been asked many times and would go on being asked.

**Why a Warning Does Not Need a Timetable**

The Book addresses the psychology directly when it explains why the hour is hidden at all: *“The Hour is sure to come. My Will is to keep it hidden, so that every soul may be rewarded according to their efforts.”* (20:15). The concealment is not an evasion; it is the condition under which the test in this sūrah can operate.

**The Answer Already Given in Other Sūrahs**

The Qur'an's standard reply is to move the question from the calendar to its owner. *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, “When will it be?” Say, “That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.””* (7:187). The sūrah of the Cessation states the messenger's position as plainly as possible: *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, “When will it be?” But it is not for you to tell its time. That knowledge rests with your Lord ˹alone˺.”* (79:42–44).

Every exchange follows the same three-part shape: the question, the referral, and the reminder of what the messenger's job actually is. The demand for a date assumes that the messenger is in a position to disclose it, and the Book's answer removes that assumption permanently.

**The Patience the Verse Requires of the Messenger**

The verse ends with the mockers in possession of the question, and the next verse gives the Prophet ﷺ his lines. Nothing in the passage allows him to argue or to bargain, and nothing in it allows him to be shamed into supplying a date. He is told to say what he has been told to say, which is that the knowledge is not his. That division of labour is the model the Book sets for everyone who carries a message: state the warning, refuse the speculation, and leave the timing to the only one who holds it. The Book states the job description without ornament elsewhere: *“Your duty is only to warn whoever is in awe of it.”* (79:45). Then, in the same passage, describes how quickly the interval will seem to have passed once the event arrives — *“On the Day they see it, it will be as if they had stayed ˹in the world˺ no more than one evening or its morning.”* (79:46). The mockers were measuring a delay; the verse is telling them that the delay is shorter than anything they can measure.

## Verse 67:26

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “That knowledge is with Allah alone, and I am only sent with a clear warning.”

**The Referral, and Who Is Included in It**

The reply to the demand for a date is a transfer of jurisdiction. Knowledge of the hour is not withheld from the Prophet ﷺ alone; it is placed beyond every created being. The Book states the principle repeatedly, and each time it includes the highest creatures in its exclusion: *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, “When will it be?” Say, “That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.””* (7:187); *“That knowledge rests with your Lord ˹alone˺.”* (79:44); *“No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die.”* (31:34). Even the information that concerns a person most closely is not issued to anyone in advance.

**Why the Hour Is Kept Hidden**

The concealment is stated in the Book as a deliberate decision with a stated purpose: *“The Hour is sure to come. My Will is to keep it hidden, so that every soul may be rewarded according to their efforts.”* (20:15). The two halves of the sentence belong together. A reward according to efforts is only meaningful where a person must decide without knowing how much time remains. If the date were published, virtue would become arithmetic; the sinners would convert at the last hour and the test would be over.

**"I Am Only Sent With a Clear Warning"**

The second half of the verse draws the boundary of the messenger's office. *Innamā* — only — restricts the commission; a warner gives notice, and notice is what the Prophet ﷺ was sent to deliver. The Book uses the same self-description elsewhere in the same words: *“Have they not ever given it a thought? Their fellow man is not insane. He is only sent with a clear warning.”* (7:184). The formula appears each time the audience tries to expand the messenger's role into something else — a miracle-worker, a soothsayer, or a man who can be pressed for dates.

**The Question in the Report of Jibrīl**

The clearest illustration of this verse comes from a report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, narrated by ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb. A man sat with the Prophet ﷺ and asked about the Hour; the answer he received was that the one being asked knew no more about it than the questioner. The man then asked for its signs, received them, and left, and the Prophet ﷺ told ʿUmar that the visitor had been Jibrīl, who had come to teach them their religion (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8). The report draws the boundary of the office in a single line. Even an angel sent with a question and a man receiving revelation stood on the same side of the boundary when it came to that one piece of knowledge.

The same report closes by reciting the verse that lists what is kept from every creature: *“Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and knows what is in the wombs. No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die.”* (31:34). The Hour is placed inside that list, not above it, and the lesson taught to the community was that saying "I do not know" is part of the knowledge being conveyed.

## Verse 67:27

> Then when they see the torment drawing near, the faces of the disbelievers will become gloomy, and it will be said ˹to them˺, “This is what you claimed would never come.”

**Faces That Register the Verdict**

The Book uses the face as the register of a person's condition on the last day, and it does so with the confidence of a culture in which a face was a public document. In this verse the faces of the disbelievers turn dark — the Arabic carries the sense of a shadow falling across them, the expression of a man who has just been told something final in public. Elsewhere the Book reports that the condemned will be identifiable by their appearance and will be seized by their forelocks and feet (55:41 — *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet”*), and that the guilty will be known by their marks. The body's testimony begins before any word is spoken.

**"This Is What You Claimed Would Never Come"**

The Book records the same demand in its rawest form in the sūrah of the Spoils, where the Makkan leaders challenged God directly: *“And ˹remember˺ when they prayed, “O Allah! If this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky or overcome us with a painful punishment.””* (8:32). The taunt is preserved, and the answer to it is this verse's scene. A demand for immediate proof is granted at the end of the interval, and the granting is not a vindication of the demanders.

**The Demand to Hasten the Punishment**

The pattern is not unique to this passage, and the Book answers it the same way each time. In Sūrah al-Ḥajj the demand is named outright: *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise…”* (22:47). Sūrah Yūnus turns the request back on the one making it: *“Imagine if His torment were to overcome you by night or day—do the wicked realize what they are ˹really˺ asking Him to hasten?”* (10:50). A man who asks for the punishment to be moved forward is asking for the closing of his own record, and the Book reports the request as evidence against him rather than as a difficulty for the message.

**Faces Across the Book's Descriptions of That Day**

The sūrah's portrait of darkened faces belongs to a set of images the Qur'an develops from sūrah to sūrah. In one passage the faces are divided into two parties and both are described: *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing, while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom—”* (80:38–41). The dust on a face is not a metaphor for sadness; it is the condition of a person who has been dragged somewhere against his will. In another passage the humiliation is paired with a physical incapacity: *“˹Beware of˺ the Day the Shin ˹of Allah˺ will be bared, and the wicked will be asked to prostrate, but they will not be able to do so, with eyes downcast, totally covered with disgrace. For they were ˹always˺ called to prostrate ˹in the world˺ when they were fully capable ˹but they chose not to˺.”* (68:42–43).

## Verse 67:28

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Consider this: whether Allah causes me and those with me to die or shows us mercy, who will save the disbelievers from a painful punishment?”

**"Whether He Causes Us to Die or Shows Us Mercy"**

Both outcomes are treated as acceptable, and the balance is worth noticing. The Book does not promise the believers safety, and this verse does not argue that the Prophet ﷺ would survive. The two possibilities named are death and mercy; both are delivered by the same hand, and neither is a defect in the mission. That composure is the foundation of everything the early community did under threat, and it kept them from converting the truth of their message into a claim about their own survival.

**Who Could Save Them From the Punishment?**

The question in the second half of the verse has already been answered twice in this sūrah. The patrons of Makkah cannot deliver protection, as the earlier verse about the powerless helper established, and the Book states the general principle in a challenge to idolaters: *“Ask ˹them˺, “Consider then whatever ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah: if it was Allah's Will to harm me, could they undo that harm? Or if He willed ˹some˺ mercy for me, could they withhold His mercy?” Say, “Allah is enough for me. In Him ˹alone˺ the faithful put their trust.””* (39:38). The logic is symmetrical, and the verse applies it to the party that assumed it was safe.

**Why the Believers' Outcome Is Not the Argument**

The same question is put to the same audience in another sūrah, where the Book supplies the answer by naming the silence: *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet,˺ ‘Who can defend you by day or by night against the Most Compassionate?’ Still they turn away from the remembrance of their Lord.”* (21:42). The deniers are not being told that the believers' fate is uncertain; they are being asked who, among all that exists, would stand between them and the One they have angered. The question has no candidate on the list, and that absence is its force.

**The Avenues That Close on That Day**

The question in this verse has an answer that the Book states by elimination. The first avenue to close is intercession. The Book instructs the believers to spend before a day arrives when the arrangements of this world no longer function: *“O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession. Those who disbelieve are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers.”* (2:254). The second avenue is ransom: *“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given.”* (2:48). Bargaining, friendship, and intercession are the three currencies of a tribal society, and all three are declared void.

What remains is stated positively elsewhere: *“Say, “All intercession belongs to Allah ˹alone˺. To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. Then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.””* (39:44). In a city where a man's protection was the protection his patrons could negotiate, the verse removes the negotiation and leaves the authority where the sūrah's first verse had placed it.

## Verse 67:29

> Say, “He is the Most Compassionate—in Him ˹alone˺ we believe, and in Him ˹alone˺ we trust. You will soon know who is clearly astray.”

**Tawakkul: Trust as Delegation, Not Passivity**

The verb *tawakkalnā* comes from a root meaning to rely on someone and to entrust an affair to him. The Qur'an defines the practice through the character of the one trusted rather than through a method: *“Say, “Allah is enough for me. In Him ˹alone˺ the faithful put their trust.””* (39:38). Reliance in this vocabulary is not the cancellation of effort but the transfer of the outcome — a man does what is his to do and does not treat the result as his own possession.

**The Reading That Ends the Sentence Earlier**

The commentators record a variant recitation of this verse. In the reading attributed to al-Kisāʾī, the last word is pronounced *sayaʿlamūn*, "they will soon know," which changes the scope of the sentence: what the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to say ends with the declaration of trust. The announcement of coming knowledge becomes part of God's speech rather than part of the community's. The difference is small and the effect is instructive — in both readings the outcome is left to God, and the believers' line ends with the reliance they are told to profess.

**The Name That Opens a Sūrah**

The Most Compassionate is not only a name used in the middle of arguments; it is the opening of a sūrah devoted to the gifts by which creation is sustained: *“The Most Compassionate — taught the Quran, created humanity, ˹and˺ taught them speech.”* (55:1–4). The sūrah of the Most Compassionate then lists sign after sign, and its refrain asks which of the favours of the Lord the audience will deny. The placement in that sūrah matches the use made of the name here. Both passages attach the mercy in the name to something concrete: rain, fruit, guidance, and the ability to speak.

## Verse 67:30

> Say, “Consider this: if your water were to sink ˹into the earth˺, then who ˹else˺ could bring you flowing water?”

**The Water That Sinks Out of Reach**

The Arabic verb describes water going down into the earth and staying there — a sinking beyond the reach of wells, ropes, and hands. A spring does not run dry gradually in this image; it disappears into the ground, and the city is left standing above an aquifer it cannot touch. The Book states the same possibility elsewhere as a plain fact about divine control: *“We send down rain from the sky in perfect measure, causing it to soak into the earth. And We are surely able to take it away.”* (23:18). The rain that soaks in is a gift; the rain that continues downward is a sentence.

**Who Could Bring It Back?**

The second half of the question has the same structure as the sūrah's other challenges. Every candidate is considered and excluded: not the gods of the sanctuary, not the tribal patrons, not the wealth of the merchants, not the engineering of any people in the region. The Book gives the same challenge to the Makkans in the sūrah of the Event about their food and their drink: *“Have you considered the water you drink? Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so? If We willed, We could make it salty. Will you not then give thanks?”* (56:68–70), and in the sūrah of the Frowning Face it directs people to consider the whole chain of their meals, from rainfall to grain and fruit (80:24–30 — *“Let people then consider their food”*).

**Water as the Measure of Provision**

The Book treats water as the clearest image of provision in general. It recurs in that role: rain sent down to give life to a dead land and to provide for every creature in it (25:48–49 — *“giving life to a lifeless land, and providing water for countless animals and humans of Our Own creation”*). The earth stirred to life by rain and brought back from lifelessness (41:39 — *“And among His signs is that you see the earth devoid of life, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell.”*); every living thing created from water (21:30 — *“And We created from water every living thing.”*); the earth made habitable and the sky turned into a canopy over it (2:22 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you.”*). Each of these passages makes the same argument in a different register: the ordinary gift is the one that is never counted until it is gone.

**Flowing Water and the Word Behind It**

The adjective rendered *flowing* is *maʿīn*, which describes water that runs and shows itself — the opposite of water standing still, hidden, or brackish. The commentators record two explanations of the word: some connected it with the eye, since flowing water is water that one can see, and others with the verb for running. Either way the contrast with the imagined disaster is exact.

**The Same Water Can Bring the Garden or End It**

Two other passages complete the argument. In one, a garden reaches its fullest beauty and the owners conclude that they control what happens to it: *“Then just as the earth looks its best, perfectly beautified. Its people think they have full control over it, there comes to it Our command by night or by day, so We mow it down as if it never flourished yesterday!”* (10:24). The Book's image of the owners' mistake is not the neglect of their land; it is their confidence in it. In another passage, rain produces crops of every colour, and then the same crops dry, wither, and are reduced to chaff (39:21 — *“Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky—channelling it through streams in the earth—then produces with it crops of various colours, then they dry up and you see them wither, and then He reduces them to chaff?”*). The water given to the garden is the same water that makes the harvest possible, and its withdrawal leaves both the harvest and the confidence gone.
