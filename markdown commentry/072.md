# Sūrah al-Jinn (Chapter 72) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Its Occasion: The Years of Pressure**

The reports place this sūrah in the period of the Prophet's greatest vulnerability in Makkah. In the year 619 he lost the protection of his uncle Abū Ṭālib, travelled to Ṭāʾif seeking support, was reviled and set upon by a mob there, and returned across the desert without a patron. It was on such a journey, at Nakhlah — the valley between Makkah and Ṭāʾif — that the encounter reported in this sūrah is set.

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4921 records the event from Ibn ʿAbbās: a party of jinn came upon the Messenger of Allah ﷺ at Nakhlah while he was on his way to the market of ʿUkāẓ, leading the dawn prayer with his Companions. When they heard the Qur'an they listened, and said to one another that this was what had been intervening between them and the news of the heavens.

A second account, in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 450 from Ibn Masʿūd, describes a different night: an inviter came from the jinn, the Prophet ﷺ went with him, recited the Qur'an to them, and afterwards showed his Companions their traces and the traces of their fires.

**The Two Orders of Creation**

The Qur'an treats jinn as creatures, not as powers. They are made from a smokeless flame (55:15; 15:27), created for the same purpose as human beings — *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56) — addressed by messengers from among themselves (6:130), divided into the submitted and the deviant (72:14), gathered with humanity on the Day (6:128), and liable to the same Fire (11:119; 32:13). Iblīs, the Qur'an states, was one of them (18:50).

This sūrah gives that account its most detailed treatment, and it does so in the jinn's own reported words. They describe their factions, their former doctrine about God, their stations in the heavens, the closing of those stations, and their ignorance of what is intended for the earth. The portrait is of a community with a religious history, a trade, teachers it trusted too long, and a conversion — not of a force to be courted or feared.

That portrait was aimed at a specific practice. In pre-Islamic Arabia a traveller stopping for the night called on the master of the valley for protection, and the sūrah reports the transaction's result: some men sought refuge with some jinn, and the two increased each other in wrong (72:6). The chapter dismantles the practice by describing the ones being called — beings who cannot frustrate God on earth, cannot escape Him by flight, and will be fuel for the Fire if they deviate.

**Its Place Beside Sūrah Nūḥ**

The chapter follows Sūrah Nūḥ, and the pairing is deliberate. Noah is reported in the tradition as the first messenger sent to the people of the earth — the title given him by humanity in the intercession narration recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 — while al-Jinn reports listeners arriving at the last messenger's recitation. The first chapter of the pair ends with a prayer for the destruction of a people who refused for nine and a half centuries; the second ends with an account of the escorted channel by which truth reaches creatures at all.

Both chapters carry the same promise in the same image: water in abundance for a people who follow the way (71:11; 72:16). Both describe a divided community and name the two outcomes. And both place the Prophet ﷺ, who was told both stories in Makkah, between the first messenger's exhausted mission and a delegation of listeners he never saw — a man with no patron, no power, and no date, carrying a message that had already converted an audience from another world.

The jinn's stations in the heavens were closed (72:8–9); they do not know whether evil or good is intended for the earth (72:10); the messenger does not know whether what is promised is near or far (72:25); and God is the Knower of the unseen, who discloses none of it to anyone except a messenger He approves, escorted by guards before and behind (72:26–27).

## Verse 72:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened ˹to the Quran,˺ and said ˹to their fellow jinn˺: ‘Indeed, we have heard a wondrous recitation

**A Revelation About a Revelation**

The sūrah opens with a command to speak, and the content of the speech is a report the Prophet ﷺ did not witness and could not have known. It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened. The revelation here is not the Qur'anic text being heard by the jinn; it is the news that they heard it, delivered to a man who was not told at the time.

That structure is the sūrah's central gift, and it explains the chapter's occasion. The reports place this revelation in the period after the loss of Abū Ṭālib's protection and the journey to Ṭāʾif, when the Prophet ﷺ returned from a town that had set a mob on him and stood praying in the desert with no patron and almost no followers. A man in that position, whose human audience was closing against him, is told that an audience he never saw had stopped to listen and had gone home believing. The message was being received. The receivers were invisible to him, and the news of them arrived as revelation.

The verse also fixes the sūrah's form. Everything from here to verse fifteen is quoted speech — the jinn's own account, relayed to their own people, relayed to the Prophet ﷺ, relayed to Makkah. Four layers of transmission, and the outermost is a command: *say*.

**The Night at Naḥlah**

The event is described in the ḥadīth literature with a precision that fixes its place and its circumstances. Ibn ʿAbbās reports, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4921, that a party of jinn from Tihāmah came upon the Messenger of Allah ﷺ at Nakhlah — the valley between Makkah and Ṭāʾif — while he was on his way to the market of ʿUkāẓ, and that they found him leading the dawn prayer with his Companions. When they heard the Qur'an being recited they listened, and said to one another: this is what has been intervening between you and the news of the heavens. Then they returned to their people and said: O our people, we have heard a wondrous recitation that guides to the right, and we have believed in it.

Three details in that report carry weight. The recitation was liturgical — the dawn prayer, aloud, with a congregation. The listeners were on an errand of their own, searching for the cause of the barrier that had been placed between them and the sky. And the conclusion they drew connected the two: the same event that had closed the heavens had opened the earth, and the thing recited in a desert valley at dawn was the reason.

The second account, in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 450 from Ibn Masʿūd, describes a different night: an inviter came from the jinn, the Prophet ﷺ went with him and recited the Qur'an to them, and afterwards showed his Companions their traces and the traces of their fires. The commentators reconcile the two by treating them as two encounters — one in which the jinn came upon him unannounced, and one in which he went to them at their invitation. Both are authentic reports, and the reconciliation preserves both without collapsing the sūrah's own account, which describes listening rather than a meeting.

**ʿajabā — Wonder as the First Word of the Report**

The thing they heard is described with a single word placed after the noun: *qurʾānan ʿajabā*, a recitation of wonder, a wondrous recitation. The construction puts the wonder in the position of a quality inseparable from the object.

The word *ʿajab* denotes what exceeds expectation — a thing that stops a person because it does not fit what he had prepared to encounter. It is not admiration in the aesthetic sense only. The jinn who said it were beings older than humanity, accustomed to the speech of the heavens, and their report uses the vocabulary of an expert witness who has met something outside his range.

The Qur'an makes the same claim in its own voice, and it makes it about the same two audiences: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other.’”* (17:88). Humans and jinn are named together as the two orders of express creation, and the challenge is addressed to their combined capacity. That the jinn in this verse register wonder on first hearing is the narrative form of the same claim.

## Verse 72:2

> It leads to Right Guidance so we believed in it, and we will never associate anyone with our Lord ˹in worship˺

**The Order of the Sentence: Guidance First, Belief Second**

The jinn's report gives a reason for its belief, and the reason precedes the belief in the sentence: it guides to right guidance, *therefore* we believed in it. The particle *fa* marks the sequence as consequence, not chronology alone. They did not believe and then discover that the recitation was sound. They recognized what it did and submitted to it on that basis.

That order is the Qur'an's ordinary account of how faith arrives, and it is worth stating against the common assumption that belief comes first and understanding follows. Here the object is examined, its function is identified, and the will follows the identification. The jinn behave as competent witnesses: they report what the recitation does before they report what they decided about it.

The same structure appears in the other account of this delegation, in al-Aḥqāf, where the listeners describe the book as revealed after Moses, confirming what came before it, guiding to the truth and to a straight way (46:30). Both reports place the guiding function first and the response second, and both make the response immediate. The evidence and the assent are not separated by a period of deliberation.

**Two Acts in One Clause: Belief and the Refusal of Partners**

The confession has a positive half and a negative half, joined by *wa*: we believed in it, and we will never associate anyone with our Lord. The second half uses *lan*, the particle of emphatic future negation — not "we do not" but "we will not, ever.".

The pairing is the standard shape of Islamic confession and it appears in the same order throughout the Qur'an: affirmation of God, then exclusion of everything else. What is notable here is that the exclusion is attached to the belief in a book. The jinn do not say we believed and we will behave accordingly; they specify the one behavior that belief rules out immediately, which is partnership in worship.

That specification tells the reader what their previous condition had been. Beings who announce a permanent refusal of association are beings who had been associating, or who had lived among those who did. The verse that follows confirms it: they had believed that their Lord had taken a mate and offspring, and they name the foolish among themselves as the source of the claim (72:3–4). The confession is therefore a correction of a prior teaching, issued by the students to the school that taught it.

**A Community Announcing Its Correction**

The speech in this verse is addressed to the jinn's own people, and its content is a doctrinal reversal. The delegation has come home to say that the theology it was raised on is wrong, and that the source of the error is identifiable.

The structure of that announcement is unusual and it deserves notice. Nobody is accused by name. The confession uses the first person plural throughout — *we* believed, *we* will never associate, and in the following verse, the foolish one *among us*. A community correcting itself in this way does not need an inquisition; it needs only to state where it went wrong and to name the direction it now holds.

The Qur'an describes the same dynamic from the outside in a verse about the enemies of every prophet: *“And so We have made for every prophet enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception.”* (6:112). Both orders of creation have their deceivers and their deceived, and in both the deception travels by speech that sounds refined. The jinn's report is the counter-case: a group that heard something more compelling than the elegant deception and said so in public, to the people who had believed them.

## Verse 72:3

> ˹Now, we believe that˺ our Lord—Exalted is His Majesty—has neither taken a mate nor offspring

**Neither Mate Nor Child**

Two things are denied, in this order: *ṣāḥiba*, a female companion taken in partnership, a mate; and *walad*, a child. The order is not interchangeable.

The mate comes first because it is the premise of the child in every human case, and because the notion of a divine mate imports something worse than genealogy — it imports need. A being who takes a companion does so out of incompleteness, desire, or loneliness, and each of those is a creature's attribute. The child then imports a second impossibility: continuation of a kind, which requires mortality somewhere in the line, since offspring exist to carry on what cannot guarantee its own permanence.

The Qur'an denies both, always in this pairing and always with the same implication. It asks the pagans whether their Lord has daughters while they prefer sons for themselves (37:149), and answers the claim in two words: *“Allah has children.” They are simply liars.* (37:152). In Maryam the statement is met with cosmic language: *“They say, ‘The Most Compassionate has offspring.’”* (19:88), followed by *“It does not befit ˹the majesty of˺ the Most Compassionate to have children.”* (19:92). The phrase *does not befit* is the same argument the jinn perform with their parenthesis — a matter of what majesty permits, not of what evidence supports.

**The Shortest Statement of the Same Faith**

The denial in this verse is the content of the shortest sūrah in the Qur'an, and the two texts cover identical ground in the same order. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺; Allah—the Sustainer ˹needed by all˺. He has never had offspring, nor was He born. And there is none comparable to Him.’”* (112:1–4).

The correspondences are exact. Oneness rules out the mate. Self-sufficiency — the *ṣamad*, the one whom all need and who needs nothing — rules out the desire that would produce a mate. The explicit denial of offspring and of being born rules out genealogy in both directions. And incomparability rules out the whole category of creature attributes from which family relations are drawn.

That a delegation of jinn, hearing a recitation once at dawn in a valley, should state the doctrine in the terms of 112 is the sūrah's own evidence for the unity of the message. They had not studied it. They had not been catechized. They heard the recitation, recognized what it was doing, and drew the conclusion the whole Qur'an draws — which is what verse two means when it says the recitation guides to right guidance.

**The Human Error This Confession Answers**

There is a further edge to the verse that the Qur'an's first audience would have felt immediately. The same passage in al-Anʿām that denies divine offspring names the specific error being corrected: *“Yet they associate the jinn with Allah ˹in worship˺, even though He created them, and they falsely attribute to Him sons and daughters out of ignorance. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they claim!”* (6:100).

Two errors sit side by side there, and they are mirror images. Human beings assigned jinn a share in the divine government; jinn, according to this sūrah, had assigned God a family. Each order of creation had inflated something — the humans inflated a created being, the jinn inflated their own conception of the Creator — and both errors are answered by the same sentence: He created them, and He has no mate or child.

The Day is described with the same correction in place. The angels are asked whether they were the ones being worshipped, and they answer: *“Glory be to You! Our loyalty is to You, not them. In fact, they ˹only˺ followed the ˹temptations of evil˺ jinn, in whom most of them had faith.”* (34:41). The beings credited with divine knowledge are themselves creatures who followed temptations. The jinn of this sūrah say the same thing from inside: exalted is the majesty of our Lord, and we are not partners in it.

The same test is applied to the Qur'an's own audience repeatedly, and it is applied to the objects of their devotion as well as to their ideas: *“Glorified is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, the Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim.”* (43:82).

## Verse 72:4

> and that the foolish of us used to utter ˹outrageous˺ falsehoods about Allah

**Who the Fool Is**

The tradition identifies the *safīh* with Iblīs, and the identification has scriptural support of a specific kind. The Qur'an states his origin plainly: *“And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Adam,’ so they all did—but not Iblîs, who was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord.”* (18:50). The one who refused was of the jinn, which makes him a candidate for the foolish one among us.

The identification also fits the content. What Iblīs uttered about God was not a small error; it was a claim about the divine command itself — that he was better than the being he was told to honor, and that the instruction was therefore wrong. That is the paradigm of *shaṭaṭ*, the extravagance named in this verse: a statement about God that overruns what can be said about Him.

The class reading does not compete with the individual one. Iblīs is the first instance of a type, and the type has been productive ever since. The Qur'an describes its operation in both orders of creation: *“And so We have made for every prophet enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception.”* (6:112). Elegant words are exactly what a *safīh* produces, and the elegance is why the deception travels.

**shaṭaṭ — Speech That Overruns Its Bounds**

The word translated *˹outrageous˺ falsehoods* is built on sh-ṭ-ṭ, a root whose basic sense is distance from the middle, remoteness from the shore, excess beyond a limit. A *shāṭṭ* is one who has gone too far, and *shaṭaṭ* in speech is the claim that exceeds what the subject permits.

The nuance matters because it distinguishes this verse from an ordinary accusation of lying. A liar says what he knows to be false. A *shāṭṭ* says what cannot be true — a statement so far outside the bounds of its subject that the speaker has not merely erred but abandoned the discipline of speaking about that subject at all. The verse therefore describes the divine-offspring claim not as a mistake in reporting but as a category violation.

The Qur'an treats this category as the gravest form of speech. *“Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah…”* (6:93), and the answer to the question is that nobody does. Fabrication against God is named as the summit of wrongdoing, above murder and above denial, because it misrepresents the source of everything and does so in His name. The prohibition is legislated in the same terms: forbidden are indecencies, aggression, association, *“and attributing to Allah what you do not know”* (7:33). The last item is the definition of *shaṭaṭ* — saying about God what one does not know.

**"About Allah" — The Preposition That Fixes the Crime**

The phrase is *ʿalā Allāh*, upon God. Arabic uses this preposition for speech that is laid on top of its subject — a claim attributed to him, a burden placed on his account. The same construction appears in the warnings about inventing law: *“Do not falsely declare with your tongues, ‘This is lawful, and that is unlawful,’ ˹only˺ fabricating lies against Allah. Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed.”* (16:116).

The preposition indicates that the offense is against the one spoken about, not merely against the truth. A lie about a market price harms a transaction. A lie upon God reorganizes the reality of everyone who believes it, and it does so in the name of the only authority that could correct it. That is why the jinn's confession treats the matter as a communal catastrophe rather than as a doctrinal disagreement: their whole community had been living inside a statement that could not be true, and the statement was about the One they were accountable to.

The verse that follows explains how long they lived inside it — they had assumed that no one, human or jinn, would lie about God (72:5). The assumption is what made the crime effective.

## Verse 72:5

> We certainly thought that humans and jinn would never speak lies about Allah

**Two Orders of Creation in One Sentence**

The sentence names humans and jinn together, and the pairing is a fixed feature of Qur'anic speech about the express creation. The two are addressed jointly, judged jointly, and challenged jointly.

They are created for one purpose: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56). They are warned through messengers from among themselves: *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?”* (6:130). They are gathered together on the Day and addressed as two parties in one company: *“O assembly of jinn! You misled humans in great numbers.”* (6:128). And they are challenged together by the text of this sūrah: if all humans and jinn came together to produce its equal, they could not (17:88).

That last pairing is the one that bears on this verse. The two orders are the two producers of speech in the created world, and the Qur'an's claim is that neither of them, separately or combined, can produce anything like it. The jinn's premise — that neither humans nor jinn would lie about God — assumed a virtue in both orders that neither has, and the correction they received came from a speech neither order could produce.

**What the Correction Changes**

To discover that people lie about God is not only to add a fact. It is to lose the shortcut by which most claims about God were previously accepted.

Before the correction, a claim about the divine needed no examination, because the seriousness of the subject guaranteed the honesty of the speaker. After it, every claim about God requires a criterion, since the seriousness of the subject now guarantees only the size of the stakes. This is why the Qur'an insists on its own function as the standard by which other statements are tested — a guide and a healing (41:44), a book with no crookedness in it, made upright to warn and to give good news (18:1–2).

The jinn's conversion follows exactly that path. They did not arrive at a new set of opinions; they arrived at a criterion. The recitation guides to right guidance (72:2), which means it supplies the instrument by which a claim about God can be judged. Having obtained the instrument, they applied it to their own inheritance and found the doctrine of divine offspring wanting. That sequence — hear the criterion, apply it to what you already believe, revise — is the whole content of the sūrah's first fifteen verses.

**The Same Premise Held in Makkah**

The verse is spoken by jinn, and it lands on a human audience that was holding the identical premise in a different form. The Quraysh did not defend their religion with arguments either. They defended it with the integrity of their predecessors: we follow what we found our forefathers practicing (2:170); *“We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our forefathers”* (23:24); *“These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up”* (53:23).

The last of those three is the Qur'an's own verdict on the premise, and it is the same verdict the jinn have just pronounced on themselves: the ancestors were not reliable speakers about God. What the Makkan audience is being shown, through the mouth of an audience they have never seen, is that the assumption which held their religion together has been examined and abandoned by beings with far better access to the unseen than they have.

The sūrah's placement of this verse immediately after the confession is therefore strategic. The correction of a doctrine is easy to describe; the correction of the premise that allowed the doctrine is what makes the change permanent. The jinn report both, in that order, and the human listener is being invited to perform the same two operations on his own inheritance.

The Qur'an's counter-instruction is stated as a demand for evidence rather than for sincerity: *“Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.”* (2:111).

## Verse 72:6

> And some men used to seek refuge with some jinn—so they increased each other in wickedness

**The Grammar of Mutual Increase**

The clause *fa-zādūhum rahaqā* — so they increased them in wickedness — has been read in two directions, and the ambiguity is in the Arabic itself rather than in the translation.

On one reading the subject is the men: the humans increased the jinn in arrogance. By calling on them for protection, treating them as powers, and acknowledging their authority over a valley, the men fed the jinn's estimate of themselves. A creature that is prayed to begins to believe it deserves prayer.

On the other reading the subject is the jinn: they increased the men in sin and fear, leading them further into the practice and into the errors attached to it. The men became more dependent, more frightened, and more superstitious.

The stronger reading holds both, and the translation reflects that by rendering the clause reciprocally. The mechanism is circular: the man's fear elevates the jinn, the elevated jinn produces more fear, and each round leaves both parties worse than the last. The Qur'an describes the same circuit from the divine side: *“This is how We make the wrongdoers ˹destructive˺ allies of one another because of their misdeeds.”* (6:129). Wrongdoers are paired with wrongdoers, and the pairing is itself part of the punishment.

**The Two Accounts of the Same Arrangement**

In al-Anʿām the same partnership is described on the Day of Judgment, from the human side, and the wording confirms the reading of this verse. The company is gathered and addressed: *“O assembly of jinn! You misled humans in great numbers.”* Their human associates reply: *“Our Lord! We benefited from each other’s company, but now we have reached the term which You appointed for us.”* (6:128).

The reply is remarkable for what it concedes. We benefited from each other — the mutual advantage is acknowledged, in the past tense, by people standing at the end of it. The arrangement worked while it lasted: the man got the feeling of protection, the jinn got the standing of a protector. Both parties were paid in a currency that turned out to be worthless, and the verse in this sūrah names that currency as *rahaq*.

The two passages together give the complete picture. This verse describes the practice as it operated on earth; 6:128 describes the same practice as it is evaluated at the end. The evaluation is not that one party deceived the other. It is that both took something from the exchange and both were increased in wrong by taking it.

**The Refuge Formula Replaced**

The Qur'an does not leave the traveller without protection. It relocates the protection, and the relocation is total. The one to be called upon at the moment of fear is named repeatedly and exclusively: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (7:200; cf. 41:36). Before recitation itself, the same instruction: *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.”* (16:98).

The last sūrah of the Qur'an is built as the replacement for the valley formula. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I seek refuge in the Lord of humankind, the Master of humankind, the God of humankind…’”* (114:1–3) — three titles of one being, each of which answers a different aspect of the fear the old formula tried to manage. The one who whispers into the hearts of humankind, from among jinn and humankind (114:5–6), is named as the object of the refuge rather than as its provider.

That final identification is the sūrah's verdict on the practice of verse six. The being a frightened traveller called upon for protection against the jinn is himself, in the Qur'an's account, one of the jinn or one of their human collaborators. The refuge was being sought from the danger. The verse states the transaction in one line and leaves the reader to work out what it cost.

## Verse 72:7

> And those ˹humans˺ thought, just like you ˹jinn˺, that Allah would not resurrect anyone ˹for judgment˺

**The Denial Both Orders Held**

The content of the shared thought is specific: God will not raise anyone. Not that the resurrection is uncertain, or distant, or reserved for some — that it will not happen to anybody.

That denial is the Qur'an's most frequently reported objection, and it is reported in the language of its holders. *“The disbelievers claim they will not be resurrected. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Yes, by my Lord, you will surely be resurrected, then you will certainly be informed of what you have done. And that is easy for Allah.’”* (64:7). They swear their most solemn oaths that God will never raise the dead (16:38). They ask: *“Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones?”* — and the answer is immediate: *“Yes ˹indeed˺! We are ˹most˺ capable of restoring ˹even˺ their very fingertips.”* (75:3–4).

The phrasing in this verse is notable for its generality. It does not say "will not resurrect us." It says *aḥadan*, anyone — a denial of the event as such, extended to every creature. That universality is what makes the error worth naming in a report by jinn to jinn: it was not a tribal opinion or a local custom. It was a cosmological position held across two orders of creation.

**Why the Denial Carries Everything Else**

The refusal of resurrection is not one doctrine among others in the Qur'an's analysis; it is the doctrine on which every other refusal depends. Without a return, no act has a consequence beyond its immediate effects, no warning has force, and the whole enterprise of prophethood becomes advice rather than news.

This is why the Qur'an argues for the resurrection more often and more variously than for anything else. It argues from origin — *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then— behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77), followed by the man who holds up a decayed bone and asks who will give it life (36:78). It argues from the dead earth revived by rain (30:19; 50:11). It argues from the first creation as the precedent for the second (2:28; 20:55). And it argues from the inconsistency of the deniers themselves, who confess the creation and refuse its sequel: *“And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, ‘Allah!’”* (31:25), and *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, ‘Allah!’ How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?”* (43:87).

The last form of the argument is the one this verse supports. A person who accepts a maker and refuses a reckoning has accepted the harder claim and rejected the easier one, and the rejection is therefore not intellectual. It is motivated.

**Accountability Extended to Both Orders**

The verse also settles a question the sūrah will return to at its close: whether jinn are subject to judgment. If the resurrection was denied by both orders, then both orders stand in need of it, and both will be gathered for it.

The Qur'an states the inclusion explicitly. The address on the Day is to both: *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?”* (6:130). The Fire is filled from both: *“I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together.”* (11:119; 32:13). And the purpose of creating both is one: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56).

That the jinn's own delegation announces the shared denial is therefore not merely a historical note. It is the premise of their own accountability, stated by them before the sūrah draws the conclusion — which it does in the final two verses of their speech, where the righteous among them are distinguished from the deviant, and the deviant are named as fuel for the Fire.

## Verse 72:8

> ˹Earlier˺ we tried to reach heaven ˹for news˺, only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars

**The Errand That Found the Door Closed**

The jinn explain what they were doing in that valley on the night they heard the recitation. They had gone looking for news from heaven, and they found the approach to heaven occupied by guards and by projectiles.

The bracketed insertion in the translation — *˹for news˺* — supplies what the Arabic leaves understood: the object of the attempt was information. The phrase *an-nabaʾ*, news of consequence, is what the eavesdroppers were after, and it is the same word the Qur'an uses for a report of weight, a tidings that changes the position of whoever hears it. Their business was intelligence, and the intelligence channel had been secured.

That detail gives the verse its narrative function inside the sūrah. These listeners were not strolling. They were on an errand connected to their traditional occupation — obtaining fragments of the unseen and passing them on — and the errand failed. What they heard instead, in a valley on the road to ʿUkāẓ, turned out to be the reason the errand failed. The Ibn ʿAbbās report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4921 records their conclusion in their own words: this is what has intervened between you and the news of the heavens.

**Sentries and Projectiles**

The description has two components: *ḥarasan shadīdan*, a stern or mighty guard, and *shuhub*, flares or meteors. The guard is personal and organized; the projectiles are the means of enforcement.

The same arrangement is described from the divine side in three other places. *“And We protected it from every accursed devil, except the one eavesdropping, who is then pursued by a visible flare.”* (15:17–18). *“Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil. They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side, ˹fiercely˺ driven away. And they will suffer an everlasting torment. But whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is ˹instantly˺ pursued by a piercing flare.”* (37:6–10). And: *“And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps, and made them ˹as missiles˺ for stoning ˹eavesdropping˺ devils.”* (67:5).

Three features recur in all of them, and together they fix the sense of this verse. The barred party is specifically the devil who tries to eavesdrop, not every created being. The bar is enforced physically rather than merely decreed. And the object of the attempt is the *al-malaʾ al-aʿlā*, the highest assembly — the company of angels where decrees are spoken.

**When the Bar Was Installed**

The verse says the guard was found there, and the reports connect the installation to the beginning of the revelation. The jinn had been reaching the heavens before; now they could not; and the change coincided with the arrival of a prophet whose message had to be protected from contamination before it was delivered.

The logic is explicit in the verse that closes this sūrah: guards are dispatched before and behind a messenger so that he may know the messages of his Lord have been conveyed intact (72:27–28). The protection of revelation and the barring of eavesdroppers are the same measure seen from two sides. A message that could be intercepted, garbled, and pre-announced by fortune-tellers would not be a message; it would be material in circulation.

The Qur'an also states the theological consequence for the intercepted fragment: whoever steals a hearing is pursued, and the punishment attached is everlasting (37:9–10). The severity indicates what is at stake. This is not a boundary around a private divine space; it is a boundary around the process by which truth reaches creatures, and its violation corrupts the religion of everyone downstream.

## Verse 72:9

> We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping, but whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for them

**The Word for Eavesdropping**

*Istamaʿa*, to listen in, is used here twice, and its tenth form carries the sense of listening deliberately and stealthily — leaning in to catch something not addressed to you. The same verb is used in verse one for what the jinn did to the Prophet's recitation, and the contrast between the two uses is the hinge of the sūrah.

In verse one they listened to a recitation addressed to everyone present, in a valley, during a communal prayer, and the listening produced belief. In this verse they listened at a station to a conversation they were not meant to hear, and the listening produced a trade in fabrication. One verb, two objects, two outcomes. The sūrah places them seven verses apart and leaves the comparison to the reader: the same faculty, directed at an open revelation rather than a closed council, is what converts a class of eavesdroppers into a delegation of warners.

The Qur'an elsewhere describes the closed council as *al-malaʾ al-aʿlā*, the highest assembly, from which the devils are driven away and pelted from every side (37:8). Eavesdropping on that assembly is what the flare exists to prevent.

**A Flare Lying in Wait**

The second half of the verse changes tense and register. Whoever listens now will find a *shihāb raṣad* — a flare, or a piercing firebrand, held in readiness for him. The noun *raṣad* denotes an ambush, a watch set in advance, a position from which something is awaited.

The reversal is exact. The eavesdroppers used to hold stations in readiness for the angels. Now a station is held in readiness for the eavesdroppers. The verse describes the same arrangement turned around: the listening post has been replaced by an ambush at the listening post.

The parallel descriptions confirm the mechanism: *“except the one eavesdropping, who is then pursued by a visible flare”* (15:18), and *“whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is ˹instantly˺ pursued by a piercing flare”* (37:10). The pursuer in both cases is described as *shihāb*, a streak of fire — the same word used for the meteor that crosses the sky visibly. The enforcement is not hidden from human eyes; it is the thing human eyes see when they look up at night.

**What the Closure Did to the Trade**

The practical consequence is stated in the ḥadīth literature without ambiguity. The Prophet ﷺ told his Companions that fortune-tellers are nothing, and explained the residual accuracy of their predictions as a single stolen word mixed with more than a hundred lies — a report agreed upon by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿĀʾishah. Once the word cannot be stolen, only the lies remain.

The Qur'an describes the downstream end of the same trade: *“Shall I inform you of whom the devils ˹actually˺ descend upon? They descend upon every sinful liar, who gives an ˹attentive˺ ear ˹to half-truths˺, mostly passing on sheer lies.”* (26:221–223). The chain is complete in three links — the eavesdropper at his station, the devil descending on his human associate, the associate retailing the mixture — and verse nine reports that the first link has been cut.

The effect on the Arabian religious economy was immediate and total. Every soothsayer's claim rested on access to a source that no longer existed. This is why the jinn's errand in verse eight was undertaken at all: the practitioners of the trade, unable to reach their usual posts, went looking for an explanation of the closure, and the explanation was a man reciting at dawn.

## Verse 72:10

> Now, we have no clue whether evil is intended for those on earth, or their Lord intends for them what is right

**Two Possibilities, and Neither One Known**

The alternatives stated are exhaustive and opposed: evil intended for those on earth, or good intended for them by their Lord. The verse does not narrow the field to a specific event — a drought, a war, a punishment, a blessing. It covers the general direction of affairs.

The formulation is careful in two ways. Evil is described as *intended for* the inhabitants of the earth, in the passive, without naming an agent; good is described as *their Lord intending it for them*, with the agent named. The asymmetry is not accidental. When good arrives, its source is stated; when harm arrives, the verse does not attribute it to a rival power. There is one Lord in the sentence, and the ignorance concerns His purposes, not His existence or His control.

The same discipline appears in the Qur'an's own idiom: the good that befalls a person is from God, and the harm is described in ways that keep the divine government intact. The jinn have learned the idiom. Their speech in this sūrah consistently attributes lordship, majesty, and intention to one God, and consistently denies themselves any share in knowledge of His plans.

**The Doctrine the Verse Establishes**

The claim that the unseen belongs exclusively to God is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an without qualification, and this verse is its narrative confirmation from an unexpected witness.

*“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah. Nor do they know when they will be resurrected.’”* (27:65). *“With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him.”* (6:59). The exclusion covers every created being, and the phrase *in the heavens and the earth* is comprehensive enough to include both orders of creation that this sūrah is about.

The Qur'an also supplies a case study of the same limitation, involving jinn of the highest rank. Solomon's subjects among the jinn labored at his command for years, and his death was not known to them: *“When We decreed Solomon’s death, nothing indicated to the ˹subjected˺ jinn that he was dead except the termites eating away his staff. So when he collapsed, the jinn realized that if they had ˹really˺ known the unseen, they would not have remained in ˹such˺ humiliating servitude.”* (34:14). Jinn who could not detect the death of the man standing in front of them are jinn who cannot read the decrees above them. Verse ten states the same conclusion from inside.

**The Right Response to Not Knowing**

The verse is not a statement of despair, and it is not offered as a complaint. It follows immediately on the report that the heavens are guarded, and it functions as the acceptance of a boundary.

Knowing that you do not know is the beginning of the sūrah's positive teaching. The next verses describe what the jinn did with their ignorance: they recognized that they cannot frustrate God on earth and cannot escape Him by flight (72:12), and they believed when they heard the guidance (72:13). The sequence is instructive — a group that has lost its claim to foreknowledge does not become nihilist. It becomes obedient. The ignorance is accepted as a condition of creatureliness rather than as a deprivation.

That is the alternative the Qur'an offers to divination. A person who needs to know the future is a person who is trying to manage it, and management of the future by creatures is what the verse denies. What remains for a creature who cannot know is trust in the One who does, and the sūrah's remaining verses work out what that trust looks like in practice.

## Verse 72:11

> Among us are those who are righteous and those who are less so. We have been of different factions

**A Community That Is Not One Thing**

The jinn describe themselves, and the description refuses every stereotype their human clients had built. Among us are the righteous, and among us are others below that. We have been ways divided.

The two categories are stated with deliberate understatement. *Ṣāliḥūn*, the righteous, the sound, the ones whose condition is right — and then *dūna dhālik*, literally "other than that," "below that," a phrase that does not name the second group at all. The understatement is honest rather than evasive: the verse that follows describes them as on divided paths, and verses fourteen and fifteen will name the second category precisely and assign it its outcome. Here the speakers are simply refusing to be counted as a single bloc.

That refusal is the verse's argument. A human being who consults a jinn has already assumed something about the class — that it is powerful, that it knows, that it can protect. The class now reports that it contains the good and the not-good, that it is factionalized, and that its factions have been at odds across time. The assumption on which the consultation rests is dismantled from inside.

**Moral Agents, Not Forces of Nature**

The Qur'an's treatment of the jinn is consistently moral rather than magical. They are addressed, they are commanded, they are warned, they are gathered, they are judged, and they are punished — all of which requires that they choose.

The purpose of their creation is identical to the purpose of humanity's: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56). Messengers come from among both orders, and the Day is described as the moment both are questioned together: *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?” They will say, “We confess against ourselves!”* (6:130). Their fate is stated with the same finality: *“Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell.”* (7:179), and *“I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together.”* (11:119; 32:13).

Verse eleven is the premise of all of that. A being with factions has choices; a being with choices is accountable. The sūrah establishes the accountability by having the jinn describe their own internal divisions in their own reported words.

**The End of an Exalted Status**

The verse answers a specific human error, and the Qur'an names that error elsewhere: people took jinn as objects of faith. *“Glory be to You! Our loyalty is to You, not them. In fact, they ˹only˺ followed the ˹temptations of evil˺ jinn, in whom most of them had faith.”* (34:41). Others associated them with God in worship, though He created them (6:100).

Both errors require the jinn to be a class apart — either divine associates or bearers of a knowledge denied to humans. This verse removes the ground for both. A community containing the righteous and the less-than-righteous, split into strips, is not a class of powers. It is a community of moral agents with the same problem humanity has: some of its members are good and some are not, and nobody outside can tell which is which from a distance.

That is why the practice described in verse six was so costly. A man who called on the master of a valley for protection was calling on a being whose moral standing he could not determine, in a community whose factions he could not map, using a formula that increased both parties in wrong. The verse does not need to forbid the practice. It only needs to describe the ones being called.

The same structure reappears at the end of the speech, where the two groups named here are given their outcomes: those who submitted have attained right guidance, and the deviant will be fuel for the Fire (72:14–15).

## Verse 72:12

> ˹Now,˺ we truly know that we cannot frustrate Allah on earth, nor can we escape from Him ˹into heaven˺

**muʿjizīn — Rendering God Incapable**

The word translated *frustrate* is built on ʿ-j-z, the root of incapacity and of the miracle — *muʿjiza*, the thing that renders its opponents incapable of answering. To be *muʿjiz* is to outstrip, to defeat, to make the other party unable.

The claim the jinn now disown is that they could be *muʿjizīn Allāh* — that they could defeat God, outwit Him, or place themselves beyond His reach. The Qur'an denies this possibility in a dozen places, and always in the same terms: *“They will never frustrate Allah on earth, and they will have no protector besides Allah.”* (11:20). *“But there is nothing that can escape Allah in the heavens or the earth. He is certainly All-Knowing, Most Capable.”* (35:44). The challenge is put to both orders of creation together: *“O assembly of jinn and humans! If you can penetrate beyond the realms of the heavens and the earth, then do so. ˹But˺ you cannot do that without ˹Our˺ authority.”* (55:33).

The two directions of escape are named in this verse and both are closed. On earth — where a creature might hide, run, or entrench. By flight — the alternative reading of the second clause, escape into the heavens, the direction the jinn used to travel. The beings whose traditional movement was upward now report that upward is not an exit.

**The Collapse of the Refuge Economy**

Verse six described men seeking refuge with individuals among the jinn. This verse supplies the jinn's own assessment of their capacity to provide it.

The logic is devastating in its brevity. A being who cannot frustrate God on earth, and cannot escape Him by flight, is a being who cannot protect a traveller in a valley. The refuge offered was an illusion purchased by both parties, and the price — described in verse six as an increase in *rahaq* for each — was paid in full. The client got no protection; the patron got an inflated estimate of himself that would be corrected only on the Day.

The Qur'an makes the same point about every supposed protector. On the Day of gathering, the human associates of the jinn say they benefited from each other's company and have now reached their appointed term (6:128); the idols deny their worshippers and turn against them (19:82); the leaders disown the followers and the bonds between them are cut (2:166). Every arrangement built on a created power's ability to shield a person from God's reach is described as ending in the same discovery — that the shield was never there.

The jinn's confession here is that discovery made early, in a valley, before the term arrives. That is what makes the verse good news rather than bad. A creature who learns he cannot escape has learned the first thing he needs to know to stop trying.

**Knowledge of Limits as the Ground of Faith**

The verse states a limitation and treats it as knowledge rather than as loss. That treatment is the Qur'an's ordinary one. Creatureliness is defined by limits, and the acceptance of limits is the beginning of religion.

The alternative is the posture of the one who thinks he can prevail. The Qur'an describes it in the man who amasses wealth and counts it, supposing his wealth will make him immortal (104:2–3), and in the one who exceeds bounds because he sees himself self-sufficient (96:6–7). Both errors are forms of the claim this verse disowns: that a creature has a margin within which God cannot reach him.

The correction is stated in the verse that begins the sūrah's second half — the places of worship belong to God, so call on nobody besides Him (72:18) — and it rests on this recognition. Worship is possible only for a being who has accepted that there is nowhere to stand outside the relation. The jinn arrive at that acceptance in verse twelve, and their belief is reported as completed in verse thirteen.

## Verse 72:13

> When we heard the guidance ˹of the Quran˺, we ˹readily˺ believed in it. For whoever believes in their Lord will have no fear of being denied ˹a reward˺ or wronged

**Belief at the Moment of Hearing**

The verse reports a sequence with no interval in it: when we heard the guidance, we believed in it. The particle *fa* attaches the belief directly to the hearing, and the noun for the thing heard is *al-hudā*, the guidance — the Qur'an's own name for itself in dozens of places.

The immediacy is the sūrah's evidence for the nature of the text. These listeners had no preparation, no teacher, no prior exposure, and no relationship to the reciter. They were on an errand connected to a trade the recitation had just made obsolete, and they believed within the hearing.

That is not presented as a story about unusual people. It is presented as a story about a book. The same claim is made in the Qur'an's own voice: the recitation is a guide and a healing for the believers, while for those who disbelieve there is deafness in their ears and blindness in their hearts toward it, as if they were being called from a faraway place (41:44). The difference between the two outcomes is located in the hearer's condition, not in the text's power. The jinn of this verse heard from close up and without interference, and the result was belief inside one prayer.

**A Guarantee Resting on God's Character**

The promise is not a contract negotiated between parties of comparable power. It rests on a property of the one who judges, and the Qur'an states that property without qualification.

*“Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight. And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace.”* (4:40). On the Day, the record is laid open and the wicked see everything listed, small and large, and the verse ends with the same assurance: *“And your Lord will never wrong anyone.”* (18:49). The absence of injustice is asserted at the moment of the fullest exposure, which is where it would matter most if it were false.

That is the ground of the jinn's confidence. They are not hoping for leniency. They are reporting that the one who judges does not do the two things a judge can do wrong — under-pay and over-charge — and that the report was given to them at the moment of their belief. Faith arrives with its terms stated, and the terms include the guarantee.

**A Statement About Whoever Believes**

The final clause generalizes, and the generalization is the verse's gift to its human readers. The subject is not "we" but *whoever believes in his Lord* — a formula that includes every creature in both orders of creation, in every century.

The speakers are jinn reporting to jinn, but the sentence they report is not limited to them. It is the standing promise attached to belief, stated once, in the Qur'an, in the mouth of a delegation that had been believers for less than a day. A Makkan listener hearing this sūrah recited was hearing his own case described by witnesses he had never met, and the description included the guarantee he needed: nobody who believes will be cheated in the reckoning.

That is why the verse sits where it does, at the center of the jinn's speech. Before it, the report describes what they gave up — the stations, the trade, the doctrine of their teachers, the claim to knowledge of the future. After it, the report describes what they became — a community divided into those who submitted and those who deviated. The guarantee in the middle is what makes the exchange rational. They gave up a knowledge they did not have for a promise that cannot be broken.

The Qur'an describes the resulting condition elsewhere with the same vocabulary of settledness: those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of God, and in whose remembrance hearts find comfort (13:28).

## Verse 72:14

> And among us are those who have submitted ˹to Allah˺ and those who are deviant. So ˹as for˺ those who submitted, it is they who have attained Right Guidance

**qāsiṭ — The Word That Looks Like Justice**

The term for the second group is a linguistic trap, and the classical commentators set it out explicitly. The root q-s-ṭ produces *qisṭ*, justice, and *muqsiṭ*, one who acts justly. The same root produces *qāsiṭ*, which means the opposite — one who deviates from what is just.

The explanation given by the grammarians is that the active participle of the verb *qasaṭa* denotes departure from justice, while the participle of *aqsāṭa* denotes arrival at it. Al-Qurṭubī's formulation is the one usually quoted: the *qāsiṭ* is the one who turns away from the truth, and the *muqsiṭ* is the one who turns toward it. Two forms built on one root, describing motion in opposite directions.

The word is used of disbelievers in only two places in the Qur'an — here and in its companion verse fifteen — which makes its appearance deliberate. A reader who confuses it with *muqsiṭ* reverses the meaning of the verse, and the confusion is available to anyone who knows the family of justice-words without knowing their directions. The choice of a term this easily mistaken, in the mouth of a community describing itself, is a small demonstration of why revelation needs to be precise about its vocabulary: the difference between the just and the unjust in Arabic can be one vowel pattern.

**Submission and Belief**

The verse uses *muslimūn* where verse thirteen used *āmannā*, we believed, and the two words are not interchangeable in Qur'anic usage.

The distinction is drawn explicitly for a human community in al-Ḥujurāt: *“˹Some of˺ the nomadic Arabs say, ‘We believe.’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘You have not believed. But say, ‘We have submitted,’ for faith has not yet entered your hearts.’”* (49:14). Submission is the outer act — the acknowledgment, the compliance, the declared position — while faith is the inner condition that may or may not have arrived. The verse goes on to describe the true believers as those who believe in God and His Messenger without doubting and strive with their wealth and their lives (49:15).

Applied here, the jinn's self-description is careful. They report belief (verse thirteen) and then report submission (this verse), and the submission is what has attained guidance. Nothing in the verse claims a completed interior state for anyone. The report is of a position taken and a direction found, which is exactly what a delegation of new believers can honestly testify to about itself. The Qur'an's precision about the two terms is what keeps the testimony from becoming a boast.

**A Community Reporting Its Own Two Outcomes**

The speech that began in verse one ends with the next verse, and its last two sentences are the outcomes of the division. That structure gives the whole report its function: it is a warning delivered by jinn to jinn.

The parallel account in al-Aḥqāf says so directly: *“˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when We sent a group of jinn your way to listen to the Quran. Then, upon hearing it, they said ˹to one another˺, ‘Listen quietly!’ Then when it was over, they returned to their fellow jinn as warners.”* (46:29). They went home as *mundhirūn*, warners — the same office Noah held in the preceding sūrah, and the same office the Prophet ﷺ held in Makkah.

The content of their warning is what these two verses contain: we are divided, and the division has consequences, and the consequences are already visible to us. A warner does not need to describe the punishment in detail. He needs to establish that there are two outcomes and that the hearer is standing at the point of division. That is precisely what the jinn's final sentences do.

## Verse 72:15

> And as for the deviant, they will be fuel for Hell.’”

**ḥaṭab — Firewood**

The word for what the deviant become is *ḥaṭab*, dry firewood, the material a fire consumes. Not inhabitants, not residents, not even the punished in the abstract: fuel.

The metaphor is chosen for what it removes. Fuel has no function in a fire except to be burned. It does not participate, it does not govern, it does not benefit, and it is not consumed for its own sake but to sustain something else. To describe a group as fuel is to say that on the Day their existence serves the fire rather than the fire serving as their place.

The same image is used elsewhere for the objects of worship and their worshippers together, and for the fire's material in general: the Fire whose fuel is people and stones is what the believers are told to guard themselves and their families from (66:6), and the description of the Fire prepared for the disbelievers names the same fuel.

**The Inclusion of Jinn in the Fire**

The verse states a doctrine the Qur'an asserts repeatedly and that its first audience needed stated plainly: jinn are subject to the Fire.

*“I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together.”* (11:119; 32:13). *“Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell. They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with.”* (7:179). The devils who attempt to eavesdrop on the highest assembly have the torment of the Blaze prepared for them (67:5). And the address on the Day is to both orders jointly, with both confessing against themselves (6:130).

The inclusion is required by the moral account given in the previous verses. Beings who are divided into submitted and deviant, who choose, who are warned by their own, and who report that they cannot frustrate God are beings with responsibility. Responsibility without consequence is not responsibility. The verse supplies the consequence, and it supplies it in the jinn's own reported words rather than in a divine pronouncement — which makes it a confession rather than a sentence passed on absentees.

**The Warning Delivered to Their Own Kind**

The delegation's function is stated in the parallel account: they returned to their people as warners (46:29), telling them to respond to the caller of God and believe in him, so that He may forgive their sins and protect them from a painful punishment (46:31). The consequence of refusing that caller is given in the same passage: *“And whoever does not respond to the caller of Allah will have no escape on earth, nor will they have any protectors against Him. It is they who are clearly astray.”* (46:32).

Verse fifteen is the negative half of that warning, and it matches. No escape, no protectors, clearly astray — and fuel. The two accounts of the same night, in two sūrahs, agree on every element: the listening, the belief, the return, the warning, and the outcome for those who refuse.

For the human reader the verse does one further piece of work. It removes the last reason anyone might have had to fear or to court the jinn. They are not powers. They are a divided community of moral agents, some of whom have believed and some of whom will be burned, and neither group can help a human being at all. The refuge formula of verse six is not only forbidden by this verse. It is rendered pointless by it.

## Verse 72:16

> Had the deniers followed the Right Way, We would have certainly granted them abundant rain to drink—

**ghadaqā — Water in Quantity Enough to Drink**

The water is described with *ghadaq*, copious, abundant, flowing in quantity — and the purpose is specified: to drink. Not to look at, not to be promised, but to be consumed.

The specification matters because it fixes the promise at the level of survival. Drinking water is the first requirement of a settled community in a dry region, and the verse names it rather than naming wealth, victory, or honor. The Qur'an's promises of provision consistently begin where need begins. Noah's sermon to his people opened the same way: seek forgiveness and He will send the sky upon you with abundant rain (71:11), using *midrār*, the word for rain that keeps returning. Hūd said the same thing to ʿĀd: seek forgiveness and turn to Him, and He will send rain in abundance and add strength to your strength (11:52).

Three prophets, three communities, one formula: uprightness and repentance produce water. The formula is stated as a general rule for towns in al-Aʿrāf: *“Had the people of those societies been faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, We would have overwhelmed them with blessings from heaven and earth. But they disbelieved, so We seized them for what they used to commit.”* (7:96).

**The Two Sūrahs and One Promise**

This sūrah sits directly after Sūrah Nūḥ, and the two share the promise almost word for word. Noah's people were offered abundant rain for seeking forgiveness (71:11); the deniers here are told that abundant water was available for following the way.

The pairing is one of the strongest structural links in this part of the Qur'an. The first sūrah tells the story of a people who were offered water for nine and a half centuries, refused it, and were drowned by water instead. The second describes a people being offered water now, and warns them in the next verse about what follows a refusal. Read in sequence, the two chapters present the same offer, the same condition, and the same two outcomes, with the flood of the first as the visible precedent for the punishment of the second.

The image also works in the opposite direction. Water in the first sūrah destroyed a town that had been given a millennium to accept it. Water in this sūrah is what the town would have received. The element is the same; the difference lies entirely in which side of the condition a people is standing on.

**Water as Provision and as Revelation**

The Qur'an uses water for both, and the double use is not accidental. Rain revives a dead land (30:19), and the same revival is the standing image for resurrection (50:11): with this rain We revive a lifeless land, and similar is the emergence from the graves. Provision and revelation are described with one vocabulary because both do the same thing — they arrive from above, they are not produced by the recipient, and they produce life where there was none.

That is why the promise of water in this verse is not only agricultural. A people who follow the way are given what they need to drink, and the giving is a test (72:17) rather than a reward. The abundance is a condition to be lived in, not a prize to be collected, and the sūrah's next verse states the alternative for the person who turns away from the remembrance of his Lord — an ascending punishment, in the same terms of overwhelming quantity, arriving from the other direction.

The tradition's association of the verse with the drought years in Makkah fits that reading, and the Qur'an elsewhere describes the same sequence — a community seized by hardship and famine after years of plenty (44:10–11).

## Verse 72:17

> as a test for them. And whoever turns away from the remembrance of their Lord will be admitted by Him into an overwhelming punishment

**The Water Was a Test, Not a Prize**

The purpose clause attaches itself to the promise of the previous verse: We would have given them abundant water *to test them thereby*. The gift is defined by its function, and the function is examination.

That redefinition is the verse's central move. A reader who takes provision as payment for belief has misunderstood both, and the Qur'an corrects the misunderstanding at every opportunity. Wealth and children are a test (8:28); the generosity that makes a person boast and the restriction that makes him protest are both tests (89:15–16); and the reason abundance is not given universally is stated as a mercy: *“Had Allah given abundant provisions to ˹all˺ His servants, they would have certainly transgressed throughout the land. But He sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure.”* (42:27).

The last verse supplies the logic behind this one. Provision is measured because unmeasured provision destroys the recipient. A test that cannot be failed is not a test, and a gift given without condition produces the boast of 89:15 rather than gratitude. The water of verse sixteen would have been given in abundance, and the abundance would have been an examination in how abundance is handled.

**Two Readings of the Same Water**

The commentators have read this verse in two directions, and both are textually available.

The first reads the water as a genuine blessing and the test as a trial of gratitude: God would have given abundance so that the recipients might show whether they would use it well. The trial is real but the gift is good, and failing it is a failure of the recipient rather than a feature of the gift.

The second reads the water as *istidrāj* — the gradual drawing-on of a people by means of what looks like favor. On this reading the abundance would have been the instrument of their further delusion, given so that they would go deeper into the error they had chosen. The Qur'an's statements of that mechanism are explicit: *“As for those who deny Our signs, We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend.”* (7:182), sealed by *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:183); and *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44).

The first reading is stronger for this verse, because the promise is addressed to people who *would have followed the way* — a condition that already implies uprightness, and uprightness is not what istidrāj operates on. But the second reading belongs beside it as the account of what happens when the condition is not met, and the Qur'an's own practice is to keep both meanings of prosperity in view at once: abundance is a gift to the grateful and a trap for the proud, and the recipient cannot always tell which he is holding.

**dhikr — The Remembrance**

The second half of the verse names what the person turns away from: the *dhikr* of his Lord. The word covers a range that the translation compresses — the Qur'an itself, which is called a *dhikr* repeatedly; the act of remembering God; and the message as a reminder delivered by a messenger.

The range is not a problem here, because the three senses converge. To turn away from the reminder is to turn away from the thing that reminds, from the act of being reminded, and from the One who sent the reminder. The Qur'an states the consequence of that turning in the same vocabulary: *“But whoever turns away from My Reminder will certainly have a miserable life, then We will raise them up blind on the Day of Judgment.”* (20:124). The pairing of the two verses is close enough to be a commentary on this one — a miserable life now, and blindness then, for the same act of turning.

The addition of *of their Lord* is significant. The reminder is not an abstract teaching; it belongs to a Lord, and turning away from it is turning away from a relation. That is why the punishment that follows is described as an admission *by Him* — the same Lord who owns the reminder performs the admission.

## Verse 72:18

> The places of worship are ˹only˺ for Allah, so do not invoke anyone besides Him

**Houses Where the Name Is Mentioned**

The Qur'an describes the function of these places in al-Nūr: *“˹That light shines˺ through houses ˹of worship˺ which Allah has ordered to be raised, and where His Name is mentioned. He is glorified there morning and evening”* (24:36). Two marks identify them: they are raised by divine order, and God's name is mentioned in them.

Both marks are exclusive. A house ordered to be raised for the mention of God's name is not available for the mention of another's, and the glorification described in that verse is performed morning and evening — a schedule that leaves no interval in which the place could be lent to another object of worship.

The instruction that follows in this verse is stated in the same terms as the general command of the Qur'an: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘My Lord has commanded uprightness and dedication ˹to Him alone˺ in worship, calling upon Him with sincere devotion.’”* (7:29). Uprightness, dedication, and invocation with sincere devotion are the three requirements for standing in a place that belongs to God, and all three are excluded by the presence of a second name in the prayer.

**Invocation Is Worship**

The command is not to build correctly or to face correctly. It is not to call anyone alongside God, and the verb is *tadʿū*, from the same root as *duʿāʾ*, supplication — the act of asking.

The Qur'an treats supplication as worship rather than as an accompaniment to it. The act of calling on someone for what only God can give is the essence of the relation, and performing it toward another object transfers the relation. This is why the verse's second half is short and absolute: do not call on anyone besides Him. No qualification, no allowance for calling on God primarily and others secondarily, no room for intermediaries in the act of asking.

The futility of the alternative is stated in the same vocabulary elsewhere: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Invoke those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Him—they do not have the power to undo harm from you or transfer it ˹to someone else˺.’”* (17:56). The invocation directed elsewhere does not merely offend; it fails. The beings invoked cannot remove a harm or move it to someone else, which means the worshipper has performed the act of religion and received nothing.

**The Positive Replacement for the Valley Formula**

Verse six described men seeking refuge with individuals among the jinn, and the result was an increase in wrong on both sides. This verse supplies the replacement, and the replacement is not a better formula for the same purpose. It is a different address entirely.

The refuge now has one object. The Qur'an's instruction is uniform: seek refuge with God when tempted (7:200; 41:36), before recitation (16:98), from the whisperer who whispers into the hearts of humankind, from among jinn and humankind (114:4–6). The last of those identifies the party from whom refuge used to be sought as one of the parties from whom refuge must now be sought.

That reversal is the sūrah's practical teaching in its most compact form. The places where a person stands — valleys, roads, houses, towns — belong to God, and the beings who used to be addressed in them are creatures in the same position as the person addressing them. The instruction that follows from this is therefore simple enough for a frightened traveller at night: call on God in the place you are standing, because the place is His.

The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 521a from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh.

## Verse 72:19

> Yet when the servant of Allah stood up calling upon Him ˹alone˺, the pagans almost swarmed over him

**ʿabd Allāh — The Title at the Center of the Scene**

The figure in this verse is named by his relation, not by his name: the servant of God. The Qur'an uses this title for the Prophet ﷺ at the moments of highest significance, and it treats servanthood as an elevation rather than a limitation.

The Book is described as revealed to the servant: *“All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it”* (18:1). The night journey begins with the same title in the same position. Servanthood here is not a status of inferiority but of total belonging — the condition of a person who has no independent claim, no private interest, and no divided loyalty, which is precisely what qualifies him to speak for God.

The verse puts that title in an active scene: he stood up, calling on Him. The standing is the standing of prayer, and the calling is the invocation commanded in the previous verse. What is described is therefore the performance of the command of 72:18 by the one who received it — a man praying to God alone, in public, while others closed in on him.

**Two Readings of the Scene**

The first reading takes the crowd as the jinn. When the servant of God stood to pray, they packed around him to listen — the delegation of verse one arriving in numbers, astonished by the recitation, pressing close to hear it. This reading is supported by the report transmitted with the account of Ibn ʿAbbās in Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3323, where the jinn's words to their people include the statement that when the worshipper stood calling upon Him they became a dense mass around him. The same source notes that this portion rests on Ibn ʿAbbās's understanding of the verse and that al-Bukhārī did not include it in his Ṣaḥīḥ, which is a fair measure of its standing: an early exegetical reading, transmitted, not established as a Prophetic report.

The second reading takes the crowd as the Quraysh, who pressed on the Prophet ﷺ when he prayed at the Kaʿbah, nearly overwhelming him. The context supports it: the verses before describe the jinn, but the verses after are addressed to the deniers of Makkah — say, I call only on my Lord; say, I have no power to harm or benefit you; say, no one can protect me from God. That sequence answers a human pressure, not an admiring crowd.

The stronger reading is the second, on the ground that a sequence of commands beginning with *say* is directed at an objector who has made a demand. But the first is not excluded, and the ambiguity may be intentional: the same act of standing to pray attracted a packed crowd of listeners from another order of creation and a packed crowd of opponents from his own.

**The Offense Was the Prayer**

Nothing in the verse describes preaching, arguing, or performing a sign. The act that produces the crowding is worship — standing and calling on God alone.

That detail is the sūrah's sharpest statement of what the conflict in Makkah was actually about. The Prophet ﷺ was not opposed for claiming political power, for abolishing trade, or for insulting the tribe's honor in the ordinary sense. He was opposed for praying to one God in a sanctuary that housed three hundred and sixty idols. The offense was liturgical before it was social, and the crowd's density is the measure of how intolerable the liturgy was.

The Qur'an records the same offense in the language of those who commit it: *“And when you mention your Lord alone in the Quran, they turn their backs in aversion.”* (17:46). The aversion is triggered by the mention of God alone, not by anything else in the recitation. Verse nineteen shows the aversion at its most physical — a body of people nearly becoming a mass on top of the man who mentioned Him.

## Verse 72:20

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I call only upon my Lord, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺.”

**"I Call Upon My Lord"**

The verb is *adʿū*, I call, I invoke, I pray to — the same verb the previous verse used for what he was doing while standing, and the same verb commanded in verse eighteen in its negative form: do not call on anyone besides Him.

The three verses form a single sequence of act, command, and confession. He stood calling on God alone (verse nineteen). The places of worship belong to God, so call on nobody else (verse eighteen). Say: I call only on my Lord (verse twenty). The confession is the public statement of the practice, and the practice is the fulfillment of the command. Nothing in the sequence is theoretical.

The word *duʿāʾ* in Qur'anic usage is not a peripheral religious act. It is the core of the relation, and the Qur'an states its reciprocity: *“Your Lord has proclaimed, ‘Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled.’”* (40:60). Calling and worship are treated in that verse as the same thing viewed from two ends — the one who is too proud to call is too proud to worship. The Prophet's confession in verse twenty is therefore a statement of his worship, in the most concrete terms available: this is what I do, to this one, and to no other.

**Associating No One**

The second clause denies *shirk* in the first person: I do not associate anyone with Him. The denial is placed on the speaker rather than on the audience, and its position immediately after the confession of calling makes it a qualification of the same act.

The qualification is necessary because the act of calling is exactly where association happens. A person who calls on God and also calls on another has not added a devotional practice to his religion; he has divided the act that constitutes the religion. The verse therefore denies the division at its point of occurrence, and it denies it with the strongest available particle: not one, *aḥad*, the numeral used for an indivisible unit.

The same denial is put in the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ elsewhere in identical terms: *“˹And˺ say, ‘There is only One God. And I totally reject whatever ˹idols˺ you associate with Him.’”* (6:19). There the rejection is explicit and addressed to the audience's practice; here it is a statement about the speaker's own. Both forms appear in the Qur'an because both are needed — the audience must be told what is wrong with their worship, and they must be shown what is right about his.

**No Compromise Under Pressure**

The occasion of the verse is a man nearly overwhelmed by a crowd, and the reply contains no concession of any kind. Not a suggestion that the prayer could be performed privately, not an offer to include the sanctuary's other residents in the invocation, not a postponement until the pressure eases.

That refusal is the sūrah's practical lesson for its first audience, and it is stated rather than argued. The Makkan Muslims of the following years faced the same choice in smaller forms — whether to pray openly near the Kaʿbah, whether to acknowledge a tribal deity in a moment of danger, whether to let a formula of respect pass uncorrected. Verse twenty supplies the answer in a single sentence and places it in the mouth of the man whose position was hardest.

The Qur'an's general instruction runs in the same direction: the messenger's duty is delivery, not management of the audience's reaction. *“The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺.”* (5:99). *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22). A person whose office is delivery does not modify the content to improve reception.

## Verse 72:21

> Say, “It is not in my power to harm or benefit you.”

**Why Harm Is Named First**

The audience pressing on the Prophet ﷺ had a specific fear, and the verse addresses it before it addresses their hope. They believed he was dangerous — that his prayer against their gods would bring harm on them, or that his growing movement threatened their standing, or that the man himself could do them injury.

The denial removes the ground for both persecution and negotiation. If he cannot harm them, there is nothing to defend against and no reason to crush him; and if he cannot benefit them, there is nothing to buy from him and no reason to make terms with him. A man with neither capacity is not a political actor. He is a speaker, and the next verses describe exactly what his speech consists of.

The Qur'an puts the same denial in the mouths of the objects of worship themselves. The invoked cannot undo a harm or transfer it (17:56); on the Day they deny their worshippers and turn against them (19:82). If the gods of the sanctuary have no power to harm or benefit, and the man praying in the sanctuary has none either, then the entire economy of religious fear in Makkah is left with one address — and verse twenty has already named it.

**The Messenger as a Human Being**

The disclaimer belongs to a consistent pattern in the Qur'an's presentation of its messengers, and the pattern is enforced against every tendency to elevate them.

Messengers are described as men who ate food and walked in the markets (25:20), as men inspired like other men (16:43; 21:7), as men with wives and children and no sign except by God's permission (13:38). The instruction to say *I have no power* appears in the same vocabulary for the Prophet ﷺ in Yūnus: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah.’”* (10:49). There the denial is directed inward — he cannot benefit or protect even himself — which is the strongest form of the claim and the ground of the weaker form addressed to others here.

The purpose of the pattern is protective in both directions. It protects the message from being confused with the man, so that a rejection of the messenger's person is not mistaken for a refutation of his content. And it protects the man from being made into an object of worship, which is the error the Qur'an identifies as the ruin of earlier communities: no human given the Book and wisdom would tell people to worship him instead of God (3:79).

**The Accusation This Answers**

The charge against the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah is recorded in the Qur'an with precision, and it is the charge this verse removes. The disbelieving chiefs tell the masses: this is only a human like you who wants to be superior to you, and had God willed He would have sent angels (23:24). The same accusation appears in Noah's account — he wants to set himself above you (23:24), and how can we believe in you when only the lowest follow you (26:111).

The accusation assumes a motive: ambition. The answer does not dispute the motive by defending the man's character. It disputes the premise by removing the object of the ambition. What would superiority consist of, in a person who cannot harm or benefit anyone? Dominion over people is exercised through exactly those two capacities, and both are denied in one sentence.

That is why the verse is short. A long defense of the Prophet's intentions would concede that the question is about his intentions. The denial of power makes the question unanswerable in the form it was asked, and it does so in fifteen Arabic words.

## Verse 72:22

> Say, “No one can protect me from Allah ˹if I were to disobey Him˺, nor can I find any refuge other than Him

**multaḥadā — A Place to Lean Toward**

The second noun is rarer still. *Multaḥad* is a noun of place from a root meaning to incline, to lean, to turn aside toward — the same root that gives the word for a single indivisible unit and for the one who disputes. A *multaḥad* is the place a person turns toward when he is pressed, the direction he leans in when he cannot stand upright on his own.

The denial is therefore not only of an asylum but of a direction. I will find no place to lean toward other than Him. Every other inclination is closed. The image complements the first: asylum is a wall you stand behind, and a *multaḥad* is a slope you turn toward. Neither exists apart from God.

The Qur'an uses the same idea in a verse about the Day: no one will have any refuge or protection, and the only turning available is toward the Lord Himself. The theological content is identical and it is stated here in the mouth of the messenger, which is what makes it applicable to the audience. If the best-positioned human being has no refuge other than God, then no refuge other than God exists, and the audience's searches for one — in idols, in jinn, in tribal alliances, in wealth — are searches in the wrong direction.

**The Protection He Does Have**

The verse denies protection *from God* and does not deny protection at all. The distinction is drawn elsewhere in the Qur'an with equal clarity: *“O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message. Allah will ˹certainly˺ protect you from the people.”* (5:67).

Two protections and two sources of danger. Against people, God protects him — which is why the crowd of verse nineteen did not swallow him and why the plots of the Quraysh failed. Against God, nobody protects anybody, and the messenger says so about himself. The pair of verses together give the complete position: entirely safe from the created, entirely exposed to the Creator.

That arrangement explains a great deal about prophetic behavior in the Qur'an. A messenger who fears no human being can say anything to anyone, which is what makes the public preaching possible. A messenger who fears God absolutely cannot say anything he has not been given, which is what makes the preaching reliable. The two fears, correctly distributed, produce the office described in the next verse: conveyance from God and His messages, nothing more.

## Verse 72:23

> ˹My duty is˺ only to convey ˹the truth˺ from Allah and ˹deliver˺ His messages.” And whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger will certainly be in the Fire of Hell, to stay there for ever and ever

**balāgh — The Whole Office in One Noun**

After three denials — no association, no power, no refuge — the verse states what remains, and what remains is a single noun: *balāgh*, conveyance, the act of making something arrive. I have nothing except conveyance from God and His messages.

The noun is the standard description of the prophetic office throughout the Qur'an, and it is used in contexts where the audience has refused to listen. *“The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺.”* (5:99). *“But if they turn away, then your duty ˹O Prophet˺ is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly.”* (16:82). *“Obey Allah and obey the Messenger. But if you turn away, then he is only responsible for his duty and you are responsible for yours.”* (24:54). In every case the delivery is defined as the limit of the responsibility, and the limit is stated to the messenger's face at moments of maximum pressure.

The definition is precise about what conveyance includes. It includes making the message arrive intact, in a form the recipient can understand and cannot claim to have missed. It excludes producing the response. The three previous verses removed every capacity that could produce a response by force or by benefit, and this verse names what is left: a person who says what he was given and stops.

**The Grammar of the Exception**

The particle *illā* in this verse has been read two ways, and the two readings produce different sentences.

On the first reading it is a true exception attached to verse twenty-one: I have no power over harm or guidance for you *except* conveyance from God — that is, the only thing I can do to you is deliver a message. On the second it is the contraction of *in lā*, "if not," attached to verse twenty-two: no one will protect me from God and I will find no refuge apart from Him *if I do not* convey from God and His messages.

The second reading is grammatically clean and it makes the two halves of the confession interlock: the messenger's exposure to God is exactly proportional to his fidelity in delivery. If he fails to convey, he has no asylum and no refuge — the condition of verse twenty-two becomes actual. The first reading is also sound and it completes the sequence of denials by naming the single exception.

Both readings agree on the substance and neither can be ruled out from the text. The traditional preference runs toward the exception reading because it follows the immediate sequence — no power over you, except conveyance — and because the conditional reading requires supplying a clause the verse does not state. What matters for the sūrah is that either way, conveyance is the messenger's only function and his only safety.

**Disobedience to God and to His Messenger as One Act**

The second half of the verse leaves the quotation. The Prophet ﷺ has finished speaking, and the sentence that follows is God's own: whoever disobeys God and His Messenger will be in the Fire of Hell, remaining in it forever.

The coupling is the theological point. Disobedience is described as one act with two objects, not as two separate offenses — the way the verse elsewhere states the positive case: *“Whoever obeys the Messenger has truly obeyed Allah.”* (4:80). Obedience to the messenger is obedience to God because the messenger has no content of his own; he conveys. Refusing the conveyor is refusing the sender, and the Qur'an does not permit the distinction the refuser tries to draw.

The coupling also answers a specific Makkan position. Some of the audience objected to the man rather than to the message — he is only a human being like you (23:24), only a man possessed, a poet, a soothsayer. The verse closes that route. An objection to the messenger's person is registered as disobedience to God, because the person is the only channel the message has.

## Verse 72:24

> Only when they see what they have been threatened with will they know who is weaker in helpers and inferior in manpower

**Knowledge Postponed to the Moment of Sight**

The verse states a single fact about time and knowledge: they will know, and they will know only when they see what they were promised. The verb for knowing is in the future, and the condition attached to it is seeing.

That arrangement is the Qur'an's account of every warning it issues. A warning is knowledge offered in advance, and it can be refused — a person can hear it and treat it as rhetoric. The verse describes the refusal's end: the same content arrives again, but now as sight rather than as speech, and the knowing that was declined becomes unavoidable.

The same formulation appears in Maryam with the same structure: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Whoever is ˹entrenched˺ in misguidance, the Most Compassionate will allow them plenty of time, until—behold!—they face what they are threatened with: either the torment or the Hour. Only then will they realize who is worse in position and inferior in manpower.’”* (19:75). The parallel is close enough to be a commentary: the delay is granted, the delay ends, and the comparison the deniers thought they had won is decided in public.

**Two Measures: Helpers and Numbers**

The comparison is stated in two terms — *nāṣir*, helper, and *ʿadad*, number — and both are the measures by which the Quraysh assessed themselves.

Helpers are the allies who act: the tribe, the patron, the clients, the gods invoked in a crisis. Numbers are the countable resources: men, sons, wealth, bodies available for a fight. The two together constitute the whole of a tribal society's power, and the elite of Noah's people were described in the previous sūrah by exactly these resources — those whose wealth and children increased them only in loss (71:21).

The verse announces that both measures will be read out again on a day when neither can be inflated. The helpers will be absent: no one will protect anyone (2:166 describes the leaders disowning the followers and the bonds being cut). The numbers will be irrelevant: they will come individually, as they were first created. The boast of manpower in verse nineteen's crowd — a mass nearly packing over one man at prayer — is the specific thing being answered here.

**The Day as the Day of Decision**

The moment of sight is named elsewhere with a title that fits this verse exactly: the Day of Decision, the day that separates. *“˹They will be told,˺ ‘This is the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision which you used to deny.’”* (37:21); *“Surely the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is the time appointed for all—”* (44:40); *“Indeed, the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is an appointed time—”* (78:17).

A day of decision is a day on which questions that were arguable stop being arguable. The specific question this verse defers is about relative strength, and the Day's function is to answer it in a form nobody can dispute. The Qur'an makes the same point with the deniers' own words on that Day: *“He would have almost tricked us away from our gods, had we not been so devoted to them.” Soon they will know, when they face the punishment, who is far astray from the ˹Right˺ Way.* (25:42). The knowing arrives with the facing.

That is the epistemology of the verse in one line: there are facts that cannot be known by argument in this world, not because they are unclear but because the parties have an interest in not knowing them. Such facts are not debated to a conclusion. They are shown.

## Verse 72:25

> Say, “I do not know if what you are promised is near or my Lord has set a distant time for it

**The Question They Would Not Stop Asking**

The verse answers a question, and the question is one the Qur'an reports with remarkable frequency: when will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true? The formulation appears in Yūnus (10:48), in the Prophets (21:38), in Sabaʾ (34:29), in Yā Sīn (36:48), and in the Dominion (67:25), always in the mouths of the deniers and always with the same conditional challenge attached.

The question is not a request for information. It is a form of mockery with a logical shape: if the threat is real, it should be schedulable; if it cannot be scheduled, it is not real. The asker treats the absence of a date as evidence against the thing dated, and he uses the demand for a date to postpone having to respond to the warning.

The Qur'an's answer to that move is not to supply a date. It is to deny that the date belongs to the messenger's knowledge at all, and this verse is the denial in its plainest form — commanded, in the first person, to be said out loud to the people asking.

**Two Possibilities, Neither Excluded**

The alternatives named are nearness and a distant term, and both are held open. The verse does not lean toward either.

The openness has a practical function for the audience. If the threat were known to be distant, the warning would lose its force — a person with a long runway postpones everything. If it were known to be near, the warning would produce panic rather than reform, and the reform would be arithmetic rather than conviction. Holding both possibilities open leaves the listener in the only condition in which a warning can do its work: he must act as though the time may be short, without knowing that it is.

The same structure appears in the preceding sūrah, where Noah tells his people that God will postpone them to a term appointed, and that when God's term comes it will not be delayed (71:4). There the term is fixed and named as fixed; here the distance to it is withheld. Both verses place the listener inside a deadline whose length he cannot compute, and both make the same demand in that condition.

The Qur'an states the general rule about timing elsewhere: every destined matter has a set time (13:38), and no community's term can be advanced or delayed by an hour (7:34). The term exists; its position is not disclosed.

**A Confession That Confirms Everything Else**

Read with the verses around it, this confession completes the sūrah's account of knowledge. The jinn do not know whether evil or good is intended for the earth (72:10). The One who knows is named in the verse that follows: the Knower of the unseen, who discloses none of it to anyone (72:26).

Three statements, three positions, and the arrangement is exhaustive. Created beings in both orders — jinn and human, including the best of the humans — are outside the domain of the unseen. The domain has one owner. The only channel out of it is the one described in the next two verses: a messenger He approves, guarded before and behind.

That channel is the reason the sūrah exists. The recitation the jinn heard, and the confessions the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to make, are both instances of disclosure through the approved channel — and the confession of ignorance is as much a disclosure as the message it accompanies. A man who says what he was given, and says when he was given nothing, is the kind of messenger verse twenty-six describes.

The same confession appears in the Prophets with the same structure: *“If they turn away, then say, ‘I have warned you all equally. I do not know if what you are threatened with is near or far.’”* (21:109).

## Verse 72:26

> ˹He is the˺ Knower of the unseen, disclosing none of it to anyone

**ʿālim al-ghayb — The Title That Owns the Domain**

The verse names God by a title and then states the exclusive right that goes with it. *ʿĀlim al-ghayb*, the Knower of the unseen — the one whose knowledge covers everything that lies outside the reach of created perception.

The title appears throughout the Qur'an in paired and unpaired forms. God is the Knower of the unseen and the seen (59:22), the One to whom belong the keys of the unseen, which none knows except Him (6:59), the One who says: *“None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah. Nor do they know when they will be resurrected.”* (27:65). This verse and 34:3 are the two places where the title stands alone — the Knower of the unseen, without the visible added — and in both it is attached to an oath or an assertion about the coming of what has been promised.

The unseen, *al-ghayb*, is not a mystical residue. It is a defined category: everything a creature cannot reach by perception or by inference. The Qur'an itemizes it: the Hour, the sending of rain, what is in the wombs, what a soul will earn tomorrow, and the land in which it will die (31:34). Five items, none of them accessible, and the list is introduced by the statement that the knowledge of them belongs to God alone.

**lā yuẓhiru — Nobody Is Made to Prevail Over It**

The verb describing what God does not do is built on ẓ-h-r with the preposition *ʿalā*, and the construction means to make someone appear upon a thing, to give him mastery over it, to let him rise above it and take from it. The denial is absolute: He does not make anyone prevail over His unseen — *aḥad*, anyone at all, the numeral of the indivisible unit.

The image in the verb is spatial. The unseen is described as a height, and knowledge of it as an ascent. To prevail over it is to climb to it and take something down. That is exactly what the jinn of this sūrah used to attempt: they took up stations for listening (72:9), they reached out toward the heaven (72:8), and they found the ascent guarded.

The verse therefore states the rule that the narrative of verses eight and ten illustrated. The closure of the heavens was not a temporary measure taken for one revelation. It is the ordinary condition of the domain: nobody ascends to it, and nobody takes from it, and the enforcement described elsewhere is permanent — the one who eavesdrops is pursued by a piercing flare (15:18; 37:10).

**The Exception That Proves the Rule**

The verse does not end with a flat denial. It ends with a suspension that the next verse completes: no one prevails over the unseen *except a messenger He approves*.

That structure is what distinguishes revelation from every other claimed access. The domain is closed, and the only opening in it is one God makes, in a person He selects, for a content He determines. Nothing about the opening is available on application, by technique, by birth, by purity of practice, or by the assistance of another creature.

Every soothsayer's claim rested on access to the unseen; the Qur'an describes the mechanism of that claim — a stolen word from the heavens mixed with a hundred lies (26:221–223) — and this verse removes the mechanism's premise.

The same exclusion is stated to the Prophet ﷺ himself as an instruction: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I do not say to you that I possess Allah’s treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel. I only follow what is revealed to me.’”* (6:50).

The verse also diagnoses the human condition inside that market: *“No! Their knowledge of the Hereafter amounts to ignorance. In fact, they are in doubt about it. In truth, they are ˹totally˺ blind to it.”* (27:66).

## Verse 72:27

> except messengers of His choice. Then He appoints angel-guards before and behind them

**What a Messenger Is Given**

Disclosure to an approved messenger takes a specific form, and the Qur'an describes it from the messenger's side with equal precision. The Prophet ﷺ is told to say that he does not have the treasuries of God or know the unseen, and that he follows only what is revealed to him (6:50), and again that if he knew the unseen he would have benefited himself enormously and no harm would have touched him (7:188).

The two statements are consistent and together they define the gift. A messenger receives content — commands, news of the past, descriptions of the Day, warnings about what is coming — and he does not receive a faculty. He cannot consult the unseen when he chooses; he cannot extend the disclosure to matters he was not given; and he cannot use it to advantage himself.

That is why the confession of verse twenty-five — I do not know whether what you are promised is near or far — is part of the message rather than a gap in it. The timing was not among the things disclosed. A messenger who reported knowing it would be reporting something he had not been given, which is the definition of the trade the verse has just excluded.

**Guards Before and Behind**

The measure attached to the disclosure is a guard, and the guard is positioned in two directions: *min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihī*, from between his hands and from behind him — before him and behind him, in front and at the back, covering the whole of the route.

The noun used is *raṣad*, the same word that described the flare lying in wait for the eavesdropper in verse nine. There the ambush was set against those trying to take information down; here it is set around the one bringing it. The identical term in both verses is a deliberate echo: the heavens are guarded in both directions, against theft and for delivery.

The same arrangement is described for human beings generally in al-Raʿd: *“For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah’s command.”* (13:11). The phrase *from before him and from behind him* recurs, and the agents are named there as angels in succession. The guard in this verse is of the same order and serves a higher purpose — not the protection of a life but the protection of a message in transit.

The tradition adds a number, with some reports specifying four angels as the guard; the verse itself does not count them, and the count adds nothing to its claim. What the verse requires is that the disclosure be escorted, and that the escort cover every direction from which it could be interfered with.

**The Integrity of Revelation**

The purpose of the guard is stated in the next verse, and it explains why the measure is necessary. Revelation is a communication crossing a domain that other beings are actively trying to enter, and a communication that can be intercepted can be corrupted.

The Qur'an treats its own preservation as a divine act rather than a human achievement. It is a reminder whose guarding is undertaken by the One who sent it; it is described as a book with no crookedness in it (18:1); and the instruction to the Prophet ﷺ includes not rushing the recitation before it is conveyed to him in full (20:114). The escort described in this verse is the same undertaking in narrative form — the message arrives as sent, or it does not arrive.

That guarantee is what makes the messenger's denial of power coherent. A man who can neither harm nor benefit his audience (72:21), who has no refuge except God (72:22), and who does not know the date of the promised thing (72:25) has nothing to offer except what he carries. The verse establishes that what he carries is intact — selected by God, disclosed by God, and escorted by God — so that the only remaining question for the audience is whether to accept it.

The inspired utterance of a poet or a diviner has no guard; it has a descender of a different kind, and the Qur'an names them as devils who descend on every sinful liar (26:222).

## Verse 72:28

> to ensure that the messengers fully deliver the messages of their Lord—though He ˹already˺ knows all about them, and keeps account of everything.”

**The Purpose Clause, and the Question of Who Knows**

The verse states why the guards are appointed, and the grammar of the purpose clause has been read in several ways. The phrase *li-yaʿlama* can be taken as "that He may know," with God as the subject, or as "that he may know," with the messenger as the subject, or as a form meaning "that He may inform.".

Each reading produces a coherent sentence. On the first, the knowing is God's knowledge of the actualized event — not the acquisition of information He lacked, but the bringing about, in the world, of what He already knows, so that the delivery is a fact and not merely a decree. On the second, the messenger knows that the messages of his Lord have reached him intact, which is the assurance a receiver under escort needs. On the third, the appointment of guards informs people that the messengers have conveyed what they were given.

The readings converge on one point, which is what the verse is about: the delivery is verified. Something is established — for God in the order of events, for the messenger in the order of knowledge, or for the audience in the order of evidence — and the thing established is that the messages of their Lord were conveyed in full. The clause is a warranty on the channel described in the previous verse, and the traditional preference for the messenger as the subject rests on the immediate context: the escort surrounds him, so the knowledge it produces is most naturally his.

**The Object of the Guard Is the Message, Not the Man**

The clause specifies what is being secured, and it is plural: *risālāt rabbihim*, the messages of their Lord. The protection is over the content in transit.

That specification has a consequence for the messenger's own status. He is guarded because he carries, not because he is important in himself, and the sūrah has spent its second half removing every claim to personal importance he might have been thought to hold — no power to harm or benefit (72:21), no refuge (72:22), no knowledge of the timing (72:25), no association in worship (72:20). What remains is conveyance (72:23), and the guard exists for the conveyance.

The same distribution appears in the messenger's own instruction: God will protect you from the people (5:67). The protection promised there is of a man whose function requires that he survive long enough to complete it. Neither verse promises the messenger anything for himself; both secure the delivery, and the man is secured inside it.

**He Encompasses What Is With Them**

The verse then turns from the delivery to the deliverers, and the statement is comprehensive: He encompasses what is with them. The verb *aḥāṭa* denotes a surrounding that leaves nothing outside — the same verb used of God's knowledge enclosing all things, and of a punishment enclosing a people.

What is "with them" is everything a messenger holds: the messages he has received, the knowledge he has been given, the state of his heart, the words he has spoken, and the parts of the disclosure he has not been given. The encompassing is total and it applies to the approved channel as much as to anyone else.

That is the verse's final safeguard against the corruption of religion. A messenger is not a semi-divine being with private access and unexamined authority. He is a creature inside a complete knowledge, carrying a specified content, escorted, and accounted for. The tradition's distinction between the evidentiary miracle of a prophet and the lesser grace granted to a righteous person belongs here too: both are disclosures, neither is a possession, and neither places its recipient outside the encompassing.
