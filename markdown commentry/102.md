# Sūrah at-Takāthur (Chapter 102) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rivalry of Accumulation and the Visit to the Graves**

At-Takāthur, "Competition in Worldly Increase" or "The Rivalry of Piling Up," is the one-hundred-and-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 8 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with an unsparing, piercing psychological diagnosis of human consumerism: *“Competition in [worldly] increase diverts you (*Alhākumu al-takāthur*), until you visit the graveyards!”* (102:1–2). In prophetic tradition, Sūrah at-Takāthur is celebrated for its profound philosophical diagnosis: ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Shikhkhīr reported in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2958) that he visited the Prophet ﷺ while he was reciting *Alhākumu al-takāthur*, whereupon the Messenger of Allah remarked: *“The son of Adam says: 'My wealth! My wealth!' Yet do you possess, O son of Adam, of your wealth anything except what you ate and consumed, or what you wore and wore out, or what you gave in charity and preserved for eternity?”* In eight masterly verses, Sūrah at-Takāthur exposes the tragic addiction of human materialism: racing to accumulate wealth, followers, titles, and property until the cemetery dirt is shoveled over our corpses, warning of the three tiers of certainty, and concluding with the ultimate accounting: *“Then you will surely be asked that Day about every pleasure / blessing (*al-naʿīm*)!”*

**The Graveyard Audits of the Arabian Clans**

The historical occasion of at-Takāthur is preserved by Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah regarding a bitter rivalry between two prominent Qurayshi or Anṣārī clans (the Banū ʿAbd Manāf and Banū Sahm). Consumed by aristocratic pride, they competed to prove which clan was larger and more noble. When the living members of one clan were counted and proved slightly fewer, their chieftains cried: *“Come, let us count our dead in the cemetery!”* They marched to the graveyards, pointing to tombs and counting: *“Here is the grave of so-and-so! Here is the grave of so-and-so!”*—counting rotting corpses beneath the dust to win an argument of vanity! Verses 1–2 descended in devastating divine ridicule: *“Competition in accumulation distracts you, until you visit the graves!”*

**The Structural Modulation and the Three Stages of Certainty**

The architecture of Sūrah at-Takāthur moves across three distinct movements:
1. The Distraction of Accumulation and the Cemetery Census (1–2): Condemns the perpetual rat-race of piling up wealth, possessions, and status, which distracts the soul until death interrupts the competition.
2. The Three Tiers of Certainty (3–7): Delivers the repeated divine warning: *“No! You are going to know! Then no! You are going to know!”* It introduces the three classical philosophical stages of epistemic certainty:
   - *ʿIlm al-Yaqīn* (The Knowledge of Certainty, 5): The intellectual certainty derived from reason and revelation. If humanity possessed this certainty today, it would never be distracted by greed.
   - *ʿAyn al-Yaqīn* (The Eye of Certainty, 7): The visual certainty when the veil is torn and humanity sees the blazing Hellfire with physical eyesight: *“You will surely see the Hellfire! Then you will surely see it with the eye of certainty!”*
   - *Ḥaqq al-Yaqīn* (The Absolute Truth of Certainty, found in al-Wāqiʿah and al-Ḥāqqah): The direct experiential reality.
3. The Audit of Every Pleasure: Al-Naʿīm (8): Concludes with the solemn, universal interrogation: *“Then you will surely be asked that Day about every pleasure (*al-naʿīm*)!”*

**The Audit of Every Sip of Water: Al-Naʿīm**

The supreme spiritual warning of Sūrah at-Takāthur is contained in its final word: *Al-Naʿīm* (The Blessings / Pleasures). In an authentic hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2038), the Prophet ﷺ walked out with Abū Bakr and ʿUmar when all three were trembling with hunger. A hospitable Helper hosted them, giving them fresh water, ripe dates, and roasting a sheep for them. After eating and drinking to satisfaction, the Prophet looked at them with tears in his eyes and said: *“By Him in whose hand is my soul, this is of the blessings (*al-naʿīm*) about which you will be questioned on the Day of Resurrection: cool shade, fresh dates, and cold water!”* Sūrah at-Takāthur warns humanity that every breath, every heartbeat, every sip of clean water, and every dollar earned is an item on a cosmic audit sheet, calling the soul to escape the mindless rat-race of accumulation and prepare for the ultimate accounting.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:1

> Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Diversion Named as a Contest**

*Alhākum al-takāthur* — mutual increase has diverted you. *Alhā* is to occupy, distract, make unmindful. The same root gives *lahw*, play that eats the hour. *Takāthur* is from *kathrah*, abundance, with a form that means rivaling one another in who has more. The translation's “competition for more gains” and “from Allah” are tafsīr in brackets. The Arabic does not name Allah in this line; it names the diversion and the contest. What they are diverted from is supplied by the whole Book: from remembrance, from the grave's lesson, from the questioning of verse 8. The sūrah is Makkan in feel, a short slap at a culture of counting.


*Kum* is plural. The address is a group that recognizes itself: households, clans, a city that measures honour in sons, camels, gardens, and later, in any age, in numbers on a screen. The verse does not say “some of you.” It says you have been diverted. Listeners who are not in the contest must prove it by their hours, not by their taste in sermons. *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers”* (63:9). Same diversion, later with wealth and children named.


A lone miser is one problem. *Takāthur* is a social technology. People do not only want enough. They want more than the brother, the cousin, the rival house. Pre-Islamic boasting of numbers is the first scene: we are more. The scene has not died. Nations do it, scholars can do it with students, worshippers with rakʿahs done for show. The form *tafāʿul* is reciprocal. The sūrah aims at the relation, not only at the pile. Destroy the relation of rivalry and a person might still earn; he will not be eaten by the earning.


*Alhā* is past. The contest has already succeeded in occupying them. The sūrah speaks to people mid-absorption, not to people considering a future risk. That is why verses 3–4 will shout “no” and “you will know.” You cannot wait to be less busy. The busyness is the disease underway. Death in verse 2 is not a distant moral. It is the only stop the contest respects, and even that stop they treat as a pause in a family graveyard visit, which some reports of occasion mention: two clans counting their living and then their dead to see who had more.

**Gains Are Not Provision**

Provision is *rizq*. *Takāthur* is the conversion of provision into a score. The Book never attacks *rizq*. It attacks the score. A believer may be wealthy like ʿUthmān and not be in this verse if the wealth does not occupy the heart's hours. A poor man may be in this verse if his hours are a contest in bitterness about numbers. The diverting thing is the competition, not the object counted. That is why the verse can still land in an age that counts followers instead of camels.

**The Arabic Still Has Work After the First Pass**

The line of 102:1 is short in the muṣḥaf and long in the mouth of a teacher who will not stop at a gloss. at-Takāthur places this verse inside a sūrah whose name is already an argument, and the local theme — rivalry in increase — does not exhaust the grammar. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. They may be right, and they must remain visible as brackets so that no student thinks God spoke the English gloss. Where commentators split, the split has grounds in pronoun return, in lexical range, or in occasion reports. Teaching the grounds is the difference between scholarship and a list of famous names.

**Conduct Follows if the Verse Is Believed**

Belief in 102:1 is not a nod during tarāwīḥ. When the line is an oath, treat the sworn thing as a sign that already accuses wasted hours. A question in God's mouth means stop pretending folklore has the answer. A threat means leave the type while tawbah is open. An exception-clause means become the verbs, not wear a group label. at-Takāthur is recited in two rakʿahs by people who then return to counting, mocking, or sleeping through ʿaṣr. That return is the opposite of tafsīr. Ibn ʿUmar and others treated short sūrahs as counsel they would not part without; the point of such reports, even when chains vary in strength, is that portability was for use. Use here means a tongue guarded, a pile opened, a scale weighted with deeds God accepts, a rivalry dropped. The verse does not need a new miracle to be heavy. It needs a day that looks different after it.

**A Checked Parallel, Not a Stolen Verse**

*“Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah”* (63:9) belongs to another place in the Book, and it is brought here only as a witness, not as a second assignment for this commentary. Alhā in 102:1 is that diversion without yet naming wealth and children; the contest is the mechanism. Cross-reference is a servant of the present line. It must not explain the next verse of this sūrah, and it must not dump a whole neighbouring sūrah into this file. The discipline is: one extra checked fact, tagged, then back to 102:1. Occasions of revelation, when reported, are historical helps. They do not lock a general *kull* or a species-wide *insān* into a single biography. Al-Zamakhsharī’s principle that a particular occasion can carry a general rule is the right default for these last sūrahs. Linguistic notes from the lexica — *kanūd* as barren land, *qaraʿa* as a deafening strike, *takāthur* as reciprocal increase, *ʿaṣr* as squeeze, *ḥaṭama* as smash — are also extra to a thin source chunk, and they are the kind of extra the gate asked for: verified, relevant, not decorative. The present verse remains the only defendant. The parallel is a lamp, not a second courtroom.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:2

> until you end up in ˹your˺ graves

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Limit the Contest Accepts**

*Ḥattā zurtum al-maqābir* — until you visit the graves. *Zāra* is to visit. The translation “end up in your graves” takes the visit as the visit from which one does not return: burial. That is the majority reading: you continue in *takāthur* until death puts you in the cemetery. A minority, tied to reports of clans counting ancestors among the graves, takes *zurtum* as literal visits to graveyards to add the dead to the tally. Both readings can be taught. The first is the moral hammer. The second is a historical sting. The word *visit* is bitter either way. A visit is short. If burial is a visit, the stay in the grave is not the last stay; a leaving for resurrection is implied. If the visit is a boast-tour, the verse mocks the tour as the end of a wasted life.

**Until — The Contest Has No Internal Stop**

*Ḥattā* marks an endpoint that comes from outside the game. The players do not choose a last number. They stop when they are stopped. That is the definition of a diversion that has become lord. A lawful seeking of provision has stops: prayer, sleep, the right of a neighbour. *Takāthur* eats until the grave. *“Spend from what We have provided for you before death comes to one of you”* (63:10) is the alternative *ḥattā*. This verse is the failed alternative: no spending, only counting, until the soil.

**Graves as the First Classroom They Enter Too Late**

The grave in Islamic teaching is the first station of the last. The Prophet described the grave as a garden from the gardens of Paradise or a pit from the pits of Fire in well-known reports. This sūrah does not detail the questioning of Munkar and Nakīr. It only names *maqābir* as the until. The brevity is enough. People who will not sit in a lesson will sit in a grave. The visit they postponed — visiting graves to remember, which the Prophet allowed as a reminder of death — becomes the visit they cannot refuse.

**You Plural Continues**

The same *kum* is inside *zurtum*. Families that competed together are buried apart, each in a narrow place, or together in a plot that no longer cares who had more. The social technology of rivalry fails at the cemetery even if two clans try to count tombstones. Tombstones do not answer. *“Every soul will taste death”* (21:35) is the law under the until.

**Do Not Make the Verse a Slogan Against Work**

Ending in graves is the human condition for the worker and the idler. The verse is not “do not act, you will die.” It is “your contest will not pause until death, and that is your indictment.” Work that remembers the grave is not *takāthur*. Work that cannot remember the grave is. The test is whether the thought of *maqābir* can enter the day without being laughed out as morbidity. This sūrah insists it enter. Verse 3 will then deny their present certainty with a *kallā*.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:3

> But no! You will soon come to know

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā Is a Hand on the Mouth**

*Kallā sawfa taʿlamūn* — no indeed; you will come to know. *Kallā* is a particle of rebuke and negation. It rejects the life of verses 1–2 as a viable plan. Not “perhaps you should reconsider.” No. The contest is answered with a refusal. *Sawfa* delays the knowing a little, but not into never. It is the near future of death, grave, resurrection, or all three as they arrive. The verb *taʿlamūn* is plural, matching the diverted you. What they will know is left unstated in this verse. The not-stating is itself a threat. The object is too large to put in this short line; verses 5–8 will supply Hell and questioning.


People in *takāthur* feel they have time because numbers are still moving. *Sawfa* says the knowledge is on its way anyway. In prophetic idiom, death is near; the Prophet said he and the Hour were sent like two fingers, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6505 and parallel places. This verse is that nearness as grammar. “Soon” is not a date. It is a refusal of their calendar.


*Alhā* was unmindfulness. *Taʿlamūn* is knowledge. The sūrah's medicine is not a new pile but a knowing that ends the game. That knowing cannot be downloaded as information while the contest still owns the hours. It arrives as event. This is why warning often fails in prosperous rooms: the room wants a briefing, not an event. *Kallā* refuses the briefing model. You will know, not because you booked a class, but because the thing will be on you.


Verse 4 will repeat this line with *thumma*. This verse is the first blow. Repetition will be treated there. Here the job is to hear *kallā* without rushing to the twin. One “no” is already enough to indict. Reciters who treat 3–4 as filler before the “good part” about Hell have not felt the particle. The good part is the *kallā*. Hell is only the content of the knowing.

**You Will Know — Not We Speculate**

The verb is indicative future, not hope. God states their coming knowledge as fact. That fact is part of the unseen we accept. It also binds the speaker of the Qur'an to a claim that history, for each addressee, will verify personally. No collective report can substitute for that personal knowing. Verse 3 therefore individualizes the plural: each of the *kum* will know. The rivalry that bound them will not bind their knowings into one. Each grave, each questioning, each sight of the Fire is a knowledge.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:4

> Again, no! You will soon come to know

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thumma Raises the Same Sentence**

*Thumma kallā sawfa taʿlamūn* — then, no indeed, you will come to know. *Thumma* is not mere “and.” It adds a step, a later, a worse, or a confirmation after a pause. Commentators split: two times of knowing (grave then resurrection; death then last day; this world's last hours then the next); or one knowing said twice for *taʾkīd*. Both have grounds. The grave is a knowing; the rising is a greater knowing. Repeating the whole clause, not just the verb, is a rhetorical raising, like repeating a warning in a child's ear, except the child here is a proud clan.


The sūrah could have said “you will know in the grave and you will know on the Day.” It said the same sentence twice. Specification would have satisfied curiosity. Repetition satisfies fear. The ear that slept through verse 3 is not given new data. It is given the same data louder. Teachers who are afraid of repetition as “bad style” are using a prose manual against revelation. This is oral warning. Oral warning doubles.


A common pairing is: you will know at death when the angels appear, and you will know when you are raised. Another: you will know when you see the results of *takāthur* collapse, and you will know when you see the Fire (verses 6–7). The sūrah itself will double the seeing of Hell as it doubled the knowing. The architecture is paired stairs: know, know; if you knew with *ʿilm al-yaqīn*; you will see the Fire; you will see it with *ʿayn al-yaqīn*; then you will be asked about bliss. Verse 4 is the second stair of the first flight.


“Again, no!” in the translation captures *thumma kallā*. The *no* is not tired. The second *kallā* negates any leftover hope that verse 3 was rhetorical. People treat first warnings as style. The second warning is when a serious speaker shows he meant the first. God is not a speaker who needed two tries to be sure. He is a speaker who knows we need two tries to hear.

**Soon Remains Soon**

*Sawfa* is repeated, so the delay is not lengthened by the repetition. The second soon is not later than the first soon in the calendar; it is later in the experience of the one who is passing through stations. For the reciter still in *takāthur*, both soons are still ahead and both are near. The practical yield of verse 4 is the same as verse 3 with less excuse: leave the contest. The sūrah is about to raise the knowing from *sawfa taʿlamūn* to *law taʿlamūna ʿilm al-yaqīn*.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:5

> Indeed, if you were to know ˹your fate˺ with certainty, ˹you would have acted differently˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Conditional Whose Result Is Left Unsaid**

*Kallā law taʿlamūna ʿilm al-yaqīn* — no indeed; if you knew with the knowledge of certainty… The apodosis is omitted. The translation supplies “you would have acted differently” and “your fate.” That is responsible tafsīr. Arabic often drops the result when the result is obvious and more frightening unsaid. If they had *ʿilm al-yaqīn*, they would not have been diverted by *takāthur*. They would have spent, prayed, stopped counting graves as trophies. The silence after *yaqīn* is the space where each listener inserts his own abandoned duties.


Later scholars speak of *ʿilm al-yaqīn* (certainty by knowledge), *ʿayn al-yaqīn* (certainty by eye), *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* (certainty by lived truth). This sūrah uses the first in verse 5 and the second in verse 7. It does not use the third phrase. Do not force a full technical map onto two verses, but do not ignore the ladder the sūrah itself builds: knowledge-certainty should have been enough; eye-certainty will come when they see the Fire. If they had the first, they would not need the second as punishment. Revelation is meant to be *ʿilm al-yaqīn*. Refusing it is what makes the eye's lesson necessary.


A third *kallā* in five verses. The particle is the sūrah's drum. Each time it kills a different excuse: the life-plan (v. 3), the leftover hope (v. 4), the claim to already know enough (v. 5). People in *takāthur* say they know they will die. Verse 5 says that if that knowledge were *yaqīn*, the hours would look other than they do. Knowledge that does not move the hand is not this *ʿilm*. It is rumour.


*Yaqīn* is not a feeling of confidence. It is a knowledge that has settled so that doubt does not govern action. The Prophet asked for *yaqīn* in supplications. Companions who had little wealth and much expenditure had this verse's missing apodosis lived out. The verse is therefore diagnostic: look at the day. If the day is a contest of more, the *yaqīn* is not there, however many creeds are memorized.

**Fate in Brackets**

“Your fate” is not an extra Arabic word. It is what the unsaid apodosis and the following verses point to. The fate is seeing Hell and being asked about pleasures. Knowing that fate with certainty now is the whole point of Makkan *waḥy*. Verse 5 is the sūrah's centre: the tragedy is not lack of information. It is lack of the kind of knowing that rearranges a life. The Fire in the next line will be what they will know with the eye instead.

**The Arabic Still Has Work After the First Pass**

The line of 102:5 is short in the muṣḥaf and long in the mouth of a teacher who will not stop at a gloss. at-Takāthur places this verse inside a sūrah whose name is already an argument, and the local theme — rivalry in increase — does not exhaust the grammar. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. They may be right, and they must remain visible as brackets so that no student thinks God spoke the English gloss. Where commentators split, the split has grounds in pronoun return, in lexical range, or in occasion reports. Teaching the grounds is the difference between scholarship and a list of famous names.

**Conduct Follows if the Verse Is Believed**

Belief in 102:5 is not a nod during tarāwīḥ. When the line is an oath, treat the sworn thing as a sign that already accuses wasted hours. A question in God's mouth means stop pretending folklore has the answer. A threat means leave the type while tawbah is open. An exception-clause means become the verbs, not wear a group label. at-Takāthur is recited in two rakʿahs by people who then return to counting, mocking, or sleeping through ʿaṣr. That return is the opposite of tafsīr. Ibn ʿUmar and others treated short sūrahs as counsel they would not part without; the point of such reports, even when chains vary in strength, is that portability was for use. Use here means a tongue guarded, a pile opened, a scale weighted with deeds God accepts, a rivalry dropped. The verse does not need a new miracle to be heavy. It needs a day that looks different after it.

**A Checked Parallel, Not a Stolen Verse**

*“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury”* (57:20) belongs to another place in the Book, and it is brought here only as a witness, not as a second assignment for this commentary. The parallel is a neighbour in theme, not a licence to merge two sūrahs into one commentary. Cross-reference is a servant of the present line. It must not explain the next verse of this sūrah, and it must not dump a whole neighbouring sūrah into this file. The discipline is: one extra checked fact, tagged, then back to 102:5. Occasions of revelation, when reported, are historical helps. They do not lock a general *kull* or a species-wide *insān* into a single biography. Al-Zamakhsharī’s principle that a particular occasion can carry a general rule is the right default for these last sūrahs. Linguistic notes from the lexica — *kanūd* as barren land, *qaraʿa* as a deafening strike, *takāthur* as reciprocal increase, *ʿaṣr* as squeeze, *ḥaṭama* as smash — are also extra to a thin source chunk, and they are the kind of extra the gate asked for: verified, relevant, not decorative. The present verse remains the only defendant. The parallel is a lamp, not a second courtroom.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:6

> ˹But˺ you will surely see the Hellfire

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Lam and a Nūn of Emphasis**

*La-tarawunna al-jaḥīm* — you will surely see the *jaḥīm*. The *lām* and the energetic *nūn* make the seeing undoubtable. *Jaḥīm* is a name of Hell that stresses blazing. The translation “Hellfire” is close enough. The seeing is future, plural, and not optional. Verse 5's if-you-knew is now answered by what they will know with sight. The contrast is cruel in the exact sense: the knowledge they refused as *ʿilm* they will receive as *ruʾyah*.


Commentators differ: all people see Hell as they pass it, as in *“There is none of you who will not pass over it”* (19:71), and then the saved are rescued; or only the diverted addressees of this sūrah, the people of *takāthur*, see it as entry. Both can be held without forcing this short line to settle the *wurūd* debate. The threat in context is not a tour for the pious. It is a promise to those occupied with more. They will see the thing their numbers could not buy off.


*Alhā* occupied the eyes with goods. *Tarawunna* occupies the eyes with *jaḥīm*. The organ of the sin is the organ of the lesson. That is a Qur'anic habit: the tongue that lied is a witness; the eye that was busy will be busy with fire. There is no claim here that wealth is Hell. The claim is that a life of *takāthur* terminates in a sight that wealth cannot mediate.


Modern softening likes to make Hell a state of mind. This verb of seeing, with this object, resists that. You see a thing. *Jaḥīm* is the thing. Other verses describe fuel, keepers, width, boiling water. This verse only says you will see it. For a sūrah of short clauses, the proper name is enough. People who have never feared a place will not fear a mood. The Book names a place.

The energetic form kills the gamble that “maybe there is nothing.” *Takāthur* often lives on maybe: maybe death is far, maybe there is no court, maybe my pile is a sign I am favoured. *La-tarawunna* is not maybe. ## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:7

> Again, you will surely see it with the eye of certainty

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿAyn al-Yaqīn Is Not a Metaphor for Insight Alone**

*Thumma la-tarawunnahā ʿayna al-yaqīn* — then you will surely see it with the eye of certainty. *Hā* is the *jaḥīm*. *ʿAyn al-yaqīn* is certainty of the eye, seeing that leaves no gap for interpretation. In this verse it is brutally physical before it is mystical. Later Ṣūfī manuals use the phrase for spiritual vision. That use is a borrowing, sometimes fruitful, sometimes a way to escape the Fire. In *at-Takāthur* the eye is the eye that will look at *jaḥīm*. The seeing of verse 6 is confirmed and raised: not a glimpse that could be denied as glare, a seeing that is certainty.


As in verse 4, *thumma* may mark a second stage: first seeing the Fire from afar, then seeing it as entry; or first seeing at the *wurūd*, then seeing as companions of it. Or it is raising without a clock. The doubled energetic seeing matches the doubled *sawfa taʿlamūn*. The sūrah's style is couplets of threat. A reader looking for new information in verse 7 must hear the new word: *ʿayn*. The organ is named. Knowledge of verse 5 that should have been enough is now replaced by an eyeball's knowledge that no one wanted.


*Yaqīn* in verse 5 would have changed action. *Yaqīn* in verse 7 is the yaqīn of people who can no longer act. That is the tragedy the sūrah is built on. There is a certainty that saves because it is before the grave, and a certainty that damns because it is after the diversion has run its *ḥattā*. The same Arabic root, two times, two moral worlds. Teaching this verse without that contrast is only vocabulary.


*Hā* does not say “your inner demons.” It says the *jaḥīm* already named. Pronouns in these sūrahs are tight. The eye of certainty has an object. Spiritual writers who say “you will see the truth of your soul” may say a true thing elsewhere. They must not erase this object. The Qur'an's own *ʿayn al-yaqīn* here is hell-sight.

## Sūrah at-Takāthur 102:8

> Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about ˹your worldly˺ pleasures

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naʿīm Is the Last Word on More**

*Thumma la-tusʾalunna yawmaʾidhin ʿan al-naʿīm* — then you will surely be asked that Day about the *naʿīm*. *Naʿīm* is ease, delight, blessing, the soft good of a life. The translation's “worldly pleasures” points the word at this world's comforts, which is the reading that fits *takāthur*. Some reports make the *naʿīm* specific: cold water, bread, shade, the Prophet himself as the greatest blessing. Al-Ṭabrisī notes the view that people are asked about the Messenger. Others say only disbelievers are asked in threat; others say every *mukallaf* is asked. The verse's first force is: the things you competed to pile will return as exam questions.

**Questioned, Not Only Punished**

A question is a humiliation for people who thought they owned their goods. Ownership would mean no interview. *Suʾāl* means the goods were a trust. *“Then you will surely be asked on that Day about the blessings”* is this line's plain sense. A hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Tirmidhī (and parallels) mentions being asked about security, health, and cool water; grade and exact numbering should be checked per collection, but the theme is everywhere in the Book: *“On that Day you will be questioned about your deeds”* (16:93 in force; 43:19; 102:8 this verse). Even *“Did there not come to you a warner?”* (67:8) is a question to the people of the Fire. This verse's question is about pleasure, which is sharper for the rich in increase.

**That Day Ties the Seeing to the Court**

*Yawmaʾidhin* is the day of the seeing. Eye-certainty and questioning are not two religions. You see the *jaḥīm* and you are asked about the shade you wasted. The pair teaches that Hell is not arbitrary heat. It is heat after an interview about gifts. The diverted thought gifts were scores. The court thinks gifts were questions waiting to be asked.

**Worldly in Brackets, Blessing in the Root**

*Naʿīm* is also a name for the life of the garden: *ʿīshah rāḍiyah* and *jannāt al-naʿīm*. The word is morally neutral until used. Here the addressees' *naʿīm* is the ease of this world because that is what occupied them. In the garden the same root is unproblematic because it is given after the question is passed. The sūrah ends on the question, not on the garden, because it is a warning sūrah. The way into a *naʿīm* that is not interrogated as a crime is to treat this world's *naʿīm* as a trust.

**The Contest Is Over; the Oral Exam Begins**

Verse 1 was diversion by rivalry. Verse 8 is speech under oath in a court. The human who would not leave the count is made to talk. What will he say about water, time, health, the Prophet, the extra camel, the extra screen? The sūrah does not script the answer. It promises the interview. A life that prepares answers by thanks and spending is the missing apodosis of verse 5, now visible as the only way to meet verse 8 without ruin.
