# Sūrah as-Sajdah (Chapter 32) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:1

> **Alif-Lãm-Mĩm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reciter Begins, the Voice Does Not Apologize**

*The sūrah begins with three isolated Arabic letters — alif, lām, mīm — sounded as separate phonemes rather than woven into a word. They arrive without verb, without predication, without gloss. One hears three sounds and then a stop. That is the entire first verse. Nothing could be further from the rhetorical habits of pre-Islamic Arabia, where a skilled orator opened with flourishes designed to display his artistry: the prelude over lost encampments, the praise of the patron, the customary oaths by stars and camels and rain-clouds. The Qur'an declines that performance. It opens with a sound whose function is precisely to resist being folded back into familiar eloquence. A hearer in the Meccan marketplace, trained to judge speech by how cleverly it turned, would have found himself unable to do so here. He had heard words before; he had not heard three letters announced like a verdict. That inability is the opening's whole point. The voice about to speak will not ask to be graded by the usual standards.

This cluster belongs to the *muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the separated letters that stand at the head of twenty-nine sūrahs, and alif-lām-mīm is the most frequent of them all. It opens al-Baqarah, Āl ʿImrān, al-ʿAnkabūt, al-Rūm, and Luqmān as well as the present sūrah, appearing across Meccan and Medinan material from every period of the revelation. That spread is worth pausing over. The letters are not a late mannerism of one phase or one mood. They act as an auditory seal: anyone who has heard the Qur'an recited knows, before any complete sentence is formed, that what is coming is Qur'an and not poetry, not a kāhin's rhymed prose, not a sermon a man composed on his own. The opening sound belongs to the register of revelation the way a particular bell belongs to a particular rite. Long before any doctrine is asserted, the listener knows he is addressed in a specific mode.

**Why the Letters Are Not Translated**

Every translator faces a choice at this verse, and the majority choice — to leave the letters as Alif-Lãm-Mĩm — reflects a settled instinct. They do not form a lexical item one can render into another language. They are not an acronym whose components add up to a phrase, despite centuries of pious attempts to read them that way. From the earliest generations, the dominant position among Sunnī exegetes has been that their precise signification rests with God alone. One affirms them as they came, one recites them in the positions the recitation assigns them, and one does not pretend to a key that God chose not to provide. This is not intellectual laziness. It is a discipline of restraint that guards the reader from a subtler error — the error of supposing every divine sentence is a puzzle he is meant to crack open. Some parts of the text exist to teach him that he is not master of its terms.

That restraint does not shut down contemplative reading. Al-Maybudī, in his Persian commentary, hears in the three letters a miniature of the entire revelatory event: alif pointing to Allāh the source, lām pointing to Gabriel the bearer, mīm pointing to Muhammad the recipient. On that reading, three syllables compress the three poles of the event — sender, messenger, receiver — before any content is delivered. The reading is beautiful and has fed centuries of meditation, but it is offered as *ishārah*, a hint, not as decoding. A reader who treats it as a cipher that unlocks hidden doctrine will end up saying things the text never authorizes, and the mainstream tradition has consistently refused to go there. One may savor the allusion; one must not mistake it for lexical meaning.

**The Pause Between Verse One and Verse Two Does Work**

In live recitation the first verse ends and there is a pause. That silence does not vanish. It puts the auditor in a posture of attention before argument begins. In a cultural setting where listeners interrupted freely and judged quickly, the opening letters created a kind of hush. The reciter was not asking for approval; he was already in a different register. When verse two arrives — *The revelation of this Book is beyond doubt from the Lord of all worlds* — it lands on ground the three letters have already cleared. The listener is no longer in a position to say "speak to me in my own idiom and I will judge"; the idiom has been set from the first sound.

**A Confidence That Does Not Perform**

A lesser scripture would have opened with arguments for its own truth: miracles cited, proofs laid out, objections answered before they were raised. The Qur'an chooses a stranger, more confident beginning. It says three letters and stops. In that stop is the whole claim the next twenty-nine verses will unfold: the speaker is not competing with poets, orators, or tribal wise men. He begins on His own terms, and the proper response of the listener — before he agrees or disagrees with any individual claim — is to recognize that the terms have been set.

One sees the same confidence across the other sūrahs that open with isolated letters. They never pause to justify the strangeness; they speak, and the audience must adjust. That pattern is itself a theological statement. A book that needed human permission to be heard would start by ingratiating itself. A book that comes from the Lord of the worlds can afford to begin with what looks, to an impatient ear, like three meaningless sounds, and wait for the auditor to catch up. The haste to dismiss is on the listener's side, not on the speaker's.

One should observe how frequently the sūrahs that open with alif-lām-mīm move quickly into the claim that the Book is guidance, light, or truth — never into a defense of the opening letters themselves. The letters are allowed to remain unexplained because their function is rhetorical and liturgical before it is doctrinal. In the mouth of a reciter they establish a rhythm, a gravity, a sacred sound-signature. Across the Muslim world the same syllables are pronounced in every daily prayer, at the start of recitation, in moments of reflection. They have become part of the soundscape of Muslim life precisely because they do not mean something that can be replaced by a paraphrase. To ask for their "meaning" in a single phrase is to miss what they are doing. They are not a sign pointing to something else; they are an event. When they are recited, the Qur'an has begun.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:2

> **The revelation of this Book is—beyond doubt—from the Lord of all worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Sending Down," Not "Composing"**

After the opening letters, the first complete sentence names what has arrived: *tanzīl al-kitāb*. The key word is *tanzīl*, a verbal noun from the root n-z-l, "to send down, to cause to alight." The Qur'an does not describe itself as a book that was authored, assembled, or collected by human reflection; it describes itself as something that descended. That spatial metaphor is not an archaic cosmology one has to defend or dismantle. It is the theological nerve of the sentence. A revelation that "comes down" comes from a source outside the listener, beyond his control, outside the circle of his own opinions. He can respond to it, reject it, or surrender to it, but he cannot treat it as a peer production to be graded against other books the way one poem is graded against another. Four other sūrahs open with nearly identical language — al-Zumar, Ghāfir, al-Jāthiyah, al-Aḥqāf (39:1; 40:2; 45:2; 46:2) — and each time the announcement of descent precedes very different content: a call to sincere worship, a warning of the Hour, a catalog of signs, a rebuttal of the accusation of forgery. The sameness of the opening across different subjects makes a structural point: the origin does not need to be reargued for every topic. It is fixed first, so that every later topic is heard as speech from the same speaker.

**Where Doubt Has No Place**

The phrase *lā rayba fīhi*, "in which there is no doubt," is placed early in the sentence, before the Lord of the worlds is named — a rhetorical choice that puts the certainty at the front of the listener's attention. Classical exegetes note two possible referents for the pronoun. On one reading, "there is no doubt" attaches to the Book itself: its contents are certain, its guidance reliable, its promise secure. On another, it attaches to the fact of revelation: there is no doubt that it is from the Lord of the worlds. Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī report both possibilities and do not find them in tension. A book whose origin is divine deserves to be trusted in its claims, and a book whose claims cohere the way this one does points back to that origin. The ordering in the Arabic sentence is important for another reason: the listener hears the verdict of certainty before he is told what the certainty is about. The sentence is not offering him a proposition he may entertain; it is stating a fact he is expected to reckon with.

Doubt, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, is not honest inquiry. Honest inquiry is called for, even commanded — *Do you not reflect? Do you not see? Do you not reason?* — and the Book is full of invitations to examine evidence. Rayb is the wobble that refuses to settle even when the evidence has arrived, the persistent inner resistance that prefers its own comforts to the conclusion it can feel coming. "No doubt" here is not a demand for blind faith; it is a description of the book's own weight. When something is genuinely from the Lord of the worlds, it does not remain forever in the zone of "perhaps."

**"Lord of All Worlds," Not Lord of a Tribe**

The title *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* is the first theological predicate the sūrah gives to God, and it is chosen with care. The audience that first heard these verses in Mecca did not deny Allāh outright. They acknowledged a high God who created the heavens and earth, who was the Lord of the Kaʿbah, to whom one turned in extremity. What they did was to surround that high God with lesser intermediaries — angels, jinn, ancestral idols, patron saints — who could be approached independently, who belonged more immediately to tribe and locale, who served as protectors in a world where divine transcendence felt too remote for daily business. *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* cuts through that arrangement at once. He is not the God of the Quraysh only, not the God of the Arabs only, not the God of the shrine only. He is the Lord of every ʿālam — every kind of creature, every community, every epoch, every realm of being. The word ʿālamīn is plural; there is more than one world, and He is Lord of all of them. That universal frame is essential to what the sūrah will go on to say about creation from clay, the breathing of the Spirit, the role of the Angel of Death, the resurrection, the reward of the Night Vigil, and the final distinction between the believer and the iniquitous. None of these is a tribal teaching.

**A Provenance That Changes the Reader**

The verse does not end with an argument; it ends with a fact. The Book is from the Lord of the worlds. Once that fact is received, the reader's posture toward what follows cannot remain unchanged. He is not a critic in a position to accept or reject pieces of the text the way he accepts or rejects opinions in a marketplace conversation. He is a creature addressed by the One who made him, whose world he inhabits, whose provision he eats, whose breath is in his nostrils. The whole sūrah will unfold the implications of that address — creation, death, the interval between, the gathering, the recompense — but none of those implications will have any weight if the first sentence has not landed. That is why the verse is short and unqualified. It does not argue; it establishes. The next verse will take up the first objection of those who refuse the establishment, and the refutation will be equally brief.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:3

> **Or do they say, “He has fabricated it!”? No! It is the truth from your Lord in order for you to warn a people to whom no warner has come before you, so they may be ˹rightly˺ guided.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetoric of Instant Dismissal**

Verse three begins with *am*, a small word that can mark a plain disjunction ("or") or, as most early grammarians take it here, sharpen and continue what has come before. The second reading fits the tone better. Far from offering listeners a calm fork in the road where acceptance and rejection sit as equal options, the text says they are already declaring — while the verses are still being delivered — that the man put this together out of his own head. The accusation precedes any real hearing. That is an old, recognizable human reflex. When a message lands too close to practices people are unwilling to disturb, they do not examine it; they diagnose the speaker. Inventing, forging, stitching the whole thing together — *iftarā ʿalayhi* — was the stock Quraysh retort every time the recitation cornered them on idolatry, on economic unfairness, or on the afterlife. The charge costs nothing, requires no patient listening, and clears the ground in a single breath so that life can continue undisturbed.

The Qur'an meets the charge with one word, *bal*: "Nay," "On the contrary." There is no long apologia, no marshaling of wonders or witnesses, no anxious plea for a fair hearing. A book that genuinely felt pressed by the accusation would need a chapter to defend itself; this one cuts the charge off in mid-sentence and reaffirms what the prior verse has set down — this is truth that issued from your Lord. When a thing originates with the Lord of all the worlds, forgery is ruled out by the very nature of the source, and the point does not need to be argued at length before moving on. The terseness of the reply is itself an enactment of the confidence it asserts. After *bal*, the verse is already talking about purpose, not about the accusation. The charge is dealt with; the message has work to do in the world.

**A Generational Silence Before the Warner Came**

The purpose clause defines the audience: *that you may warn a people to whom no warner has come before you*. The Arabs of the Prophet's generation — Quraysh at the center, surrounding Ḥijāzī tribes around them — had not lived under a scripture-bearing messenger within any span they themselves remembered. The Abrahamic current that had once run through Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl had, over generations of idol worship, thinned into shrine customs and tribal lore; the memory of earlier warners — Hūd among ʿĀd, Ṣāliḥ among Thamūd, Shuʿayb in Midian — survived as campfire stories of peoples destroyed, not as a living teaching. Sixth-century Arabs held no book in their own tongue, no unbroken prophetic line, no authoritative doctrine of the one God or of the Day of Judgment; their operative moral framework was the customary code of tribal honor and commercial practice. Two other verses register that same historical gap in plain language: one notes that these Arabs possessed no books they could study and that no warner had been sent to them before the Prophet (34:44); another says the Qur'an was sent that the Prophet might warn a people whose ancestors had not been warned, so that they had grown heedless (36:6). The long silence is not a defense they will be able to raise on the Day. It is exactly why a messenger has now come. The gap has been closed; no one will be able to claim he was never told.

**Reminder as Well as Fresh News**

A second construal of the clause is grammatically possible, and several early readers noted it: "that you may warn a people of that which came to them by a warner before you." On that construal the verse is not saying warners had been altogether absent, but that distant ancestors had been warned in earlier generations, those warnings faded, and the Qur'an now arrives to call them back to what was once theirs. The two readings do not in practice compete. The community being addressed lived at the intersection of both realities: no living messenger walked among them in their own historical moment, yet buried traces of older revelations — the residual Abrahamic piety around the Kaʿbah, tales of destroyed peoples, the faint moral vocabulary of an effaced monotheism — testified quietly to truths they had ceased to honor. Any messenger who comes after a long silence is simultaneously bringer of fresh news and a caller back to what human beings know at the level of their created nature but have covered over. The Qur'an captures both those functions across its pages, calling itself in one place *nabaʾ* (news arriving for the first time) and in another *dhikrā* (remembrance of what was never entirely lost).

**The Particle That Leaves Room for Choice**

The closing clause — *laʿallahum yahtadūn*, "that haply they may be guided" — states the purpose of the sending in a grammatical mode worth attending to. *Laʿalla* means "perhaps" or "that they may," and it deliberately stops short of "so that they will certainly be guided." The warning is sent so that guidance becomes available, not so that it becomes irresistible. A response remains the hearer's to give, and that small word keeps the whole verse from sliding into determinism. The messenger comes to open a door; he does not drag people through it. The point will be stated directly later in the sūrah: *Had We willed, We would have given every soul its guidance* (32:13). God did not will that; He willed to send messengers so that a free acceptance or refusal could occur, and the "perhaps" here is the linguistic marker of that freedom. The truth is from the Lord, the warning has reached an unwarned people, and what remains is their choice. Everything that follows — on creation, on death, on resurrection, on judgment and reward — unfolds the weight of that choice.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:4

> **It is Allah Who has created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne. You have no protector or intercessor besides Him. Will you not then be mindful?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Six Stages, Not a Seven-Day Week**

After establishing the Book's provenance and the audience it is sent to, the sūrah turns to the sender Himself. *Allāhu alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyām* — "God it is Who created the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in six days." The formula recurs at several points across the Qur'an, and the "days" have been handled with care by the exegetical tradition from the Companions onward. They are not the twenty-four-hour cycles of an earth spinning beneath a sun when neither earth nor sun yet existed; they are stages or phases of the creative act, named with a word familiar to human beings because duration of any kind has to be called something. The particle *thumma*, translated "then," can carry the sense of "and" or "moreover" in classical Arabic rather than a strict temporal "afterwards," which is why one cannot simply read off a seven-day calendar from the verse and conclude that God sat on the throne on the seventh day like a laborer resting after his week. The tradition has consistently resisted that anthropomorphic reading without draining the verse of its force.

**The Throne: Sovereignty, Not Furniture**

*Thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh*, "then He established Himself on the Throne," belongs to the class of verses the tradition calls *mutashābih*, whose exact modality is not to be pressed with the imagination. The position hammered out by classical Sunnī theology — by al-Ashʿarī, by al-Māturīdī, by the schools that grew from them — is straightforward to state and difficult to hold consistently: one affirms the wording as it came, without asking "how" (*bilā kayf*), without likening God to a body occupying a physical seat, and without stripping the verse of its meaning by reducing *istawā* to a bare synonym for "He reigned" in the abstract. The word *ʿarsh* in Arabic usage, ancient and Qur'anic, carries the entire semantic field of sovereign authority and royal presence. What the sentence asserts is that the God who created is the God who reigns — that the world is not abandoned after being made, that its affairs are governed from above rather than left to drift. To picture a giant body perched on celestial furniture is to slip back into idolatry of the imagination; to allegorize *istawā* away into nothing but an abstract assertion of lordship is to lose the note of royal presence that the throne image is there to convey.

**Every Other Protector Collapses**

The shift to the second person is sudden and decisive. After speaking of God in the third person as creator and sovereign, the verse turns to the listener and says: *Mā lakum min dūnihi min waliyyin wa-lā shafīʿ*, "Apart from Him you have neither protector nor intercessor." A *walī* is a patron, a guardian, a helper on whose loyalty one can repose in danger; a *shafīʿ* is an intercessor whose favorable word can alter the outcome. The Quraysh did not, for the most part, deny Allāh's existence as high God. What they maintained was a network of lesser beings — angels treated as daughters of God, jinn worshipped as powers, idols representing ancestral intermediaries, tribal patrons — who could be approached independently, who stood between the worshipper and a remote high God, who could be called upon without needing permission. This verse dismantles that network at one stroke.

It does not, it must be noted, deny that intercession will occur on the Day. Other verses affirm that it will, but they always attach the condition: *There is no intercessor save by His leave* (10:3; 19:87; 20:109; 39:44). An intercessor who speaks only by the sovereign's leave is not an alternative protector; he is a channel of the sole Protector's will. That fact reshapes the whole of religious practice. If no one else can protect, there is no point arranging spiritual insurance around the edges of one's life — a saint here, a talisman there, a whispered bargain with a lesser power. The path to safety simplifies: approach the One directly, seek help from the One directly, repent to the One directly. Every other arrangement is a version of the same pagan impulse, which survives monotheism in new dress whenever a person starts relying on something other than God.

**"Will You Not Be Mindful?"**

The closing question — *afalā tadhakkarūn*, "Will you not then be mindful?" — operates on more than one level. *Tadhakkur* in Qur'anic Arabic means remembering in the deepest sense, taking admonition, waking up to a reality that has always been there. Some exegetes connect it to the pretemporal covenant of 7:172, when all the offspring of Adam were drawn from his loins and bore witness that their Lord was God. That covenant is stamped on human nature, the *fiṭrah*, and "remembering" is what happens when the dust of habit and convention is blown off that innate witness and a person recognizes the One he has been dealing with all along.

The question is a prod, not a request for information. The listener has just been told that the heavens and earth were made by God, that He reigns, that no one else can protect or intercede for him. If he has absorbed those three facts at all, the expected response is not further debate but heedfulness. The verse therefore ends on the right note. It does not say "therefore conclude the following syllogism." It says "wake up."

The entire verse is a masterpiece of compression. In a single Arabic sentence it moves from creation to sovereignty to the nullity of every rival protector to a direct call on the listener's conscience. Other scriptures might have spread those four themes across chapters; here they are bound tightly together because they belong together. The Creator is the sovereign; the sovereign is the only protector; recognition of that fact is what mindfulness means. Nothing in the sentence can be detached without the whole losing its force. Remove creation, and sovereignty becomes an abstraction. Remove sovereignty, and the rejection of other protectors becomes a theological technicality. Remove the call to mindfulness, and the verse becomes doctrine without demand on the hearer. Held together as they are, the four clauses describe a complete theological vision in the space of a breath.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:5

> **He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Governance That Does Not Retire**

*Yudabbiru al-amr min al-samāʾ ilā al-arḍ* — "He directs the affair from the heaven to the earth." The verse that follows immediately upon the throne establishes that God is not a distant architect who built the world and then withdrew. *Tadbīr* is the active ordering of affairs toward their ends, the carrying out of a plan through every level of causation. The word is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for the same idea (10:3, 31; 13:2), and its presence here corrects a misunderstanding that the throne language can tempt. A king on a throne can be imagined as passively reigning while subordinates do the work; this verse insists that the sovereign is the one who directs the affairs. Every decree, every provision, every ordinance, every event descends from the highest level of reality to the lowest level of the earth and is worked out there for the duration appointed. Some exegetes narrow *al-amr* to revelation specifically — the sending down of the Qur'an and the commands of the law — but the wider reading fits the context better, because the verse goes on to speak of the ascent of all things back to God on a Day of immense length. Revelation is one strand of the governance, not the whole of it.

**What Goes Up**

*Thumma yaʿruju ilayhi* — "then it ascends to Him." The Arabic verb is singular, "it ascends," which suggests that the referent is *al-amr*, the affair itself, rather than any plurality of beings. Commentators have offered two accounts. Some say what ascends is the angels, who descend with the commands and ascend with the record of deeds; others say it is the Command itself, meaning that everything created by the divine Command returns to Him on the Day of Resurrection (Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Zamakhsharī). The two are not really in conflict. The angels ascend as servants of the Command; the Command is the thread that pulls every creature back to its origin. The picture drawn is of a complete circuit. From God the order comes down; through the creation the order runs its course, generating deeds, histories, lives; on a fixed Day the whole of it — the works, the souls, the record, the angels who bore the commands — is gathered back up to Him. A universe that only descended and never returned would be a broken line, not a divine government.

**A Thousand Years by Your Counting**

*Fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu alf sanatin mimmā taʿuddūn* — "in a Day whose measure is a thousand years of that which you reckon." The same reckoning appears in 22:47, and in 70:4 the measure is given as fifty thousand years. Al-Jalālayn notes that such numbers point to the severity of that Day for the disbelievers, who will experience its duration with an intensity that makes a day feel like a thousand or fifty thousand years, but the verse is saying more than "it will feel long." It is giving the reader a lesson in the nature of time itself. The "days" of the divine realm are not measured by the sun and moon; they are measures of a different order, and the thousand-year figure is offered in earthly units precisely so that the auditor can grasp that the scale is not his scale. The discrepancy between "a thousand" in one place and "fifty thousand" in another is not a contradiction. It is a reminder that duration in the presence of God has no single fixed translation into the time of a rotating planet. Trying to reconcile the numbers arithmetically misses the point. The point is that the stretch of the Day is vast beyond the habits of earthly reckoning, and the affairs that ascend to God will be dealt with on a scale that corresponds to His sovereignty, not to our impatience.

This has a direct bearing on how a believer thinks about delay and answer. A prayer that seems unanswered, a wrong that seems unpunished, a promise that seems slow in coming — these are measured against the thousand-year Day, not against the span of one human life. The Qur'an repeatedly warns against treating the delay of punishment as evidence that punishment will not arrive; it equally warns against treating the delay of help as evidence that help will not arrive. Both warnings rest on the same fact: the Commander of the affair does not run His government by the clock His creatures use.

**Al-Tustarī on the Veil of Self-Direction**

Sahl al-Tustarī, one of the formative early voices in Qur'anic spirituality, drew a deeply personal lesson from this verse that has shaped its meditation ever since. God reveals to His servants from His knowledge what is a means of guidance and salvation for them. The servant who becomes content with the provision and direction God has decreed for him finds that the evil of his own self-direction — his own anxious plotting against his own good, his own effort to wrest control of outcomes that were never his to control — falls away, and he is returned to a state of rest in the divine decree. Al-Tustarī's closing sentence is blunt: God created human beings without any veil between Himself and them, and then their own self-directing became their veil.

That reading does not replace the plain cosmological sense of the verse; it completes it. The descent and ascent of the Command is not only the outer shape of world history; it is the inner shape of every soul. A soul that ceases trying to direct itself in competition with its Maker finds that the Command descends into it as mercy and ascends from it as surrender, and the thousand-year Day that terrifies those who were running their own government becomes for such a soul the day when all things are gathered back to the Beloved.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:6

> **That is the Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, Most Merciful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Resumptive Pronoun That Gathers What Precedes**

The verse opens with *dhālikum*, "That is," a deictic pronoun that points back to the God described in verses 4 and 5 — the creator of the heavens and earth in six days, the one who established Himself on the Throne, the governor who directs every affair from heaven to earth so that it ascends back to Him on a day whose measure is a thousand years. Rather than introducing new subject matter, this verse pauses to attach four names to the subject of those actions. The structure is typical of the Qur'an: first the acts, then the names that tell you who the actor is. The reader is not asked to assent to abstract attributes in a vacuum; he is asked to recognize that the God who did all that has just been described is the Knower, the Almighty, the Merciful. The "that" is resumptive, pointing backward rather than forward, so the names land as a summary of what has already been established.

**Knower of the Unseen and the Seen**

*ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa-l-shahādah*, "Knower of the Unseen and the seen," is one of the great paired names of God in the Qur'an, recurring frequently across both Meccan and Medinan material (6:73; 9:94, 105; 13:9; 23:92; 39:46; 59:22; 62:8; 64:18). *Al-Ghayb* is what is hidden from created perception — what lies beyond the reach of sight and hearing, what has not yet happened, what lies in the hearts, what takes place in the heavens, what is concealed by the grave and the veil of the Hereafter. *Al-Shahādah* is what is witnessed, what is present, what lies open before the senses. The pairing carries a theological weight that the simpler "omniscient" misses. God does not merely know everything in the flat sense of possessing a list of facts. He knows the hidden as hidden — all that creatures conceal from one another and from themselves — and He knows the visible as visible, the world they live and move in. There is no corner of reality that falls outside His perception; and because He is sovereign over the descent and ascent of every affair, His knowledge is not that of a passive observer but of the one who governs what He knows.

The pairing also answers a deep human anxiety. A great deal of moral life is lived in the unseen: intentions, whispered betrayals, quiet acts of generosity nobody witnessed, struggles of the heart that no friend ever hears about. If God knew only the seen, He would be a poor judge — and a poor comforter. If He knew only the unseen, He would be remote from the lived world of action. The pairing insists that both registers are equally and simultaneously before Him. The prayer uttered in the dark of night, the offense committed behind closed doors, the passing thought held in the heart and never acted on — each is as present to Him as a public declaration in the marketplace.

**The Almighty, the Merciful: A Pair That Belongs Together**

*Al-ʿAzīz al-Raḥīm* — "the Almighty, the Merciful." These two names are paired often in the Qur'an, and their pairing is never casual. *Al-ʿAzīz* means the Mighty, the Invincible, the One who cannot be compelled, resisted, or frustrated in what He wills. Power without mercy would be terror; mercy without power would be sentiment, unable to deliver on its promises. The Qur'an refuses to separate them. The God who directs every affair down from the throne and back up to Him is not a vague benevolence that cannot act on its compassion, and He is not an omnipotent will unconstrained by kindness. He is both at once, and the combination is the ground on which a creature can stand without either presumption or despair.

That combination has a particular resonance in this sūrah. The preceding verses have just spoken of a day whose measure is a thousand years — a day that sounds, to the disobedient, entirely terrifying. The names "Almighty, Merciful" placed immediately after that description remind the listener that the sovereign who holds that day is not only mighty enough to bring it about but merciful enough to have sent warners in advance, to accept repentance, to provide for the obedient the "comfort kept hidden" that verse 17 will describe. The one name corrects a misreading of the other.

**Why a Short Verse Still Deserves Its Place**

This is the shortest verse in the opening section, and a hurried reader might pass over it as a mere string of names. The placement is the key. Between the long cosmological verse of v. 5 and the creation-of-humanity passage that begins in v. 7, the sūrah gives the reader three names to hold in mind as the story turns from the making of worlds to the making of a single creature from clay. The God who is about to be described forming Adam, breathing into him of His Spirit, endowing him with hearing, sight, and hearts, is the same Knower of the unseen and the seen, the same Almighty and Merciful. Those names will give shape to everything that follows: the certainty of resurrection rests on His might, the promise of reward rests on His mercy, and the whole account of judgment is administered by One who knows both the open and the hidden. A short verse is not a pause in the argument; it is the hinge on which the argument turns.

This verse also does the quiet work of preventing the reader from misunderstanding what kind of sovereignty was described in vv. 4–5. A God who directs every affair from heaven to earth and before whom a day is a thousand years can sound, to an anxious ear, like sheer power over creatures who are crushed by the scale of what they face. The names set beside that power correct the impression. He is the Knower — nothing done to a creature is unseen by Him; every tear, every inward struggle, every unnoticed act of devotion is within His perception. He is the Merciful — His power is not wielded capriciously, and the terrors of the thousand-year Day are the backdrop against which mercy operates, not the substance of His relationship with those who turn to Him. The names and the acts belong together; neither half can be read alone without distortion.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:7

> **Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Universe Made Beautifully, Not Serviceably**

The verse opens with a clause whose full force is easy to miss in translation: *alladhī aḥsana kulla shayʾin khalaqahu*. The verb *aḥsana* carries the sense both of perfecting and of making beautiful; it is the same root that gives *iḥsān*, the Islamic virtue of excellence, of doing things as they ought to be done in a way that reflects well on the doer. Some classical readers glossed it here as *aḥkama*, "determined" or "ordered with precision," a reading that points to design rather than aesthetic quality narrowly conceived. Both senses belong. Nothing God makes is shoddy, approximate, or thrown together as a means to an end; and nothing God makes is ugly in its essence, though creatures can deform what was given them. The verse states this as a universal before narrowing down to the human being: every created thing bears the mark of perfect workmanship, and the human being is one particular instance of it, whose material origin is now traced to clay.

The claim that God "made beautiful" all He created is not a soft observation about pretty landscapes. It is a metaphysical thesis. Order and goodness are not later additions to a raw material originally indifferent; they inhere in creation as it issues from His hand. Disorder, cruelty, and deformity enter the picture through the misuse of creaturely freedom, not through a flaw in the Maker's work. That matters for what follows in the sūrah. When the text later describes the rebellion of the disobedient, the shame of the guilty on the Day, the punishment of those who turn from the signs, it is describing a rupture with something originally whole, not the unfolding of a world botched from the start. The human story, in other words, is a story of deviation from an originally beautiful design, and that fact gives both hope and gravity to the call to return.

**Iḥsān as the Human Reflection of Divine Workmanship**

From this clause the Prophet drew a saying that has shaped Islamic ethics across the centuries: "Verily God has enjoined *iḥsān* — excellence, the doing of what is beautiful — for everything. When you kill, kill in a beautiful manner; when you sacrifice, sacrifice in a beautiful manner." Found in the Ṣaḥīḥ of Muslim (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1955), the report draws a direct moral line from the verse to human conduct. Because God perfected all that He made, human beings are called to mirror that perfection in their own acts, even the painful and necessary ones. Slaughter is not to become cruelty; punishment is not to become vengeance; difficult things are to be done with the same care God built into the making of the world. The verse therefore does not merely describe God at a distance. It lays down a pattern for human action. A creature formed by a Maker who "makes beautiful" is himself summoned to beautify — his character, his transactions, his craft, his worship.

**The Lowliness of the Starting Material**

The second half of the verse narrows its focus from "everything He created" to one creature in particular: *wa-badaʾa khalqa al-insāni min ṭīn*, "and He originated the creation of humankind from clay." The specification is not accidental. Human beings occupy a high rank among terrestrial creatures, as the next verses will make clear — proportioned in due measure, breathed into from the divine Spirit, endowed with hearing, sight, and hearts — yet their origin is humble, a fact the Qur'an never allows its reader to forget. Clay is lowly stuff; you find it under your feet, water shapes it, fire hardens it, it is the material of ordinary pots.

Across the Qur'an the clay of Adam is described in a range of terms that together suggest stages of preparation. Pottery clay (*ṣalṣāl*) in 55:14; dried clay of molded mud (*ṣalṣāl min ḥamaʾin masnūn*) in 15:26; a draught of clay (*ṭīnin lāzib*) in 37:11; and before all of these, dust (*turāb*) in 3:59, where the text observes that the likeness of ʿĪsā before God is as the likeness of Adam, created from dust. From dust to viscous clay to molded mud to dried earthenware, the material moves through phases until it is ready to receive the breath of the Spirit that v. 9 will describe. The sequence matters for the larger argument of the sūrah. A God who can shape a living, conscious, morally accountable human being out of a handful of dust can certainly raise him again after he has returned to dust — which is exactly the objection the sūrah will confront in v. 10.

**Why the Humility of the Material Refuses to Let Pride Rest**

Tracing human origin to clay serves two persistent Qur'anic ends, and a third more subtle one. First, it undercuts arrogance. A creature formed from the same mud he walks on has no basis for grandstanding before his Maker, and no basis for looking down on other human beings whose clay came from the same supply. Social hierarchy, tribal pride, racial pretension — all of it looks ridiculous in the light of v. 7. Second, it establishes resurrection as a coherent hope rather than a fantasy. If God launched the whole human story from a substance as low as clay, producing the creature who reasons, loves, prays, and rebels, He does not require a miraculously preserved body in order to remake what was once made. He has done harder things already.

Third, and more quietly, clay in the ancient world was the material of the artisan. The human being is presented from the first as *made*, not self-generated. That fact grounds both our creatureliness and our dignity. We are not accidents of a blind process; we are the work of a craftsman who perfects what He makes. Every human being, however disadvantaged by birth or circumstance, carries that workmanship in his frame. A potter does not despise the vessel he shaped because it began as mud; he values it because of what he made from the mud. The lowliness of the origin does not diminish the worth of the finished thing; it intensifies the wonder of the making.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:8

> **Then He made his descendants from an extract of a humble fluid,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Clay to Progeny**

Verse 7 located the origin of humankind in clay, the material from which Adam was fashioned. Verse 8 moves to the next chapter of the story: *thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min māʾin mahīn*, "then He made his progeny from an extract of a humble fluid." The shift from the singular first human being to his descendants is marked by the word *nasl*, progeny or seed — what issues from the original. The verse does not linger on Adam's individual story; it moves to the origin of every human being born after him. Where Adam came from clay, his posterity comes from a fluid — a drop, an extract, a thing the body excretes without ceremony.

**What *Sulālah* Conveys**

The word rendered "extract" is *sulālah*, from a root meaning to draw forth, to extract gently, to flow in a thin delicate line. Classical lexicographers note that the word suggests something drawn out from something else, fine and subtle, rather than something gross or voluminous. The fluid in question is semen, as the parallels confirm (77:20 refers to the same *māʾin mahīn*, and 86:6 speaks of a "gushing fluid"), but *sulālah* captures a particular nuance: the reproductive substance is a delicate extract, a small drawn-out portion, not the whole person. From that extract, carefully drawn, God builds a new human being. The word keeps the sense of craftsmanship already introduced by the "making beautiful" of v. 7. This is not random biology; it is an art that works with humble material.

**The Humility of the Material Is the Point**

The fluid is described as *mahīn*, "humble" or "despised" — a word that carries connotations of lowliness, something easily dismissed, something ordinary and unimpressive on its face. The description is deliberate. The Qur'an repeatedly calls attention to the lowliness of the material from which human beings are generated, not because sex or the body is shameful but because pride looks ridiculous in the light of that origin. A creature who begins as a drop of humble fluid, who spends nine months developing in darkness, who enters the world helpless and wet, has nothing to be arrogant about — not before God, not before his fellow human beings. Every tyrant, every boaster, every person who looks down on others began exactly the same way. The verse does not describe sex as something impure; it describes the starting point of every human life as something humble, and that humility is spiritually useful.

**Theological Weight of a Low Origin**

Commentators have pointed out, rightly, that the verse also functions as a prelude to the resurrection argument that is coming in v. 10. If God can produce a fully formed, conscious, reasoning human being from something as slight and low as an extract of a humble fluid, He can certainly reassemble a person who has died and decomposed into the earth. The one miracle is no more impressive than the other; in fact the second looks easier than the first, because a person who once existed has, as it were, already been built once. The objection that runs through the Meccan surahs — *"What! When we are bones and dust, are we to be raised anew?"* — depends on a failure to look at one's own beginning. A drop of fluid looks, on its face, far less promising than a grave full of dust, and yet from the drop God produced the living man. The logic is not a strict proof, because resurrection rests on divine will rather than on a natural inference, but it is a powerful reminder that power is not confined to the pristine materials the skeptic prefers.

**Two Creations That Mirror Each Other**

The pairing of v. 7 and v. 8 sets up a twofold account of human creation that the Qur'an will invoke repeatedly. Adam is created from clay by the direct fashioning hand of God and the breathing of the Spirit; every subsequent human being is created from an extract of humble fluid, passing through the known stages of embryonic development (23:12–14 gives the most detailed account, from clay to a clinging clot to a lump to bones to flesh). Both are acts of God; both begin from unlikely material; both end with a fully formed human being who hears, sees, thinks, and is accountable. The miracle is not lessened by being repeated through generation; it is multiplied. Every birth is a new creation, as much a sign of God's power as the forming of Adam, and the humble fluid is as much a display of His art as the clay was.

That insight also reshapes how a believer thinks about the beginning of his own existence. He did not come into the world by accident or as a merely biological event. He was made — made from something unpromising, made beautifully, made by the same God who made the heavens and the earth in six days and established Himself on the throne. The lowliness of his physical origin does not diminish his worth, because his worth does not come from the material; it comes from the One who fashioned the material and breathed into it. The next verse will complete the picture by turning to that breath and to the faculties that lift the human being above the clay from which he came.

One should pause over the fact that the Qur'an does not describe the mechanics of conception with a false delicacy. It names the fluid plainly, calls it humble, and invites the reader to look his own beginnings in the face without flinching. That directness is characteristic of the Book. It does not offer human beings a flattering myth of their own origin; it tells them where they actually came from, and then it tells them what they are being summoned to become. The gap between the lowly material and the high calling is not a problem the text tries to smooth over. It is the ground on which the summons is issued. A creature made from a drop of fluid, who is offered the status of a vicegerent and an eternity of bliss, has only one reasonable response: gratitude. The next verse will note how rarely that response is offered.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:9

> **then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Inanimate Matter to a Living Soul**

After the clay (v. 7) and the humble fluid (v. 8), the account of human creation reaches its decisive moment: *thumma sawwāhu wa-nafakha fīhi min rūḥihī*, "then He fashioned him (or proportioned him) and breathed into him of His Spirit." The verb *sawwā* means to level, to proportion, to bring into balanced form, to make symmetrical. It is the stage at which the material becomes, in the fullest sense, a body — arranged, completed, ready. What follows is not an assembly of parts but an infusion of life. The Qur'an describes the same moment in parallel accounts: when God says to the angels concerning Adam, *When I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My Spirit, fall down before him prostrating* (38:72; 15:29). At that moment the inanimate figure of clay becomes a living, conscious being. Al-Jalālayn notes the straightforward implication: the breathing of the Spirit is what turns a shaped lump into a creature with awareness.

**"Of His Spirit" — Not a Division in God**

The phrasing *min rūḥihī*, "of His Spirit," requires careful reading. The preposition *min*, "of" or "from," does not mean that a fragment of the divine being is cut off and placed in a human body as though God were partitioned. The tradition has consistently refused that reading — it would amount to an incarnation of a divine part, a claim Islam rejects without hesitation. The phrasing indicates origin and honor. The spirit that animates the human being is not a product of the clay, not an emergent property of the material, not something the body secretes as the liver secretes bile; it is a command breathed into the body by God, given from His own act, and it carries with it a particular dignity. That dignity is the reason for the prostration of the angels before Adam (15:29–30; 38:72–73). A creature made of clay yet animated by a spirit from God is neither divine nor merely animal; he stands at a meeting point between the lowly material and a special divine act, and that is the ground of his unique rank in the terrestrial order.

The Spirit (al-Rūḥ) also carries the sense of the activation of the intellect — that by which a human being is able to control hearing, sight, and the heart in the pursuit of truth rather than being driven by appetite. The faculties named next are not separate from the breathing-in; they are its fruit.

**Hearing, Sight, and Hearts — The Order Is Not Accidental**

*Wa-jaʿala lakum al-samʿa wa-l-abṣāra wa-l-afʾidah*, "and He endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts." The three faculties are listed in a fixed order across several Qur'anic passages, and the order is itself significant. Hearing comes first because most human knowledge begins with hearing — receiving testimony, hearing revelation, listening to a messenger, learning language and truth from others. Then comes sight, by which one sees the signs in the world and verifies for oneself what was heard. Then comes the heart — *al-fuʾād*, which in classical Arabic is the seat of perception, understanding, direct insight — by which one grasps the inner nature of things and arrives at a deeper, more direct comprehension. The sequence describes the ordinary path of human knowing, from received report to observation to insight. It also mirrors the spiritual path: first one hears the message, then one sees its truth in the world and in one's own experience, and then the heart understands.

"Little do you give thanks!" is a sharp rebuke placed immediately after the gift. The faculties of hearing, sight, and understanding are the apparatus by which every other good thing in life is appropriated — faith, guidance, knowledge, relationship, beauty. A person who cannot hear cannot be addressed by revelation; a person who cannot see cannot read the signs; a person whose heart is veiled cannot understand what is right in front of him. To have all three and not to use them for the purpose they were given — namely, to know God and to act accordingly — is the deepest form of ingratitude.

**What It Looks Like Not to Give Thanks**

The Qur'an describes the failure clearly elsewhere: *They have hearts with which they understand not, and they have eyes with which they see not, and they have ears with which they hear not* (7:179; see also 46:26). Those verses do not describe physical disabilities; they describe a moral condition in which the faculties are present but idle, or deployed only in the service of appetite and caprice. The person who uses his hearing to listen to slander instead of truth, his sight to gaze at what harms him instead of reflecting on signs, his heart to rationalize his desires instead of perceiving the Real — such a person has the faculties but is not really using them. That is the ingratitude the verse points to.

Thanksgiving for these faculties is not a verbal formula. It consists in using them as they were meant to be used. A ear turned to the word of God, an eye that sees signs in the creation and in its own experience, a heart that bends toward the truth — that is the gratitude the verse expects. The "little" in "little do you give thanks" is a mercy. It does not say "you never give thanks." It says the gratitude offered is scant, partial, intermittent, well below what the gift deserves. The honesty of that "little" is what makes the rebuke bearable, and what opens the door to doing better.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:10

> **˹Still˺ they ask ˹mockingly˺, “When we are disintegrated into the earth, will we really be raised as a new creation?” In fact, they are in denial of the meeting with their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mocking Question**

After the account of human creation — from clay, from humble fluid, proportioned, breathed into, endowed with hearing, sight, and heart — the sūrah brings forward the question the disbelievers keep asking. *Wa-qālū a-idhā ḍalalnā fī al-arḍi a-innā la-fī khalqin jadīd*, "And they say, 'What! When we have become lost in the earth — disintegrated, scattered, vanished into the soil — shall we indeed be in a new creation?'" The verb *ḍalla* means both "to be lost" and "to become dispersed, to disappear," and it captures perfectly the physical image the disbelievers have in mind. The body dies, decays, turns to dust, mixes with the soil, loses its form and individuality, and is scattered — lost in the earth the way a drop is lost in the ocean. How, they ask, can such a thing be reassembled as a new creation?

The question is posed mockingly across many surahs. The language varies — *"When we are dead and become dust and bones, are we to be resurrected?"* (56:47; 17:49, 98; 23:82–83; 36:78; 37:16–17, 53; 50:3; 79:11) — but the objection is the same: dead bodies decomposing into the earth, bones crumbling, identity dissolving, and yet the preachers say we will be raised. The objection has a surface common sense to it. Dead people do not come back. Dust does not reassemble itself into the person it was. That is why the objection is raised with such confidence; the questioners believe they are stating the obvious.

**The Diagnosis That Replaces the Answer**

What the Qur'an answers is not a physics lesson about how resurrection works. It answers with a diagnosis: *Bal hum biliqāʾi rabbihim kāfirūn*, "Nay — they are in denial of the meeting with their Lord." That is, the real problem is not that resurrection is physically impossible; it is that they do not want to meet the God who will raise them. The objection about dust and bones is a surface symptom. The deeper reality is *kufr* — covering over, denying, refusing — the encounter with their Lord.

That diagnosis is precise and important. If the objection were merely intellectual — "I cannot picture how this would work" — it could be answered with argument, and the Qur'an does offer argument elsewhere, pointing to the first creation, to the revival of dead earth by rain, to the ease with which God says "Be" and it is. But the present verse cuts deeper. People who ask "how can dust be raised" are usually not neutral inquirers who would believe if the mechanics were explained. They are people who do not want to stand before a God who will judge them, and the physical impossibility they cite is a convenient intellectual cover for a moral refusal. The diagnosis does not dismiss the question; it redirects the questioner's attention to his own heart.

**"Meeting with the Lord" as the Real Issue**

*Liqāʾ al-rabb*, "the meeting with the Lord," is one of the Qur'an's weighty phrases for the afterlife. To meet one's Lord is not to be absorbed or to vanish into the divine; it is to stand before the One who made you, who breathed His Spirit into you, who gave you hearing, sight, and heart, who recorded every deed, who knows the hidden and the open, and to be recompensed. That encounter is what the denier is really refusing, and his mockery of resurrection is a way of refusing it without having to say so directly. If he can persuade himself that death is the end — that the body is lost in the earth, that there is no reassembly — then he does not have to reckon with the meeting. He can behave as though his deeds will have no sequel, as though the Lord he has offended will never be encountered face to face.

The same diagnosis appears in other contexts: *They deny the meeting with their Lord* (6:31; 30:8). In each case the Qur'an is pushing past the intellectual formulation to the volitional core. Belief in the afterlife is never a purely theoretical matter. It shapes how you live, how you treat people, what you do in secret, what you prioritize. A person who has a settled interest in avoiding accountability will find reasons to disbelieve in the accounting; a person who is prepared to meet his Lord will find the signs — the first creation from clay, the breathing of the Spirit, the revival of dead earth after rain — entirely persuasive.

**Why the Answer Must Come in the Form It Does**

Verse 10 does not offer an extended proof of resurrection. It names the refusal instead. That is appropriate in the structure of the sūrah, because the very next verse will answer the practical question — not with a physics of resurrection but with a description of what happens at death: the Angel of Death takes the soul, and to God one returns. The mechanical question (how can dust be remade) is set aside for a moment in favor of the existential truth that the questioner has not yet died, and when he does, he will learn the answer in the most direct way possible. The objection raised in mockery will be answered not by argument but by experience. In the meantime, the objection itself becomes a sign. It reveals the state of the heart that produced it: a heart unwilling to meet its Lord.

People sometimes treat the Qur'anic response to resurrection skepticism as though it were evading a reasonable philosophical question, but the structure is more honest than that. The Qur'an does offer reasons for resurrection in other places — the first creation, the quickening of dead earth by rain, the sheer ease of the divine "Be" — but it always pairs those reasons with the diagnosis offered here. It does so because it recognizes that disbelief in the afterlife is almost never a purely intellectual problem solved by more evidence. It is a volitional problem, an orientation of the heart away from accountability. A person who wants to evade the meeting with his Lord will find a way to doubt whatever evidence is offered; a person prepared to meet Him will find in the signs of his own making more than enough to confirm the hope. The verse invites every reader to ask which of those two postures he occupies.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:11

> **Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, who is in charge of you. Then to your Lord you will ˹all˺ be returned.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Direct Answer to a Mocking Question**

The previous verse ended with a diagnosis: those who scoff at being raised from dust are really in denial about meeting their Lord. Verse 11 supplies the practical answer the scoffers are inviting. "Say," God instructs the Prophet — and the imperative *qul* marks a shift in register, from third-person description to a direct address the Prophet is to deliver to the questioners. What he is told to say is blunt and concrete. Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, the one placed in charge of you; afterward, to your Lord you will be returned. The response does not detour into a physics of how decomposed bodies get reconstructed. It meets the mockers at the one point in the process they cannot evade: they are going to die, and when they die something specific will happen to them — a designated angel will take their souls, and then they will be brought back to God. The rest of the eschatological sequence follows from that.

**The Angel of Death in Islamic Angelology**

In Islamic tradition the angel who discharges this office is commonly called ʿIzrāʾīl (Azrael), though that name does not occur in the Qur'an itself; the Qur'an refers to him by his function — *malak al-mawt*, "the Angel of Death" (here and in the only other Qur'anic use of the title, at 32:11's sister passage). Several reports indicate that he is assisted by other angels who attend the dying; the Qur'an describes these helpers in various contexts as the ones who draw out the souls of the righteous with gentleness and the souls of the wicked with severity (see 4:97; 8:50; 16:32; 6:61; 7:37). Al-Qurṭubī and others have noted that the Qur'an also attributes the taking of souls directly to God — *Allah takes the souls at the time of their death* (39:42) — and have reconciled the two ways of speaking by explaining that the Angel of Death seizes the soul as the appointed agent, the assisting angels minister to it, and God Himself is the one who brings about the death and receives the soul. The ascriptions operate at different levels. God is the primary cause; the angel is the appointed instrument.

The idea of an angel charged specifically with the termination of earthly life is not a piece of folklore the tradition grew around an obscure verse. It serves a theological function. Death is not random chaos; it is not a biological accident that catches God by surprise. It is an administered event, carried out by a created functionary under divine command, at a moment fixed in the divine knowledge. That fact strips death of its apparent arbitrariness. The hour is known, the agency is appointed, and the process is orderly. Human beings are not snatched arbitrarily by impersonal forces; they are taken, one by one, by an angel who has been given charge over them. The phrasing *alladhī wukkila bikum*, "who has been entrusted with you," even carries a faint overtone of stewardship, though his task is severe.

**"Then to Your Lord You Will Be Returned"**

The second half of the verse — *thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn*, "then to your Lord you will be returned" — is the real weight of the sentence. The taking of the soul is not a termination that ends the story. It is a transfer from one domain to another, and the destination is the Lord the scoffers had been refusing to meet. The passive verb *turjaʿūn*, "you will be returned," is used consistently across the Qur'an for the eschatological return; it carries the sense of being sent back to an origin, as something returns to its owner. The human being came from God — from the clay He shaped, from the Spirit He breathed — and back to God he comes at death.

This final clause is the direct answer to the objection voiced in v. 10. "When we are lost in the earth, will we be raised anew?" — "Here is what will happen: your souls will be taken, and you will be returned to your Lord." Resurrection is not a second creation ex nihilo tacked on to a natural order that has finished with you. It is the completion of a return that begins the moment the soul is taken. The body's decomposition in the earth is not an obstacle to the God who first fashioned it from clay and who has already taken the soul into His custody. Once the angel has done his work, the rest of the process is, from the divine side, straightforward.

**The Verse's Rhetorical Aim**

One might have expected the answer to the scoffers to be a proof, a miracle, a piece of evidence designed to overwhelm their doubt. Instead the Prophet is told to inform them of something they will experience personally and unavoidably. The rhetorical function is to shift the conversation from "how could this happen" to "it will happen, and here is the first step." A person who is genuinely persuaded that an appointed angel will take his soul and that he will then be returned to his Lord does not need a detailed physics of resurrection to take the next step — repentance, preparation, reorientation. The mockery rests on a pretense that death is far away or that the decomposition of the body closes the question; the verse collapses that pretense by describing the encounter that is waiting for them in terms so concrete they cannot evade the image. The Angel of Death is not a mythic figure; he is the one assigned to them, he will do his job, and afterward they will stand before the Lord they have been denying. The question "how" shrinks before the certainty of "when."

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:12

> **If only you could see the wicked hanging their heads ˹in shame˺ before their Lord, ˹crying:˺ “Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good. We truly have sure faith ˹now˺!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene the Reciter Is Invited to Behold**

*Law tarā*, "If only you could see" — the conditional opens a window onto the Hereafter and asks the Prophet, and every listener, to look: the guilty standing with heads bowed before their Lord. The phrase describing their posture — "hanging their heads" — captures the bodily stance of utter shame, the person who cannot lift his face to meet the gaze of the one he has wronged. This is the post-mortem condition of the very people who scoffed at the idea of return in v. 10 and were told in v. 11 of the certainty of the Angel of Death. Now they have died, now they have seen the realities they once derided, and their raised bodies assume the position appropriate to what they know. The conditional is a standard Qur'anic rhetorical device for marking how overwhelming a sight it would be for anyone still in this world. A single glimpse would settle every argument disbelievers keep reopening. It would drain the bravado out of every mocker.

**The Faculties Once Used to Reject Now Compel Admission**

Their cry is short and shattering: *"Our Lord! We have now seen and heard."* The two verbs take the reader back to v. 9, where hearing and sight were named among the gifts God breathed into human beings and for which so few give thanks. In worldly life they had used their hearing to block the warning, their sight to ignore the signs set before them; now those same faculties leave them nowhere to hide. Seeing and hearing are no longer avoidable, because the realities of judgment and resurrection they once denied stand in front of them without a veil. A structural pattern runs through the Qur'an on this point: in this life people refuse to see and hear; in the next life seeing and hearing are forced upon them, and acknowledgment comes too late to change their state.

They continue: *"Send us back and we will do good. We truly have sure faith now."* The plea is for return to the world — a second chance, a fresh opportunity to perform the righteous deeds they neglected, to believe now that belief is compelled by sight. It is the universal cry of those who realize too late that the world of trial is over and the world of recompense has begun. Similar pleas echo through the eschatological passages (6:27–28; 23:99–100; 35:37; 40:11–12). Every time it is recorded, the divine answer is refusal.

**Why No Second Probation Is Granted**

The reason for refusal is made plain elsewhere. Even were they sent back, they would revert to the very things they had been forbidden (6:28). Of the dying person making the same plea the Qur'an says, *Nay, these are words he speaks* (23:100) — words, not a transformed heart. The long life they already enjoyed was arena enough for anyone who would reflect, as 35:37 asks: *Did We not give you long life, enough for whosoever would reflect to reflect therein? And the warner came unto you.* A second chance after the veils have been pulled back is no real trial at all, because faith under the immediate sight of judgment is not the kind of faith the world was designed to elicit. Faith in this world is faith in the unseen; once the unseen becomes the seen, the test is over and the response is no longer free in the relevant sense.

A psychological depth repays attention. The person who has seen the angels, the throne, the Fire, and the Garden does not "choose" faith in the way a wayfarer chooses a path in this life; he is compelled by what is directly in front of him. That is why the request, however genuine it sounds in the moment of its utterance, cannot be granted. Granting it would undo the structure of responsibility that made earthly life meaningful. "We will do good" is sincere enough standing there with heads bowed — but it is a sincerity produced by terror and unmediated sight, not by a heart that freely oriented itself toward God before it had to.

A further nuance deserves mention. The plea to be sent back is a universal one; it arises from every throat in the Fire, not from a single speaker, and its uniformity tells its own story. In this world people imagine they would be different if only they had another chance, that they would learn the lesson at last and return righteous. The Qur'an's consistent witness is that they would not, because the self that would be sent back is the very self that chose rebellion across a lifetime; the same impulses, the same pride, the same habits of evasion would reassert themselves. Second chances in that sense are mythic. The chance is the life one is already living, and the day the doors close is the day the second chance is revealed to have been now all along.

**Two Ways to Read "We Are Certain"**

The final phrase, *innā mūqinūn*, "Truly we are certain," can be read two ways. On the first, the speakers genuinely believe now, with the certainty that comes from direct sight; the problem is not their sincerity in that moment but timing — certainty has arrived when it can no longer save. On the second, the phrase is colored by the same panic-driven self-exculpation other verses record from the damned — such as *"By God, our Lord, we were not idolaters"* (6:23), a claim they know to be false but utter in fright. The first reading fits the syntax and context more naturally, but awareness of the second possibility prevents romanticization of the damned's speeches. Panic, shame, self-deception, and genuine remorse can coexist in a single outburst. What the verse is most concerned to establish is not the sincerity of the speakers but the finality of the moment. The request for return is not granted, confession arrives too late to undo a lifetime of denial, and the next verse will state that finality in the form of a divine decree.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:13

> **Had We willed, We could have easily imposed guidance on every soul. But My Word will come to pass: I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together.****

**Expanded Commentary**

**Divine Power and Divine Choice**

After the scene of the guilty begging for a second chance in v. 12, this verse answers a question that naturally arises in the mind of anyone who feels the weight of that scene. If God knew they would reject the warning and end up begging for return, why did He not simply impose guidance upon them and spare them — and spare the Prophet the pain of watching them refuse? The answer is direct: *Wa-law shiʾnā la-ātaynā kulla nafsin hudāhā*, "Had We willed, We would have given every soul its guidance." That is, God has the power to compel every soul to faith, to stamp guidance onto every heart, to make rejection psychologically impossible. The verb *la-ātaynā* is strong: We would have given, We would have furnished, We would have bestowed. Universal compulsory guidance is not beyond divine capacity. It is a choice not taken, not a limitation on power.

The verse is therefore a foundational text for the Islamic understanding of human freedom, though it is framed in language that belongs to divine sovereignty rather than to a philosophical doctrine of free will. God is not unable to make everyone believe. He is able, and the fact that He does not is not an oversight; it is a decision consistent with a wisdom He has declared. This is the same point made in 10:99: *And had your Lord willed, all those who are on the earth would have believed all together. Would you compel people until they become believers?* The rhetorical question at the end of that verse is aimed at the Prophet himself in his grief over his people's rejection. You are not responsible for forcing them, because forcing them is not the purpose for which the world was made.

**The Word That Has Gone Forth**

*Wa-lākin ḥaqqa al-qawlu minnī la-amlaʾanna jahannama min al-jinnati wa-l-nāsi ajmaʿīn*, "But My Word will come to pass: I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and human beings all together." The "Word" that has gone forth is the decree that Hell will be filled from the two races created with moral responsibility — jinn and humankind — namely those of both who choose rebellion in the context of the freedom God has granted. The wording is fearsome and deliberately so. The verse does not soften the reality of Hell or treat it as a mere possibility that will probably be avoided. It is a decree, a *qawl*, a settled word from God. The same sentence appears elsewhere in similar form (11:119; 38:85), and each time it serves the same function: it closes off the notion that divine mercy will simply overlook rebellion at the end of history, or that Hell will turn out to be empty, or that warnings can be ignored because a loving God will not really punish anyone. The Word has gone forth, and it will be fulfilled.

**Why Hell Is Filled With Two Species**

The pairing of jinn and human beings is not arbitrary. Both are moral creatures endowed with choice, both received messengers (the Qur'an is explicit that messengers were sent to communities of jinn as well), both are capable of faith and rebellion, and both will be judged. Angels are not mentioned here because angels do not rebel against their Lord in the Islamic tradition; they have no share in the kind of freedom that makes Hell a possible outcome. The two species who *are* mentioned are precisely the two to whom warnings came in the world, and from whom rejection of those warnings is a real — though never necessary — possibility. That the decree mentions both together is a reminder that the human story is not the only moral story in the universe, and that human beings are not being singled out for severity. The law is consistent across all free creatures.

**The Verse Is an Address to the Prophet's Grief**

Several exegetes note that the verse is directed, in context, at the Prophet's distress over his people's obduracy. He would grieve when they rejected the message, as though the rejection were his failure or as though he should be able to compel them to accept. This verse tells him — and every messenger — that guidance is not a coin the messenger can force into people's hands. The messenger calls, delivers, warns, and leaves the outcome to God. Had God willed universal guidance, He could have produced it Himself without messengers; since He has not willed it, the messenger is not held accountable for the rejection of those who refuse, and he is not to break himself over their obduracy. That prophetic consolation remains an important element of the verse for every caller to God in every age. Your job is to deliver the message with clarity, not to manufacture a result that lies outside your control.

**The Nature of the Decree**

One must be careful not to misread the verse as teaching that God has arbitrarily selected certain souls, jinn and human, to fill Hell regardless of their conduct. The wording — "I will surely fill Hell with jinn and humans" — states the outcome in the language of divine determination, but the Qur'an everywhere ties that outcome to what the creatures themselves do and choose. Hell is filled with those who persist in disbelief and wrongdoing, not with a random selection foredoomed before they act. The divine decree rests on perfect knowledge of how creatures will use the freedom they were given. That is why the decree does not cancel human responsibility; it establishes it within a framework of divine sovereignty that the human mind cannot fully penetrate but is not asked to penetrate — only to take seriously enough to act.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:14

> **So taste ˹the punishment˺ for neglecting the meeting of this Day of yours. We ˹too˺ will certainly neglect you. And taste the torment of eternity for what you used to do!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence at the Gate of the Fire**

After the guilty bow their heads and beg to return (v. 12), and after the divine Word goes forth that Hell will be filled with the rebellious from jinn and humankind (v. 13), a sentence is now addressed to those entering: *"So taste the punishment for having forgotten the meeting with this your Day!"* Who speaks? Commentators offer two possibilities — God Himself, or the angels appointed keepers of Hell. Either way, the utterance is a judicial sentence pronounced at the moment of entry. Similar words are spoken to the damned in other passages (3:106; 6:30; 8:35; 46:34; cf. 7:39). The verb *dhūqū*, "taste," recurs across these contexts and carries two meanings: you will not hear about this by report but will experience it directly, and you will not be able to evade it as one cannot evade a taste on the tongue. Tasting is immediate and intimate; so is the punishment.

**The Crime Named Precisely**

The offense is identified carefully: *nisyānikum liqāʾa yawmikum hādhā*, "your forgetting the meeting with this your Day." The Day is the Day of Judgment, the very meeting with the Lord v. 10 identified as the reality disbelievers denied. "Forgetting" in the Qur'an is not a passive lapse of memory; it is the active moral posture of living as though the encounter were not coming, making choices as if earthly life were all there is, pushing the Day out of active awareness even when warners repeatedly name it. The demonstrative "this" gives the sentence its bite. The Day they now enter is the very Day they spent a lifetime pushing aside; it is their Day, appointed for them, whose possibility they mocked. The word points: here it is, the one you acted as though would never arrive.

**Forgetting Mirroring Forgetting**

The second clause — *"Verily We have forgotten you!"* — uses the language of forgetfulness back against the forgetful, and it must be read with the care every Qur'anic anthropomorphism requires. God does not literally forget, as v. 6 has already made clear by naming Him Knower of the unseen and the seen. The exegetical tradition is consistent. The "forgetting" means abandonment, exclusion from mercy, being left to the recompense rather than drawn out of it. Al-Bayḍāwī states it concisely: they are cast out of mercy or into a state of requital, since God does not suffer the creaturely defect of forgetfulness, as Ibn Kathīr also notes. The language is deliberate mirroring. You forgot the meeting with Me? You are "forgotten" — left to the consequences of the alienation you chose.

Mirrored language to this effect appears across a range of surahs (7:51; 9:67; 20:126; 59:19). One passage has God announcing on the Day that He is leaving wrongdoers to themselves precisely because they forgot the meeting of that Day, naming the Fire as their refuge, and denying them any helper. The repetition reinforces a consistent claim: separation from God in the Hereafter is no arbitrary imposition from outside; it is the full flowering of the separation the disbeliever chose and cultivated across worldly life. He wanted to live without remembering God; that desire is granted its full outcome.

**Everlasting Torment Tied to Deeds**

The third clause specifies both duration and ground: *wa-dhūqū ʿadhāba al-khuldi bimā kuntum taʿmalūn*, "and taste the punishment of eternity for what you used to do." *Khuld* means everlasting abiding, used in the Qur'an for both the Garden and the Fire — both are lasting, not temporary. The eternity of the punishment answers to the settled character of the conduct that earned it; it is not a temporary correctional term. It is tied to deeds — not to divine caprice. The judgment is just because it answers to what the agents themselves chose and performed across a lifetime. Those acts were theirs, and the harvest is theirs as well.

This clause rules out two opposite errors. It rules out the error of imagining God punishes for no reason or out of arbitrary anger, by tying the punishment directly to "what you used to do." It also rules out the opposite error of imagining the punishment is a temporary purification that eventually ends for everyone regardless of the state of the heart, by calling the torment everlasting. The Qur'an holds both justice and duration together without softening either.

**Remembering the Meeting That Was Forgotten**

Implicitly the verse points to the spiritual discipline that would have saved the condemned: remembering the meeting with God, inseparable from remembering death. Prophetic reports develop this connection. The Prophet is reported to have said that the intelligent person is the one who takes account of his soul and works for what follows death. Asked who among the believers shows the greatest intelligence, he answered: those who remember death most often and prepare best for what follows it are the intelligent. These sayings are preserved in Sunan al-Tirmidhī (2459), Sunan Ibn Mājah (4260), and Musnad Aḥmad with varying chains, graded ḥasan by scholars such as al-Arnāʾūṭ. Their point is not morbidity. To live with the Day in mind is not to cringe through one's days; it is to order choices around the reality that the forgotten Day will arrive, and that the only real preparation for it is a lifetime of righteous deeds done in remembrance of the One never absent. The contrast is with the "forgetting" the verse condemns: a life lived as though the world were the whole story, which on the last day turns out to be the story of self-chosen exclusion.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:15

> **The only ˹true˺ believers in Our revelation are those who—when it is recited to them—fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who "Our Signs" Refers To**

After describing the end of the rebellious in three grim verses (vv. 12–14), the sūrah turns to the opposite camp: *innamā yuʾminu bi-āyātinā alladhīna idhā dhukkirū bihā kharrū sujjadan*, "Truly they believe in Our signs who, when they are reminded of them, fall prostrate." The particle *innamā* is restrictive — only these are the real believers, as contrasted with the disbelievers just described. "Our signs" (*āyātinā*) has a broad reference. It includes the verses of the Qur'an, as al-Jalālayn and others note; it includes prophetic miracles; it includes the signs of God in the created world, the same signs the sūrah has been pointing to from v. 4 onward — the heavens and earth, the six-day creation, the formation of human beings from clay and a humble fluid, the sending of rain. Belief is not intellectual assent to a proposition; it is the pattern of response to these signs whenever they are called to the hearer's attention.

What marks the true believers is the response that follows when they are reminded of the signs. They do not argue, deflect, or scoff, as the disbelievers did in v. 10. They fall prostrate. The verb *kharra* — to fall down, to drop — suggests a bodily action that is not casual or formalistic. They go down in prostration, *sujjadan*, the forehead to the ground, the lowest physical posture a human being can adopt before God.

**Prostration as the Embodiment of Humility**

The physical posture is not incidental. In Islamic prayer, prostration (*sajdah*) is the moment of closest proximity between the servant and his Lord, and a well-known hadith states: "The closest that a servant is to his Lord is when he is prostrating" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 482). The believer whose heart is properly attuned to the signs does not merely register them mentally; his body assumes the posture that matches the recognition. He falls, he bows low, his forehead meets the earth from which he was formed — a physical enactment of the truth vv. 7–9 laid out, that his origin is clay and his life is from God. That is why arrogance is the opposite of this response, and why the verse concludes *wa-hum lā yastakbirūn*, "and they are not too proud." Pride (*istikbār*) is the root sin that drives rejection of the signs; prostration is its opposite, the outward form of humility.

**This Verse as a Prostration of Recitation**

This verse is one of fifteen places in the Qur'an after which the sunnah prescribes an actual prostration when one is reciting. The reciter who reaches this verse performs a *sajdat al-tilāwah*, a prostration of recitation. The tradition of doing so is grounded in reports from Companions who observed the Prophet prostrating at these points, and the practice is agreed upon across the schools. The placement of a prostration verse at this exact moment in the sūrah is structurally significant. The sūrah is called as-Sajdah, "The Prostration," and its name derives from this verse. Here, right after the terrifying picture of the guilty hanging their heads in shame and being told to taste the punishment, the sūrah depicts the alternative posture — the faithful falling prostrate of their own accord in this life, out of reverence rather than shame. The two prostrations stand contrasted: forced humiliation in the Hereafter versus willing humility in the present world. One saves; the other does not.

**"Glorify the Praises of Their Lord"**

Alongside the physical prostration comes the verbal dimension: *wa-sabbaḥū bi-ḥamdi rabbihim*, "and they glorify the praises of their Lord." *Tasbīḥ* — glorifying, declaring transcendence — is paired with *ḥamd*, praise. The prostrating servant declares that God is far above everything the arrogant associate with Him, and praises Him for what He is. The pairing is a guard against a misunderstanding of prostration. A person prostrating himself before God is not groveling before an arbitrary tyrant; he is glorifying a Lord who is also worthy of praise, the Merciful, the Perfecter of all things, the Knower of the unseen and the seen. That combination of transcendence and praise is the heart of Islamic worship. You do not bow before a being whose qualities would not deserve the bow; you bow before the one who made you beautifully from clay and breathed His Spirit into you, whose mercy encompasses all things, whose justice will set the world to rights.

**The Necessity of Arrogance Being Absent**

The concluding clause — "and they are not too proud" — is essential. Prostration without humility is a posture, not a state, and the verse refuses to count it as the response of real faith. The body can bow while the heart swells with self-regard, and that is not the prostration the Qur'an is describing here. In fact the Qur'an has harsh things to say about those who pray but whose hearts are hardened or whose deeds are for show; the body bent over in the right position is not by itself evidence that the signs have been received. The absence of pride is what distinguishes the prostration of the believer from the formal prostration of the hypocrite. The sūrah's next verse will go on to describe the fuller life that grows out of this posture: they shun their beds in the night, they call upon their Lord in fear and hope, they spend from what God has given them. The prostration of v. 15 is not the whole of faith; it is the gateway into the pattern of life v. 16 will unfold.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:16

> **They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bodies That Leave the Mattress in the Dark**

The opening Arabic has a physical vividness translations tend to flatten: *tatajāfā junūbuhum ʿan al-maḍājiʿ*. The verb *jafā* means to hold a thing at a distance, to keep away; *junūb* is "sides," the parts of the body that press against the mattress in sleep. The image is specific and bodily. Deep into the night, when rest pulls the body toward warmth and ease, these believers rise. Their sides detach from the sleeping places. The classical discussion preserves some difference over which devotions the verse describes. A few early reports associate it with Companions who continued in worship between the sunset prayer and the night prayer; the weight of the tradition, however, takes the reference to be the late-night vigil, *tahajjud*, the voluntary prayer the Prophet himself used to stand until his feet swelled. The image of sides leaving beds points naturally to the vigil, because it is during the hours of darkness that the claim of sleep is most insistent and the choice to rise is most clearly a choice.

Al-Maybudī, in his Persian devotional commentary, describes night wakefulness as the first mark of nearness and the proof of love's sincerity. The Beloved, he notes, is described by the Qur'an as one whom neither slumber overtakes nor sleep (2:255); the lover who rises in the dark conforms himself, as far as a creature can, to that attribute. A widely circulated prophetic saying describes the Prophet's own state in this connection: his eyes slept while his heart remained wakeful. The saying captures a spiritual reality the vigil aims at — not total sleeplessness, but a heart that does not entirely lose hold of its Lord even in rest.

**Awe and Longing in Equal Measure**

They call upon their Lord *khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan*, in fear and hope. The pairing is foundational in Muslim piety and appears across the Qur'an wherever the posture of true believers is described. *Khawf* is awe before the divine holiness, fear of punishment, fear of falling short, fear of being veiled — not the cringing terror of a subject before an arbitrary tyrant, but the reverential awareness of a servant who knows who he is approaching and how far his own deeds fall short. *Ṭamaʿ* is longing for mercy, hope of forgiveness, hope of encounter with God, hope of the reward the next verse will describe as hidden from all eyes. Neither posture is allowed to stand alone. Longing without awe slides into presumption that takes generosity for granted while persisting in wrongdoing; awe without longing collapses into the despair the Qur'an also condemns as a lack of trust in compassion. The balanced servant holds both in place, and the vigil is the workshop where that balance is forged.

Spiritual writers such as al-Qushayrī deepen the two postures beyond their obvious meanings. The fear, they note, is finally fear of being separated from the Beloved rather than fear of a created pain; the hope is hope of meeting Him face to face rather than hope only of created gardens. The psychology is realistic too. A person who rises in the dark to pray does not do so in steady emotional weather. Some nights the heart is alive with longing; some nights awe at one's own failings dominates; the steady practice holds both moods before God rather than waiting for one ideal feeling to arrive.

**Worship That Overflows Into Giving**

The third clause moves from the private nocturnal act to the public daytime one: *wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn*, they spend of what God has provided. The transition is effortless, and the coupling is Qur'anically deliberate. Across the Book, prayer and spending appear together as characteristic marks of the believer (e.g. 2:3; 8:3; 4:39; 35:29). Given that the prayer in question is the supererogatory vigil rather than an obligatory rite, the spending here is naturally read as voluntary charity (*ṣadaqah*) beyond the obligatory alms (*zakāh*). The worshippers described here are not content with the minimum required to escape censure. They give of their nights and they give of their possessions, because encounter with the signs described in v. 15 has produced in them an orientation toward God that overflows the bare duties.

The phrasing "from what We have provided them" is a guard against the self-congratulation generosity can tempt. The wealth they give away is not independently theirs; it is provision God gave them, and they are returning a portion of it. That perspective also removes the anxiety that giving will leave one poorer before the Provider. A memorable verse elsewhere puts the point with vividness: spend of what God has given you before the moment arrives when a soul, seeing death upon it, begs for just a little more time so it might give alms and be among the righteous (63:10). The night rising and the spending described here are precisely what the faithful do before that moment of regret arrives.

**A Whole Way of Living, Not Three Isolated Acts**

Taken together, the three descriptors trace a complete way of living rather than three discrete pious performances. The person who leaves his bed in the private dark is the same person who quietly gives of his substance in the daylight; vertical devotion and horizontal care for fellow creatures are not separated. A life of prayer that yields no practical generosity is suspect; generosity not rooted in remembrance can devolve into social display. The verse holds the two together as the natural fruit of the prostration described in v. 15, and the next verse will describe the reward God has hidden for those who live this way.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:17

> **No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Coolness of Eyes" — Delight Beyond Easy Description**

*Falā taʿlamu nafsun mā ukhfiya lahum min qurrati aʿyunin jazāʾan bimā kānū yaʿmalūn.* The key phrase *qurrat aʿyūn* translates literally as "coolness of eyes," an Arabic idiom for deep joy, settled contentment, the repose of an eye that no longer wanders restlessly because what it desired is present. In a desert environment coolness was a natural metaphor for relief — coolness after heat, cool water in thirst, the stilling of burning tears when the beloved appears. What is promised is not just an inventory of pleasures but the kind of deep satisfaction for which the English "delights" is only a faint approximation.

The verb paired with the phrase is *ukhfiya*, "has been kept hidden." The reward is not on display, not exhaustively previewed, not advertised in full in the text. It is stored away, concealed, kept by God Himself for those who earn it. The verse says no soul knows what it contains — "no soul" universally, including believers in this life and, as some commentators noted, even the angels. The substance of the reward is not a scaled-up version of earthly comfort but a reality of a different order, one human sensory equipment in this world is not fitted to perceive and the human heart cannot yet frame.

**A Prepared Reward Beyond All Created Comprehension**

The most widely cited prophetic report in connection with this verse has God announcing that He has readied for His righteous servants delights no eye has ever looked upon, no ear ever heard described, and no human heart ever imagined; the Prophet then recited this very verse to confirm it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3244; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah). The hadith lines up perfectly with the verse's logic. The Qur'an does describe the Garden in sensory language elsewhere — running water, abundant fruit, honored spouses, fine garments, raised couches — but those descriptions are accommodations to human language, signposts rather than exhaustive inventories. What they point to exceeds every word used to describe it, the way a road sign is not the city.

That is one of the most consoling truths the verse offers a striving servant. The effort — leaving the bed in the dark, giving away what God provided, enduring fatigue and mockery — is directed at a reward whose reality is larger than any picture the mind can form. The servant is not working for a known quantity but for something hidden, and the hiddenness is part of its dignity. It is not a commodity advertised in advance; it is a gift kept by God Himself, reserved for those who love Him enough to prefer Him over sleep and over the hoarding of wealth.

**Muʿādh ibn Jabal: Gates of Goodness**

A well-known report from the Companion Muʿādh ibn Jabal links vv. 16–17 to a foundational instruction on the "gates of goodness." Muʿādh relates that he asked the Prophet to tell him of a deed that would bring him into the Garden and keep him away from the Fire. The Prophet replied that he had asked about a great matter, yet it was easy for the one for whom God makes it easy; he listed the five pillars — worship none but God, perform the prayer, give zakāh, fast Ramadan, make Ḥajj. Then he asked, "Shall I not show you the gates of goodness? Fasting, which is a shield; charity, which extinguishes sin as water puts out fire; and the prayer of a person in the depth of night." He then recited the two verses (16–17) as confirmation (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2616, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; Musnad Aḥmad 22096). The pairing in the Prophet's own teaching is itself significant: v. 16 describes the gate — rising in prayer and giving — and v. 17 describes what lies beyond it, hidden, unimagined.

**Reward Connected to Deed, Yet Gift**

The closing phrase — *jazāʾan bimā kānū yaʿmalūn*, as recompense for what they used to do — keeps the reward connected to the life that earned it. The hidden delights are not a random handout detached from human effort; they are *jazāʾ*, a return on a particular shape of life. This is not to say the Garden is earned by arithmetic desert — the wider Qur'anic witness is clear that entry is finally by mercy, not strict accounting of deeds. But deeds are the path on which the reward is met, the evidence of the heart's orientation that fits a person for it, and the Qur'an consistently refuses to separate the final state from the life that precedes it. The reward, even while exceeding all proportion to the labor, answers to the labor.

**The Inward Dimension**

In the contemplative tradition represented by al-Tustarī, the "coolness of eyes" carries an inward dimension as well. The eyes of the faithful are cooled by the outward and inward realities they witness in the Garden, disclosed to them by unveiling; they behold them and hold to them, so that their eyes rest and their hearts find repose in them. That reading does not replace the plain eschatological sense; it adds to it by noting that the deepest delight of the Garden is the vision of God the Qur'an points to in verses like *Faces that Day will be radiant, looking upon their Lord* (75:22–23). The "coolness" is finally the stilling of every restless desire in the presence of the One for whom it was made.

The verse stands as a permanent rebuke to any attempt to describe the reward of the Garden in merely material terms, as though the next life were only an enhanced version of the comforts of this one. The Qur'an's sensory descriptions of paradise are real on their own terms — food, drink, companionship, beauty — but they are not the point. The point is nearness to God Himself, the vision of His face, the end of every distance between creature and Creator. Anything less would leave the deepest human desire unsatisfied, because that desire was built for God Himself.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:18

> **Is the one who is a believer equal ˹before Allah˺ to the one who is rebellious? They are not equal!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Engineered to Force One Answer**

*Afa-man kāna muʾminan ka-man kāna fāsiqan? Lā yastawūn* — "Is one who is a believer like one who is iniquitous? They are not equal." The question is rhetorical in the strict sense: it is structured so that only one answer is possible, and that answer is delivered immediately. The two are not equal. After three verses depicting the faithful (their prostration, their night prayer and spending, their hidden reward), and on the cusp of describing the Fire for the rebellious, the verse sharpens the contrast into a single question and a single negation. The weight of vv. 15–17 stands behind the negation, so that it does not sound like unsupported assertion but like the conclusion of everything that has been built.

The word *fāsiq*, rendered "rebellious" or "iniquitous," comes from a root meaning to emerge or exit — used for a date that slips out of its skin, a rodent that emerges from its burrow. A *fāsiq* is someone who has exited the orbit of obedience. In some contexts the word can refer to a believer who commits a major sin, but in this verse the direct contrast with *muʾmin* places it firmly among those whose fundamental stance toward God is rejection and rebellion. The context of vv. 10–14 — mockery of resurrection, denial of the meeting with the Lord, the shame and punishment of the guilty — confirms that the iniquitous here are those who disbelieve and live accordingly.

**A Family of Stark Qur'anic Contrasts**

This verse belongs to a family of passages that use sharp antitheses to assert the qualitative difference between two fundamental human stances. Elsewhere the disparity is likened to that between the blind and the seeing (11:24; 13:19; 35:19; 40:58), the living and the dead (35:22), the knowledgeable and the ignorant (39:9), or darkness and light, shadow and scorching heat, good and bad trees (3:162; 5:100; 38:28; 59:20; 68:5). The device is consistent. In a world that constantly pressures human beings to treat everything as a matter of degree — where a bit more observance and a bit less sit on a smooth sliding scale — the Qur'an insists that the fundamental orientation of the heart places people in qualitatively different categories. Believer and rebel are not two points on a single spectrum; they are heading toward two different destinations, and vv. 19–20 will spell those destinations out.

**Why the Verse Answers v. 12**

Read in sequence, vv. 18–20 form a unit that answers the plea of v. 12 — "send us back and we will do good." The three verses explain why that plea is hollow. The difference between the two camps is not a superficial difference of verbal confession that a moment of post-mortem panic can undo. It is a difference of identity, of direction, of the practices that shape the self. A person whose whole life has been shaped by rebellion — bowing to his own desires rather than to his Lord, spending his nights in heedlessness instead of prayer, spending his wealth on himself instead of in charity — is not the same kind of being as one whose life has been shaped by faith, and a moment of terrified acknowledgment in the Hereafter cannot dissolve that shaping.

The point pushes back against a common human tendency to imagine that faith is a matter of verbal formulae — say the words and you are "the same" as any other believer in the end. The Qur'an does not treat faith that way. Real faith produces prostration, night prayer, fear and hope, generosity; the absence of those fruits is not faith in the v. 15 sense, regardless of what the tongue professes. Conversely, rebellion is not merely intellectual disagreement; it is the pattern of life that flows out of refusal to meet one's Lord.

**The Verse Is a Hinge, Not a Treatise**

Verse 18 is deliberately brief. After the lush promise of v. 17 and before the two longer verses describing the two abodes, this short declaratory sentence acts as a hinge. It forces a binary choice without getting lost in gradations. It is natural to resist binaries; human reality is messy, and many people live in confused intermediate states. The Qur'an acknowledges those gradations in other contexts, but at decisive moments like this it sharpens the contrast because the Day of Judgment will itself draw a sharp line. A person may live in ambiguity in this world; he will not be ambiguously placed in the next. The verse is not a full treatise on moral psychology; it is a verdict stated in advance so that listeners in the ambiguous present can orient themselves.

The very brevity of the verse is part of its force. A reader arriving at this point after the sustained description of the believers in vv. 15–17 — their prostration, their nights of prayer, their spending, the reward no eye has seen — might almost expect the sūrah to linger over the contrast, to paint the rebellious side at equal length before naming their destination. Instead the verdict is given in a single sentence: they are not equal. The swiftness of the negation communicates something a longer treatment would obscure. The difference is not a marginal one requiring elaborate demonstration; it is a fact plain to anyone who has understood what precedes.

The verse also acts as a guard against a lazy universalism that would erase the difference between the two camps out of a sentimental hope that everyone will somehow end up in the same place. The Qur'an does not permit that evasion. The two camps are not equal in this world — they do not live the same way, they do not love the same things, they do not orient themselves toward the same end — and they will not be equal in the next. Kindness toward those who reject the truth does not require pretending the rejection does not matter.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:19

> **As for those who believe and do good, they will have the Gardens of ˹Eternal˺ Residence—as an accommodation for what they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Side of the Binary**

After the sharp verdict of v. 18 — the two camps are not equal — the text spells out the two destinations one at a time, beginning with the faithful. *Fa-ammā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti* — "As for those who believe and perform righteous deeds" — the classic *ammā* construction that fences off a category for description. Faith and righteous action are paired here as they are across the Qur'an, never separated. Belief that does not issue in deeds is not the belief of the people of the Garden; good works performed without faith do not qualify either. The pairing answers both an empty confessionalism that imagines words on the tongue alone will save, and a moralism that treats good deeds without reference to God as sufficient. The Garden belongs to those in whom both are joined.

**Gardens of Abode**

*Falahum jannātu al-maʾwā* — "theirs are the Gardens of Refuge," or Gardens of Abode. *Al-Maʾwā* literally means place of resort, dwelling, habitation, refuge. The word evokes settledness, homecoming, the end of wandering. Earthly life at its best is marked by transience — migration, exile, fear of expulsion, the unease of living at a distance from one's true home. In the Garden the believer is finally at rest. The Qur'an names the Garden with many attributes elsewhere — Gardens of Eden, Gardens beneath which rivers flow, Gardens of Bliss, the Abode of Peace — and "Gardens of Refuge" adds the note of unthreatened settlement. There is no more leaving, no more fear of being cast out, no more journeying.

The plural *jannāt*, "Gardens," is the regular Qur'anic usage rather than a singular Garden, and it suggests multiple ranks and abodes corresponding to the differing stations of those who enter. Not all who are saved are at the same degree of nearness to God; there are levels, and the gardens reflect that diversity. The plural also evokes lushness and variety rather than a single undifferentiated enclosure.

**Hospitality Prepared for an Arriving Guest**

*Nuzulan bimā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "as a welcome (or hospitality, lodging) for what they used to do." *Nuzul* is the welcome prepared for a guest — the lodging, the refreshment, the hospitality that waits when one reaches the host's door. The reward is described not as a wage mechanically dispensed but as a host's reception, prepared in advance for an expected arrival. God has been readying this welcome while the believers were still in the world, praying in the dark hours, giving quietly, turning to Him in fear and hope; on the day they arrive, it is waiting.

At the same time, the clause anchors the welcome to their deeds: "for what they used to do." This repeats the note struck in v. 17: the reward is not random, not arbitrary, not unrelated to the life lived beforehand. Yet the Qur'an is equally clear that admission to the Garden is ultimately by God's mercy rather than by strict arithmetic of earned merit. The two claims sit together in the classical understanding without contradiction. Deeds are the path and the evidence, not the commercial price that purchases Paradise; God in His generosity accepts the deeds He Himself enabled and multiplies their return beyond measure. The language of hospitality rather than wage payment preserves the gratuity without severing the reward from the life that led to it.

**What "Believe and Do Righteous Deeds" Encompasses**

The phrase *āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* is the Qur'an's most compact description of the saved life, and its very breadth is deliberate. It covers everything vv. 15–16 describe — prostration when the signs are recited, rising from sleep to pray, calling on God in fear and hope, spending of His provision — and also the wider fabric of righteous action: honesty in trade, fulfillment of trusts, care for parents and orphans, justice between adversaries, avoidance of major sins, restraint of the tongue, kindness to neighbors, all the outworkings of a heart aligned with God. The verse does not narrow salvation to one chosen subset of pious performances. The prayerful person who cheats in business does not fit the description; the generous person who refuses to bow before his Lord does not fit it either. Both halves of the description hold.

The plural "Gardens" and the language of hospitality also counter any impression that Paradise is a uniform reward passed out identically to all who qualify. The welcome God prepares is proportioned to the lives His guests have lived, and there are dwelling places suited to every degree. The contrast verse 20 will draw is sharp enough to make the point clear: for the rebellious, the Fire. The two verses together give the sūrah's answer to the question v. 18 posed. The answer is not only a verdict of inequality but a concrete depiction of two ends, arranged so that the sweetness of the first is felt all the more sharply against the terror of the second.

The verse's placement right after the stark negation of v. 18 is worth noting. After a verdict as blunt as "they are not equal," the reader might expect a long list of the believers' virtues before naming their reward; instead the text moves directly to the reward, confident that vv. 15–17 have already described the shape of those lives. The Gardens are described as a settled abode, a prepared welcome, a recompense. No further catalog of deeds is necessary because the sūrah has already catalogued them in the previous verses — prostration, night vigil, fear and hope, spending. The reward sits at the end of that path as its natural fruit, not as an arbitrary bonus attached to a contract.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:20

> **But as for those who are rebellious, the Fire will be their home. Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it, and will be told, “Taste the Fire’s torment, which you used to deny.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Side of the Mirror**

Verse 19 gave the destination of those who believe and do righteous deeds in the language of gardens, welcome, and hospitality. This verse gives the other side, using the *ammā* construction in parallel: *wa-ammā alladhīna fasaqū*, "And as for those who are iniquitous." The noun *fisq* was already treated in v. 18: the rebellious are those who have exited the orbit of obedience, whose lives are oriented away from God rather than toward Him. Their destination is *fa-maʾwāhumu al-nār*, "their refuge is the Fire." The deliberate use of *maʾwā* — the very word used in v. 19 for the believers' abode — is a rhetorical mirror. Both groups have a *maʾwā*, a final place of resort; the difference is what fills it. The believer sought his refuge in God; the rebel sought his refuge in self-will, power, and wealth. The final abode answers to the choice. The Fire is named as "home" in many English renderings — the place of dwelling, not a temporary stop.

**A Prison They Repeatedly Try to Leave**

*Kullamā arādū an yakhrujū minhā uʿīdū fīhā* — "Whenever they want to leave it, they are returned to it." The Arabic describes a repeated action: every impulse to exit is met with being driven back, time after time. The picture is not of a single failed attempt to storm the gates but of an ongoing fruitless struggle. Suffering the Fire's torment, its inmates surge outward to escape, and they are pressed back in. The image is not merely the picture of a sealed physical prison. It reveals something persistent about the damned themselves: even in the Fire they do not accept their condition, do not repent in any sense that alters their state, do not turn toward God. Their reflex is to flee the pain rather than to acknowledge the justice that ordained it. They cannot learn the lesson, which is why the attempt repeats.

That inability is a direct continuation of their posture in worldly life. In v. 12 they begged to be sent back to earth to do good works — a request whose refusal was grounded in the knowledge that even if returned they would revert to what they had been forbidden (6:28). Here they try to exit the Fire, and they are pressed back. The two scenes are the same impulse — escape the consequence without submitting to the One who assigned it. It is the same impulse that drove them to turn away from the signs when they were recited in the world, as v. 22 describes. The Fire does not change their orientation; it exposes it as what it always was.

The picture also makes clear that the damned are not, in the Hereafter, suddenly sanctified or contrite in a way that would make their punishment unjust. They remain the same rebellious selves they chose to be in this world, only stripped of the worldly comforts and distractions that let them imagine they were content. The impulse to escape without submission persists eternally; the Fire answers it with return, because the self that would escape has not changed. That is the deepest sense in which the punishment is just: it matches not only the deeds but the settled character those deeds produced.

The picture also clarifies that the damned are not, in the Hereafter, suddenly sanctified or contrite in a way that would make their punishment unjust.

**"Taste What You Denied"**

*Wa-qīla lahum dhūqū ʿadhāba al-nāri alladhī kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* — "and it is said to them, 'Taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny!'" The verb *dhūqū*, "taste," reprises v. 14; it indicates direct, unmediated experience. The rebuke is pointed: this is exactly what you spent your lives calling a lie. When messengers warned of a Fire for the rebellious, you dismissed the warning as fable, as ancient tales, as a manipulative threat. You now experience it for yourselves, and there is no further room for denial.

The irony is precise. Their denial was not based on evidence but on the desire not to meet a Lord who judges. Now the thing they rejected is immediate and inescapable; no further argument is required, because the experience itself answers every objection they used to make. The same scene is painted in 22:22: *Whenever they desire, in their anguish, to leave it, they shall be returned into it, and told, "Taste the punishment of the burning!"* The repetition across sūrahs confirms that it is not an isolated image but a stable element of the Qur'an's depiction of the Hereafter.

**The Unit Closes**

Verse 20 completes the unit opened by v. 18. The three verses together form a tightly organized block: a rhetorical question declaring inequality (18); a sentence for the righteous, expressed in the imagery of welcome and gardens (19); a sentence for the rebellious, expressed in imagery of confinement, return, and forced taste of what they denied (20). The contrast is drawn as sharply as language can draw it, and the block answers the plea of v. 12 without ambiguity. A second chance is not granted because the two kinds of people are fundamentally different kinds of beings, shaped by different loves and different practices, and the Hereafter simply renders that difference visible and permanent. The next verse will pull back from that final scene to describe how God deals with people in this world before they reach that end, sending lesser afflictions that may yet turn them back.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:21

> **We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment ˹in this life˺ before the major torment ˹of the Hereafter˺, so perhaps they will return ˹to the Right Path˺.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lesser Affliction as Mercy, Not Vengeance**

After the eschatological contrast of vv. 18–20, which places the two camps in their final abodes, v. 21 pulls back to speak of what God does in this world before the irreversible outcome. *Wa-la-nudhīqannahum mina al-ʿadhābi al-adnā dūna al-ʿadhābi al-akbar* — "And We will surely make them taste some of the lesser punishment, short of the greater punishment," *laʿallahum yarjiʿūn*, "so that they might return." The "We" is emphatically divine; the verb *nudhīqanna* is an intensified future ("We will certainly make them taste"), indicating a settled divine practice (*sunnah*), not an occasional event.

The surprise of the verse is the purpose clause. The lesser punishment is not applied as vengeance, not as a final sentence, but so that they might return. *Laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — that they might turn back, repent, reorient. Worldly affliction is presented here as a form of mercy. It is an early taste, a warning shot, a wake-up nudge, designed to shake people out of the slumber of heedlessness before the irreversible greater punishment arrives. A God who simply wanted to punish could skip the lesser and proceed straight to the greater; that He sends smaller trials at all is an act of mercy, giving the rebellious room and reason to reconsider.

**What Counts as "Lesser Punishment"**

Classical exegetes identify *al-ʿadhāb al-adnā* along several lines. Many take it to refer to worldly afflictions generally — military setbacks, illness, drought or flood, loss of wealth, personal trials, the various distresses that visit individuals and communities. Others include the *ḥudūd*, the legal punishments applied in this world for major sins, which serve both as deterrent and as expiation. A third group adds the punishment of the grave (*ʿadhāb al-qabr*), the trial and chastisement that take place between death and resurrection, before the greater punishment of Hell itself; on that reading even the intermediate state is "lesser" in comparison with the final penalty, and still a form of warning to those still living. Al-Jalālayn notes these possibilities together, stressing that the point of the lesser is to elicit reflection and return.

The variety of referents is theologically significant. Any hardship short of final judgment can function as the "lesser punishment" in the economy of this verse. A military reverse, a crop failure, a serious illness, the ache of an uneasy conscience — all of these can shake a person out of complacency. What makes them merciful rather than cruel is precisely the "so that they might return" attached. An affliction that leads to repentance is, sub specie aeternitatis, one of the greatest blessings a person can receive. Far better a painful nudge in this world than the unrelieved torment of the next.

**Hardship, Prosperity, and Reading the World Correctly**

The verse sits as a counterweight to a common misunderstanding — that worldly prosperity is a certificate of divine favor, and worldly hardship a sign of wrath. The Qur'an repeatedly warns against reading comfort as endorsement; verses on *istidrāj* (3:178; 6:44; 68:44–45) describe how God may extend worldly ease to people who persist in rejection, drawing them further out so that they imagine they are being blessed. Prosperity can be a trap. Conversely, hardship can be a form of care, a nudging back toward the right path while there is still time. The "minor torment" is the lesser of two mercies: better a painful taste that prompts return than the major torment that closes all doors.

**Return Is Return to the *Fiṭrah***

The possibility of "return" (*rujūʿ*) presupposes that what one returns to is already there. Commentators note that human beings are created on the *fiṭrah*, the innate disposition that recognizes God and inclines to truth; heedlessness and sin cover over that disposition but do not erase it. Affliction can strip away the layers of convention and self-indulgence and let the *fiṭrah* speak again. That is why trials, for all their pain, are regularly counted among God's favors in the tradition. Prophetic reports describe trials as coming most heavily to the prophets, then to those nearest them in station, then to others according to their degree — the testing is a sign of care, not of abandonment.

**The "Perhaps" That Marks Human Freedom**

The particle *laʿalla*, "perhaps," recurs here as it did in v. 3 ("that haply they may be guided"). The lesser punishment does not automatically produce repentance; it creates an occasion for it, and the response remains the hearer's. History is full of people whose sufferings did not soften them — who responded with resentment, defiance, or deeper self-will — and for those people the lesser became a prelude to the greater. The promise is that the opportunity is real, not that every soul will take it.

This is one of the most hopeful verses in a stern passage. God is not waiting, eager to condemn. He sends signals — small tastes of what is coming — so that people may turn back while turning back is possible. The whole economy of worldly trial is oriented toward that "perhaps." The sūrah will immediately go on to name the worst response to that mercy: being reminded of the signs and then deliberately turning away. The two verses together mark out the narrow path of mercy: afflictions are sent to wake you up, and the greatest of all wrongs is to be woken and then go back to sleep.

Readers of the Qur'an across the centuries have noted how often the divine pedagogy works this way — a small pain comes first, as medicine, to prevent a greater pain. A person who treats that small pain as a random misfortune to be resented misses its point entirely; a person who receives it as a signal and reorients his life has understood the reason it was sent. The distinction between punishment-as-vindictiveness and punishment-as-warning is precisely what the particle *laʿalla* marks. In the first case there is no "perhaps," only a sentence; in the second, there is a door left open.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:22

> **And who does more wrong than the one who is reminded of Allah’s revelations then turns away from them? We will surely inflict punishment upon the wicked.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Wrong Is Worse Than Turning Away After Being Reminded**

*Wa-man aẓlamu mimman dhukkira bi-āyāti rabbihi thumma aʿraḍa ʿanhā* — "And who does greater wrong than the one reminded of the signs of his Lord, then turns away from them?" The phrasing is a Qur'anic superlative: a construction used across the Book to mark the extreme instance of a category. Here the extreme of wrongdoing is assigned not to the person born in ignorance, not to the one who sins in a sudden burst of passion, but to the one who has been *reminded* — brought face to face with the signs — and then deliberately veers away. *Aʿraḍa* means to turn aside, to turn one's shoulder, to put something deliberately out of one's field of attention. It is an act of avoidance, not an innocent lapse. The person who has heard and understood and still turns away has exhausted the available mercy.

That same superlative structure shows up in Sūrat al-Kahf, where God describes those who have been reminded of His signs and then turn aside as people whose hearts are covered so they cannot understand and whose ears are deafened, such that they will never be guided even when called (18:57). The turn away from signs is not without consequence; it leads to a hardening in which further signs no longer penetrate. Rejection is not a neutral posture leaving the faculties untouched. It reshapes the heart over time, covering it so that understanding cannot enter, closing the ear so that hearing cannot reach. The deliberate act of turning away, once repeated, settles into a condition.

**Signs Do Not Compel a Heart Set Against Them**

The verse encodes a wider Qur'anic teaching: miracles and signs, in themselves, do not convince souls whose orientation is fixed against the truth. The person described has been reminded, has heard and been exposed to the signs, yet still turns aside. The point is made sharply in multiple passages. God turns away from His signs those who are arrogant on the earth without right; even were they to see every sign, they would not believe (7:146). Among the Prophet's listeners are those who hear, but coverings have been placed over their hearts and a deafness in their ears; every sign will not persuade them (6:25, echoed in 2:145 and 10:96–97). A heart hardened by arrogance can resist any amount of evidence, because the resistance is not intellectual but volitional. Signs are addressed to listeners still capable of attending; they do not operate as magic compulsion.

That is why the "reminded" person is the worst wrongdoer rather than the most excusable. He bears the greatest responsibility because he has been given the most light and turned from it. The person who has never heard bears a lesser burden, as the Qur'an indicates (17:15); the person who has heard and turned away bears the heaviest.

**Just Recompense**

*Innā min al-mujrimīna muntaqimūn* — "Truly We shall take vengeance upon the guilty." *Intiqām*, often rendered "vengeance," is the outworking of just desert upon those who persist in rejection and opposition after every avenue of mercy has been exhausted. The word has to be heard in its Qur'anic register rather than through modern connotations of uncontrolled private revenge. Divine *intiqām* is not passionate or arbitrary; it is the meting out of what persistent refusal has earned, and it is what remains when mercy has run its course — when lesser punishments (v. 21) have failed to bring about return, when signs have been recited and reminders issued, and still the person turns away.

Qatādah, the early Successor whose remarks are preserved in the major collections, says of the one who turns from remembrance of God that he is deluded by the greatest of delusions, reduced to the severest destitution, and oppressed by the worst of sins. The description is not rhetorical excess. To turn away from the remembrance of the One in whom all reality is grounded is to sever the relationship for which the human being was made, and the "vengeance" that follows is not God striking back in anger; it is the natural outworking of a life that has cut itself off from the source of mercy.

**A Warning on the Heels of Mercy**

Within the sūrah's structure the verse sits as a warning immediately after v. 21's hope-filled statement about lesser punishments being sent to elicit return. The two verses together describe a trajectory: God sends affliction to stir reflection, He sends signs as reminders, but the person who responds to both by turning away has no further excuse and no further opening. That trajectory is why the next verse turns to Mūsā and the Children of Israel as a historical example. The pattern is not new with the Quraysh; it is written across the history of peoples who received revelation before them. The Quraysh are being told, by implication, that they are being drawn onto a road whose end they can already see if they look at the ruins along their own caravan routes.

One should not read the verse as though it condemned honest doubt, sincere questioning, or a struggle to understand. The person who hears the signs and wrestles with them, who stumbles and asks and works through objections, is not the person described here. The person described here does not wrestle; he turns away. He has been reminded, and his response is deliberate avoidance. That is a difference of posture, not a difference of intellectual sophistication. A person can be highly learned and still turn away; a person can be unlettered and still bow in honest recognition before the signs. The distinction the verse draws is not between the clever and the dull but between those who will face what they have heard and those who will not.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:23

> **Indeed, We gave the Scripture to Moses—so let there be no doubt ˹O Prophet˺ that you ˹too˺ are receiving revelations—and We made it a guide for the Children of Israel.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Quraysh to Precedent**

After the warning of v. 22 against turning away from divine reminders, the sūrah shifts to Mūsā. *Wa-laqad ātaynā mūsā al-kitāb* — "And indeed We gave unto Moses the Book." The shift is less sudden than it appears. It answers the Quraysh's implicit posture by appeal to wider history. The Quraysh are not the first people to have received a book from God. A scripture was given to Mūsā, guidance was established for the Children of Israel, leaders were raised among them to carry it forward, and when they altered what they had been given or turned from it they were called to account. The history of the Children of Israel functions in the late Meccan material both as reassurance to the Prophet — he stands in a long line of book-bearing messengers, not an innovator — and as a caution to his audience: previous recipients who did not hold to what was given them became an object lesson. The reminder also answers the charge, raised implicitly earlier, that the Prophet had fabricated the message; scriptures before this one came in the same way, to other messengers, with the same purpose of guidance.

**The Clause That Has Long Puzzled Readers**

The middle of the verse is its most discussed line: *falā takun fī miryatin min liqāʾihi*, "so be not in doubt regarding the meeting with Him." The pronoun *-hi*, "Him," has been the subject of classical debate. The reading most consistent with Qur'anic usage takes it to refer to God, and *al-liqāʾ* (the meeting) to be the eschatological encounter with the Lord that v. 10 has already identified as the reality disbelievers deny. The phrase "meeting with God" or "meeting with one's Lord" is a standard term across the Qur'an for the encounter at death, on the Day of Judgment, or in the Hereafter (numerous instances include 2:4, 2:223; 6:31; 10:7, 11, 15; 18:110; 29:5; 30:8). On this reading, the verse says: We gave Mūsā the Book, so do not — O Prophet, or O reader — be in doubt about the meeting with God, since the community of Mūsā was summoned to the same encounter.

Other readings are reported from early exegetes. One takes "meeting with him" as referring to Muḥammad's encounter with Mūsā during the Night Journey (*al-isrāʾ wa-l-miʿrāj*), which would make the pronoun refer to Mūsā (i.e., "meeting with him"). Another takes "him" as the Prophet himself and the "meeting" as receiving revelation. Another understands the phrase as referring to Mūsā's own meeting with his Lord on the mountain. These alternative readings are grammatically possible because the word *liqāʾ* can refer to a meeting with a human being as well as with God. But as al-Zamakhsharī and others observed, the overall usage of the Qur'an tells heavily in favor of the first reading. "Meeting" (with a pronominal suffix referring to God) is never, in the Qur'an's settled vocabulary, used as a direct reference to receiving revelation.

**Why the First Reading Fits the Sūrah's Flow**

Reading *liqāʾihi* as the meeting with God ties the verse tightly to the sūrah's governing concern from v. 10 onward. The disbelievers deny the meeting; the guilty on the Day will hang their heads realizing the meeting is upon them; Hell is filled with those who denied the meeting; the righteous are hiddenly rewarded because they lived in awareness of the meeting; the lesser punishments are sent so that people may return before the meeting arrives. It would be strange, after all that weight, for the text at this point to make a parenthetical remark about the Prophet's encounter with Mūsā on the Night Journey, and it would be even stranger to express a command "do not be in doubt" about something whose factual basis was established by the Prophet's own experience. The natural reading is the one that reminds the Prophet (and his community) that the meeting with God is no novel doctrine introduced by the Qur'an; the earlier book, given to Mūsā, was itself a summons to the same encounter. The Qur'an is not inventing the Day of Judgment; it is continuing what earlier scriptures taught.

**A Guide for the Children of Israel**

The final clause — *wa-jaʿalnāhu hudan li-banī isrāʾīl*, "and We made it a guide for the Children of Israel" — names the function of Mūsā's book, the same function the Qur'an announced for itself in v. 3: that the Prophet might warn and guide. The Torah, too, is a guide leading a community toward truth, righteous conduct, and the meeting with God. The parallel to the Qur'an is intentional. The following verse will specify how that guidance was sustained across generations: leaders raised from within the community who guided by God's command so long as they held to patience and certainty. A book alone, without living bearers, is not enough; and when those living bearers corrupt what they have been given, the guidance is obscured.

Classical exegetes note a subsidiary grammatical point: the pronoun in "We made *it* a guide" can in principle refer to Mūsā himself ("We made *him* a guide"), and both readings are acceptable and complementary. The book guides, and the messenger guides through the book; the two are inseparable in the logic of prophetic guidance. The point extends beyond Mūsā to every prophet and, by extension, to every community of scholars who inherit a book. The book and the living guide stand or fall together; a book without living carriers is a dead letter, and living guides without a book are guides only to themselves.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:24

> **We raised from among them leaders, guiding by Our command, when they patiently endured and firmly believed in Our signs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guides Raised Inside the Community**

Verse 23 ended with the statement that the Book given to Mūsā was made a guide for the Children of Israel. Verse 24 describes how that guidance was transmitted across the generations: *Wa-jaʿalnā minhum aʾimmatan yahdūna bi-amrinā*, "And We appointed from among them leaders, guiding by Our command." The word *aʾimmah* (singular *imām*) means leaders, guides, exemplars — those whose example is followed. These were the bearers of the Mosaic tradition after Mūsā himself: the prophets sent among the Children of Israel, the scholars, the priests, the judges who carried the Torah and taught it. They were raised *from among them*, meaning they belonged to the community itself rather than being imported from outside. God does not leave a guided community without internal guides.

Their authority is described as *bi-amrinā*, "by Our command." They did not guide by personal charisma, tribal status, or their own opinions; they guided by God's command, on the basis of the book He had sent, with the authority He conferred. This is a standing limit on religious leadership. A guide who leads by his own preferences, who twists the teaching to serve his own interests or those of his class, is no longer guiding "by Our command" in the Qur'anic sense. The point is as applicable to Muslim scholars after the Prophet as it was to the rabbis and priests of Israel; the Qur'an records it as a permanent law of guidance, not a community-specific piece of history.

**Two Indispensable Qualities**

The leadership described was effective, the verse says, *lammā ṣabarū wa-kānū bi-āyātinā yūqinūn* — "when they patiently endured and had certainty of Our signs." Two qualities are named, and both are essential. *Ṣabr*, patience, covers patient obedience to God's commands, patient abstention from what He prohibited, patient endurance of hardship and persecution for the truth, and patient resistance to worldly seductions that would bend the guidance away from its source. *Yaqīn*, certainty, is firm, settled conviction regarding the signs of God — not tentative opinion, not inherited tradition held loosely, but unshakable confidence grounded in recognition of God's reality and promise.

The pairing is practical. Patience without certainty hardens into stubborn endurance of no clear purpose. Certainty without patience curdles into brittle dogmatism unable to bear with the weaknesses of the community or survive the slow seasons of trial. A guide who anchors his leadership in both has what he needs to sustain a community across time. Commentators note that the same pair runs through the Qur'an as the foundation of religious authority, and that one cannot be a legitimate guide of others without both.

**When the Two Conditions Fail**

The phrasing — "when they patiently endured and had certainty" — implies a conditional, not a permanent guarantee. The leaders were effective guides so long as they held to these two qualities; when they abandoned them, their leadership lost its ground. The Qur'an elsewhere charges later generations of the Children of Israel with altering parts of the scripture (4:46; 5:15, 41), preferring worldly status to faithfulness, and hardening their hearts. When that happened, the office was corrupted from the inside, and the guidance they were supposed to transmit was distorted. The lesson is permanent. Religious leadership is not a permanent caste privilege that holds good regardless of the leader's state. It is conditioned on patience and certainty; when those go, the guidance goes with them.

Several scholars draw the direct implication that no person is fit to lead a religious community unless he has attained these two qualities — patience with his own soul and with his people, and certainty regarding God's signs. Without them, the position corrupts both its holder and those who follow. That warning was not addressed to Israel alone. It is addressed to any community that receives revelation, including the Muslim community; the Qur'an preserves the story of Israel's leadership in part so that those who come after can recognize the same dangers in themselves.

The point also reaches beyond formal religious office. Parents, teachers, elders, anyone whose example is followed by others, is a kind of *imām*, and the same two conditions apply. Patience without certainty cannot sustain guidance over time; certainty without patience becomes harsh and repels. The combination is rare, which is why genuine guides are always fewer than people who seek the title.

The verse offers a sober standard by which to evaluate anyone who claims to lead in religion. Patience and certainty are visible qualities over time; a leader who lacks one or the other reveals the lack soon enough. The test is not the elegance of his rhetoric or the size of his following but whether he can sustain patient guidance while anchored in unshakable recognition of God's signs, across years of opposition and seasons of ease alike.

**A Slight Re-Phrasing at the End**

The Arabic allows the last phrase to be read as a separate sentence: "And they were certain of Our signs." The slight separation (rather than reading the phrase as strictly subordinated to the "when" clause) has the effect of giving *yaqīn* independent emphasis. Patience is one virtue; certainty is the ground from which patience grows. A person who is truly certain of God's signs — of His reality, His promise, His justice — will find patience natural. A person whose certainty is weak will find patience unbearably difficult and will eventually abandon the station. Certainty is the root; patience is the fruit.

This verse completes the short Mosaic interlude (vv. 23–24) before the sūrah moves to the Day of Judgment as the final resolution of all differences (v. 25), the ruined generations of Arabia as historical warning (v. 26), and the natural sign of rain reviving dead earth (v. 27). The interlude is compact, but its point is sharp: the Qur'an is not the first book; prior communities had guides, and those guides were effective only as long as patience and certainty held. The Quraysh who now hear a new book stand in the same pattern.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:25

> **Indeed, your Lord will decide between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Arbiter**

*Inna rabbaka yaqḍī baynahum yawma al-qiyāmati fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn* — "Truly thy Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that wherein they used to differ." The verse is brief and its meaning plain on its face, but its placement and scope matter. The pronoun "them" can be read specifically — referring to the Children of Israel and their internal disagreements after Mūsā — or generally, referring to all communities and parties who differ over matters of religion. Both readings fit the context and they converge on a single point: the final resolution of religious disagreement does not occur in this world, and the judge is God, not any human authority.

The verb *yaqḍī* means to judge, to decide, to separate one thing from another definitively. It shares its root with *qaḍāʾ*, the divine decree, and the word carries the sense of a conclusive separation that admits no further appeal. In this world communities disagree about scripture, about doctrine, about what God requires, about who stands in the right and who is in error. They argue, they condemn, they split into sects and parties, they sometimes persecute one another. The Qur'an does not pretend that such disagreements can be fully settled in the here and now through human effort. It promises a Day when every disputed matter is opened and decided by the one who knows the hidden as well as the open.

**Disputes Among the People of the Book**

Read specifically, the verse refers to the long history of dispute among the Children of Israel over the Torah and later prophets — disputes over interpretation, over what was licit and forbidden, over the claims of successive messengers, over the identity of the Messiah. The Qur'an elsewhere states that God will judge between them on the Day concerning those differences (2:113; 10:93; 16:124; 39:3; 45:17). Those disputes were not trivial; they involved salvation, identity, and obedience. Yet the Qur'an does not say that such disagreements can be settled by force or by human consensus in this life. The final verdict is God's, on a Day when all veils are lifted and every claim is tested against reality.

This is not an invitation to indifferentism — the Qur'an is clear that the truth has come and that guidance is available. But it is a rebuke to the arrogance of sects that imagine they have definitively settled every contested matter and are entitled to condemn all outside their own circle. Human beings are not the judges. God is, and His judgment is deferred to a Day when the truth of every position will be visible rather than argued over.

**Consolation for the Prophet**

The verse also consoles the Prophet. His Meccan community was small, opposed, mocked; the disagreement between him and the Quraysh leadership was bitter and personal; even within his following there would in time be disagreements. The verse reminds him — and every caller to truth after him — that his task is to deliver the message clearly, not to produce universal agreement in this world, and that the resolution of disputes is in God's hands rather than his. That consolation is needed by every believer who finds himself in a minority position, grieved by the divisions that mark religious life, or impatient with the persistence of error. The final settlement is not now, and the judge is not you. Your job is to hold to what is clear, live by it, and leave the verdict to God.

**A Patience That Flows From Recognition**

This teaching calls for a particular kind of patience. A believer who knows that God will be the judge on the final Day is less likely to become a persecutor, less likely to claim final certainty about every contested matter, less likely to treat every disagreement as a battle that must be won in this world by whatever means. He is also less likely to be broken by the fact that disagreement persists and error seems to prosper. The final score is not tallied in this life, and what looks like defeat for the truth in one generation may be vindicated in ways no one expected.

**A Bridge Passage**

Structurally the verse is a bridge closing the short Mosaic interlude (vv. 23–24) and opening the door to the next pair of signs (vv. 26–27). It closes the book on the Children of Israel by noting that their internal differences await God's judgment; then it directs the attention of the Quraysh back to the signs in front of them — the ruined dwellings of destroyed peoples along their own caravan routes, and the rain that quickens the dead earth every season. Those are signs for the Quraysh themselves, parallel to the book given to Mūsā; the implication is that if they do not heed them, the same God who will judge the differences of earlier communities will judge theirs as well — not in argument but in outcome. Verse 25 is short because its work is the work of transition and of sobriety. The story of previous communities ends not with a historical verdict but with the reminder that all verdicts belong to God.

That brevity should not mislead the reader into taking the verse as a filler. It functions the way a breath functions in a long recitation — a pause that signals a turn, a shift from the panorama of previous revelation to the signs present in the immediate landscape. The shortness is deliberate. The reader is not invited to linger in the story of Israel; he is invited to look at his own roads and his own fields and see the same patterns at work there.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:26

> **Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs. Will they not then listen?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sign That Lies Along Their Own Roads**

After the brief interlude on Mūsā and the Children of Israel (vv. 23–25), the sūrah returns to the Quraysh and to a sign that lies directly in front of them: *a-wa-lam yahdi lahum kam ahlaknā min qablihim min al-qurūni yamshūna fī masākinihim*, "Does it not guide them, how many generations We destroyed before them, amidst whose dwellings they walk?" The word *yahdī* here carries its usual sense — "is it not a guidance to them?" — though the rhetorical question functions as a rebuke. If the historical lesson of destroyed peoples is not a sufficient guide, nothing will be. The point is sharpened by the last clause: the ruins of those peoples are not distant hearsay; the Quraysh walk past them on their travels. They see them with their own eyes.

The reference is to the towns and settlements of earlier Arab peoples — ʿĀd, Thamūd, Midian, the people of Lot — whose ruined habitations were strung along the caravan routes the Quraysh traveled north to Syria and south to the Yemen. The Qur'an alludes to these ruins repeatedly (6:6; 7:4; 21:11; 22:45, 48; 47:13) as well as to the generations destroyed before the Arabs (17:17; 19:74; 20:128; 36:31; 38:3; 50:36). These are not archeological curiosities; they are standing warnings, places you pass in the ordinary course of trade. The stones themselves preach a sermon to anyone who will listen. A people who dwelt in these places with power, prosperity, and pride were destroyed for their rejection of their messengers, and their empty houses are signs for those who will reflect.

**"How Many Generations" — The Logic of Precedent**

The phrase *kam ahlaknā*, "how many We have destroyed," is not a boast about destructive power; it is an appeal to the evidential value of precedent. The destruction was not random. Each of those peoples received a warner, rejected him, persisted in arrogance and wrongdoing, and was removed. The pattern is consistent across generations. A reasonable person who walks through such ruins repeatedly should draw the inference: the same Lord who destroyed them can destroy us if we follow the same course. The fact that the Quraysh do not draw that inference is itself a sign of the hardening described in v. 22: when people are repeatedly reminded of the signs and turn away, their faculties no longer function as they should.

The Qur'an's appeal to ruins is a call to empirical reflection. It does not ask its audience to believe without evidence; it points to evidence that is in front of them, along their roads, in their own landscape. The prophet Hūd's people (ʿĀd) in the south of Arabia, Ṣāliḥ's people (Thamūd) at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ with their famous rock-cut dwellings, Lot's people by the Dead Sea — these were places Arabs knew from travel. The ruins were not a myth they had to take on faith; they were a sight. That is why the question is sharp: "Will they not listen?" (or, in the next verse, "Will they not see?"). The evidence is there for eyes and ears that work.

**Signs — For Whom?**

*Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin*, "Surely in that are signs." The word *āyāt* is the same word used for the verses of the Qur'an. Ruins are also signs, verses written in the landscape by God for those who can read them. Both kinds of signs — recited and visible — point to the same realities: the sovereignty of God, the duty of creatures to their Maker, the danger of rejection, the certainty of judgment. A person who will not attend to one kind of sign is unlikely to attend to the other, which is why the Qur'an regularly links the signs recited in revelation with the signs visible in nature and history. Each confirms the other.

The Arabic is careful to say "in that are signs" in the plural. A single ruin is not the whole lesson; it is the whole network of destroyed habitations across the peninsula — some in the north, some in the south, some along the eastern trade routes — that constitutes the sign. Each tells the same story in slightly different dialects of stone. A traveler who gives himself over to noticing them, not as picturesque background to his commercial journey but as speaking witnesses, will find that the pattern is impossible to miss. A reader of the Qur'an who sees the same pattern recounted in the text will find the ruins confirming the text and the text explaining the ruins.

The verse ends with *afalā yasmaʿūn*, "Will they not then listen?" The verb "listen" is chosen deliberately. Listening is the first faculty mentioned in v. 9, the faculty by which revelation enters; and the person who will not listen to the word recited is typically the person who does not hear the testimony of the ruins either. The ears are stopped at a deeper level than the physical. The next verse will shift the question from hearing to seeing, with a different sign — rain falling on barren earth and crops growing up where there was only dust. That sign is even more immediate, because it happens every year in their own land.

**Walking Among Ruins as a Spiritual Discipline**

In the traditional reflection of Muslim teachers, passing by ruins is not simply a moment of historical information; it is a spiritual exercise. To walk where a people once lived, built, traded, reveled in their power, and then were removed is to be reminded of the fragility of every worldly establishment. The ruins do not shout; they speak quietly, the way all memento mori speak. A people who forget how to read them have lost one of the clearest teachers the world offers. The Qur'an repeatedly invites its listeners to travel in the earth and see how those before them ended, and the invitation is not tourism; it is a call to let the landscape teach the lesson that words alone cannot drive home.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:27

> **Do they not see how We drive rain to parched land, producing ˹various˺ crops from which they and their cattle eat? Will they not then see?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Ruins of the Past to a Recurring Present Sign**

The previous verse pointed to ruins along the caravan routes — silent historical evidence of peoples destroyed for rejecting their messengers. This verse turns to a sign that recurs constantly, in the present, in front of the audience's eyes: *a-wa-lam yaraw annā nasūqu al-māʾa ilā al-arḍi al-juruz*, "Do they not see how We drive the water to the barren earth?" The verb *nasūqu* — "We drive" or "We herd" — is a vivid one, used for driving animals, caravans, clouds; it portrays the rain water as though it were driven along by a shepherd to its appointed place, the cracked and thirsty land. *Al-juruz* is barren, parched, dried-up land, land that has nothing growing on it. God leads the water there, and then *fa-nukhriju bihi zarʿan*, "We bring forth thereby crops," *taʾkulu minhu anʿāmuhum wa-anfusuhum*, "from which their cattle and they themselves eat."

The sign is deliberately ordinary. It is not a miracle produced on demand at the demand of skeptics; it is something that happens every season, every year, something the Quraysh saw all their lives. Yet precisely because it is ordinary it is easily overlooked. The Qur'an repeatedly draws attention to natural phenomena that are so familiar people stop seeing them — rain, growth, the alternation of night and day, the shaping of the human embryo — and insists that familiarity dulls the faculty of wonder. People look at the crop growing after rain and see "weather patterns" or "farming"; the Qur'an asks them to look again and see the same power that will raise the dead.

**The Answer to the Resurrection Objection Already Given in Nature**

The verse is carefully placed. Verse 10 recorded the disbelievers' mocking question: when we are lost in the earth as dust, shall we be created anew? Verse 27 gives the sign that answers that question directly, without argumentation. God drives water to dead land and brings forth crops from it. A piece of earth that was dry, brown, lifeless, apparently dead, is quickened into green growth by water He sends. If He can do that to the earth — repeatedly, every season — He can do it to human bodies that have gone back into the dust. The same verb *nukhrij*, "We bring forth," is regularly used for bringing forth the dead on the Day of Resurrection; the lexical overlap between the bringing forth of crops and the bringing forth of the dead is intentional.

The Qur'an makes the same point in several other places (for example 25:48–49; 30:19; 36:33; 50:9–11). Rain reviving the dead earth is the most accessible image of resurrection the natural world offers. It does not prove resurrection in the logical-deductive sense, because resurrection is an act of God's will rather than a necessity of nature; but it makes it intelligible. A person who has seen a desert bloom after rain should not find the idea of dust being raised again intellectually absurd.

**A Metaphor for Spiritual Revivification Too**

Beyond the literal point about agriculture and the eschatological point about resurrection, the image has a spiritual dimension regularly noted by commentators. Dead land serves in the Qur'an as a metonym for the dead heart. Rain is revelation, mercy, the remembrance of God that descends upon a heart hardened by sin and heedlessness; the crop that grows is faith, righteous deeds, wisdom, spiritual life. The same God who sends rain to barren land sends His signs to barren hearts, and the result, when the heart is receptive, is life. On this reading, the verse operates at three levels simultaneously: it is a reminder of physical provision (food for them and their cattle), a pointer toward resurrection, and an image of spiritual quickening.

**"They and Their Cattle"**

The clause "from which they and their cattle eat" is not a throwaway detail. It emphasizes God's comprehensive provision. He does not merely provide for human beings directly; He provides for the animals they depend on, the livestock that supply milk, meat, hides, transport, trade. The whole food chain is sustained by the same driven water. A thoughtful person considering the verse will realize how much of his life depends on a natural process he does not control — the formation of clouds, the driving of wind, the fall of rain on land that is often distant from where he lives, the growth of grass that feeds the flocks. The sign is both a testimony to power and a reminder of dependence.

**A Metaphor With Wide Reach**

The agricultural metaphor also captures a pattern that runs through the whole of spiritual life. The human heart is like arable land; revelation and remembrance descend like rain; deeds of obedience are the crop. A heart hardened by heedlessness is like parched earth upon which the rain falls without issue. A heart softened by remembrance receives the water and produces fruit, often more than the sower expects. The Qur'an draws this same parallel explicitly in other places, but the image operates here as well, beneath the surface of the literal verse.

**"Will They Not See?"**

The verse ends with *afalā yubṣirūn*, "Will they not then see?" — shifting from the question about listening in v. 26 to a question about seeing. The two verses together appeal to both faculties. They have been given ears to hear the recitation and eyes to see the signs in the landscape and the seasons; yet they do not hear and they do not see, not because the signs are hidden but because the heart that interprets what ear and eye present has been covered over. The next verse moves to a different kind of question: the disbelievers' challenge about when the Day of Decision will arrive.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:28

> **They ask ˹mockingly˺, “When is this ˹Day of final˺ Decision, if what you say is true?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Taunt Instead of Reflection**

*Wa-yaqūlūna matā hādhā al-fatḥu in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "And they say, 'When is this Decision, if what you say is true?'" The question is not genuine inquiry. It is the same kind of mockery that greeted the Prophet throughout the Meccan period. They have heard warnings of a Day when God will judge between the truthful and the deniers, when the messenger and his followers will be vindicated and the rejecters will face their deeds. Their response is to demand a date — impatiently, tauntingly, as though the lack of a specified schedule were itself proof the Day would never arrive.

The word rendered "Decision" is *fatḥ*, literally "opening," and by extension decisive arbitration, conquest, verdict. *Fatḥ* in Arabic is used for the conquest of a city, for a judge's ruling that opens a closed case, for a victory that resolves a conflict. The disbelievers are asking, in effect: when is this promised verdict between us and you going to be handed down? The word captures the essence of the Day of Judgment: it is the great *fatḥ*, the decisive opening that settles every denial, every ambiguity, every evasion.

**The Demand for a Date as a Defensive Move**

Requests for the timing of the Hour appear repeatedly in the Meccan material (e.g. 6:57–58; 10:48–49; 21:38; 27:71; 34:29; 36:48; 67:25). The disbelievers who ask when do not ask in order to prepare themselves; they ask in order to dismiss. If the Day cannot be scheduled on their timetable, they conclude it is not real. The Qur'an's consistent answer to such demands is that knowledge of the Hour belongs to God alone, and that when it arrives, profession of faith at that moment will not benefit those who spent their lives rejecting (v. 29 will state exactly that). The very form of the question reveals the questioner's stance. A person genuinely concerned about the outcome would prepare; a person who says "when?" in mockery is looking for a way to dismiss the warning, not a way to respond.

**"Fatḥ" as Victory and Verdict**

The double sense of *fatḥ* — victory and verdict — is worth holding together. In asking "when is this *fatḥ*?" the disbelievers are simultaneously asking "when will your promised victory happen, if you are telling the truth?" From their vantage point in Mecca, the Prophet was a man without an army, without political control, with a small band of followers opposed by the city's leadership. A "victory" for him looked, by ordinary measures, impossible. They use the word ironically, inviting him to produce the great decisive event he keeps speaking about. From the reader's vantage after Islamic history, a *fatḥ* did come — the Conquest of Mecca — within the Prophet's lifetime, and it unfolded in a way the mockers never anticipated. Yet the sūrah's primary reference is to the greater *fatḥ* of the Day of Judgment, of which any historical victory is but a foreshadow.

Commentators note that *fatḥ* carries the specific sense of God's decisive arbitration between believers and disbelievers, as in 34:26: *Say, "Our Lord will gather us together, then He will decide between us with truth; He is the Arbiter, the Knower."* The Day is a *fatḥ* in that sense too: a judge's opening of a case long closed by human obduracy, the rendering of a verdict from which no appeal can be taken.

**Position in the Sūrah's Final Movement**

The question in v. 28 sets up the sūrah's final answer, which comes in v. 29. Over the preceding verses the disbelievers have been given sign after sign: the creation of the heavens and earth, the forming of human beings from dust, the Angel of Death, the reality of judgment, the two abodes, the lesser punishments meant to call them back, the example of Mūsā, the ruined habitations of bygone peoples along their trade routes, the rain that revives parched earth. None of it has moved them, and they reduce the entire argument to a taunting demand: when? The answer will not be a date. It will be a description of what the Day looks like for them when it arrives — a day on which their belief is too late and no respite is given. That is the answer they need to hear, not a calendar entry. A date would only encourage delay; a description of what the Day costs them is the only form of answer that serves the purpose of warning.

The question's persistence across the years of revelation tells us something about the psychology of denial. Demand for a date is never really a request for information; it is a stalling tactic that pretends to be inquiry. "If you only told me *when*," the questioner implies, "I would believe — but since you cannot, I am justified in continuing as I am." The entire pretense unravels when one reflects that the same person, given a date, would simply postpone preparation until the eve of it, and would probably doubt the date when it was announced anyway. The stalling has no end; that is why the Qur'an refuses to play that game. It does not give dates. It gives descriptions of reality, and leaves the reader to prepare.

People who ask "when?" in this mocking way are also curiously inconsistent about uncertainty in other domains. Every sane adult knows that his own death is certain and its timing unknown, that it may arrive at any moment, and that reasonable preparation is plain prudence. Those same people who would never dream of mocking the uncertainty of their own deaths will mock the uncertainty of the Day as though it were evidence of unreality. The double standard is revealing. One unknown is treated as the most serious fact of life; the other unknown is treated as a joke. The difference lies in desire — death is universally acknowledged, but judgment carries moral weight one would prefer to evade.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:29

> **Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “On the Day of Decision it will not benefit the disbelievers to believe then, nor will they be delayed ˹from punishment˺.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Taunt**

Verse 28 recorded the disbelievers' mocking question: when will this Decision arrive, if what you say is true? Verse 29 is the answer. *Qul yawma al-fatḥi lā yanfaʿu alladhīna kafarū īmānuhum wa-lā hum yunẓarūn*, "Say, 'On the Day of Decision, faith will not benefit those who disbelieved; nor shall they be granted respite.'" The answer is not a date. The Qur'an never gives the date of the Hour — knowledge of it is with God alone. What it gives instead is a description of what happens on that Day to the people now demanding a date. Their faith, offered when the Day has arrived, will not avail them; and they will not be given extra time.

The phrasing *yawma al-fatḥ* — "the Day of Decision" (or "Day of Opening") — picks up the word *fatḥ* from their own question and turns it against them. They asked "when is this *fatḥ*?" The answer tells them what the *fatḥ* will actually be for them: a day when belief comes too late. The same word they used to taunt the Prophet becomes the name of their own condemnation. The "decision" they mockingly demanded is not going to be the spectacle they imagine — a courtroom scene to gawk at from a safe distance — but the Day itself, and when it arrives their situation is already fixed.

**Belief That Comes Too Late**

*Lā yanfaʿu alladhīna kafarū īmānuhum* — "their faith will not benefit those who disbelieved." The verb tense captures the point precisely: they are *alladhīna kafarū*, "those who disbelieved" — a description of their settled state in this life — and the *īmān* they offer on the Day is of no benefit to them. This is the same point made by v. 12 in connection with the guilty bowing their heads and pleading "we have seen and we have heard, we are certain." Faith that arises under the immediate pressure of seeing the realities one spent a lifetime denying is not the faith that saves, because the world of trial is over. The world was designed for faith in the unseen; once the unseen becomes the seen, the nature of the response changes. A person who sees the angels and the throne and the fire is not exercising the kind of faith the world was made to test; he is simply acknowledging what he can no longer deny.

The point is underlined elsewhere in the Qur'an: *On the day when some of the signs of thy Lord come, no soul will benefit from its belief if it had not believed before* (6:158). The door is open in this life; on the Day it is closed, and affirmations made in that moment — however sincere they may be in the panic of the visible — do not change the outcome.

**No Respite**

*Wa-lā hum yunẓarūn*, "nor shall they be granted respite." *Inẓār* is to be given time, to be granted a delay, to be allowed to wait and make amends later. On the Day no such delay is granted. There is no period of negotiation, no second probation, no opportunity to return to the world and do better (as they pleaded in v. 12). The respite they enjoyed in this world — the long years of life in which they could have heeded the warnings — is precisely what they wasted; on the Day there is no more. The word contrasts pointedly with the patience of God in this world, who delays punishment in order to give room for repentance (the very point of v. 21's "lesser punishment"). The delay belongs to the time of trial; once the Day begins, delay is over.

**Why No Date Is Given**

The answer deliberately refuses to give the date the mockers asked for. The refusal is a mercy. If people knew the exact date, many would postpone repentance until the eve of the Day and live heedlessly until then — a strategy the Qur'an treats as spiritually empty. Not knowing the time is part of the structure of responsibility. Every moment could be the last; death could arrive before the Hour; the Night of Decree comes unannounced; the decisive event arrives in an hour you do not expect. Readiness is the right posture, not date-calculation.

The same principle holds for smaller-scale fulfillments — military victories, turns of fortune, times of trial. The early community did not know when Mecca would be conquered, when persecution would end, when Islam would spread; what they knew was that those who disbelieved would not benefit from last-minute belief when the decisive moment arrived, and that no respite would be granted beyond the term God had set. The answer is therefore not only eschatological but practical: live now in a state of readiness, because the Day that settles every claim will arrive without warning, and on that Day no one gets extra time.

**"Nor Shall They Be Granted Respite"**

The final clause — *wa-lā hum yunẓarūn*, "nor shall they be granted respite" — reiterates a point made already in connection with v. 12. *Inẓār* is delay, being granted time, being allowed to make amends. On the Day there is no delay, no period of negotiation, no return to the world to do better. The respite they enjoyed in this life — the long years in which they could have heeded the warnings — was precisely what they wasted. The mercy of delay belongs to the world of trial; once that world closes, delay ends.

**Setting Up the Final Verse**

Verse 29 answers the taunt and closes off one line of argument. The final verse, v. 30, will give the Prophet his posture after the answer is delivered: turn away from them and wait. The sūrah ends, as it began, with the authority on God's side and the Prophet instructed not to chase after his people's acceptance.

## Sūrah as-Sajdah 32:30

> **So turn away from them, and wait! They too are waiting.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Release From Burden**

*Fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum wa-intaẓir innahum muntaẓirūn* — "So turn away from them, and wait; they too are waiting." After twenty-nine verses that have established the Qur'an's origin with the Lord of the worlds, described the Creator's work in the heavens and the earth and in the shaping of human beings from clay and a humble fluid, declared the role of the Angel of Death and the certainty of return, portrayed the guilty bowing their heads and begging for a second chance, pronounced the decree that Hell will be filled, promised hidden delights to those who prostrate and pray and give, drawn the sharp contrast between believers and the rebellious, described lesser afflictions sent as mercy to prompt return, pointed to Mūsā and the Children of Israel as historical precedent, indicated the ruins along the caravan routes and the rain that revives dead land as visible signs, and answered the mockers' taunt about timing — after all of that, the Prophet is given his final instruction: turn away, and wait.

*Aʿriḍ* — turn away, withdraw, do not press further in grief-stricken expectation of their acceptance. The command is not to stop delivering the message, and it is not to break off social relations entirely; it is to cease the anxious inner striving that expects every hearer to accept, to give up the burden of producing an outcome that was never his to produce. He has done what he was sent to do — deliver the warning with clarity — and the rest belongs with God. The command is a form of release.

**Two Sides Waiting, Not Waiting the Same Thing**

*Wa-intaẓir innahum muntaẓirūn* — "and wait; they too are waiting." Both sides wait, but what they wait for is not the same. The believers wait for God's vindication: the help He has promised, the Day of Decision, the triumph of the truth in His time, whether that triumph takes the form of a historical opening in this world or the final verdict of the Hereafter. Their waiting is the waiting of *ṣabr*, the patience v. 24 identified as one of the two marks of true leadership, anchored in certainty. The disbelievers also wait — but their waiting is the waiting of mockers who expect the Prophet's cause to collapse, who look for his death or defeat, who assume that the future will continue as it has always continued and that no Day will arrive. Al-Jalālayn notes the irony: they imagine they are waiting for the believers to be undone, but they are in fact waiting for their own undoing, and they do not know what it is they are waiting for.

The same ironic juxtaposition appears at several other Qur'anic passages (6:158; 7:71; 9:52; 10:20, 102; 11:122; 20:135; 52:31). The pattern is consistent. The Prophet is told to wait patiently and his opponents are told they too are waiting — as if the entire conflict between truth and falsehood resolves into a contest of patience, each side awaiting an outcome whose shape only God determines. The believers wait in trust; the opponents wait in arrogance; the resolution belongs to God.

**The Calm of the Ending**

There is no thunderous closing threat, no final imprecation, no apocalyptic spectacle at the end of the sūrah. The final verse is remarkably quiet: turn away and wait. That calm is itself a form of rhetorical authority. A speaker who ends on a shouted threat reveals anxiety about the outcome; a speaker who ends on a calm instruction to wait reveals that the outcome is already decided and does not need to be forced by heightened rhetoric. The tone matches the sūrah's opening, which began with three untransliterated letters stated without defense or argument. Authority does not need to shout. It speaks, delivers its signs, answers objections, and then waits while events unfold.

The ending also mirrors the sūrah's central theological point. Human beings are not in control of the outcome. The God who directs every affair from the heaven to the earth, who breathes the Spirit into clay, who appoints the Angel of Death, who keeps hidden delights for His servants and fills Hell with the rebellious — that God does not require His messenger to win arguments or coerce assent. The messenger is asked to deliver, to be patient, to wait. The opponents are waiting too, though for what they do not understand.

**The Sūrah as a Single Arc**

The arc of the sūrah, from the three opening letters to the final instruction to wait, traces a complete journey in thirty verses. It begins with the unapologetic announcement of the Book's origin; it establishes who God is by describing what He does (creates, governs, knows, perfects, gives life and takes it); it creates the human being twice (clay and humble fluid, then breath and faculties); it faces the mocking question about resurrection with concrete description of death, the Day, the two abodes; it offers mercy through lesser trials; it appeals to history (Mūsā and the Children of Israel, ruined generations) and to nature (rain reviving dead earth); it answers the taunt about timing by describing the Day on which late belief is useless; and it ends where faithful service always ends — in patient waiting upon the Lord who controls the outcome.

The reader who has been brought through the whole argument is left with the same posture the Prophet is told to adopt: turn away from what distracts, hold to what has been given, wait in patience, for your Lord is also waiting, and the Decision belongs to Him.

**A Word for Every Reader After the Prophet**

The instruction is not addressed to the Prophet alone. Every generation of believers lives in the tension this verse names. The truth has been delivered; the signs are present; the opponents wait for the cause to fail; the cause does not appear to triumph on the timetable its adherents expect. The temptation at such moments is either to grow angry and force outcomes that are not in one's hands, or to grow despondent and abandon the path. The sūrah closes by naming neither anger nor despair but patient waiting as the proper posture. Waiting does not mean passivity — the prostration of v. 15, the night vigil of v. 16, the spending of v. 16, the mindfulness called for in v. 4, are all active stances — but it means refusing to take the outcome onto one's own shoulders. Deliver, pray, give, do justice, and wait. The rest belongs to God who directs every affair from heaven to earth.
