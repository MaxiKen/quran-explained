# Sūrah al-Kāfirūn (Chapter 109) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah al-Kāfirūn 109:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O you disbelievers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Opens the Quotation**

The sūrah begins with a command: "Say." The form is the form of the sūrahs that carry the word qul at their opening, and the command means that the words that follow are not the words of the one addressed; they are the words he is ordered to speak. The sūrah is presented in the form of a quotation that begins with the command, and the quotation runs to the end of the sūrah, and the entire sūrah is the content of the command. The first word is therefore the frame, and the frame is the claim: the separation that the sūrah states is not the position of a man; it is the word of the Lord, delivered through the man, in the form of the command. The command also withholds the argument. The qul sūrahs give the Prophet words, not reasons, and the reason is the form: a separation that is delivered as a recitation does not need the negotiation that a position would require. The words are the act, and the act is the recitation, and the recitation stands where the negotiation would have sat.

**The Proposal the Sūrah Answers**

The occasion of the sūrah is the proposal the tradition records: the leaders of Makkah came to the Prophet with the offer of the exchange. Worship what we worship, and we will worship what you worship; let the two parties share, and if the one is better, the other will take its share. The proposal was the offer of the compromise in the religion, and the sūrah is the answer, and the answer is the most complete refusal the Book contains in the shortest form. The first verse is the opening of the refusal: the address to the disbelievers, and the address is the beginning of the separation. The sūrah does not argue against the proposal in its verses; it performs the rejection, and the performance is the five verses of negation that follow the address. The offer was presented in the form of a bargain, and the form was the trap. A compromise stated as a commercial deal, with shares to be taken by the better party, makes the religion a commodity, and the commodity is what the sūrah refuses. The five verses of negation are therefore not a refusal of the terms; they are a refusal of the frame in which the terms were offered, and the frame is the deal.

**The Sūrah Named for Its Audience**

The sūrah takes its name from the people it addresses, and the naming is unusual among the short sūrahs. Most of the short sūrahs are named for a subject or an event, the elephant, the mocker, the small kindness; this one is named for the addressee, the disbelievers, and the naming is the statement of the sūrah's purpose: the separation. A sūrah that exists to draw the line takes its name from the one on the other side of the line, and the naming is the first word of the division. The sūrah is the address, and the address is named for the one addressed, and the one addressed is named by the state: the disbelief. The name of the sūrah is therefore the label of the audience, and the label is the claim that the sūrah will spend six verses performing.

**The Recitation in the Sanctuary**

The account the tradition preserves places the sūrah in a scene: after the revelation, the Prophet went to the sanctuary in the time when it was full of the people, and he recited the sūrah to them, and from that recitation the people of Makkah despaired of turning him from his religion. The scene is the occasion of the sūrah's public life: the address that was spoken to one group in the privacy of the proposal was spoken to the whole city in the open of the sanctuary, and the speaking was the end of the negotiation. The sūrah's first verse, the address, is therefore the opening of a public act, and the act is the recitation, and the recitation is the fact that the separation was made known to all. The opening address was made in the place where the whole city could hear it.

**The Short Sūrah That Carried the Division**

The sūrah is six verses, and the weight of the six verses is the division of the two ways. The proposal offered the mixing, and the sūrah states the separation, and the separation is stated in five verses of the negation and one verse of the division. The shortness of the sūrah is the form of the claim: the division is not a long negotiation, but a clean line, and the line is drawn in six verses. The opening of the line is the address to the one on the other side, and the address is the command to speak, and the command is the word of the Lord. The sūrah opens on the command, and the opening is the claim: the separation is ordered, and it is spoken, and it is the word of the one who ordered it.

**The Quarter of the Book**

The reports the tradition received give the sūrah a weight that exceeds its length. Some of the reports, received in the Ṣunan of al-Tirmidhī (2893), carry the statement that the sūrah is a quarter of the Book, and the scholars noted that the chains of those reports are not sound, while the parallel statement about the sūrah of the Sincerity, that it is a third of the Book, stands in the sound collections. The weight of the reports, sound or not, is the one that matches the content: a sūrah that states the separation of the two ways carries, in the judgment of the tradition, a share of the Book that exceeds its measure in words. The address opens that weight, and the opening is the beginning of the statement the reports weigh.

## Sūrah al-Kāfirūn 109:2

> I do not worship what you worship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Word of the Separation**

The recitation the sūrah commands begins with a negative, and the choice is the first statement of the sūrah. Before the Prophet is told what to affirm, he is told what to deny, and the denial is placed at the head of the address. The structure of the refusal is therefore fixed from the opening: the line is drawn by stating what the speaker is not, and the identity is declared through the negation. The "I" stands at the beginning of the verse, before any name of the object, and the position is the method: the person is separated first, and the object is named only in the form of what is refused. The sūrah does not open with the theology of the one God; it opens with the refusal of the other, and the refusal is the ground on which the theology stands. The order of the sūrah places the negation before the affirmation, and the order is the method. The six verses never state, in the form of the affirmation, what the one who speaks worships: the object of his worship is not named, and the not-naming is the confidence of the sūrah. The one God is the shared inheritance of the city, and no listener disputes the object of the Prophet's worship; what is disputed is the standing of the two lines, and the standing is settled by the refusal, not by the description. The sūrah therefore spends its verses on the refusal, and the description is left to the audience, because the description is the one thing the audience already has.

**The Objects Left Without a Name**

The verse does not name what the Makkans worship, and the silence is part of the separation. The idols of the sanctuary are known to every listener, and the sūrah could have named them; it does not. To name an object of worship is to admit it into the category of worship, and the category is what the sūrah refuses. The phrase "what you worship" is left indefinite, and the indefiniteness is the contempt: whatever the objects are, in whatever number, under whatever names, they are held at a distance by the grammar, and the distance is the verdict. The verse treats the whole system of the sanctuary's images as one undifferentiated thing, and the refusal of the thing does not require a list of its parts. The relative phrase does a second work that a name could not do. A name is a point, and a point can be missed; the description is the whole, and the whole cannot be missed. The verse describes the worship of the Makkans in the form of whatever it is, and the description holds the objects in whatever number and under whatever names, and the holding is the total of the refusal: nothing in the sanctuary escapes the phrase, because the phrase is not the list but the category, and the category is what is denied.

**The Present Fixed as the First Coordinate**

The form of the negation is the present: "I do not worship," and the Arabic is the simple present negative, the statement of the state as it stands now. The present is fixed as the first coordinate of the figure the sūrah will build, and the remaining verses will extend the figure into the future. The grammar of the four verses of negation is the structure of the argument: a present pair and a future pair, each pair crossing the two lines, and this verse is the first point of the figure. The present is stated first, because the present is the point at which the proposal was made: the Makkans had offered the exchange for the time to come, and the sūrah answers first with the now, and then, in the verses that follow, with the future that the proposal had tried to buy.

**The Object Denied, Not the Act**

The verse does not say "I do not worship as you worship"; it says "I do not worship what you worship," and the negation attaches to the object and not to the manner. The worship itself, the act of bowing, the act of reciting, the act of standing, is not in dispute; what is in dispute is the direction of the act. The sūrah presupposes the reality of worship, and it disputes the object of the worship, and the dispute is the one that cannot be settled by the offer of the exchange. A difference of manner can be traded; a difference of object cannot, because the object is the end, and the end is not a commodity. The verse places the whole of the separation in the object, and the object is the thing that the following verses will refuse in every tense.

**The Recitation That Replaces the Negotiation**

The verse was spoken in the place where the proposal had been made, and the act of the recitation is the first act of the separation. The line is not drawn only in the text; it is drawn in the space between the speaker and the listeners, in the sanctuary full of the people, and the drawing is public. The proposal had asked for a meeting of the two ways, and the recitation is the refusal of the meeting: the words are spoken over the distance, and the distance is kept. The verse is therefore not a sentence in a book; it is an event in a place, and the event is the separation performed, and the performance is what the text preserves. The verse is addressed in the plural, and the plural is the form of the group. The separation is not drawn against one man; it is drawn against a way of believing, and the way is held by the community. The address reaches the leaders who made the proposal, and it reaches the assembly that heard it, and the assembly is the city. The sūrah's final verse will state the balance of the two groups, and the balance is the form of the community's division: one side and the other, and the two not meeting.

## Sūrah al-Kāfirūn 109:3

> nor do you worship what I worship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror Opens**

The second verse of the address reverses the direction of the first. Where the first spoke of the "I," this one speaks of the "you," and the object is exchanged in the same measure: what the one worships is refused by the other. The structure becomes a mirror, and the mirror is the form of the separation: the two lines look at each other without touching, each holding its object, each refusing the object of the other. The mirror is not a comparison; it is a geometry, and the geometry is the claim that the two ways are parallel and not convergent. The verse opens the mirror, and the mirror will be completed in the following verses, first in the present and then in the future. The mirror is the form of the non-meeting, and the form is the claim. Two lines that meet are two lines that share, and the sharing is what the proposal offered and the sūrah refused. The mirror keeps the two lines apart at the exact distance of the objects: each line is held by its object, and the objects are two, and the two do not touch. The geometry of the verse is therefore the geometry of the theology: the separation is not a position between the lines but the space between the objects, and the space is the content of the sūrah.

**The Grammar of the Other's State**

The form of the negation in this verse is the form of the state, not the form of the act. The Arabic says, in effect, "you are not worshippers of what I worship," and the word for the worshipper is the noun of the agent, the word that names a person by his relation. The verse therefore makes a claim about identity, not about a single act: the Makkans are not, in their standing, in their relation, in their very character as worshippers, the worshippers of the object the Prophet worships. The claim is wider than the act, because it is the claim about the one who acts, and the one who acts is defined by the object he acts toward. The claim about the identity is the claim about the relation, and the relation is the standing, not the act. A man who performs the act once is not, in the standing, the worshipper of the object; the worshipper is the one whose relation to the object is the relation of the worship, held in the days and not in the hour. The verse places the Makkans outside that relation, and the placing is the state that the future will not change.

**The Two Objects That Cannot Be Shared**

The object of the Prophet's worship is the one God of the Book, and the verse asserts that the Makkans do not worship that object. The claim is theological, and it is the heart of the sūrah: the difference between the two lines is not a difference of practice, not a difference of rite, not a difference of place; it is a difference of object, and the difference of object is the difference that the offer of the exchange cannot bridge. The proposal had said "we will worship what you worship," and the verse answers that the statement is false, not as a statement about the future but as a statement about the present: the two lines already worship what they worship, and the worship is not transferable, because the object of the worship is not a thing that can be handed over. The offer of the Makkans was the form of the partnership, and the partnership is the sharing of one object between two parties. The verse denies the possibility of the sharing at its root: the object of the one is not a thing that can be divided, and the division of the object is the corruption of the object. The partnership presupposes a common object, and the common object is the one thing the two lines do not have. The verse states the absence of the common, and the absence is the refutation of the partnership, because a partnership without a common object is not a partnership but a meeting of two ways that do not meet.

**The Premise of the Compromise Refuted**

The proposal of the Makkans rested on a premise, and the premise was the possibility of the exchange: if the two parties can swap their objects, the division is a matter of arrangement. The verse refutes the premise at its root. The exchange is impossible, not because the parties would not agree to it, but because the objects are not interchangeable: the one God is not an item in a catalogue, and the catalogue is not an object of worship. The verse does not argue the point with a reason; it states it with the negation, and the negation is the refutation. The sūrah's method throughout is this: the separation is not argued; it is declared, and the declaration is performed in the grammar of the negative. The refutation is in the form rather than in the argument, and the form is the strength. The sūrah never states a reason for the refusal, and the reason is not needed, because the separation is not a conclusion drawn from a premise; it is a declaration of a state. A conclusion can be answered by a better argument, and a declaration can be answered only by the change of the state. The change is what the following verse denies in the future, and the denial of the change is the completion of the refutation. The sūrah's method is therefore the method of the declaration: the state is stated, the change is refused, and the refusal of the change is the answer to every argument that the state could be changed.

**The First Pair of the Figure**

The first two verses of the negation form the first pair of the figure, and the pair is the present: the "I" refuses the object of the "you," and the "you" does not hold the object of the "I," both in the now. The second pair, in the following two verses, will state the same crossing in the future, and the doubling is the emphasis that the grammar carries. The figure is therefore a double mirror: the present mirror of these two verses, and the future mirror of the next two, and the two mirrors together make the separation total in time. The first pair is opened here, and the opening is the claim that the division is not a temporary state of the negotiation; it is the standing structure of the two ways.

## Sūrah al-Kāfirūn 109:4

> I will never worship what you worship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Future Enters the Argument**

The third verse of the negation changes the time of the separation. The first two verses held the present: the "I" does not worship, the "you" does not worship, in the now. This verse moves the line forward: "I will never worship what you worship," and the future is now part of the claim. The argument is no longer only a statement about the state of things; it is a statement about the course of things, and the course is closed. The sūrah has said what is, and it now says what will not be, and the saying of what will not be is the answer to the proposal at the point where the proposal lived, which was the future.

**The Grammar of the Permanent Negative**

The Arabic of the verse carries the strongest form of the future negative the language has. The construction is the particle of the negation joined to the noun of the agent in the nominative: "I am not a worshipper of what you worship," and the form states not an act that will not be performed but a state that will not be entered. The claim is therefore wider than a promise about a future act. It is the statement that the one who speaks will not, in any point of the future, stand in the relation of the worshipper toward the objects of the other. The grammar closes the door from the inside: the state itself is excluded, and the exclusion is permanent, because the form of the permanent negative is the form of the nature, not of the resolution.

**The Future of the Deal Closed**

The proposal of the Makkans was a deal for the long term, and the long term is the future. They had offered the exchange and the share, and the exchange and the share were things of the days to come: a worship that would alternate, a benefit that would be divided, a relation that would be maintained over the years. The future negative of this verse is the direct answer to that future. The sūrah does not refuse the deal with a reason; it closes the time in which the deal could have stood. There is no point in the future, the verse says, at which the one who speaks will be the worshipper of the other's objects, and the closing of the future is the refutation of the deal, because a deal without a future is not a deal. The two texts are timed against each other, and the timing is the form of the answer. The proposal opened its door in the future: the exchange was to come, the share was to be taken, the worship was to alternate in the days to come. The sūrah closes the door at the point of the opening: the future in which the deal was to stand is the future that is refused, and the refusal is timed to the deal, hour for hour. The answer is not later than the question, and it is not earlier; it is at the same point of the time, and the same point is the future.

**The Permanence as the Form of the Certainty**

The declaration of the permanence is the form in which the Book states the certainty of the oneness. The oneness of God is not a position held now and possibly changed later; it is a reality that does not move, and the worship of the one who is real does not turn to what is not real, because the turning would be a change in the object, and the object does not change. The permanence of the verse is therefore not a promise made about the future; it is a statement about the nature of the relation between the worshipper and the worship. The sūrah states the certainty in the grammar of the permanent negative, and the grammar is the doctrine: the line, once drawn by the nature of the objects, is not a line that can be redrawn by the passage of the years. The Book states the permanence of its own object in the same voice. The one God does not change, and the oneness is the name of that which does not change, and the sūrah's permanence is the human form of the divine permanence: the object that does not change is worshipped by the worshipper who does not turn. The two permanences are one, and the one is the form of the other: the worshipper stands where the object stands, and the standing is the relation, and the relation is the permanence. The verse is therefore the form of the oneness in the grammar of the person, and the grammar is the doctrine.

**The Statement About the Nature, Not the Will**

The permanent negative states the nature rather than the will, and the distinction is the substance of the verse. A promise about the future is a statement about the will: the one who speaks will not perform the act in the days to come, and the promise is renewed with each day, and the renewal is the weakness. The form of this verse is the statement about the nature: the one who speaks is not such a one, and the not-being is the state that does not require the renewal. The one who worships the one God does not turn to the objects of the other, not from a decision made each morning, but from the relation itself, and the relation is the nature of the worship. The verse states the nature, and the nature is the permanence that the will could only approximate.

**The Second "I" of the Sūrah**

The verse is the second statement of the "I" in the figure, and the position is the method. The first "I" held the present, and the second "I" holds the future, and the two statements together make the "I" complete in time: the one who speaks is not, and will not be, the worshipper of the other's objects. The following verse will make the corresponding statement of the "you," and the mirror of the future will be closed. The structure of the sūrah is now visible in its full form: two lines, two times, four statements, each line refusing the object of the other in the present and in the future. The verse is the second pillar of the figure, and the pillar is the permanence.

## Sūrah al-Kāfirūn 109:5

> nor will you ever worship what I worship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror Closes**

The fourth verse of the negation completes the figure. The "you" is now stated in the future, in the same measure that the "I" was stated: "nor will you ever worship what I worship." The double mirror is closed: the present pair of the first two verses, and the future pair of these two, each pair crossing the two lines, each line refusing the object of the other in both times. The sūrah's figure is therefore complete at this point, and the completeness is the claim: the separation is not a state of the negotiation, not a position of the moment; it is the total structure of the two ways, held in the present and in the future, in both directions at once.

**The Statement About the Other's Future**

The verse makes a claim about the future of the Makkans, and the claim is the strongest form the sūrah takes. It is not a hope that they will not turn; it is not a prayer that they will not turn; it is the statement of the sūrah's certainty about the structure of the two lines. The Book states the same kind of certainty about the fixedness of the ways of the communities, and the certainty is the theological content of the verse: the object that the Makkans worship is not the object of the truth, and a line that is not the line of the truth does not end by becoming it, however long the days continue. The statement is therefore not a prediction in the ordinary sense; it is a reading of the nature of the two objects, and the reading is the claim. The certainty is not drawn from the observation of the present; it is drawn from the nature of the objects. The observation could be wrong, because the present can change, and the reading is not wrong, because the object does not change. The way that is not the way of the truth does not end in the truth, because the end is the object, and the object is the not-one. The sūrah's certainty is therefore of the same kind as the certainty of the Book about the communities and their ways: the ways are fixed by their objects, and the fixing is the permanence, and the permanence is the claim of the verse.

**The Ranges the Exegetes Recorded**

The commentators recorded a range of readings for the repetition that fills these four verses, and the range is the evidence that the repetition was not missed. Some took it for pure emphasis, the force of the statement carried by the doubling. Others took it as the negation of the repeated propositions of the Makkans, who had kept making the offer of the mixing, and the repetition answered each making of the offer with the same refusal. Another reading, the one the grammar of the verses themselves suggests, assigned the present to the first pair and the future to the second: the "I" does not now and will not ever, and the "you" are not now and will not ever. Yet another reading took the third verse to state the past as well, so that the refusal covers the time behind the speaker as it covers the time before him. The readings differ in the detail, and they agree in the point: the repetition multiplies the directions of the time, and the time is made total by the multiplication.

**Why the Sūrah Says It Twice**

The redundancy is the method, and the method is the claim. A single negation in each direction could be read as a statement about a season: the position now, possibly changed later, the refusal of this hour, open to the hour after. The double negation, in the two times, closes the time itself. The sūrah uses the form of the repetition to do what the single statement cannot do: it makes the separation total, and the totality is the emphasis that no single verse could carry. The doubling is therefore not a defect of the style, and it is not the habit of the speech; it is the doctrine in the form of the grammar, and the doctrine is that the two ways do not meet, in the now and not in the ever-after. The repetition in the presence of the audience does the work of the witness, and the work is the record. Each statement is heard by the same listeners, and the hearing is the public form of the separation: the line is not drawn in the privacy of the heart but in the open of the sanctuary, before the people. The four statements of the negation are the four entries of the record, and the record is the sūrah itself, preserved in the Book as the record was made in the air. The repetition is therefore the form of the witnessing, and the witnessing is the completion of the declaration.

**The Asymmetry of the Certainty**

The statement about the "you" in the future is the last word of the negative series, and the position is the method. The speaker states himself in the two verses of the "I," and the listeners are stated in the two verses of the "you," and the series ends on the listeners. The sūrah places its final word about them where they hear it: the last statement of the negation is the statement about the audience, and the audience receives the verdict in the order of the recitation, the "I" first and the "you" last. The asymmetry is the form of the address: the one who speaks is stated as the one who speaks, and the one who is addressed is stated as the one who is addressed, and the last statement is the one that belongs to the one who is addressed.

**The Figure Complete, the Division to Come**

The four verses of the negation end here, and the figure is complete. Each line has refused the object of the other, in the present and in the future, and the space between the two lines is now the content of the sūrah: the separation itself, held in all four directions of the time. The final verse of the sūrah will state the division in the positive form, the form of the possession: each line holds its own, and the holding is the last word of the address. The negations have done their work, and the work is the drawing of the line; what remains is the naming of what stands on each side of it.

## Sūrah al-Kāfirūn 109:6

> You have your way, and I have my Way.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Positive Form of the Separation**

After four verses of the negative, the sūrah closes in the affirmative, and the closing is the form of the verdict. "You have your way, and I have my Way": the division is now stated not as a mutual refusal but as a mutual possession. Each line holds what it holds, and the holding is the final shape of the separation. The verse does not repeat the negation; it states the fact that the negation produced, which is the fact of the two lines, each with its own, and the two not sharing. The last word of the sūrah is the "Way" of the one who speaks, and the position of the word is the position of the sūrah: the address ends on the side of the speaker, and the ending is the claim.

**The Word Dīn and Its Two Faces**

The word translated "way" or "religion" is dīn, and the word carries the two faces that the sūrah of the Māʿūn opened: the religion with its rites and its creed, and the judgment with its account and its end. The verse can therefore be read as the statement of the two religions, and it can be read as the statement of the two endings: each line will have the consequence of its own, and the consequence of the one is not the consequence of the other. The two readings converge on the point that the sūrah needs: the two lines do not end together, and the ending is the last form of the separation. The division of the verse is therefore the division in the days and the division in the end, and the end is the one that does not change.

**The Book's Parallel Formulas**

The Book has the parallel formulas, and the parallel is the confirmation of the figure. When the believers hear the idle talk of the opponents, they turn away and state the division in the form of the deeds: "We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours" (28:55), and in the same voice, addressed to the Prophet in the face of the denial: "My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do!" (10:41). The formula of this verse is the form of the same figure raised from the deeds to the religion: the deeds are the acts of the days, and the religion is the whole of the line, and the separation that holds for the acts holds for the line, and the holding is the standing rule of the Book for the place where the two ways meet.

**The Separation as the Form of Mercy**

The verse is also the Book's rule for the relation of the believer to the disbeliever, and the rule is the refusal of the forced meeting. The Book commands the leaving: "So leave to Me ˹O Prophet˺ those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend" (68:44), and "I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless" (68:45), and in the gentler form, "So bear with the disbelievers ˹O Prophet˺. Let them be for ˹just˺ a little while" (86:17). The division of this verse is the form of the rule that "let there be no compulsion in religion" (2:256): the line is drawn, the two are left to their own ways, and the meeting is refused not in anger but in the nature of the two objects. The sūrah's closing is therefore the mercy of the separation: each is left to what he is, and the leaving is the verdict that the end belongs to the Lord.

**The Debate of the Abrogation and Its Grounds**

Some of the exegetes held that the verse was abrogated by the verse of the sword, the command given after the Sacred Months for the communities that broke their treaties: "kill the polytheists ˹who violated their treaties˺ wherever you find them, capture them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them on every way" (9:5). Others went further and held the entire sūrah abrogated. Others yet held that the verse stands, because it is a report and not a command, and the abrogation, in the doctrine of most of the scholars, reaches the commands and not the reports. The reading that holds the two together is the strongest, and its ground is the ground of the domains: the verse of the sword deals with the political question of the community that makes war and breaks its bond, and this sūrah deals with the theological question of the two ways of worship, and the two questions are not the same question, so that the two texts stand in their own places without the falling of either.

**The Final Balance of the Sūrah**

The sūrah ends on the balance, and the balance is the form of the whole of it. The six verses move from the command to the address, from the address to the four negations, and from the negations to the balance, and the balance states what the negations performed: two lines, each with its own, and the two not meeting. The last word of the sūrah is the "Way" of the one who speaks, and the word stands where the sūrah stands, on the side of the speaker, and the audience is left on its own side of the line. The shortest of the divisions ends on the longest of the statements: the ways are two, and they are two in the days and in the end, and the end of each is with the Lord who keeps the account.
