# Sūrah Quraysh (Chapter 106) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah Quraysh 106:1

> ˹At least˺ for ˹the favour of˺ making Quraysh habitually secure—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse That Has No Main Clause**

The sūrah opens with something unusual: a noun phrase with no verb, a statement that stops before it becomes a sentence. "For the habitual security of the Quraysh" is a fragment, and the fragment is the first move of the argument. The grammarians have a name for the device: the expression is left suspended, its main clause withheld, so that the listener carries the unfinished thought forward into the next verses, where it is completed. The security named here is not described or explained; it is simply invoked, and the invocation does the work of a premise. What the first verse withholds, the fourth gives back: the suspended clause is completed, at a distance, by the statement of the Lord who fed and who secured. The sūrah is, in its structure, a single sentence of four verses, and the opening fragment is its first word.

**What the Word Īlāf Carried**

The Arabic word behind "habitually secure" is īlāf, from a root that means to be familiar with a thing, to resort to it by habit, and the word entered the language with a specific history. It came to name the grants of safe passage that the Quraysh held from the powers around the Peninsula: the Byzantine, the Persian, the Abyssinian, and the rulers of Yemen. A people's security was not a single fact but a set of repeated recognitions, renewed with the seasons, and the word īlāf captured exactly that: a familiarity, a standing habit of being let through. The first verse of the sūrah therefore does not name a state; it names an institution, a web of truces that the tribe had accumulated, and it names it in the genitive, as a favor received. The security was habitual in the sense that it had to be renewed, or it would have been broken.

**A Sūrah Named for a Tribe**

Most of the short sūrahs take their names from a subject: the elephant, the mocker, the small kindness. This one takes its name from a people, the Quraysh, and the naming is significant. The sūrah addresses the Prophet's own tribe, the tribe of the city where the sūrah was spoken, and it names them in the title the way a document names its party. The effect is directness. There is no veil of parable between the words and the people. The Quraysh hear their own name as the title of the address, and the address that follows is about the security they enjoy, the trade they run, and the worship that the one and the other require. The naming makes the sūrah an occasion: it is spoken to them, in their city, about their condition.

**The Sūrah That Came as a Pair**

The connection to the preceding sūrah is so close that some of the earliest records of the Book kept them together. In the manuscript attributed to the companion Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, al-Fīl and Quraysh stand as a single sūrah, and ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have recited the two in that joined order when he led the prayer. The majority of the scholars treat them as separate, and the separation does not break the meaning: the second sūrah is the address that the first sūrah prepares. Al-Fīl shows the dealing; Quraysh names the ones the dealing protected and asks them to respond. The pair forms a complete thought, the proof and the demand, and the fact that two of the most authoritative voices of the first generation read them as one is the strongest evidence that the Book intends them to be read that way.

**A Favor That Was Not Bought**

The fragment of the first verse presents the security as a favor, and the opening words mark the beginning of a list the sūrah will not finish in its own terms. The security was not the product of the Quraysh's strength; the tribe had no army that could have enforced the truces it enjoyed. The grants came from powers that had no reason to respect a desert tribe except the respect that attached to the sanctuary the tribe guarded. The favor was, in other words, borrowed from a trust, and the trust was the custodianship of the House. The first verse states the favor without stating its source, and the third verse will state the source: the Lord of the House. The fragment is the debt, and the sūrah is the demand for its recognition. The favor is stated in the form of a gift, and the form is the claim. A tribe that had bought its security with its own power would not hear the word favor without protest. The Quraysh did not protest, because the security was not bought: it was granted, renewed, and received, and the receiving is what the first verse records. The fragment that opens the sūrah is the fragment of the receipt, the entry that states the favor and withholds the signature, which the last verse supplies.

**The Suspense the Fragment Creates**

The effect of the opening on the listener is the point of the opening. A phrase that stops in the middle of its grammar holds the mind in a state of waiting, and the sūrah uses that waiting as its instrument. The listener who hears "for the habitual security of the Quraysh" is left asking: for what purpose, to what end, on what condition? The questions are not answered by an explanation; they are answered by a command. The sūrah refuses to say "you are secure, therefore be grateful" in plain words, and instead lets the grammar do the arguing: the favor is stated, the response is stated, and the space between them is the space in which the tribe must place itself. The unfinished first verse is therefore not a defect of style but the whole of the sūrah's method, compressed into its first line.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:2

> secure in their trading caravan ˹to Yemen˺ in the winter and ˹Syria˺ in the summer—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Seasons, Two Routes, One Economy**

The second verse unfolds the first: the security of the Quraysh is specified as the security of the caravan, and the caravan is given its calendar. In the winter the trains went down to Yemen, where the goods of India, the spices and the aromatics that traveled north by sea, were taken in. In the summer the trains went up to Syria, where the wares were sold and the grain and the products of the settled land were bought. The two seasons are named together, winter and summer, and the pairing covers the whole year: there was no season in which the trade stood still, and no season in which the security was not required. The verse states the economy of the city in a few words, and the statement is the most concise account in the Book of what made Makkah rich. The two directions are also two dependencies. The city was fed from the north in one season and from the south in the other, and the security had to hold in both directions at once, for both trades, for both the goods that came and the goods that went. A truce that covered only one of the two routes would have covered half the city's life. The verse names the whole circle, and the whole circle is the favor.

**The Security of the Body on the Road**

The īlāf that the first verse invoked is here applied to the traveler himself. The caravan did not move inside the territory of the Quraysh; it crossed the lands of other tribes and the frontiers of foreign powers, and at every point of the crossing the traveler needed the recognition that the first verse named. The grants of safe passage were what turned a road into a trade route, and the road was the life of the city. Without the winter and summer truces, the caravans would have been a moving treasury with no protection, and the city would have been a market without a market. The verse does not say "your trade"; it says "their caravan," and the possessive is the same third person the sūrah keeps: the tribe is being described, from outside, in terms of what it has been given.

**The Trust That Bought the Passage**

The question the verse raises is the question the sūrah answers: why did the powers and the tribes let the Quraysh through? The accounts received in the tradition give the answer: the respect attached to the custodianship of the Kaʿbah. A people who fed the pilgrims, watered them in the season of the circuit, and kept the sanctuary in the year of the truces were owed a passage that their own arms could not have won. The economic fortune of the city was built on a religious office, and the office was a trust held for the Lord of the House. The verse places the trade immediately after the security, and the order is the argument: the security came first, the trade followed, and the trade was the fruit of the trust.

**The Safe Haven in a Contested Land**

The geography of the two routes explains the permanence of the favor. Makkah stood at the crossing of the two great movements of the Peninsula: the caravan that carried the goods of the East to the markets of the Levant, and the pilgrimage that carried the tribes of the desert to the House in the season of the circuit. Around the city, the land belonged to shifting alliances and the reach of distant empires, and the Book describes the same position in a parallel verse, the safe haven that stands while the people around it are snatched away (29:67). The verse of Quraysh is the economic face of that same fact. The caravans moved because the crossing was respected, and the crossing was respected because the city at its centre was a sanctuary, and the sanctuary was kept by a tribe whose office came from the Lord.

**A Verse That Names the Fruit but Not the Root**

The second verse, like the first, states the gift without stating its source. The trade is described in full, the seasons in full, the routes in full, and the cause is absent. Nothing in the verse attributes the caravans to the skill of the merchants, the strength of the guards, or the goodwill of the neighbors. The cause is withheld for the same reason the main clause was withheld in the first verse: the sūrah is building a single argument, and the argument needs its root to be stated only once, at the point where the response is demanded. The fruit is named here, the winter and the summer, the Yemen and the Syria, and the root will be named in the third verse: the Lord of the House. The structure is a plant: the root under the ground, the two branches in the air.

**The Calendar as Evidence**

The pairing of the seasons is the quiet evidence of the verse. A favor that is required twice a year, at fixed intervals, for a route of fixed length, is a favor that is institutional, not occasional. The truces were not made when the danger appeared; they were renewed with the turning of the year, and the turning of the year is what the verse records in its last two words. Winter and summer: the whole circle of the year, held safe by the same hand, for the same people, in the same two directions. The verse ends, like the first, in a fragment, and the fragment again carries the listener forward, past the trade, to the worship that the trade exists to answer. The suspended grammar does its work a second time, and the sūrah moves on.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:3

> let them worship the Lord of this ˹Sacred˺ House,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Completes the Sentence**

The third verse is the main clause the first two had been waiting for. The fragment of the opening, the habitual security, and the enumeration of the second, the caravans of the winter and the summer, are both completed by a single command: let them worship. The sūrah has withheld its verb for two verses, and now the verb arrives, and it is the verb of worship. The structure is the argument: the favors are stated, and the consequence is stated, and the consequence is not prosperity or gratitude in general but the worship of a specific Lord. The sūrah refuses to let the tribe keep the gift and return nothing. The command is the price of the security, and the price is named at the exact point where the security has been fully described, so that nothing can be added to the favor without the debt being restated.

**The Lord of the House, Not the Images Inside It**

The object of the command is the pivot of the sūrah. The House named here is the sanctuary of Makkah, the building in which the tribe's idols stood, and the verse commands the worship of the Lord of the House, not of what is inside the House. The distinction is the whole of the address. The Quraysh had attributed their security to the images they set up within the precinct, and the sūrah says that the protection came from the Lord to whom the House belongs. The favor is the argument: the one who made the caravan safe is not the stone in the corner of the sanctuary, and the one who fed the people from hunger is not the figure they named in their prayers. The command does not argue against the idols; it simply attributes to the right owner the gift that the tribe was keeping for the wrong one.

**The Third Person That Does the Accusing**

The command is given in the third person: "let them worship," not "worship." The sūrah has named the Quraysh in the first verse, and it keeps the distance throughout. They are described, not addressed; their favor is stated, their response commanded, all in the grammar of observation. The distance is not a courtesy; it is the tone of a verdict. A man who is addressed is a participant; a people who are described in the third person while a demand is made of them are being judged. The sūrah's choice of person is therefore part of its content: the tribe is not yet inside the conversation. It is standing outside it, being shown what it has and what it owes, and the command is the line drawn across the space between the two positions. The sūrah makes the shift once, and it does not reverse it. The first verse names the tribe, Quraysh, and from the third verse onward the sūrah speaks of them without the name, in the bare third person. The naming is spent, like a stamp on a document: once applied, it is not repeated, and the document proceeds under the mark. The grammar of the sūrah is therefore the grammar of a verdict that has already been pronounced.

**"This House" — A Word That Only Works in Makkah**

The deictic "this" anchors the sūrah in a place. The House is not an institution, not a symbol, not a general reference to the sanctuary of a people; it is the building at the end of the street, the one every listener could see from the place where the words were spoken. The sūrah is among the shortest in the Book, and its shortness is made possible by its locality: it says "this" because it is said there. The Lord of this House is the Lord whose sanctuary stands within earshot, and the worship commanded is the worship of the God who has just been named in relation to a building the audience can see. The specificity is the strength of the command. A general duty of gratitude could be deferred; the worship of the Lord of this House, the House at hand, the House whose custodians the listeners are, admits no deferral.

**The Debt Stated by the Patriarch**

The Book states the same relation between the city and its Lord in the words of the patriarch. When Abraham asked for the city, his prayer was that the city be made secure and its people provided for, and the answer came in a form that fixed the terms: those who believe will be provided, and those who disbelieve will be left to a little while and then to the fire (2:126). The sūrah of Quraysh is the living form of that same exchange. The security and the provision that Abraham asked for are the security and the provision the sūrah describes in its first two verses, and the response the sūrah commands is the response the prayer set as the condition. The tribe stands inside a promise made a thousand years before it, and the sūrah reminds them that the promise has a side to it.

**The Response That the Favor Requires**

The command is not a suggestion attached to a list of blessings; it is the logical completion of the list. A favor, in the grammar of the Book, is not a gift in the full sense, because a gift that requires nothing creates nothing, and the favor of the sūrah creates exactly one thing: the relation between the one who received and the one who gave. The sūrah states the favor in full and then states the relation in a single verb, worship. The logic is complete, and it is the reason the sūrah can be so short. It needs no parable and no narrative, because its argument is a syllogism of two verses: the favor came from the Lord of the House; therefore the worship goes to the Lord of the House. Everything else in the sūrah is the statement of the premise.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:4

> Who has fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Needs That Underlie All the Rest**

The last verse returns to the ground under the caravans. The security of the passage and the profit of the trade are the visible fruits, and the verse names the two needs without which no fruit is possible: the food that removes the hunger, and the safety that removes the fear. Hunger and fear are the two states of a creature without protection, and the verse says of the Lord of the House that He has fed the people against the one and made them secure against the other. Everything the first two verses described, the truces, the routes, the seasons, stands on these two provisions, and the sūrah ends by naming the foundations. The structure is a house: the walls and the roof stated first, the foundation stated last, and the foundation is the Lord whose name the relative clause carries.

**The Verbs of a Completed Favor**

The two verbs are perfective: He fed, He made secure. The favors are not promised; they are recorded. The sūrah does not say the Lord will feed the Quraysh or will keep them safe; it says He has done both, in the time of the tribe's memory, in the winter and the summer that the second verse named. The perfective form is the evidence of the argument. A promise could be argued with; a completed deed could only be acknowledged or denied. The sūrah places its audience before a deed already done, and the deed is one the tribe cannot refute, because the tribe is the evidence of it: it is fed, it is safe, it has been both for a generation. The verse closes the sūrah on the ground that makes the command of the third verse impossible to treat as a new demand. It is a demand for the recognition of a debt already contracted.

**"Against Hunger" and "Against Fear"**

The prepositions do work. The feeding is not abundance for its own sake; it is relief from a specific state, the state of hunger, and the security is not comfort; it is relief from fear. The two states are the two ways a people knows that it is unprotected: the empty hand and the watching eye. The sūrah chooses the two because they are the two that no amount of trade can remove by itself. A caravan can bring the grain, but it cannot guarantee the road, and a truce can guarantee the road in the season it lasts, but it cannot guarantee the season after. The Lord of the House is named as the one who removes both states, and the removal is presented as a standing act, renewed with every season, the act that made the seasons of the second verse possible.

**The Parable of the Secure City**

The Book gives the consequence of refusing this verse in a parable that is its mirror image. There is a society, the parable says, that is safe and at ease, its provision coming to it in abundance from every direction, and its people meet the favors with ingratitude; the result is that the taste of hunger and fear is given to them for their misdeeds (16:112). The parable is the future form of the sūrah of Quraysh: the same favor, the same security, the same provision, and then the refusal. The sūrah ends on the favor; the parable ends on the consequence. Read together, the two texts form the complete statement: the favor is real, the refusal is possible, and the refusal has a price that is exactly the reversal of the favor. The city that was fed against hunger and made secure against fear can be made to taste hunger and fear, and the parable is the Book's way of saying that the last verse of Quraysh is not a description but a warning.

**The Security That Is Not the Tribe's**

The last clause of the sūrah is the last word of its argument, and the argument is that the security belongs to no one in the city. The truces were granted by powers that could have withdrawn them; the routes crossed lands that the tribe did not own; the sanctuary was kept by a trust that the tribe held but did not possess. The sūrah passes all of these over, because they are not the point. The point is the two verbs at the end: He fed, He made secure. The agents of the favors are not the empires, not the alliances, not the office of the custodianship; the agent is the Lord of the House. The sūrah's final move is therefore a move of attribution: it takes the security that the tribe understood as its own, the security of its name, its truces, its trade, and reassigns it, clause by clause, to the one whose House stands in the middle of the city.

**The Last Word of the Sūrah**

The sūrah ends on the word for security, the same root that opened it in the first verse as the favor of the habitual safety. The opening and the closing share the word, and the sharing is the form of the argument: the sūrah begins with the security as a fact and ends with the security as a gift from a named Lord. In between, the caravans, the seasons, the command to worship. The four verses form a ring: the security is stated, the security is described, the security is answered, and the security is owned. The ring closes on ownership, which is the only point that matters. The tribe may keep the truces and the trade; the sūrah does not ask for them. It asks only for the recognition of the one who made them possible, and it asks in the last verse, in the two verbs, in the perfective, as a fact already done.
