# Sūrah Yā-Sīn (Chapter 36) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Heart of the Scripture and the Resurrection of the Bones**

Yā-Sīn is the thirty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 83 verses of mature Makkan revelation. It opens with the mysterious, majestic letters *Yā-Sīn*, which classical commentators such as Ibn ʿAbbās interpreted as a divine address to the Prophet ﷺ in the ancient Ethiopic or Arabic dialect meaning *“O Perfect Human Being!”* In the devotional life of the Islamic world, Sūrah Yā-Sīn occupies a sacred, central throne: celebrated in prophetic tradition as *Qalb al-Qur'ān* (The Heart of the Qur'an). Anas ibn Mālik reported in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (2887) that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: *“Indeed, everything has a heart. The heart of the Qur'an is Yā-Sīn; whoever recites Yā-Sīn, Allah records for them the reward of reciting the Qur'an ten times.”* Traditional practice across centuries has prescribed its recitation beside the sick, the dying, and the graves of the departed, because its rhythmic, pulsing verses infuse the soul with absolute certainty in bodily resurrection, divine omnipotence, and the reality of the World to Come.

**The Cosmic Rhythm and the Decisive Argument**

The architecture of Sūrah Yā-Sīn moves with astonishing symphonic momentum across three great movements. The opening movement (1–32) affirms the prophetic mission of the Messenger upon the Straight Path, warns of the inherited heedlessness of nations, narrates the dramatic martyrdom of the Believer of the Town, and laments the tragic tragedy of humanity: *“How sorrowful for the servants! There never comes to them a messenger except that they used to mock him”* (36:30). The second movement (33–50) presents three cosmic signs of supreme divine power: the dead earth revived by water to bring forth grain, date palms. Vineyards; the night stripping away the day leaving humanity in darkness, with the sun running to its resting place (*mustaqarr*) by the decree of the Almighty, and the moon measured in phases until it returns like an ancient withered date-palm frond (*ka-l-ʿurjūn al-qadīm*), neither able to overtake the other; and the laden ship (*al-fulk al-mashḥūn*) carrying human ancestry across the abyss of the ocean. The third movement (51–83) delivers the climactic, terrifying staging of the Resurrection: the Horn blown, the graves burst open, and humanity crying: *“O woe to us! Who has raised us up from our sleeping place? This is what the Most Compassionate promised, and the messengers were true!”* (36:52). It contrasts the blissful inhabitants of Paradise, reclining in cool shade with fruit and peaceful greetings from a Merciful Lord (*Salāmun qawlan min rabbin raḥīm*), against the disgraced criminals commanded to stand apart (*Wamtāzū al-yawma ayyuhā al-mujrimūn*). It stages the horrifying moment when human mouths are sealed, and their hands speak and feet bear witness to their deeds (36:65). The climax answers the famous challenge of Ubayy ibn Khalaf, who brought a decayed bone, crumbled it into dust before the Prophet's face. Scoffed: *“Who will revive these bones when they have rotted away?”* The sūrah answers with thunderous logic: *“Say, 'He will revive them who produced them the first time; and He is, of all creation, Knowing'”* (36:79), concluding with the immortal, sovereign reality of the Divine Command: *“His command is only when He intends a thing that He says to it, 'Be,' and it is! So exalted is He in whose hand is the realm of all things, and to Him you will be returned”* (36:82–83).

The town martyred him, whereupon divine speech depicts him entering Paradise in the twinkling of an eye, crying with heartbreaking compassion: *“Oh, I wish that my people knew how my Lord has forgiven me and made me of the honored!”* (36:26–27).

## Verse 36:1

> Yâ-Sĩn

**Why the Meaning Is Left With God**

The dominant position among the Companions and early exegetes is that the precise intent of Yā Sīn belongs to divine knowledge. Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd, and a group of Successors treated the disconnected letters as part of the mutashābih, the ambiguous whose interpretation is deferred. This is not an evasion but a religious claim about language. The Qur'an elsewhere describes itself as containing verses that are decisive and others that are ambiguous, and it praises those who say *“We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord”* (3:7).

**Yā Sīn as a Name and a Call**

A second line of interpretation reads Yā Sīn as an address. In Arabic yā is the vocative particle, O, and sīn is taken as a contraction of insān, human being, or as an affectionate diminutive. On this reading the phrase means O man, or O little human, spoken tenderly to the Prophet Muḥammad. The appeal of this reading is contextual: the very next verses say *“You are truly one of the messengers upon the Straight Path”* (36:3-4), so an opening call to the man who is then confirmed as messenger forms a coherent sequence. Other reports present Yā Sīn as one of the Prophet's own names, given by God in the Qur'an alongside Muḥammad and Aḥmad. One report attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib lists seven names said to be given to the Prophet in the Qur'an, including Yā Sīn and Ṭā Hā, though the chain of that report is debated among hadith critics and is often cited without a graded number. Whether as name or call, this reading personalizes the opening: the sūrah of resurrection, signs, and town messengers begins by naming its immediate addressee.

**The Disconnected Letters Tie This Sūrah to Its Argument**

The disconnected letters are not ornamental. In almost every sūrah that opens with them, the text that follows immediately mentions revelation, the Book, or the Qur'an itself, as if the letters were a reminder of the raw material of the message. Here verse two follows with an oath: *“By the Quran, rich in wisdom!”* (36:2). The sequence mirrors Ṭā Hā, Yā Sīn, Ṣād, Qāf — each time letters built from the same alphabet the Arabs used for poetry and dispute become the building blocks of a book they cannot match. The sūrah later argues from signs in dead earth, gardens, night and day, sun and moon, ships and cattle. All those signs are external. The opening sign is internal to language: these are your letters, yet you cannot produce their like. The Qur'an elsewhere makes that challenge explicit: *“If you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sūrah like it”* (2:23). Yā Sīn places the reader inside that challenge before any proof is offered.

**An Opening That Establishes Authority Without Explanation**

Yā Sīn claims authority precisely by not explaining itself. A human author anxious to be understood would begin with a thesis. This text begins with what its first audience could not fully parse and then immediately swears by the wisdom of the Qur'an that follows. The oath in 36:2 functions as a seal on the opaque opening: you may not know what Yā Sīn means, but you are told what kind of book follows it. That order reverses ordinary persuasion, which explains first and then asks for trust. Here trust is asked first, explanation follows across eighty-three verses. The structure mirrors how signs work in the rest of the sūrah. Dead earth looks inert until God gives it life, *“producing grain from it for them to eat”* (36:33). Night looks permanent until daylight is stripped away.

## Verse 36:2

> By the Quran, rich in wisdom

**An Oath That Answers the Opening**

The sūrah opened with two unexplained letters, and the immediate oath by the Qur'an gives those letters a home. Arabic oaths work by invoking something weighty as witness, and here the witness is the book itself. *“By the Quran, rich in wisdom!”* (36:2) is not a decorative flourish. It tells the listener that what follows Yā Sīn is not a puzzle for its own sake but a book whose character is ḥakīm. The word translated as rich in wisdom carries three strands that early lexicographers recorded: wise in itself, firmly constructed so falsehood cannot enter it, and wisdom-giving to those who receive it. The verse that says *“falsehood cannot approach it from before it or behind it”* (41:42) explains the second strand, while the description of the Qur'an as a book that guides to the straight path explains the third.

**What Ḥakīm Means for This Sūrah**

Ḥakīm in this verse is usually rendered wise, but the commentators note it can also mean muḥkam, made firm, or muḥkim, that which makes wise. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both mention that the Qur'an is called ḥakīm in several places, including *“Alif, Lām, Rā. These are the verses of the wise Book”* (10:1) and *“By the clear Book”* (43:2) followed by its description as wise. The firmness reading points to structure: verses are set in place, guarded, not open to tampering. The wisdom-giving reading points to effect: this book produces ḥikmah, sound judgment, in its reader. Yā Sīn needs both meanings. The sūrah will move through stories, natural signs, resurrection arguments, and Last-Day scenes. What holds those diverse materials together is not topic but wisdom — a way of connecting dead earth to raised bodies, sun and moon to accountability. Calling the Qur'an ḥakīm at the start promises that the transitions to come are not random; they follow a wise arrangement that the reader will only see after traversing the whole chapter.

**Why an Oath by the Qur'an Itself**

Oaths in the Qur'an usually invoke created things — sun, moon, fig, olive — to point to the Creator. An oath by the Qur'an is rarer and more self-referential. It occurs in Sūrah Ṣād, *“By the Qur'an containing the Reminder”* (38:1), and in other openings. The logic is that the Qur'an is the most immediate evidence of what it claims. The Arabs who first heard it were judges of speech; they knew poetry, oratory, and soothsaying. Swearing by the Qur'an puts that literary experience on the stand. If the book is wise, firmly made, and wisdom-giving, then its claim about the messenger in the next verse stands on something the audience can test. The sūrah later says *“We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him”* (36:69), separating revelation from familiar genres. The oath in 36:2 begins that separation. Poetry can be wise in moments; this book is characterized as wisdom throughout, not incidentally.

**How This Oath Functions in Recitation and Memory**

Those who memorize Yā Sīn often note how verse two anchors the opening. Yā Sīn alone could be forgotten or misheard as a name; *“By the Quran, rich in wisdom!”* (36:2) fixes it as scripture. In congregational recitation, the pause after verse one and the lift into the oath create a small drama: silence, then attestation. Classical reciters prolong the oath slightly, giving ḥakīm its full weight. This is also why Yā Sīn is called the heart of the Qur'an in a well-known report, *“Everything has a heart. The heart of the Qur'an is Yā Sīn”* recorded in Sunan al-Dārimī and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, though its chain is graded as weak by many critics; the sentiment it expresses is that this sūrah concentrates themes found elsewhere. An oath at the start that names the Qur'an's central quality — wisdom — supports that concentration. The reader is told at the threshold what kind of book he has entered, so the stories and arguments that follow are received not as scattered topics but as facets of one wise address.

## Verse 36:3

> You ˹O Prophet˺ are truly one of the messengers

**A Direct Confirmation After an Oath**

The oath in the previous verse required an answer, and this verse provides it. *“You are truly one of the messengers”* (36:3) is addressed to the Prophet Muḥammad, and the particle of emphasis plus the oath together form one of the strongest affirmations in the Qur'an. The structure is oath, then statement: By the wise Qur'an, you are among the messengers. In Arabic rhetoric that pairing is deliberate. The truth of the second clause is guaranteed by the weight of the first. If the Qur'an is wise and firmly constructed, then its testimony about its bearer is trustworthy. The sūrah so moves in three beats: obscure letters, a book described as wise, a man described as sent.

**What “One of the Messengers” Implies**

Saying one of the messengers rather than the messenger places Muḥammad within a lineage. The Qur'an repeatedly stresses that he is not an innovation among messengers: *“Say, I am not something original among the messengers”* (46:9). The lineage includes Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, Mūsā, ʿĪsā and many others, and the sūrah will soon give an example of earlier messengers sent to a town. By saying one of, the verse does two things. It dignifies the Prophet by joining him to a known, honored company, and it normalizes opposition to him. If previous messengers were rejected and mocked, then rejection of this messenger is not evidence against him; it is evidence that his community follows an old pattern. The verse also quietly answers a Meccan objection that a messenger should be an angel. If he is one of the messengers, and all messengers before him were men who ate food and walked in markets, then his humanity is not a defect. The Qur'an states that principle elsewhere: *“We never sent before you any but men to whom We revealed”* (16:43).

**How This Verse Frames the Town Story to Come**

The affirmation that you are among the messengers prepares for verses thirteen onward, where *“the residents of a town, when the messengers came to them”* (36:13) are described. The Prophet's own experience of rejection is placed alongside an older town's rejection, so his community sees their behavior in a mirror. The town received two messengers, then a third, and still said *“You are only humans like us”* (36:15). The Quraysh said the same about Muḥammad. The parallel is not accidental. By first confirming that you are truly one of the messengers, the sūrah establishes the standard case, then shows the repeated pattern of denial. The reader who accepts the first affirmation is led to see the town's deniers as unjust, and by extension to see Meccan denial as unjust. The verse is therefore not only theology but strategy: it secures the premise before presenting the example that will test the audience's consistency.

**A Verse That Defines Prophethood as Service, Not Privilege**

Being one of the messengers in Qur'anic language does not mean being elevated above accountability; it means being entrusted with a clear delivery. The next verses define that duty as only to deliver the message clearly (36:17). Prophethood is so framed as responsibility rather than status. This matters for the sūrah's later critique of those who take gods hoping to be helped (36:74-75). True help comes from God, not from intermediaries, and the messenger does not claim intercession as his own power. He delivers. The confirmation in 36:3 is therefore modest in one sense: you are one among many, not a new kind of being. It is monumental in another: among all humans, you are among those chosen to carry the wise Qur'an. The tension between modesty and magnitude is the Qur'anic portrait of prophethood, and this verse holds both together in a single line.

## Verse 36:4

> upon the Straight Path

**A Path Described in Three Words**

*“Upon the Straight Path”* (36:4) is a complete sentence fragment that continues the affirmation before it: you are among the messengers, upon the straight path. The brevity is eloquent. The Qur'an does not say you follow the straight path or you guide to it here; it says you are upon it, as one stands on a road. The same construction appears in the opening sūrah, where believers pray *“Guide us to the Straight Path”* (1:6), and the answer across the Book is that messengers are already upon it and invite others onto it.

**Why Straightness Is the Chosen Image**

The Qur'an could have described the Prophet as truthful, wise, or chosen. It chooses a spatial image. Path imagery works because it captures both direction and movement. A path can be seen, followed, left, or blocked. The sūrah later describes barriers placed before and behind those who refuse to see (36:9), which is the inverse of being upon a path: instead of an open road ahead, there is a wall. The straight path also implies that other paths exist and are not straight. The sūrah will later warn *“Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy”* (36:60) and then *“but to worship Me alone? This is the Straight Path”* (36:61). The same term returns at the end as at the beginning, forming an inclusio. What begins as a description of the messenger's location ends as an invitation to the audience to stand where he stands. The image so carries the whole sūrah.

**Messengership and Path Are Linked**

The verse links two ideas: being among the messengers and being upon the straight path. The link matters because it defines what messengers do. They do not invent a path; they are placed upon one and call others to it. The Qur'an says of the Prophet elsewhere *“You guide to a straight path”* (42:52), which complements rather than contradicts being upon it. One cannot guide to a road he does not himself walk. The town messengers later say *“Our duty is only to deliver the message clearly”* (36:17). Their clarity is a function of their location: because they are upon the straight path, their call is straight. A crooked caller cannot issue a straight call. This is why the sūrah stresses that its messengers ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21). Guidance is not only content but position. The straight path authenticates the messenger, and the messenger makes the straight path visible.

**A Short Verse That Carries a Theological Weight**

Three words in Arabic, four in English, yet this verse carries a religious weight that exegetes have expanded. Is the straight path the Qur'an itself, Islam as a way of life, or the Prophet's own character? The commentators answer that it is all three, because the Qur'an describes itself as guiding to the straight path, Islam is called the straight path in hadith, and the Prophet's conduct was described by ʿĀʾishah as the Qur'an walking. The hadith *“I have left you upon a clear white path, its night is like its day”* recorded in Sunan Ibn Mājah 43 and graded ḥasan by some, uses the same imagery of a path that remains visible even at night, linking back to the sūrah's sign of night stripped of daylight (36:37). The brevity of 36:4 allows that breadth. By not specifying, it includes. The messenger is upon the straight path in every sense: doctrinally, morally, practically. There is no gap between what he says and where he stands.

## Verse 36:5

> ˹This is˺ a revelation from the Almighty, Most Merciful

**A Revelation Attributed to Two Names**

*“A revelation from the Almighty, Most Merciful”* (36:5) identifies the source of what the messenger carries. The Qur'an is not described here as his speech but as tanzīl, a sending down, from al-ʿAzīz al-Raḥīm. The pairing of Almighty and Most Merciful appears repeatedly in the Qur'an, especially in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ where each story of a rejected messenger ends with *“your Lord is truly the Almighty, Most Merciful”* (26:9). The pairing is deliberate. Almighty conveys power that cannot be resisted, dominance that prevails; Most Merciful conveys gentleness that gives respite and forgives. Revelation comes from both, so it carries both qualities. It cannot be defeated, and it is not sent to destroy without warning. The sūrah's later threats of a single blast (36:29, 36:49) show might, while its promises of forgiveness and honorable reward (36:11) show mercy. The double name at the start tells the reader that both will appear.

**Why Tanzīl Matters More Than Kitāb Here**

The word tanzīl, revelation, stresses descent. The Qur'an uses kitāb, book, when it wants to stress completeness and record, and tanzīl when it wants to stress origin and movement from above to below. Here tanzīl fits the argument: a people whose forefathers were not warned (36:6) are now receiving something that has come down to them. The descent is not random; it is from the Almighty, so it cannot be blocked, and from the Most Merciful, so its descent is a gift. The sūrah later says *“We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him”* (36:69), reinforcing that this descent is not a human composition elevated by eloquence but a distinct kind of speech with a distinct source.

**Almighty and Merciful as a Balance in Daʿwah**

The contrast of might and mercy shapes how warning works. If revelation came only from the Almighty, warning would be pure threat; if only from the Merciful, warning would be easy to dismiss. Together they create the conditions for real choice. The Almighty can enforce the consequences about which the book warns; the Most Merciful gives time to respond before they arrive. The sūrah illustrates that balance in its stories. The town that rejected its messengers was seized with a single mighty blast (36:29) — might. Yet the believing man from the farthest end of the city was told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) and wished his people knew how his Lord had forgiven him (36:27) — mercy. The same balance appears in the natural signs: the sun runs for a fixed term set by the Almighty, All-Knowing (36:38), yet the earth that was dead is revived to produce grain for them to eat (36:33), an act of mercy. Naming the source as Almighty, Most Merciful at the outset prepares the reader to see both aspects in every sign.

**A Verse That Frames the Warning to Come**

The next verse explains the purpose of this revelation: *“so that you may warn a people whose forefathers were not warned”* (36:6). Purpose clauses need a source, and verse five supplies it. The warning is not the Prophet's personal anxiety about his people; it is the merciful act of the Almighty who sends a warning before judgment. The Qur'an states this principle as an universal rule: *“We never punish until We have sent a messenger”* (17:15) and *“Your Lord would never destroy towns until He sends a messenger to their mother city”* (28:59). By naming the source as Most Merciful, the sūrah presents warning itself as mercy. To be warned is better than to be seized without warning. The people described as heedless in 36:6 are therefore not without excuse and not without opportunity. They have received a book whose very descent is an act of mercy from a source whose might makes the warning credible. The two names together make the warning both kind and serious.

The Qur'an elsewhere says *“God has sent down to you a reminder, a Messenger reciting to you the clear signs”* (65:10-11), where reminder and messenger are paired as both sent down.

## Verse 36:6

> so that you may warn a people whose forefathers were not warned, and so are heedless

**Warning a People Without a Recent Warner**

*“So that you may warn a people whose forefathers were not warned, and so are heedless”* (36:6) gives the immediate purpose of the revelation described in the previous verse. The people are the Arabs, and the commentators explain that no messenger had come to them in the generations immediately before Muḥammad with a book in their own language. The Qur'an states this elsewhere: *“We have not given them any books that they study, nor have We sent them a warner before you”* (34:44). That does not mean the Arabs had never received guidance at all — Ismāʿīl lived among them, Ibrāhīm built the House — but the pure monotheism had been obscured and replaced by idol worship, so from the perspective of living memory they were without a warner. The verse so presents the Prophet's mission as both new and expected: new because no one in their fathers' time had warned them, expected because every people eventually receives a warner. The Qur'an states the rule plainly: *“There is no community but a warner has passed among them”* (35:24).

**What Heedlessness Means in This Sūrah**

Ghaflah, heedlessness, is not ignorance of facts but inattention to what facts mean. The sūrah will illustrate heedlessness with signs that are present yet unseen: dead earth that comes alive, gardens, springs, night and day, sun and moon, ships, cattle. All are visible, but their character as signs is missed. A heedless person sees grain but does not see resurrection in it; sees night but does not see a covering that will one day be removed. The verse therefore diagnoses a moral state, not an intellectual one. The sūrah later says *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46), which shows heedlessness hardening into avoidance.

**Why the Past Matters for Responsibility**

Mentioning forefathers raises a question about fairness. If their fathers were not warned, are the children blamed for their fathers' lack? The Qur'an answers that each soul is responsible for its own response once warning arrives. *“No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another”* (6:164). The purpose clause so that you may warn makes the present generation the first accountable generation in a new cycle. The principle that God does not destroy towns until a messenger has been sent to their mother city (28:59) means that punishment only comes after warning, not before. By sending a messenger now, God establishes the condition for accountability. The ominous tone noted by some exegetes comes from this: once a warner has come, the excuse of never having been warned is removed. The sūrah's later threats of a single blast that seizes people while they dispute (36:49) are only just after the warning has been given and rejected.

**Mercy in the Act of Warning Itself**

To warn is itself an act of mercy, and the previous verse named the source as Most Merciful. The Arabs could have been left in heedlessness until the blast seized them without notice, but they were not. They were given a messenger from among themselves, speaking their language, reciting a book described as rich in wisdom. The Qur'an says *“We have made it easy in your language so that they may be mindful”* (44:58). Ease of language is part of mercy. Heedlessness is cured not by harshness but by clarity, and the sūrah's clarity is remarkable: short sentences, vivid images, repeated refrains. Even the harsh images — shackles, barriers, sealed mouths — are warnings given before the events they describe, so that they need never happen to the listener. A heedless people is not insulted here; it is addressed. The address itself is the mercy that the heedlessness had made necessary.

The sūrah will later give a parable of a town where messengers came and most rejected, and one man believed and was killed, then entered Paradise (36:20-27).

## Verse 36:7

> The decree ˹of torment˺ has already been justified against most of them, for they will never believe

**Why “Most” and Not “All”**

The verse says most of them, aktharuhum, not all. That single word keeps the door open. Even in a context of widespread rejection, the Qur'an preserves the possibility of faith. The sūrah will illustrate that possibility immediately: a town rejects its messengers, yet a man comes from the farthest end of the city and believes (36:20). One believer among a town shows that most does not mean all. The same precision appears elsewhere: *“Most of them do not believe”* (2:100) often follows a description of a majority, leaving room for a minority. Theologically, this matters because it avoids determinism. The decree is justified against those who fulfill its condition — persistent disbelief — but the condition is not forced. The verse also consoles the Prophet. If most do not believe, his task is not failure; it is the same ratio previous messengers faced. The Qur'an tells him *“If you obey most of those on earth, they will lead you away from God's path”* (6:116), indicating that majority is not a measure of truth.

**The Word as Both Threat and Proof**

Qawl in Qur'anic usage can mean speech, decree, or promise. Here it is the threat of punishment that has become true. Some commentators also read it as the Qur'an itself: the Qur'an is a proof either for you or against you, as a well-known hadith states. On that reading, the book that came as mercy becomes a witness against those who ignore it. The idea appears in the sūrah's later verse *“On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify”* (36:65). First the word testifies, then the limbs testify. The progression is from external evidence to internal evidence. The word that came down as *“a revelation from the Almighty, Most Merciful”* (36:5) now stands as evidence that mercy was offered and refused. This is why the verse follows the mention of heedlessness (36:6). Heedlessness is not a neutral state; it allows a just decree to become binding because the warning that could have prevented it was present and ignored.

**How the Decree Becomes Justified**

The verb ḥaqqa means to become true, binding, incumbent. It is not simply said; it becomes real through fulfillment. The Qur'an uses the same verb for promises and threats that materialize. The threat *“I shall fill Hell”* (32:13) becomes ḥaqq when people choose the path that leads there. Exegetes debate whether this becoming-true refers to this life or the next, and the answer is both. In this life, the decree manifests as sealing, as barriers, as inability to benefit from signs. In the next, it manifests as actual punishment. The sūrah describes both stages: shackles and barriers (36:8-9) in the present, and burning in Hell (36:64) in the future. The present state is a foretaste that confirms the future state is coming. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the word becomes due because they do not believe, not the reverse. Their disbelief is the cause, the decree becoming due is the consequence. This preserves moral responsibility while acknowledging divine foreknowledge.

**A Verse That Explains the Prophet's Experience**

For the Prophet, this verse explained why his clearest arguments met denial. The Meccans were not lacking intelligence; they were fulfilling a pattern the Qur'an describes elsewhere as hearts covered and ears made heavy. The sūrah will later say *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46). That turning away is the human side; the decree becoming justified is the divine side. Both are true at once. The Qur'an holds divine decree and human choice together without collapsing one into the other. The verse does not say God prevented them from believing; it says the decree has been justified against them because they will not believe. The future tense *“they will never believe”* (36:7) is a statement of knowledge, not compulsion.

## Verse 36:8

> ˹It is as if˺ We have put shackles around their necks up to their chins, so their heads are forced up

**An Image of Restraint That Becomes Character**

This verse gives a physical image for a spiritual condition. *“We have put shackles around their necks up to their chins, so their heads are forced up”* (36:8) describes aghlāl, iron collars with cuffs that bind the hands to the neck, lifting the chin and preventing the head from bowing. The picture is of a person who cannot lower his head, cannot look down, cannot extend his hands to do good. The Qur'an uses the same term in *“We have not shackled your hand to your neck”* (17:29) as a warning against miserliness, and in *“they will be dragged, with shackles and chains around their necks”* (40:71) for the Hereafter. Here the image is placed in the present, as the state of those against whom the decree has become justified (36:7). They are not yet in the Fire, but they already live as if shackled, because their rejection has become a habit that restrains them. The shackles are not imposed arbitrarily; they are the form their own choices have taken.

**Why the Chin Is Forced Up**

The detail that heads are forced up, muqmaḥūn, is rare and evocative. Lexicographers explain it as a camel whose head is lifted because its nose-rein is pulled tight, or a man whose chin is pushed up by a collar so he cannot see the ground in front of him. Both convey inability to look where one should. A person whose head is forced up cannot see the path at his feet, cannot bow in prayer, cannot look with humility. The sūrah later says *“Had We willed, We could have transfigured them on the spot, so they could neither progress forward nor turn back”* (36:67), another image of immobilization. The shackles of 36:8 are the moral equivalent of that physical freezing. Commentators differ on whether the shackles are in this world or the next, and the strongest view is that they are both, with this world as metaphor and the next as literal. In this life, the shackles are the love of status, wealth, and ancestral custom that bind hands from giving and necks from bowing. The disbelievers who said *“Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed if He wanted to?”* (36:47) show hands shackled from charity. The barrier before and behind them in the next verse (36:9) follows the same pattern.

**The Relation to Miserliness and Generosity**

The link to 17:29, *“Do not shackle your hand to your neck”* (17:29), is not incidental. That verse warns against withholding charity. The sūrah later condemns those who refuse to donate from what Allah has provided (36:47). Shackled hands cannot give. The image therefore connects theology to economics. A community that refuses to feed the poor because it rationalizes that God could feed them if He wanted (36:47) is a community whose hands are bound to their necks by their own reasoning. The Qur'an presents generosity as a sign of a heart that is not shackled. The believer who follows the Reminder and is in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him (36:11) is promised forgiveness and a honorable reward, and part of that awe is shown in giving. The shackles are so both cause and symbol of a refusal to be open-handed, and open-handedness is presented as liberation.

The Qur'an elsewhere says *“We will turn their hearts and eyes away, as they did not believe the first time”* (6:110).

## Verse 36:9

> and have placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and covered them ˹all˺ up, so they fail to see ˹the truth˺

**Encircled From Every Side**

Verse eight described shackles around necks that force heads up; this verse adds barriers in front and behind and a covering over all. *“We have placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and covered them up, so they fail to see”* (36:9) completes the image of total enclosure. A person whose hands are bound to his neck and whose sight is blocked front and back cannot move purposefully. The barrier, sadd, is a dam or wall that blocks passage. Placing one before and one behind means neither advance nor retreat is possible. The covering, aghshaynāhum, is a veil that prevents seeing at all. The result is *“so they fail to see the truth”* (36:9). The truth is present — the sūrah will list signs in earth, night, sun, moon — but the veiled eye does not register them as signs. The verse so describes not absence of evidence but incapacity to read evidence, and that incapacity follows the moral state described before it.

**Why Barriers Before and Behind**

Exegetes offer several readings of what the two barriers represent. Some say the barrier before them is the delusion of this world that distracts, and the barrier behind is denial of the Hereafter that makes them think there is no consequence. Others say the barrier before is what is coming — punishment, resurrection — that they refuse to anticipate, and the barrier behind is what has passed — destroyed nations, previous warnings — that they refuse to remember. The Qur'an elsewhere says *“Have they not considered what is before them and what is behind them of heaven and earth? If We willed, We could cause the earth to swallow them”* (34:9). The sūrah's later warning *“Beware of what is ahead of you and what is behind you so you may be shown mercy”* (36:45) echoes the same pairing.

**Blindness That Follows Rejection**

The Qur'an distinguishes between blindness of the eye and blindness of the heart. *“It is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts in the chests that go blind”* (22:46). The covering here is of that second kind. The disbelievers can see the sun, moon, grain, and ships, but they do not see them as signs. The addition *“the truth”* in the translation clarifies that what is missed is not sensory data but its meaning. This is why the sūrah repeatedly says *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46). First they fail to see, then they turn away, which deepens the failure. The covering is both punishment and consequence: punishment because it is attributed to God — We placed, We covered — and consequence because it follows their choice to ignore earlier signs. The divine act and human act are not in competition; the divine act is the fixing of the human act into a state.

**How Barriers Relate to Guidance**

If barriers are placed, can guidance ever reach? The Qur'an answers that barriers are not permanent for those who seek removal. The same sūrah that describes barriers also says *“You can only warn those who follow the Reminder and are in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him”* (36:11). Those who follow the Reminder have not had barriers removed by accident; they have turned toward the Reminder, and turning removes barriers. The Prophet is told *“So do not let their words grieve you. Indeed, We fully know what they conceal and what they reveal”* (36:76), which implies that his duty is to deliver, not to remove barriers by force. Removal is God's act in response to a human turning. The image of barriers before and behind therefore does not teach fatalism; it teaches that persistence in rejection has a cumulative effect that eventually feels like enclosure, and that enclosure is a warning to turn before it becomes complete.

## Verse 36:10

> It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe

*“It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe”* (36:10) is a verse that resembles *“It is the same for them whether you warn them or not, they will not believe”* (2:6), which opens Sūrah al-Baqarah's description of those who disbelieve. The equality described — same whether warned or not — is not because warning lacks power, but because the hearers have placed barriers before and behind themselves (36:9) and wear shackles that force heads up (36:8).

A reader might think that if warning makes no difference, the Prophet should stop warning. The Qur'an explicitly rejects that conclusion. The Prophet is told *“Your duty is only to deliver the message clearly”* (36:17) through the town messengers, and elsewhere *“So remind, for you are only a reminder, you are not a controller over them”* (88:21-22). The duty to warn is independent of its immediate success. The verse describes a fact about this group — they will not believe — but it does not permit the messenger to abandon them, because among the most there may still be some who will believe, and because warning itself establishes proof. The Qur'an says *“We never punish until We have sent a messenger”* (17:15). Warning must be given so that on the Day when mouths are sealed and hands speak (36:65), no one can claim they were not told. The equality of warning and non-warning for this group is therefore a statement of their state, not a cancellation of the Prophet's mission.

**A Warning Against Becoming Like Them**

For the contemporary reader, the verse functions as a warning against becoming someone for whom warning makes no difference. How does a person become like that? The sūrah shows the steps: heedlessness (36:6), then justification of the decree through persistent disbelief (36:7), then shackles (36:8), then barriers (36:9), then equality of warning and non-warning (36:10). Each step is a deepening. The progression is not inevitable; it is chosen at each stage. The Qur'an says *“God does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves”* (13:11). The verse is placed early to alert the reader that spiritual states have momentum. If reminders are consistently ignored, a point arrives where reminders no longer register. That point is described here, but it is described as a warning while there is still time to avoid it. The very fact that the reader is reading this verse means he is not yet among those for whom warning and non-warning are the same, because he is still being warned and can still respond.

**The Pastoral Purpose for the Messenger**

Finally, the verse has a pastoral purpose for the Prophet. He grieved over his people's rejection, and the Qur'an repeatedly tells him not to let their words grieve him (36:76). Knowing that some will never believe, despite warning, protects the messenger from self-blame. His success is not measured by universal acceptance but by clear delivery. The town messengers said *“Our Lord knows that we have truly been sent to you”* (36:16) and *“Our duty is only to deliver the message clearly”* (36:17). Their town mostly rejected, yet their duty was fulfilled. The same is true for Muḥammad. The verse that says warning makes no difference for some is therefore not a statement of failure but of realism. It allows the Prophet to continue warning those who will benefit — those who heed the Reminder and hold reverent fear of the unseen — and to leave the result of the others to God, who knows what they conceal and what they reveal (36:76).

*“God knows what is in your hearts”* (33:51) and *“God does not wrong people in the least, but people wrong themselves”* (10:44).

## Verse 36:11

> You can only warn those who follow the Reminder and are in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him. So give them good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward

**Who Actually Benefits From Warning**

After describing those for whom warning makes no difference (36:10), this verse identifies those for whom warning does work. *“You can only warn those who follow the Reminder and are in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him”* (36:11) defines effective warning by two traits. First, following the Reminder, al-dhikr, which is a name for the Qur'an itself, as in *“We have sent down the Reminder and We will preserve it”* (15:9). To follow the Reminder is to let the Qur'an shape action, not merely to hear it. Second, being in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him, which is خشية بالغيب, a reverent fear that persists even when God is unseen. The same phrase appears in *“who feared the Most Compassionate without seeing Him and came with a penitent heart”* (50:33). The two traits together describe a person whose outward behavior follows revelation and whose inward state is aware of God even in private. Warning benefits such a person because he is already oriented toward guidance; the reminder reminds him of what he already cares about.

**The Reminder as a Name for the Qur'an**

Calling the Qur'an the Reminder, al-dhikr, is significant. Dhikr means remembrance, mention, reminder. The Qur'an presents itself not as new information about an earlier unknown deity but as a reminder of what the human already knows at a deeper level — that he was created, that he will return, that signs surround him. The sūrah's signs — dead earth revived, night stripped of day, sun and moon in orbits — are reminders because they point to what was forgotten through heedlessness (36:6). The Qur'an says *“This is only a Reminder and a clear Qur'an, to warn whoever is truly alive”* (36:69-70). The truly alive are those who follow the Reminder; the dead are those for whom warning is the same as no warning (36:10).

**Awe Without Seeing**

The second trait, awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him, describes faith in the unseen, which is the foundation of Qur'anic piety. *“This Book, there is no doubt in it, guidance for those who are mindful of God, who believe in the unseen”* (2:2-3). To be in awe of al-Raḥmān without seeing Him is to let the name Most Compassionate shape conduct even when no human is watching. The choice of al-Raḥmān here, rather than al-ʿAzīz or al-Jabbār, is deliberate. Awe of the Almighty might be fear of power; awe of the Most Compassionate is reverence that includes love and gratitude. The person described fears disappointing mercy, not just power. That kind of fear produces the generosity the sūrah later calls for, as opposed to those who refuse to donate (36:47). It also produces the humility that the shackled necks of 36:8 lack. A head forced up cannot bow; a heart in awe without seeing bows willingly.

**Good News of Forgiveness and Honourable Reward**

The command *“So give them good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward”* (36:11) follows the description of those who benefit. Forgiveness, maghfirah, comes first, because even the person who follows the Reminder and fears the unseen still needs forgiveness. No one is without fault. The honourable reward, ajr karīm, is Paradise, described later as busy enjoyment, shade, couches, fruits, and peace (36:55-58). The adjective honourable, karīm, indicates a reward that is generous, dignified, and given by a generous host. The same phrase appears in *“For them is forgiveness and an honourable reward”* (67:12) for those who fear their Lord unseen. The pairing of forgiveness and honourable reward shows the balance of mercy: sins are covered, then goodness is honoured. The Prophet is told to give good news, bashshir, which is the opposite of warning, indhār. Warning and good news are the two modes of prophetic speech, and they are directed to different audiences. Warning is for all, but good news is specifically for those who follow and fear.

## Verse 36:12

> It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and what they leave behind. Everything is listed by Us in a perfect Record

**Resurrection and Record Together**

This verse pairs two divine acts that the sūrah's audience might have thought separate: giving life to the dead and keeping a record. *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and what they leave behind”* (36:12) says both are God's work, and both are certain. The first clause answers the central doubt the sūrah will address — *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78) — by asserting resurrection as fact. The verse that says *“On that Day no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do”* (36:54) depends on both.

**The Perfect Record, Imām Mubīn**

*“Everything is listed by Us in a perfect Record”* (36:12) uses imām mubīn, a clear registry or leading record. Imām means leader, something followed, and mubīn means clear. Some exegetes take it as the Preserved Tablet, al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ, in which everything is inscribed from beginning to end, as in *“No calamity strikes on earth or in yourselves but it is in a Book before We bring it into being”* (57:22). Others take it as the record of deeds that will be given to each person, as in *“Every person's deeds We have fastened to his neck, and on the Day of Resurrection We will bring out for him a book which he will find wide open”* (17:13). Both are true at different levels: an universal record that encompasses all, and individual records that are derived from it. The sūrah later says *“This is the Hell you were warned of. Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:63-64) and *“On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak”* (36:65), showing that the record is not only written but also testified by limbs. The perfect record is so both preserved and made manifest.

**Traces as an Encouragement and a Warning**

The idea that traces are written is both encouragement and warning. Encouragement, because a small act that continues — teaching a verse, planting a tree, building a place of prayer — keeps being recorded after the actor is gone. Warning, because a harmful trace also continues. The town story that follows shows both: the messengers' message left a trace in the believing man who came rushing (36:20), and the town's rejection left a trace in its destruction by a single blast (36:29). The reader is invited to think about what traces he is leaving now. The sūrah later says *“Had We willed, We could have transfigured them on the spot”* (36:67), which would cut off traces immediately, but God allows time — *“except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a little while”* (36:44) — so that traces can accumulate and repentance remains possible. The record is perfect, but the time to add to it is limited.

The final verse of the sūrah says *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is the authority over all things, and to Whom you will all be returned”* (36:83). Return to Him is the same as resurrection of the dead, and authority over all things includes authority to keep a perfect record. Verse twelve so anticipates the ending. It also anticipates the scene of judgment where *“no soul will be wronged in the least”* (36:54) because everything is listed. The perfect record guarantees justice; resurrection guarantees that justice will be experienced. Together they answer the heedlessness diagnosed earlier. The Qur'an says *“Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:7-8).

The hadith *“When the son of Adam dies, his deeds end except for three: ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, or a righteous child who prays for him”* — recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1631 — illustrates what is left behind.

Placing resurrection right after the division between those who do not benefit from warning and those who do (36:10-11) is strategic.

## Verse 36:13

> Give them an example ˹O Prophet˺ of the residents of a town, when the messengers came to them

**Why a Town, Not a Nation**

The word qaryah, town, suggests a settled, identifiable community with markets, walls, and a sense of collective identity. Towns in the Qur'an often represent self-sufficiency that breeds arrogance. *“We never sent a warner to a town but its affluent said, We disbelieve in what you have been sent with”* (34:34). A town is small enough that messengers can walk its streets, yet large enough that its rejection is communal. The sūrah's earlier description of barriers before and behind (36:9) fits a town whose gates are closed to truth. The town in this example receives messengers, not a book directly, which matches the Meccan situation where a messenger walks among them. The verse also echoes the principle *“We never destroy towns until We send a messenger to their mother city”* (28:59). A town is destroyed only after warning, so telling its story is itself an act of mercy to the next town — Mecca — which is still being warned.

**Messengers as a Test**

*“When the messengers came to them”* (36:13) presents arrival as a test. The messengers come; the town must respond. The Qur'an elsewhere says *“Every community has a messenger, and when their messenger comes, judgment is passed between them with justice”* (10:47). The coming of messengers initiates accountability. The sūrah does not say the town was already doomed; it says messengers came, implying opportunity. The later verse *“Oh pity, such beings! No messenger ever came to them without being mocked”* (36:30) shows that mocking is the usual response, not the exception. By framing the story as an example for the Prophet to give, the verse also defines his role: he is to give examples, not just rules. Stories carry what arguments alone cannot — the emotional weight of a man rushing from the farthest end of the city (36:20) and a community extinguished at once (36:29). The example is therefore pedagogical, not merely historical.

**How Example Functions in Qur'anic Persuasion**

The Qur'an uses mathal frequently to move from abstract to concrete. *“God presents an example: a servant owned and unable to do a thing, and one We have provided with good provision”* (16:75) is one such. Examples work because they bypass defensiveness. Once he judges that town's rejection as unjust, he has judged his own. This is the rhetorical strategy of Nathan with David: tell a story about another man's lamb, then say you are the man. Yā Sīn does the same. It tells of a town that said *“You are only humans like us”* (36:15), which is exactly what the Quraysh said about Muḥammad.

**An Example That Prepares for the Believer**

The town story is not only about rejection; it contains a model of belief. A man comes from the farthest end of the city, rushing, advising his people to follow the messengers (36:20). He is the counterpoint to the town. By introducing the story as an example of residents of a town when messengers came, the verse sets up both the majority and the minority response. The majority rejects; one man believes. That division mirrors the earlier division between those for whom warning is the same as no warning (36:10) and those who follow the Reminder (36:11). The example therefore does not end in despair. It shows that even in a town that as a whole rejects, an individual can break away, follow the messengers, and be told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26). The command to give an example is so also a command to give hope: the pattern of rejection is common, but the possibility of individual faith remains open, even when it costs the believer his life.

## Verse 36:14

> We sent them two messengers, but they rejected both. So We reinforced ˹the two˺ with a third, and they declared, “We have indeed been sent to you ˹as messengers˺.”

**Two Messengers, Then a Third**

This verse details the town's test. *“We sent them two messengers, but they rejected both. So We reinforced the two with a third, and they declared, We have indeed been sent to you as messengers”* (36:14) shows escalation on both sides. The town receives two, rejects both, and instead of being left to its rejection, it receives a third as reinforcement. The Arabic ʿazaznā, We reinforced, implies strengthening with might and numbers. The sequence shows divine patience. One messenger could have been enough to establish proof; two provide corroboration; three make denial harder to justify. The Qur'an elsewhere says *“We sent messengers in succession”* (23:44) and that communities who denied them were seized. Here succession is within a single town. The declaration *“We have indeed been sent to you as messengers”* (36:14) is simple and direct, without elaborate argument. Messengers do not begin with philosophy; they begin with identity: we have been sent to you. The content of the message is implied in the sending.

**Why Two, Then Three**

The number matters for legal and psychological reasons. In Islamic law, testimony of two is stronger than one; two witnesses establish a claim. Sending two messengers provides that corroboration. The town's later excuse — *“You are only humans like us”* (36:15) — would have applied to one as well as three; increasing the number does not change the excuse, which reveals that the excuse is pretext. The sūrah's own context supports this: Muḥammad is one messenger among many (36:3), yet his single presence is enough to establish proof. The town's persistence in rejection despite abundance makes its eventual seizure by a single blast (36:29) proportionate.

**The Declaration as Clear Proclamation**

The messengers' statement *“We have indeed been sent to you as messengers”* (36:14) is a model of clear proclamation, balāgh mubīn, which they later define as their only duty (36:17). Clear proclamation does not mean elaborate rhetoric; it means unambiguous claim. We have been sent to you. The clarity is in identity and address: we are sent, and you are the recipients. No ambiguity, no hidden agenda. The sūrah stresses that true messengers ask no reward (36:21) and are rightly guided, so their claim is supported by character. The town's response will be to call them liars (36:15) and threaten stoning (36:18), which are attacks on character, not refutations of message. The messengers' simple declaration exposes that. When a clear claim is met with insult and threat rather than argument, the inadequacy lies with the respondents, not the claimants. The verse teaches how to recognize prophetic speech: it is clear, direct, and about being sent.

**Reinforcement as Mercy and Proof**

Sending a third messenger after two have been rejected can be read as mercy — another chance — and as proof — additional witness. Both are true. Mercy, because the town is given more time and more voices before judgment; proof, because the town can no longer say it did not understand or did not have enough evidence. The Qur'an says *“We never punish until We have sent a messenger”* (17:15), and here three are sent. The reinforcement also shows that divine guidance does not withdraw after first rejection. The sūrah later says *“Had We willed, We could have blinded their eyes”* (36:66) and *“transfigured them on the spot”* (36:67), but God does not. Instead, He reinforces messengers. That patience is part of the name Most Merciful mentioned at the sūrah's start (36:5). Even when reinforcement is rejected, the fact that it was given will be part of the record that is kept in a perfect registry (36:12). The town's traces include both its rejection and the abundance of warning it received.

The Qur'an says *“Whoever obeys the messenger has obeyed God”* (4:80).

The Quraysh said Muḥammad was a man like them, but the Qur'an says *“We have sent you as a messenger”* (4:79).

## Verse 36:15

> The people replied, “You are only humans like us, and the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything. You are simply lying!”

**The Humanity Objection**

The town's reply is the classic objection to messengers: *“You are only humans like us, and the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything. You are simply lying”* (36:15). The objection has two parts. First, you are humans like us, so you cannot be messengers; messengers should be angels or superhuman. Second, the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything, so there is no message to carry. Both parts aim to disqualify the messengers without engaging their message. The Qur'an records this objection repeatedly: *“They said, Has God sent a human as messenger?”* (17:94), *“What ails this messenger who eats food and walks in markets? Why was not an angel sent down with him?”* (25:7). The objection assumes that humanity and messengership are incompatible, while the Qur'an asserts they are inseparable. *“We never sent before you except men to whom We revealed”* (16:43). A human messenger is necessary because he can be an example; an angel cannot model human patience, charity, or repentance.

**Why the Name Most Compassionate Appears Here**

The town says al-Raḥmān, the Most Compassionate, has not revealed anything. The choice of name is pointed. Al-Raḥmān is the name that conveys universal mercy, the mercy that sends rain, gives life to dead earth, and provides ships and cattle. To say the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything is to deny that mercy includes guidance. It is to accept mercy in the form of grain and gardens but reject mercy in the form of warning. The sūrah later shows that al-Raḥmān's mercy includes both provision and warning: *“Glory be to the One Who created all things in pairs”* (36:36) is mercy in creation, and *“Peace will be their greeting from the Merciful Lord”* (36:58) is mercy in the next life. The town wants mercy without message, provision without accountability. The Qur'an elsewhere says *“They know the outward aspect of worldly life, but of the Hereafter they are heedless”* (30:7). Recognizing mercy in nature but denying it in revelation is a selective acknowledgment that the sūrah critiques.

**Accusation of Lying as a Defense**

*“You are simply lying”* (36:15) is the town's conclusion. It does not say we disagree or we need more evidence; it says you are lying. Accusing messengers of lying is easier than refuting them, because lying attacks motive, not content. If messengers are liars, their message need not be examined. The Qur'an notes that this accusation was made against many messengers: *“They said, You are but liars”* (26:185) to Shuʿayb, and similar to others. The messengers' response in the next verse — *“Our Lord knows that we have truly been sent to you”* (36:16) — appeals to God's knowledge rather than to self-defense, showing that their concern is not reputation but truth.

**How the Qur'an Answers the Humanity Objection Elsewhere**

The Qur'an answers the humanity objection in several ways. First, it says an angel messenger would still appear as a man to be understandable: *“Had We made him an angel, We would have made him appear as a man”* (6:9). Second, it says human messengers are a mercy because they can be followed: *“There has come to you a messenger from among yourselves, grievous to him is what you suffer”* (9:128). Third, it says the message, not the messenger's nature, is the point: *“Say, I am only a man like you, it is revealed to me that your God is One God”* (18:110). The difference between messenger and ordinary human is not biology but revelation. The town focuses on shared humanity — like us — and ignores the distinction — revelation. That selective focus is the essence of heedlessness (36:6): seeing what is similar and missing what is distinctive. The sūrah later says *“Follow those who ask no reward and are rightly guided”* (36:21), giving criteria for distinguishing true messengers from impostors that do not depend on superhuman nature.

*“Nothing is said to you but what was said to messengers before you”* (41:43).

## Verse 36:16

> The messengers responded, “Our Lord knows that we have truly been sent to you

**Appeal to Divine Knowledge**

The messengers answer the accusation of lying not with self-praise but with appeal to God's knowledge. *“Our Lord knows that we have truly been sent to you”* (36:16) places the dispute before a higher judge. The town said you are lying (36:15); the messengers say God knows we are truthful. The shift from human argument to divine witness is characteristic of prophetic speech. Messengers do not need to prove sincerity by elaborate oaths; they invoke the One who sent them. The Qur'an records similar appeals: *“God is sufficient as witness between me and you”* (13:43) and *“Say, God suffices as witness between me and you”* (29:52). The appeal also shows confidence. A liar would avoid invoking God as witness because divine witness would expose him; a truthful person welcomes it. The messengers' calm reliance on God's knowledge contrasts with the town's agitated accusation, and that contrast itself is evidence. Truthful speech is often quieter than false accusation, and the sūrah lets the quiet answer stand without embellishment.

**Why the Response Is Shorter Than the Accusation**

The town's accusation had three claims: you are humans like us, the Most Compassionate revealed nothing, you are lying. The messengers' reply addresses only the last, but by addressing it they address all. The brevity is rhetorical. The sūrah later defines their duty as only clear delivery (36:17), not exhaustive debate. Those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21) do not waste words defending ego; they state the fact of being sent and leave judgment to God. The Qur'an praises such restraint elsewhere: *“When the ignorant address them, they say Peace”* (25:63).

**Our Lord Knows as a Theological Claim**

Saying Our Lord knows is not only a claim about this incident but about God's attribute of knowledge. The sūrah stresses divine knowledge throughout: *“That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing”* (36:38) for the sun's course, *“He has perfect knowledge of every created being”* (36:79) for resurrection, *“We fully know what they conceal and what they reveal”* (36:76) for the Prophet's consolation. Knowledge is what makes judgment just. If God knows that messengers were sent, He also knows how they were received, and He records both in a perfect registry (36:12). The messengers' appeal to divine knowledge therefore anticipates the final accounting where *“no soul will be wronged in the least”* (36:54). The town thinks its rejection is a private matter between humans; the messengers say it is known to the Lord who knows all traces. That reframes the dispute from social to cosmic.

**A Verse That Prepares for Clear Delivery**

Verse sixteen is incomplete without seventeen. The messengers say Our Lord knows we have been sent, and then *“Our duty is only to deliver the message clearly”* (36:17). Knowledge and duty are paired. The first is God's concern, the second theirs. This division protects messengers from despair when their message is rejected. They are responsible for clarity, not for conversion. The Qur'an says to Muḥammad *“You do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills”* (28:56). The town messengers embody that principle. They have delivered clearly to a town that called them liars, and they still affirm that God knows they were sent. Their confidence is not in the town's response but in divine knowledge and in having fulfilled their duty. That confidence is what allows them to continue despite threat of stoning (36:18). The verse is short, but it holds a theology of prophetic mission that sustains messengers in every generation.

## Verse 36:17

> And our duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly.”

**Defining the Limits of Messengership**

*“Our duty is only to deliver the message clearly”* (36:17) defines prophetic responsibility with precision. The messengers do not claim power to guide, to compel, or to punish. Their duty is balāgh mubīn, clear delivery. The Qur'an repeats this definition for Muḥammad and all messengers: *“The messenger's duty is only to deliver clearly”* (5:99, 29:18, 64:12). Clear delivery has two components: clarity of content — the message is understandable — and clarity of manner — the delivery is open, not hidden or coercive. The town messengers have delivered in both senses. They declared *“We have indeed been sent to you as messengers”* (36:14) and appealed to God's knowledge (36:16). Their message is clear; the town's rejection is not due to obscurity. This definition protects both messenger and audience. The messenger is protected from blame for others' disbelief; the audience is protected from compulsion, because belief must be chosen after clear delivery, not forced.

**Why Clarity Is Enough**

The Qur'an presents clarity as enough to establish proof. *“We have made it clear”* appears as a refrain after signs are given. If a message is clear and still rejected, the rejection is willful, not excusable by confusion. The town said *“You are only humans like us”* (36:15), which is not a response to message content but a dismissal of messengers. Clear delivery exposes that dismissal. The sūrah's later signs — dead earth revived (36:33), night stripped of day (36:37), sun on fixed course (36:38) — are also clear, yet *“Whenever a sign comes to them, they turn away from it”* (36:46). Clarity does not guarantee acceptance, but it guarantees justice. On the Day when *“no soul will be wronged in the least”* (36:54), no one can say the message was unclear. The messengers' statement therefore anticipates final judgment. Their duty was to deliver clearly; they did; the record in a perfect registry (36:12) preserves both delivery and response.

**The Meaning of Mubīn, Clear**

Mubīn means clear, manifest, making clear. It describes both the message and the delivery. The message is clear in itself — its monotheism, its warning, its promise — and the delivery makes it clear to others. The Qur'an calls itself a clear book, *“a clear Qur'an”* (36:69), and says it was made easy in the Prophet's language so people may be mindful (44:58). The sūrah's arguments about resurrection from green trees producing fire (36:80) and from creation of heavens and earth (36:81) are clear in logic but require reflection. It says *“We see you as a bad omen”* (36:18), shifting from content to superstition. Clear delivery cannot cure superstition unless the hearer is willing to examine his own omens, which the messengers point out lie within himself (36:19).

**How This Verse Consoles the Prophet**

For Muḥammad, whose people said *“You are only humans like us”* (36:15) in different wording, this verse is consolation. His duty is only clear delivery, not conversion of all. The sūrah opens by affirming he is truly among messengers upon straight path (36:3-4) and that his revelation is from Almighty, Most Merciful (36:5). His delivery is therefore clear by virtue of source and path. If most do not believe (36:7), that does not diminish clarity. The verse also links to the later assurance *“So do not let their words grieve you. Indeed, We fully know what they conceal and what they reveal”* (36:76). God knows both the clarity of delivery and the concealment of rejection. The messenger's duty is done; God's knowledge encompasses the rest. This division allows the Prophet to continue warning those who follow Reminder (36:11) without being crushed by those who do not. Clear delivery is a sustainable mission because its success is measured by fidelity, not numbers.

## Verse 36:18

> The people replied, “We definitely see you as a bad omen for us. If you do not desist, we will certainly stone you ˹to death˺ and you will be touched with a painful punishment from us.”

**From Argument to Superstition and Threat**

The town's response escalates from denial to superstition to violence. *“We definitely see you as a bad omen for us. If you do not desist, we will certainly stone you to death and you will be touched with a painful punishment from us”* (36:18) shows how rejection hardens. First they said you are humans like us and liars (36:15). When messengers responded that God knows they were sent (36:16) and their duty is clear delivery (36:17), the town abandons argument and resorts to ṭiyarah, seeing bad omens. Ṭiyarah in Arab culture was interpreting bird flight or other signs as portending evil, then attributing that evil to persons. The Qur'an records similar accusations: Pharaoh's people said to Mūsā *“We see you and those with you as a bad omen”* (27:47), and earlier communities said the same. The move from you are liars to you are bad omens is a move from rational to superstitious rejection, which is harder to counter because it does not engage content at all.

**Why Bad Omen Accusation Is Convenient**

Calling messengers bad omens is convenient because it reverses causality. Instead of we are being warned because we are doing wrong, it says we are suffering because you arrived. The town attributes its misfortunes — drought, illness, economic hardship, whatever they experienced — to the presence of messengers rather than to its own deeds. The messengers' reply in the next verse exposes this: *“Your bad omen lies within yourselves”* (36:19). The Qur'an says elsewhere *“When good comes to them, they say This is from God, but when evil befalls them, they say This is from you”* (4:78), and *“No affliction befalls except by God's permission”* (64:11).

**Stoning as a Communal Punishment**

The threat *“we will certainly stone you to death”* (36:18) is rajm, stoning, a public, communal form of execution that involves the whole town. It is not assassination but collective rejection made physical. Stoning appears elsewhere as threat against messengers: *“If you do not desist, O Ibrāhīm, I will surely stone you”* (19:46) says his father, and the people of Shuʿayb say *“Were it not for your clan, we would have stoned you”* (11:91). The threat is meant to intimidate through numbers. A single person can be ignored; a town that stones together enforces conformity. The addition *“and you will be touched with a painful punishment from us”* (36:18) suggests torture before or alongside stoning, indicating that the town claims authority to punish. It says punishment will be from us, as if it has the right to chastise. The messengers, who said their Lord knows they were sent (36:16), face a town that says punishment will be from us. Two authorities are contrasted: divine sending versus human threat.

**The Psychological Mechanism of Omen Thinking**

Omen thinking, ṭiyarah, is addressed in hadith. The Prophet said *“There is no ṭiyarah, but the best is good optimism”* and *“Ṭiyarah is shirk”* in some narrations, recorded in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3910 and graded authentic by many, indicating that attributing causation to omens compromises reliance on God. The psychological mechanism is that humans seek cause for misfortune, and when true cause — own deeds — is uncomfortable, they project cause onto outsiders. The town's misfortune, if any, likely came from its own injustice or natural cycles, but attributing it to messengers is easier. The Qur'an says *“Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your hands have earned, and He pardons much”* (42:30). It tells the town that its bad omen is its own deeds that have become a collar around its neck (36:8) and barriers before and behind (36:9). The town externalizes what is internal.

## Verse 36:19

> The messengers said, “Your bad omen lies within yourselves. Are you saying this because you are reminded ˹of the truth˺? In fact, you are a transgressing people.”

**Your Omen Is Within Yourselves**

The messengers' reply reframes the town's superstition. *“Your bad omen lies within yourselves”* (36:19) — ṭāʾirukum maʿakum — says the cause of misfortune is not the messengers but the town's own deeds. The Arabic ṭāʾir, literally bird, was used for omen because Arabs divined by birds, but it came to mean fortune, destiny, or omen. The Qur'an uses it elsewhere: *“For every person We have fastened his omen to his neck”* (17:13), meaning his deeds and their consequences are attached to him. The messengers say your bird, your omen, is with you, not with us. It is your choices that produce your outcomes. The same idea appears in *“Whatever affliction befalls you is from what your hands have earned”* (42:30). By locating omen within selves, the messengers dismantle scapegoating. The town wants to stone messengers to remove bad luck; the messengers say bad luck, if any, will remain because its source is internal. This is the reflexive nature of deeds emphasized throughout the Qur'an: miserliness harms the miserly (47:38), deception deceives the deceiver (2:9), plotting plots against the plotter (52:42).

**Are You Saying This Because You Are Reminded**

The next phrase *“Are you saying this because you are reminded of the truth?”* (36:19) exposes the town's motive. The town's accusation of bad omen comes precisely when it is reminded. Reminder, dhikr, is what messengers bring, and the town's hostility is triggered by reminder, not by harm. The rhetorical question implies: you call us bad omens only because we reminded you, so your accusation is not about omens at all but about dislike of being reminded. The Qur'an says *“When God alone is mentioned, the hearts of those who do not believe in Hereafter shrink with aversion”* (39:45). Aversion to reminder is the real cause; omen language is cover. Some readings of the Arabic render the phrase as *“Even if you are reminded, you still say this?”* or *“Because you are reminded?”* All point to same: reminder is the trigger, not misfortune. The town is not suffering because messengers came; it is accusing messengers of bringing bad luck because it does not want to be reminded.

**You Are a Transgressing People**

The final clause *“In fact, you are a transgressing people”* (36:19) names the condition. Mus rifūn, transgressing, prodigal, exceeding limits. The word isrāf means crossing bounds, whether in wealth, power, or denial. The town exceeds limits by threatening stoning for clear delivery, by calling mercy bad omen, by attributing to messengers what belongs to itself. The Qur'an uses musrif for those who exceed in denial: *“so We reward the transgressor, the disbeliever”* (20:127) and Pharaoh is called *“indeed a transgressor”* (10:83). Transgression is not just sin but excess. The town does not merely disbelieve; it threatens painful punishment from itself (36:18), claiming authority that belongs to God. That excess is isrāf. The messengers' diagnosis moves from specific — your omen is with you — to general — you are transgressing people. Specific misattribution is symptom; transgression is disease. By naming it, they offer the town a chance to see itself accurately, which is first step to change.

**How This Verse Corrects Superstition**

This verse is one of the Qur'an's clearest corrections of superstition. The Prophet taught *“There is no contagion without God's permission, no bad omen, no hāmah, no Ṣafar”* in a hadith recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5707, rejecting pre-Islamic superstitions that attributed causation to omens. The messengers' reply does same: no, your fortune is not determined by our arrival; it is determined by your response to reminder. This teaching liberates. If omen lies within self, then change of self changes fortune. The town can stop transgressing and its omen changes. The sūrah later says *“Had We willed, We could have blinded their eyes”* (36:66) but God does not; He allows time for change.

## Verse 36:20

> Then from the farthest end of the city a man came, rushing. He advised, “O my people! Follow the messengers

**A Man From the Farthest End**

After the town's threat of stoning (36:18) and the messengers' correction (36:19), a new figure appears: *“Then from the farthest end of the city a man came, rushing”* (36:20). The detail farthest end, aqṣā al-madīnah, is not incidental. It suggests distance, marginality, and effort. This man is not from the town center where power resides; he is from the outskirts, yet he rushes, yasʿā, running with urgency. The verb saʿā implies striving, hurrying for a purpose. The Qur'an uses it for those who strive for Hereafter: *“Whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it”* (17:19). His rushing contrasts with the town's static barriers before and behind (36:9). While the town is enclosed, he moves. While the town threatens, he advises. His appearance breaks the deadlock between messengers and town, showing that even in a rejecting community, an individual can respond. The sūrah earlier said *“You can only warn those who follow the Reminder”* (36:11), and here is one who does.

**Follow the Messengers: The Core Advice**

His advice is simple: *“O my people! Follow the messengers”* (36:20). He says my people, qawmī, claiming kinship even as he disagrees, showing that his counsel comes from belonging, not enmity. He does not say follow me; he says follow the messengers, directing attention away from himself to those who were sent. That self-effacement is mark of sincerity. He is not seeking leadership; he is pointing to those who ask no reward (36:21). The command to follow, ittabiʿū, is the same used for following the Reminder (36:11) and following the straight path. Following messengers means accepting their message that God alone is to be worshiped and that return is to Him (36:22). The rushing man's advice is so not tribal but religious. He asks his people to follow those who call to worship the Originator, not those who threaten stoning. The contrast between follow the messengers and we will stone you (36:18) is stark: one invites, the other coerces.

**Rushing as a Sign of Faith**

Rushing, coming quickly, is praised in the Qur'an when it is toward good. *“Race to forgiveness from your Lord”* (3:133) and *“Hasten to remembrance of God”* (62:9) use similar urgency. The man rushes because he senses time is short — messengers are threatened, town is about to overstep, and opportunity to support truth is fleeting. His rushing is physical expression of inner conviction. The sūrah later describes those who will rush from graves to their Lord when trumpet is blown (36:51), using same root. One rushing is in this life toward messengers; the other rushing is in next life toward judgment. The parallel suggests that how one rushes now determines how one will rush then. The man from farthest end rushes toward guidance now, so his later rushing will be toward mercy, as he is told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26). The town that does not rush now will rush then in terror.

**How This Verse Prepares for His Martyrdom**

This verse begins the man's intervention, which will end with his killing and entry into Paradise (36:26). It shows that following messengers can be dangerous, but that danger is followed by forgiveness and honor (36:27). His example answers the question of what it means to follow the Reminder and fear the Most Compassionate unseen (36:11) when following is costly. It means rushing from farthest end, advising even those who may kill you, and trusting that return is to the One who originated you (36:22). The verse is short, but it holds a complete portrait of a believer who acts alone against a crowd and whose single act is recorded in a perfect registry (36:12).

The Qur'an often notes that early believers were not elites: *“The arrogant among his people said, We see you only as a man like us, and we see only the lowest among us following you”* (11:27).

## Verse 36:21

> Follow those who ask no reward of you, and are ˹rightly˺ guided

**Two Criteria for True Guides**

The rushing man continues: *“Follow those who ask no reward of you, and are rightly guided”* (36:21). He gives his people two tests to distinguish true messengers from impostors. First, they ask no reward, ajr, from you. Second, they are rightly guided, muhtadūn. The first is about motive, the second about content. A person who seeks no worldly gain — money, status, power — is more likely to be sincere; a person who is rightly guided has something worth following. The Qur'an stresses the first criterion repeatedly. Nūḥ says *“I ask you no reward for it; my reward is only with God”* (10:72), and similar statements come from Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ, Shuʿayb (26:109, 127, 145, 164, 180). Muḥammad is told to say *“I ask you no reward for it”* (6:90, 38:86, 42:23). The absence of reward-seeking is a sign that the call is for God, not self. The town messengers have asked nothing; they have only delivered clearly (36:17) despite threat of stoning (36:18).

**Why No Reward Matters**

Asking no reward matters because it removes conflict of interest. If messengers sought payment, their message could be dismissed as self-serving. By asking nothing, they leave the town with no excuse. The town cannot say they want our wealth; they want only that we worship the One who originated us (36:22). The criterion also protects the community from exploitation. False leaders often seek reward — money, obedience, deference — while true guides seek God's pleasure. The believing man's own behavior matches the criterion: he rushes from farthest end, not for payment, but out of concern for his people. The sūrah later contrasts those who take gods besides God hoping to be helped (36:74) with messengers who seek no help from people.

**Rightly Guided as Second Test**

The second criterion, rightly guided, means they follow guidance themselves and call to it. Guidance in Qur'anic usage includes correct belief, worship of God alone, and moral uprightness. The messengers are guided because they worship the One who originated them and to whom return is (36:22), they do not attribute bad omens to others (36:19), and they deliver clearly without transgression. The town is transgressing (36:19), so it is not guided. The contrast is between those who exceed limits and those who are upon straight path (36:4). The rushing man tells his people to follow the guided, not the transgressing. This is practical wisdom: follow those whose lives show guidance. The sūrah earlier said messengers are upon straight path (36:4); now the believer says they are rightly guided, linking his testimony to the opening affirmation. His statement is so not new doctrine but recognition of what was already affirmed.

**The Connection to the Prophet's Own Mission**

For Mecca, this verse directly describes Muḥammad. He asked no reward; his reward was with God. He was rightly guided, upon straight path (36:4), delivering a revelation from Almighty, Most Merciful (36:5). The Quraysh accused him of seeking leadership or wealth, but his life showed otherwise. He lived simply, gave charity, and endured hardship. The town's believing man, by giving criteria that fit Muḥammad, indirectly validates him. The Meccan listener who accepts that one should follow those who ask no reward and are guided must then ask whether Muḥammad fits. The sūrah does not force answer; it lets story do work. The believing man's criteria also answer the earlier objection that messengers are only humans like us (36:15). Yes, they are humans, but humans who ask no reward and are guided are not like those who seek reward and transgress. Shared humanity does not mean shared character. The distinction is moral, not biological.

The Qur'an says *“Follow the way of those who turn to Me”* (31:15), indicating that following is about turning, not lineage.

## Verse 36:22

> And why should I not worship the One Who has originated me, and to Whom you will be returned

**Worship as Recognition of Origination**

Worship, ʿibādah, in Qur'anic language is not only ritual but recognition of lordship. To worship the Originator is to acknowledge that existence is gift, not self-produced. The man asks why should I not worship, implying that not worshiping would be strange, contrary to reason. The Qur'an says *“How can you disbelieve in God when you were dead and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die, then give you life again, then to Him you will be returned?”* (2:28). The argument from origination to worship is foundational. The sūrah later asks *“Do they not see that We singlehandedly created for them cattle which are under their control?”* (36:71) and *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop?”* (36:77), both arguing from creation to gratitude. The believing man's question is the personal version of those arguments. He has been originated; therefore he worships. His people have also been originated; why do they not?

**Return as Motivation**

*“To Whom you will be returned”* (36:22) introduces accountability. Origination alone might produce gratitude, but return produces urgency. If return is to God, then deeds done in between will be judged. The sūrah stresses return throughout: *“Everything is listed in a perfect Record”* (36:12), *“They will all be brought before Us”* (36:32), *“You will be rewarded except for what you used to do”* (36:54), *“To Him you will all be returned”* (36:83). Return is not annihilation but meeting. The believing man reminds his people that their return is to the same One who originated them, so their origin and destiny are linked. This link undermines idol worship, which he will address next: *“How could I take besides Him other gods whose intercession would not benefit me nor could they save me?”* (36:23). If return is to the Originator, intercession from others is irrelevant.

The word faṭara connects to fiṭrah, primordial nature, mentioned in 30:30. The Qur'an says God originated mankind upon a primordial nature that inclines to tawḥīd, and that there is no altering God's creation. The believing man's question appeals to that nature. The sūrah later says *“Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy, but to worship Me alone? This is the Straight Path”* (36:60-61). The Qur'an says *“We have certainly created man in best stature, then We return him to lowest of low, except those who believe and do righteous deeds”* (95:4-6).

**A Personal Creed That Becomes Universal**

Although spoken by one man in one town, this verse became a creed quoted by believers in many contexts. It is personal — why should I not worship — yet universal — to Whom you will be returned. It models how to argue for faith without arrogance: start from own origination, include others in return, and let question do work. The man does not say you must worship because I say so; he says why should I not worship, inviting listeners to ask same of themselves. His question is still asked today whenever someone reflects on existence. Why should I not worship the One who originated me? The sūrah answers with signs: dead earth revived (36:33), night stripped of day (36:37), sun and moon in orbits (36:38-40), ships and cattle (36:41-42, 71-72). All are acts of origination and sustenance that point to the Originator. The believing man's short question opens the door to that long catalogue of signs, and his fate — entering Paradise and wishing his people knew (36:26-27) — shows where that question leads when answered sincerely.

The Qur'an uses similar shifts: *“He is the One who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop”* (40:67) moves from general to specific.

## Verse 36:23

> How could I take besides Him other gods whose intercession would not be of any benefit to me, nor could they save me if the Most Compassionate intended to harm me

**Why Other Gods Cannot Intercede**

The believing man continues his argument: *“How could I take besides Him other gods whose intercession would not be of any benefit to me, nor could they save me if the Most Compassionate intended to harm me?”* (36:23). He has argued from origination and return (36:22); now he argues from powerlessness of idols. Intercession, shafāʿah, in Arab belief was that idols or lesser deities could plead for their worshipers before the high God. The Qur'an corrects this: intercession belongs completely to God, and none can intercede except by His permission. *“Who is there who can intercede with Him except by His permission?”* (2:255) and *“On that Day intercession will not benefit except one to whom the Most Compassionate has given permission”* (20:109). If the Most Compassionate intends harm — as consequence of disbelief — no idol's intercession can prevent it, because idols have no independent power. The man's question exposes the futility: why worship what cannot help?

**The Choice of Most Compassionate Again**

He says if al-Raḥmān, the Most Compassionate, intended harm for me. The use of Most Compassionate for harm seems paradoxical, but it is deliberate. Even harm that comes from God is within His compassion, because it is just and may lead to repentance, or because His compassion is the standard against which harm is measured. The town earlier said the Most Compassionate revealed nothing (36:15), denying His merciful guidance. He so affirms both that al-Raḥmān does reveal and that His will is decisive. The sūrah uses al-Raḥmān for both mercy and decisive will: *“This must be what the Most Compassionate warned us of”* (36:52) on the Day of Resurrection.

Verse twenty-three is the early, personal version of that critique. The believing man, facing his people, says idols cannot intercede or save. The sūrah, addressing its audience, says idols cannot help even though they are guarded. Both point to reversal: worshipers think they are protected by idols, but in fact they end up protecting idols. The man's question also anticipates the sealing of mouths and testimony of hands and feet (36:65). The only intercession that benefits is by God's permission, and permission is given to those whose word is pleasing to Him (20:109).

**A Creed That Remains Relevant**

The question remains relevant because humans still take besides God other things hoping for intercession or rescue — wealth, status, connections, ideologies — that cannot benefit when divine will decrees otherwise. The believing man's criterion is practical: does this thing benefit me or save me if the Most Compassionate intends otherwise? If not, why take it as god? The Qur'an says *“If God touches you with harm, none can remove it but He, and if He intends good for you, none can repel His bounty”* (10:107). The man's argument is that monotheism is not only true but rational. To worship the Originator to Whom return is (36:22) and to reject what cannot intercede or save (36:23) is to align belief with reality. His question, asked while rushing from farthest end of city, becomes a timeless test for any object of devotion: can it intercede? Can it save? That clarity is why his words are preserved, and why he is told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) immediately after.

## Verse 36:24

> Indeed, I would then be clearly astray

**A Consequence He Accepts for Himself**

The believing man concludes his argument about idols with a self-judgment: *“Indeed, I would then be clearly astray”* (36:24). If he took besides God other gods whose intercession does not benefit and who cannot save (36:23), he would be in ḍalāl mubīn, manifest error. The phrase ḍalāl mubīn appears throughout the Qur'an for error that is obvious, evident, not hidden. *“Whoever takes Satan as protector instead of God has certainly incurred manifest loss”* (4:119) and *“Indeed, the wrongdoers are in manifest error”* (19:38) use similar language. By saying I would be clearly astray, the man shows he applies to himself the same standard he asks of his people. He does not say you are astray, though they are; he says I would be astray if I did what you do. That humility makes his warning more acceptable. He places himself under judgment first, which is characteristic of sincere callers. The Qur'an says *“Why do you say what you do not do? It is most hateful to God that you say what you do not do”* (61:2-3). He says what he does, and says what he would be if he did otherwise.

**A Short Verse With a Complete Theology**

Four words in Arabic, six in English, yet this verse contains a complete theology of error. The man earlier said *“Why should I not worship the One who originated me, and to Whom you will be returned”* (36:22). Worshiping the Originator is guidance; worshiping others is error. If you were originated by Him and will be returned to Him, worshiping others is deviation from both start and end. The sūrah says *“This is the Hell you were warned of. Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:63-64). Manifest error in this life leads to manifest punishment in next, just as manifest guidance leads to *“Peace from Merciful Lord”* (36:58).

**How This Verse Functions in the Narrative**

In the narrative, this verse is the man's final self-warning before his public declaration of faith: *“I do believe in your Lord, so listen to me”* (36:25). He first argues why he should worship the Originator (36:22), why he cannot take other gods (36:23), what he would be if he did (36:24), then declares belief (36:25). The sequence is logical: reason, refutation, self-judgment, declaration. It models how faith is expressed under pressure. He does not begin with I believe; he begins with why belief is rational, what alternative would mean, then states belief. That order is persuasive for his people, who are being asked to follow messengers who ask no reward (36:21). By saying I would be clearly astray if I took other gods, he shows he has considered alternative and found it wanting. His faith is not blind; it is reasoned, and its reason is that alternative is manifest error. The narrative then moves quickly to his martyrdom and entry into Paradise (36:26), showing that avoiding manifest error has immediate reward, even if it costs life.

**The Link to the Sūrah's Larger Theme of Error**

The sūrah uses ḍalāl, error, and its derivatives to describe those who take other gods, deny resurrection, and mock messengers. *“Yet he already misled great multitudes of you. Did you not have any sense?”* (36:62) says about Satan, who misleads into error. The believing man's statement that he would be clearly astray if he took other gods aligns him against Satan's misleading. Satan's path is manifest error; the Originator's worship is straight path (36:61). The man's short verse so places him on straight path and identifies the alternative as error. The sūrah later says *“Indeed, on that Day the residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55), implying they avoided error. The believing man is among them, as his wish *“If only my people knew how my Lord has forgiven me and made me one of the honourable”* (36:26-27) shows. His avoidance of manifest error led to honor, while his people's persistence in it led to being extinguished by a single blast (36:29).

## Verse 36:25

> I do believe in your Lord, so listen to me.”

After reasoning about worshiping the Originator (36:22), rejecting powerless intercessors (36:23), and stating that taking them would be manifest error (36:24), the believing man declares: *“I do believe in your Lord, so listen to me”* (36:25). The declaration is addressed to messengers — your Lord — not to his people directly, though they overhear. The immediacy is important because he is in a hostile crowd that just threatened stoning (36:18). Declaring belief in that context is an act of courage that matches his rushing from farthest end (36:20).

**So Listen to Me as Request for Testimony**

*“So listen to me”* (36:25), fasmaʿūn, can mean listen to my declaration and accept it, or listen and bear witness for me. If addressed to his people, it means listen to my advice to follow messengers (36:20-21). If addressed to messengers, it means listen to my faith and testify that I believed before God. Both are plausible, and the ambiguity is meaningful. A believer wants both: his people to listen and be guided, and messengers to listen and testify. The Qur'an says *“How will it be when We bring a witness from every community and bring you as witness over these?”* (4:41). Messengers will testify about their communities. The believing man asks messengers to listen so they can testify that he believed despite his people. His request anticipates the scene where he is told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) and wishes his people knew how his Lord forgave him (36:27). His listening request is granted in next life: his testimony is preserved in Qur'an itself, which is recited until Day of Resurrection.

**How This Verse Models Daʿwah Under Threat**

The man's sequence models how to call to God under threat: rush from farthest end (36:20), advise to follow those who ask no reward and are guided (36:21), argue from origination and return (36:22), refute powerless intercessors (36:23), state consequence of taking them (36:24), then declare belief and ask for listening (36:25). He does not begin with declaration; he begins with reasoning that his people can follow, then declares. This order respects audience. It also shows that declaration is not mere words; it is conclusion of reasoning. For Mecca, where Prophet faced similar threats, this model was instructive. The Prophet was told *“So do not let their words grieve you”* (36:76) and his duty is only clear delivery (36:17). The believing man's delivery is clear and ends with declaration, even though his people will kill him. The sūrah so teaches that success of daʿwah is not measured by immediate acceptance but by clarity and sincerity of declaration, and that declaration may be preserved and rewarded beyond life.

**A Short Verse That Holds a Complete Faith**

Four words in Arabic, seven in English, yet this verse holds complete faith: belief, attribution, and request. I believe — internal conviction. In your Lord — correct object of belief, not idols. So listen to me — desire to share and to be witnessed. The three together form a minimal creed that any believer can adopt. It is also a bridge to next verses: after declaration, he is told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26), showing that declaration is accepted. The sūrah then says *“We did not send any soldiers from heavens against his people after his death, nor did We need to”* (36:28), showing that God's response to killing a believer does not require armies, only a single blast (36:29). The man's short declaration so triggers both his personal reward and his people's punishment, illustrating that individual faith and communal rejection have consequences that are recorded in perfect registry (36:12) and will be manifested on Day when *“they will all be brought before Us”* (36:32). The verse invites every reader to make same declaration, regardless of cost, and to ask others to listen.

The Qur'an records similar shifts: the magicians of Pharaoh, after seeing Mūsā's sign, say *“We believe in the Lord of all worlds, the Lord of Mūsā and Hārūn”* (26:47-48), attributing lordship to the messengers' Lord.

## Verse 36:26

> ˹But they killed him, then˺ he was told ˹by the angels˺, “Enter Paradise!” He said, “If only my people knew

**From Martyrdom to Paradise**

The translation adds context that the text implies: they killed him, then he was told *“Enter Paradise!”* (36:26). The Arabic qīla udkhul al-jannah, it was said Enter the Garden, is passive, indicating angels or divine command welcoming him immediately after death. The immediacy is striking. No waiting, no intermediate questioning described; entry is direct. The man who rushed from farthest end of city (36:20), advised to follow messengers who ask no reward (36:21), argued from origination and return (36:22), rejected powerless intercessors (36:23), declared himself clearly astray if he took them (36:24). Finally declared *“I believe in your Lord, so listen to me”* (36:25), is now told Enter Paradise. The sequence shows that faith declared under threat is accepted without delay. The Qur'an says *“Do not think those killed in God's path are dead; rather they are alive with their Lord, provided for”* (3:169). This man's entry illustrates that principle. His killing by his people is not end; it is doorway.

**Why Enter Paradise Is Said Immediately**

The command Enter Paradise is given right after his declaration, suggesting that his martyrdom is cause of entry, but also that Paradise is near for the sincere. The sūrah earlier promised *“good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward”* (36:11) for those who follow Reminder and fear Most Compassionate unseen. The believing man fits that description, and his reward is now shown as realized. The immediacy also contrasts with his people's fate, who will be extinguished by a single blast (36:29). One man enters Paradise with a word; a whole town is extinguished with a blast. The asymmetry shows divine justice: individual faith is honored individually, collective rejection is punished collectively, and neither requires armies (36:28). The passive qīla, it was said, also indicates honor; he is addressed by angels, not left alone. The sūrah later describes Paradise residents as busy enjoying themselves, reclining, having fruits, greeted with peace (36:55-58). His entry is entry into that described bliss.

**The Connection to Forgiveness and Honor**

The next verse completes his wish: *“of how my Lord has forgiven me, and made me one of the honourable”* (36:27). Forgiveness and honor are exactly what was promised in 36:11: forgiveness and honourable reward. His entry into Paradise is realization of that promise. Forgiveness, maghfirah, indicates his sins are covered; honor, being among mukramīn, indicates his status is elevated. The two together show mercy and generosity. The sūrah earlier said *“Everything is listed in a perfect Record”* (36:12). His forgiveness indicates his record, though containing human faults, has been covered by mercy. His honor indicates his good deed — rushing, advising, believing — has been recorded and rewarded. The verse so ties narrative to doctrine: record is kept, forgiveness is possible, honor is given. The man's wish that his people knew is wish that they knew forgiveness and honor are available to them too, if they follow messengers.

## Verse 36:27

> of how my Lord has forgiven me, and made me one of the honourable.”

**Forgiveness and Honor as Twin Gifts**

This verse completes the believing man's wish: *“of how my Lord has forgiven me, and made me one of the honourable”* (36:27). Two gifts are named: forgiveness, maghfirah, and being made among mukramīn, the honoured. Forgiveness is removal of sin; honor is elevation of status. The first is negative — what is taken away — the second positive — what is given. Together they describe complete salvation. The Qur'an often pairs them: *“For them is forgiveness and an honourable reward”* (36:11, 67:12). The man's wish is that his people knew both, so they would be motivated to seek same. He does not wish they knew his suffering or his people's crime; he wishes they knew God's forgiveness and honor, because that knowledge would attract them to faith. His concern remains evangelical even in Paradise. The wish also shows that forgiveness and honor are from My Lord, rabbī, not from messengers or himself. He attributes both to God, preserving tawḥīd even in gratitude.

**Why Forgiveness Comes First**

Forgiveness is mentioned before honor because forgiveness is prerequisite. A person cannot be among honoured while sins remain unpardoned. The sūrah earlier promised forgiveness and honourable reward for those who follow Reminder and fear Most Compassionate unseen (36:11). The man's entry into Paradise shows that promise fulfilled: forgiveness first, then honor. The Qur'an says *“Whoever is removed from Fire and admitted to Paradise has indeed succeeded”* (3:185). Removal — forgiveness — then admission — honor — is the sequence. The man's sins, whatever they were, are forgiven, and then he is placed among honoured. Honour here likely means among prophets, martyrs, and righteous, or among those near to God, as in *“Indeed, the honoured servants”* (21:26) for angels. The term mukramīn appears for guests of Ibrāhīm who were angels (51:24) and for those in Paradise. Being among honoured means being in noble company, not solitary bliss.

**The Relation to the Sūrah's Promise**

The sūrah promised *“good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward”* (36:11) for those who follow Reminder. The believing man's story is the narrative proof of that promise. He followed Reminder — follow messengers (36:20) — feared Most Compassionate unseen — worshiped Originator (36:22) — and now receives forgiveness and honor. The story so functions as tafsīr of the promise. It shows what following looks like in practice — rushing from farthest end, advising, arguing, declaring — and what reward looks like — immediate entry, forgiveness, honor. The story also shows that forgiveness and honor are not delayed until final Day only; they begin at death for martyrs. The Qur'an says *“Do not say of those killed in God's path, They are dead; rather they are alive, but you perceive not”* (2:154). The man's being among honoured while his people are still alive shows that aliveness.

**A Verse That Defines True Honor**

True honor in Qur'anic vocabulary is not wealth, lineage, or power, but being forgiven and placed among honoured by God. The town's leaders sought honor through stoning messengers and maintaining status, but they were extinguished by a single blast (36:29). The man from farthest end, with no status, sought no reward (36:21) and was made among honoured. The reversal is deliberate. The sūrah says *“Indeed, on that Day residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55), and this man is among them, busy in enjoyment, while his killers are busy being punished. Honor is so redefined. It is not who has power to stone, but who is forgiven and honoured by God.

## Verse 36:28

> We did not send any soldiers from the heavens against his people after his death, nor did We need to

**No Army Needed to Punish a Town**

After the believing man's entry into Paradise and his wish, the sūrah says: *“We did not send any soldiers from the heavens against his people after his death, nor did We need to”* (36:28). The verse stresses that destroying a town that killed a believer and rejected messengers does not require heavenly armies. A single blast will suffice (36:29). The statement has two purposes: to show God's power — He does not need armies — and to show the town's insignificance — it is not worth an army. The Qur'an says elsewhere *“We sent against them a single blast, and they became like dry twigs of a sheepfold”* (54:31) and *“It was but a single cry, and they were extinguished”* (36:29). The pattern is that great punishment can come through small means when God wills. The town that threatened stoning and painful punishment from itself (36:18) is answered not by angelic legions but by a single sound. The disproportion between threat and punishment shows who truly holds power.

**The Relation to the Sūrah's Theme of Power**

This verse contributes to the sūrah's theme that true power belongs to God alone. The sūrah says *“If We willed, We could drown them, then no one would respond to their cries, nor would they be rescued, except by mercy from Us”* (36:43-44) about ships, and *“Had We willed, We could have blinded their eyes”* (36:66) and *“transfigured them on the spot”* (36:67). All show that God can punish without intermediaries, but He often refrains out of mercy, allowing enjoyment for a little while (36:44). The town that killed the believer received that little while after his death, then a single blast. The verse also contrasts with the believing man's honor. He was made among honoured (36:27) without army; his people were destroyed without army. Both reward and punishment are easy for God. The sūrah's final verse says authority over all things is in God's Hands (36:83), and this verse illustrates that authority: no soldiers needed to enforce it.

**How This Verse Warns Mecca**

For Mecca, which prided itself on military strength and tribal alliances, this verse warns that divine punishment does not require equal military force. The sūrah was revealed when Mecca was persecuting believers, and some believers were killed or tortured. The story of a town that killed a believer and was then destroyed without heavenly army would have resonated. It says God can avenge believers without sending visible armies; a single blast, a single cry, suffices. The perfect record (36:12) preserves the killing, and authority over all things (36:83) ensures response.

**A Verse That Shows Divine Economy**

The verse shows divine economy: God does not waste means. When a single blast suffices, He does not send hosts. This economy appears throughout Yā Sīn. Dead earth is revived with water, not with elaborate machinery (36:33). Night is stripped of day, not demolished (36:37). Sun runs for fixed term by design (36:38). Each act is efficient and enough. The destruction of a town by a single cry is of same type: minimal means, maximal effect, because means are backed by divine command *“Be, and it is”* (36:82). The verse teaches that power is not measured by size of army but by authority of command. One command, Be, creates heavens and earth; one blast, a cry, destroys a town. The town that threatened painful punishment from itself (36:18) needed stones and numbers; God needs only a sound. The contrast is intentional and humbling. It tells listeners that true might is not in numbers but in permission from the One in Whose Hands is authority.

## Verse 36:29

> All it took was one ˹mighty˺ blast, and they were extinguished at once

**A Single Blast Ends a Town**

*“All it took was one mighty blast, and they were extinguished at once”* (36:29) describes the town's end. Ṣayḥah wāḥidah, a single cry, blast, shout, is the means of destruction. The same term appears for Thamūd and others: *“We sent against them a single blast, and they became like dry stubble”* (54:31). The blast is not described as fire, flood, or earthquake, but as sound, suggesting that divine command itself is enough. The verb khāmidūn, extinguished, is used for fire that goes out. A fire that was burning is suddenly cold, lifeless. The town that was vibrant, threatening stoning (36:18), boasting *“we will certainly stone you”* (36:18), is now extinguished at once, fa-idhā hum khāmidūn. The suddenness, at once, stresses that no gradual decline occurred; one moment they were alive and threatening, next they were lifeless. The verse that says *“We did not send soldiers from heavens, nor did We need to”* (36:28) is now explained: a single blast suffices.

**Why Sound as Punishment**

Sound as punishment is fitting for a people who rejected words. Messengers delivered clear words (36:17), a man from farthest end advised with words (36:20-21), yet the town responded with threats of stoning, which is also sound — shouting, collective noise. The divine response is a sound that silences all sounds. The Qur'an says *“Then they will not be able to make a last will, nor can they return to their people”* (36:50) about a future blast that will seize people while disputing. Sound that ends all disputation is appropriate. The blast is also just because it is proportionate to the town's crime of trying to silence truth with noise and stones. God silences them with a sound. The sūrah later says *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us”* (36:53) for resurrection, showing that a single blast can both destroy and resurrect. One blast extinguishes, another blast brings back. Both are easy for the One who says *“Be, and it is”* (36:82).

**How This Verse Warns Mecca and All Towns**

For Mecca, which saw itself as secure, well-watered, with trade and allies, the image of a town extinguished at once by a single blast is warning. Security is not protection against divine command. The Qur'an says *“Have they not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back?”* (36:31) right after this story, linking this town to many others. The single blast that extinguished this town is one instance of a pattern: many generations destroyed, none returning (36:31). The warning is that Mecca could be next if it continues rejecting. The sūrah was revealed before Mecca's conquest, when its power seemed intact. Telling of a town extinguished at once says power can be removed instantly. The verse also warns any community that kills a sincere adviser. The believing man was killed, then honored (36:26-27); his killers were extinguished. The Qur'an says *“We did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves”* (42:30). Extinguishing is consequence of self-wronging, not arbitrary.

**A Verse That Teaches Urgency**

Finally, this verse teaches urgency. All it took was one blast, and they were extinguished at once. No time to make gift, no time to return to people (36:50). The sūrah says blast will seize people while they are entrenched in worldly disputes (36:49). The town's story is example of that: they were disputing with messengers, threatening stoning, when blast came. The believing man rushed from farthest end (36:20) and declared belief (36:25) before blast, so he was not among extinguished but among honoured. The town's extinguishing at once is warning that time is short, and that following those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21) is urgent, not optional.

## Verse 36:30

> Oh pity, such beings! No messenger ever came to them without being mocked

**A Cry of Pity for Those Who Mock**

*“Oh pity, such beings! No messenger ever came to them without being mocked”* (36:30) is a divine exclamation of sorrow and amazement. Yā ḥasratan ʿalā al-ʿibād, pity upon the servants, can be read as God's lament for what servants do to themselves, or as statement that servants will feel pity and regret on Day of Judgment when they see consequence of mocking. Both are transmitted. The verse follows the town's destruction by a single blast (36:29) and generalizes: this town is not exception; every messenger has been mocked. *“No messenger came to them but they mocked him”* (15:11, 43:7) appears as refrain. The sūrah says *“Have they not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back?”* (36:31) immediately after, linking mocking to destruction. Mockery is not harmless humor; it is rejection that leads to being extinguished. The pity is therefore not sentimental but judicial: pity that beings given signs, messengers, and mercy choose mockery and so bring punishment upon themselves.

**Why Mockery Is the Universal Response**

Mockery, istihzāʾ, is universal response because it is easy defense against clear delivery (36:17). When messengers say *“Our Lord knows we have been sent”* (36:16) and *“Follow those who ask no reward and are rightly guided”* (36:21), the simplest way to avoid engaging is to mock. Mockery does not require argument; it requires only ridicule. The Qur'an says *“When they see you, they take you only in mockery: Is this the one God has sent as messenger?”* (25:41). Mockery protects ego: if messenger is ridiculous, his message need not be considered. The sūrah's town mocked by calling messengers liars (36:15), bad omens (36:18), and threatening stoning (36:18). Mecca mocked Muḥammad by calling him poet, magician, had. The verse says no messenger ever came without being mocked, so Muḥammad's experience is not new; it is pattern. That pattern consoles: if all messengers were mocked, mockery is not evidence against messenger but evidence that audience follows old habit.

**Mockery as Barrier to Seeing Signs**

Mockery creates barrier described earlier: *“We have placed a barrier before them and behind them and covered them up, so they fail to see”* (36:9). Mocking is a way of covering truth, of veiling. When a person mocks a sign, he prevents himself from seeing it as sign. The sūrah lists signs after this verse — dead earth revived (36:33), gardens and springs (36:34), pairs created (36:36), night stripped of day (36:37), sun and moon in orbits (36:38-40) — all of which are mocked if seen as mere nature. A mocker sees grain but not resurrection, night but not covering, sun but not fixed term. Mockery is so not only response to messengers but to signs. *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46) follows same pattern. Pity is appropriate because mockers deprive themselves of signs that could guide them. The Qur'an says *“How many signs in heavens and earth they pass by, yet they turn away from them”* (12:105). Mockery is one form of turning away.

For the Prophet, this verse is direct consolation. His people mocked him, and he grieved. The verse says pity, such beings, no messenger ever came without being mocked, telling him his experience is universal among messengers. It also tells him mockery is not final word. The town that mocked was extinguished; the believing man who was mocked was told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26). The sūrah later says *“So do not let their words grieve you. Indeed, We fully know what they conceal and what they reveal”* (36:76). The verse also prepares for the next: *“Have they not considered how many generations We destroyed before them?”* (36:31).

## Verse 36:31

> Have the deniers not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back to life again

**A Question About Past Destructions**

*“Have the deniers not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back to life again?”* (36:31) follows the lament over mocking (36:30) and asks those who mock now to look at those who mocked before. The verb yaʿtabir, consider, implies taking lesson, passing from outward sight to inward understanding. The Qur'an frequently asks *“Have they not traveled through the earth and seen how was the end of those before them?”* (12:109, 30:9). Ruins of past peoples were visible to Arabs on trade routes — Thamūd's dwellings in Ḥijr, ʿĀd's remains, Pharaoh's monuments. The question is not whether they know past peoples were destroyed — they do — but whether they consider what that means for themselves. The phrase who never came back, allā yarjiʿūn, means they never returned to this world, not that they will not be resurrected. Their return to worldly life is impossible; their return to God for judgment is certain (36:32). The verse so distinguishes two returns: return to worldly life, which never happens, and return to God, which always happens.

**The Relation to Resurrection**

The statement that destroyed peoples never came back to this life might seem to contradict resurrection, which the sūrah affirms: *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead”* (36:12) and *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78). The distinction is between return to worldly life, rajʿah to dunyā, which never happens, and resurrection to next life, baʿth, which will happen. The destroyed peoples will not return to their towns to continue worldly life, but they will be brought before God (36:32) and will rush from graves to Lord (36:51). The verse clarifies that worldly destruction is not followed by worldly return, so the chance to believe is in this life only. Once destroyed, they do not come back to try again. That makes present consideration crucial. The sūrah's later argument for resurrection from dead earth (36:33) shows that while return to same worldly life does not happen, revival to new life does. The earth that dies in winter does not return to same winter; it returns as spring with new grain.

**How This Verse Warns Against Feeling Secure**

The verse warns against feeling secure because many secure peoples were destroyed. The Qur'an says *“Did We not destroy the former peoples, then make the latter follow them? So We deal with the guilty”* (77:16-18). The pattern is that guilt, not lack of power, causes destruction. The town destroyed by a single blast (36:29) was guilty of rejecting messengers and killing a believer. Its destruction required no heavenly soldiers (36:28). The question have they not considered invites Mecca to consider that its own guilt — mocking messenger (36:30), rejecting signs (36:46), refusing to donate (36:47) — fits pattern of those destroyed. Security in sanctuary does not prevent destruction if guilt persists. The verse is also a call to travel and see ruins, which Arabs did on way to Syria. Seeing abandoned dwellings should produce consideration, not just sightseeing. More powerful peoples were destroyed; power does not protect.

**A Verse That Prepares for Universal Arraignment**

This verse prepares for next: *“Yet they will all be brought before Us”* (36:32). Destroyed peoples never came back to this life, but they will all be brought before God. The sūrah later says *“On that Day no soul will be wronged, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do”* (36:54). The perfect record (36:12) ensures that what destroyed peoples did is preserved, and being brought before God ensures they will answer. The verse invites present deniers to consider past destroyed as preview of their own future if they continue mocking, and to choose instead to follow those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21) before they become among those who never come back.

The Qur'an says *“How many towns We destroyed whose people were wrongdoers, so their roofs caved in, and how many abandoned wells and lofty palaces”* (22:45).

## Verse 36:32

> Yet they will all be brought before Us

*“Yet they will all be brought before Us”* (36:32) follows the statement that destroyed peoples never return to this life (36:31) and asserts that they will all be brought before God. The Qur'an says *“They will all be brought before Us”* again in *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us”* (36:53), linking this verse to resurrection. Those who never came back to their towns (36:31) will come before their Lord. The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they sent forth and left behind will be the basis of that arraignment, and *“On that Day no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) will be its principle.

Placing universal arraignment right after mention of past destructions corrects a possible misunderstanding: that destruction ends the story. It does not. The town extinguished at once (36:29) is not gone without accounting; its people will be brought. The many peoples destroyed before who never returned to worldly life (36:31) will be brought before God. The verse connects this world to next. The sūrah's structure moves from town story (36:13-29) to lament over mocking (36:30) to past destructions (36:31) to universal gathering (36:32) to signs of resurrection in dead earth (36:33). The sequence is logical: example of destruction, pattern of destruction, then statement that destruction is followed by gathering. The gathering is not limited to destroyed peoples; it includes present deniers who have not considered past (36:31). Yet in Arabic, kullun can refer to all peoples mentioned or all humanity. Most exegetes take it as all humanity, because other verses say *“We will gather them and not leave any behind”* (18:47). The sūrah's later scene of rushing from graves to Lord (36:51) illustrates this gathering.

**The Meaning of Being Brought Before Us**

Being brought before Us, ladaynā muḥḍarūn, implies presence before God, not merely gathering in a place. Ladaynā, before Us, with Us, indicates nearness to divine presence for judgment. The Qur'an says *“They will be presented before your Lord in rows: You have come to Us as We created you first time”* (18:48). Presentation is humbling because it is before the One in Whose Hands is authority over all things (36:83). The town that said *“You will be touched with painful punishment from us”* (36:18), claiming authority to punish, will itself be brought before the One whose punishment is truly painful. The believing man who was told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) was brought before God in honor; his killers will be brought in disgrace. The verse is short, but it holds both promise and threat: all will be brought, but not all will be treated same. *“Indeed, on that Day residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55) while others will be told *“Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:64).

**The Link to the Signs That Follow**

The verse prepares for signs of resurrection that follow. After stating that all will be brought before God, the sūrah gives signs that resurrection is possible and reasonable: dead earth revived with grain (36:33), gardens and springs (36:34), pairs created (36:36), night and day (36:37), sun and moon (36:38-40), ships and cattle (36:41-42, 71-72). Each sign is introduced as a sign for them (36:33, 36:37, 36:41), meaning for those who will be brought. The signs are evidence that the One who can revive dead earth can bring all before Him. The sūrah's argument is cumulative: past destructions show God can destroy, universal arraignment says He will gather, natural signs show He can revive, and the final blast (36:53) shows how He will do it. Verse thirty-two is the hinge between past and future, between destruction and resurrection, stating the principle that all will be brought, which the rest of the sūrah then supports with evidence and illustrates with scenes of Paradise and Hell.

The Qur'an says *“Every soul will come with a driver and a witness”* (50:21), indicating individual escort to judgment.

## Verse 36:33

> There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat

**Dead Earth as a Sign of Resurrection**

*“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”* (36:33) begins the sūrah's catalogue of natural signs. After stories of destroyed towns and universal arraignment (36:31-32), the sūrah turns to creation as evidence that resurrection is possible. Dead earth, al-arḍ al-maytah, is earth that is barren, dry, lifeless, without vegetation. The Qur'an says *“You see the earth barren, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and grows every delightful kind”* (22:5). The sign is that God gives it life, aḥyaynāhā, same verb used for resurrecting dead: *“It is We who resurrect the dead”* (36:12). The parallel is intentional. If God can give life to dead earth, He can give life to dead bodies. The sūrah later asks *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78) and answers *“They will be revived by the One who produced them first time”* (36:79). Dead earth is visible proof of that answer, seen every year. The sign is for them, lahum, meaning for the deniers who doubt resurrection and for all who need reminder.

**The Relation to the Perfect Record**

The verse connects to *“Everything is listed in a perfect Record”* (36:12) because grain that grows from dead earth is recorded as provision, and eating it is recorded as act that should lead to thanks. The sūrah says *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35) after mentioning fruit that they had no hand in making. Gratitude is expected response to sign. If gratitude is not given, ingratitude is recorded. The perfect record includes both provision and response. The dead earth sign is so not only proof of power to resurrect but also test of gratitude. Those who eat grain and give thanks are among those who follow Reminder (36:11); those who eat and do not give thanks are among heedless. The sūrah later says *“They derive from them other benefits and drinks. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:73) about cattle, repeating same question.

**Why This Sign Is Placed Here**

Placing dead earth sign after mention of universal arraignment (36:32) is strategic. All will be brought before God (36:32); is that possible? The sūrah later expresses doubt explicitly: *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78), but it has already answered with dead earth, gardens, night and day, sun and moon. A town extinguished at once (36:29) seems final, but dead earth shows that extinguishing is not final; revival is possible. The town's people will be brought before God (36:32) just as dead earth is brought to life. The sūrah says *“Have they not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back?”* (36:31), but dead earth shows that while they never come back to worldly life, they will come back to next life.

**A Sign That Is Seen Daily Yet Missed**

Dead earth revived is seen daily, yet many miss it as sign because heedlessness (36:6) makes them see only agriculture, not resurrection. The verse says there is a sign for them in dead earth, implying sign is present but they may not see it as sign because barriers before and behind (36:9) veil them. The Qur'an says *“We will show them Our signs in horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is truth”* (41:53). The verse invites reader to look at food on table and see resurrection, to eat with gratitude and awareness that the One who gave life to earth will give life to dead, and that everything is listed in perfect Record (36:12) for a Day when no soul will be wronged (36:54).

The Qur'an says *“Let man look at his food: We poured water in abundance, then We split the earth, then We grew grain in it”* (80:24-27).

The Qur'an says *“It is God who sends winds, then they stir clouds, then We drive them to dead land and revive the earth after its death; so is resurrection”* (35:9).

## Verse 36:34

> And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it

**Gardens and Springs From Dead Earth**

This verse continues the sign of dead earth revived: *“And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it”* (36:34). After grain for eating (36:33), gardens and springs are mentioned as further provision and beauty. Palm trees, nakhl, and grapevines, aʿnāb, were the most valued fruits for Arabs, providing staple and luxury, shade and sweetness. Gardens of them indicate abundance, not single tree. Springs gushing, fujjarnā, indicates water emerging from earth itself, not just rain from sky, showing that life comes from within dead earth when God commands. The Qur'an says *“We made in it gardens of palms and grapevines and caused springs to gush forth”* as part of same sign. The placement — in it, fīhā — refers to earth, showing that dead earth, once revived, produces not only grain but gardens and springs. The sign is so of transformation from barren to fruitful, from lifeless to life-giving. The same transformation will happen to decayed bones (36:78-79).

**Why Palms and Grapevines Specifically**

Palms and grapevines are specified because they were best of fruits to Arabs and because they represent different types of growth: palm is tall, perennial, provides dates, fiber, shade; grapevine is low, seasonal, provides fruit and drink. Together they cover variety. The Qur'an often mentions them together: *“Gardens of palms and grapevines”* (23:19, 2:266). Their mention also hints at Paradise, which is described as having palms, vines, and springs. The sūrah later says Paradise residents will have fruits and whatever they desire (36:57).

**The Relation to Gratitude**

The purpose clause in next verse — so that they may eat from its fruit — shows that gardens and springs are for eating and enjoyment, not just display. Eating is meant to lead to thanks. The sūrah repeats question of thanks twice: *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73). Repeating question indicates importance. Gratitude, shukr, is acknowledgment that provision comes from God and use of provision in obedience. Ingratitude, kufr, is opposite, covering favor and using provision in disobedience. The town that was extinguished (36:29) was ungrateful for messengers; those who do not give thanks for gardens are similar. The sign of gardens and springs is so test: will they eat and give thanks, or eat and forget? The perfect record (36:12) records both eating and thanks or lack of it. The sūrah says *“If you give thanks, I will surely increase you”* (14:7) elsewhere, linking thanks to increase. Gardens and springs can increase if thanks given; they can be removed if not.

Gardens and springs from dead earth strengthen resurrection argument. If dead earth can produce gardens of palms and vines and springs, then decayed bones can produce living humans. The transformation from dead to fruitful is more impressive than from fruitful to fruitful. The Qur'an says *“Look at the effects of God's mercy, how He revives earth after its death; indeed, that same One will revive the dead”* (30:50). The effects of mercy include gardens and springs. The sūrah later says *“He is the One who gives you fire from green trees, and behold, you kindle fire from them”* (36:80), another transformation of opposite from same — green, wet tree producing fire. Gardens from dead earth is same type: life from death, fruit from barren. The argument is cumulative: dead earth to grain (36:33), dead earth to gardens and springs (36:34), night to day (36:37), sun to fixed term (36:38), moon to phases (36:39). Each shows transformation that makes resurrection reasonable.

The Qur'an says *“We sent down water from sky in measure, then We caused it to settle in earth, and indeed We are able to take it away”* (23:18).

Paradise is described as having fruits and whatever they desire, shade, couches (36:56-57).

## Verse 36:35

> so that they may eat from its fruit, which they had no hand in making. Will they not then give thanks

*“So that they may eat from its fruit, which they had no hand in making. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35) concludes the sign of dead earth, gardens, and springs. The purpose clause so that they may eat shows that provision is for human benefit, and the parenthetical which they had no hand in making clarifies that fruit is not human product. The Arabic wa mā ʿamilat-hu aydīhim can be read as which their hands did not make, meaning fruit is from God, not from human creation, or as and from what their hands have made, meaning they eat fruit and also what they make from it like juice, oil, etc. The Qur'an says *“Is it you who make it grow, or are We the Grower?”* (56:64) about plants.

**Why the Question Will They Not Give Thanks**

The rhetorical question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35) is repeated later for cattle: *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:73). Repetition shows that gratitude is central response expected to signs. Shukr, thanks, in Qur'anic usage is not mere words but acknowledgment with heart, tongue, and limbs, using provision in obedience. The town that was extinguished (36:29) failed to give thanks for messengers; those who eat fruit without thanks fail similarly. The question is not informational but urging: it urges thanks. The Qur'an says *“If you give thanks, I will increase you, but if you are ungrateful, My punishment is severe”* (14:7). Thanks leads to increase; ingratitude leads to loss. The sūrah's signs — dead earth revived, gardens, springs, night and day, sun and moon — are all favors that should produce thanks. The fact that many do not give thanks is part of heedlessness (36:6) and leads to barriers (36:9) and equality of warning and non-warning (36:10).

**The Relation to the Perfect Record and Gratitude**

The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they send forth and leave behind includes thanks or lack of it. Gratitude is recorded as good deed; ingratitude as bad. The sūrah says *“On that Day no soul will be wronged, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do”* (36:54). Thanks is part of what you used to do. The verse also links to resurrection argument. If fruit that hands did not make can be eaten now, then bodies that hands did not make can be revived later. The One who creates fruit without human hands can create humans again without human hands. The sūrah later says *“Does man not see that We created him from a sperm-drop, then behold, he openly challenges?”* (36:77), arguing from creation without human hands to resurrection. Fruit that hands did not make is similar argument on smaller scale: daily creation without human agency proves power to create again.

**The Connection to Gardens of Paradise**

Fruit that hands did not make in this world prefigures fruit in Paradise that will be given without toil. The sūrah says Paradise residents will have fruits and whatever they desire (36:57). In this world, fruit requires planting, watering, waiting, though growth itself is from God; in Paradise, fruit will be without toil, pure gift. Eating fruit here with thanks prepares for eating fruit there with enjoyment. The verse so links present provision to future reward, encouraging thanks now as preparation for enjoyment later. The sūrah says residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves (36:55), and part of enjoyment is fruits. Those who gave thanks for fruit in this world will enjoy fruit in next without fear of loss. Those who did not give thanks will be told *“Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:64), and will not have fruits. The question will they not give thanks is therefore also warning: thanks now leads to fruits later; ingratitude now leads to deprivation later.

The Qur'an says *“Do you see what you sow? Is it you who make it grow, or are We the Grower? If We willed, We could make it debris”* (56:63-65).

## Verse 36:36

> Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs—˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know

**Why Pairs Indicate Oneness**

Pairs indicate oneness of Creator because pairing implies need for counterpart, while God has no counterpart. If all things are in pairs, the One who created pairs is not Himself paired. The verse argues for tawḥīd from observation of duality. The sūrah earlier said *“We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines”* (36:34) — palms have male and female, vines have varieties. The sūrah later says sun and moon each in orbit (36:40) — a pair of lights. The verse generalizes: all things are in pairs, so the Creator of pairs is One. The Qur'an says *“He creates the two mates — male and female”* (53:45) and *“From every fruit He made two mates”* (13:3). Pairing is not limitation but sign. It shows that creation is relational, needing other, while Creator is independent. The verse ends with what they do not know, indicating that pairing extends beyond known, so even unknown creation is paired, and Creator remains beyond pairing. Glory be to Him who is beyond pairing yet creates pairing.

**Their Genders and What They Do Not Know**

Their genders, anfusihim, themselves, refers to humans and perhaps animals, created male and female. The Qur'an says *“O mankind, We created you from male and female”* (49:13) and *“He created you from single soul, then made from it its mate”* (39:6). Gender pairing is fundamental to human existence and continuation. What they do not know, mimmā lā yaʿlamūn, extends pairing beyond known. It can refer to unseen creatures, to forces like positive and negative, to types of creation not yet discovered, or to spiritual pairs. The sūrah later says *“He has perfect knowledge of every created being”* (36:79), so what we do not know is known to Him.

Pairs argument supports resurrection. If God created all things in pairs, then this world and next are a pair, death and life are a pair, and creation first time and second time are a pair. The sūrah argues for resurrection from dead earth (36:33), from green trees giving fire (36:80), from creation of heavens and earth (36:81). Pairs adds another argument: just as there is male and female, there is first creation and second creation, which are paired. The verse's placement after grain, gardens, springs shows that those provisions are from paired creation and point to paired world. The sūrah says *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is authority over all things, and to Whom you will all be returned”* (36:83) at end, which is also about pairing: authority over all things in His Hands, return to Him — two aspects of same sovereignty. Glory be in 36:36 and glory be in 36:83 form inclusio, both declaring transcendence.

**A Verse That Invites Wonder**

Finally, this verse invites wonder, which is purpose of tasbīḥ. Subḥāna is said when seeing something amazing that indicates God's transcendence. The creation of all things in pairs — what earth produces, genders, what they do not know — is amazing when considered. A single earth produces countless paired kinds, each with role, each reproducing, each beneficial. The sheer variety of pairs — sweet and sour fruits, male and female animals, night and day — shows generosity and wisdom. The verse asks reader to glorify Creator, not just acknowledge. Glorification is active response: to declare God free from imperfection and partner. The sūrah's earlier question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35) is answered by *“Glory be to the One”* (36:36) — thanks leads to glorification. The believer who eats fruit that hands did not make (36:35) and sees pairs in creation says glory be to Creator of pairs. That glorification is itself recorded in perfect Record (36:12) and will be rewarded with fruits and whatever they desire in Paradise (36:57), where pairing continues but with perfect harmony.

The Qur'an says *“And of all things We created pairs, that you may remember”* (51:49).

## Verse 36:37

> There is also a sign for them in the night: We strip from it daylight, then—behold!—they are in darkness

**Night as a Sign**

*“There is also a sign for them in the night: We strip from it daylight, then behold, they are in darkness”* (36:37) presents night as sign, after earth, gardens, and pairs. The sūrah says *“a sign for them”* (36:33, 36:37, 36:41) repeatedly, indicating that signs are for the audience that doubts resurrection and neglects thanks. Night is sign because it is daily transformation that resembles death and resurrection. Daylight is stripped, naslakha, verb originally used for skinning an animal — removing skin from flesh. Daylight is like skin covering night; when stripped, night appears as underlying reality, and people are in darkness, muẓlimūn. The image is vivid: day is covering that is removed, revealing darkness beneath. The Qur'an says *“He rolls night into day and day into night”* (39:5) and *“We made night and day as two signs; We erased sign of night and made sign of day visible”* (17:12). The Book states shows that light is gift, not permanent possession, and darkness is original state that returns.

**Why Stripping Is the Chosen Verb**

Naslakha, to strip, to skin, is strong verb. The removal is divine act: We strip. The sūrah attributes act to God, as with dead earth revived (36:33) and gardens placed (36:34). The stripping is daily, yet many do not see it as act because it is regular. The verse says behold, fa-idhā, then suddenly, they are in darkness, emphasizing immediacy. One moment daylight, next darkness, as if skin removed quickly. The same immediacy appears in town extinguished at once (36:29) and blast seizing while disputing (36:49). The sūrah later says *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us”* (36:53), using same at once, fa-idhā, for resurrection.

**How Night Relates to Day and Sun**

This verse prepares for sun and moon verses that follow. Scripture notes leads to *“The sun travels for its fixed term”* (36:38) and moon phases (36:39). Night and day are pair, created in pairs (36:36), each with orbit, each not overtaking other (36:40). The sūrah says *“It is not for sun to catch up with moon, nor does night outrun day. Each travelling in orbit”* (36:40). Night and day are so ordered, not chaotic, each with term. The stripping of day from night is part of that order. The Qur'an says *“He made sun and moon subservient, each running for term appointed”* (39:5). Night as sign shows that order includes darkness as well as light, rest as well as work. The alternation is mercy: *“Out of His mercy He made for you night and day, that you may rest in it and seek His bounty”* (28:73). It is reported is mercy for rest, but also sign that rest is temporary and will be followed by awakening, just as sleep is followed by waking and death by resurrection.

**The Link to Resurrection and Gratitude**

Finally, night as sign links to resurrection and gratitude. The One who strips daylight and brings darkness can strip darkness and bring daylight, and can strip life and bring death, then bring life again. The sūrah asks *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for provision. The sūrah later says Paradise residents will be in cool shade, reclining (36:56), which is like pleasant night without darkness, rest without fear. Those who gave thanks for night and saw it as sign will enjoy shade in Paradise. Those who did not will be in darkness of Hell, told *“Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:64). Scripture notes is so both sign of power and test of gratitude, inviting reader to see daily darkness as reminder of final darkness and final light, and to prepare for the Day when *“Peace will be their greeting from Merciful Lord”* (36:58) for those who saw signs.

The Qur'an says *“We made night for rest and day for seeing”* (10:67).

The Qur'an says *“In creation of heavens and earth and alternation of night and day are signs for people of understanding”* (3:190).

## Verse 36:38

> The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing

**Sun on a Fixed Course**

*“The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing”* (36:38) continues the celestial signs after night stripped of day (36:37). The sun, shams, runs, tajrī, for a fixed term, mustaqarr, which can mean dwelling place, resting place, appointed time, or fixed course. The verb tajrī implies flowing, running, like water or ship, indicating continuous movement, not static. The Qur'an says *“Sun and moon run for term appointed”* (13:2, 31:29, 35:13, 39:5). The fixed term, mustaqarr, has been interpreted as its resting place under Throne where it prostrates, as in hadith of Abū Dharr where Prophet said sun goes till it prostrates under Throne and asks permission to rise, and one day permission will be denied and it will rise from west. That hadith is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3199 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 159. Others interpret mustaqarr as its appointed time, its term, i.e., it runs until Day of Judgment, or its fixed course, its orbit, or its solstice points where it appears to stop then reverse. All point to same: sun's movement is not random but fixed by decree.

**Why Fixed Term Matters**

Fixed term, mustaqarr, indicates that sun's movement has limit, place, and time set by God. It does not wander; it travels to appointed destination. That is sign of design, not chance. The sūrah says *“That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing”* (36:38), attributing sun's course to two names: al-ʿAzīz, Almighty, who has power to set course, and al-ʿAlīm, All-Knowing, who knows its course. The pairing appears throughout Qur'an for natural phenomena: *“That is design of Almighty, All-Knowing”* for sun and moon. Might sets course, knowledge measures it. The fixed term also implies end. Sun runs for term, then term ends. That end is Day when sun and moon are brought together (75:9) and light is folded. Sun that seems permanent has fixed term; so does human life. The sūrah says *“If We willed, We could have blinded their eyes”* (36:66) and *“transfigured them”* (36:67), showing that God can end terms early if He wills. Sun's term is fixed, but its daily rising is mercy allowing enjoyment for a little while (36:44).

**The Relation to Night and Day**

Sun's travel for fixed term follows night stripped of daylight (36:37) because sun is cause of day. When sun runs, day comes; when sun sets, night comes after stripping. The sūrah says *“It is not for sun to catch up with moon, nor does night outrun day. Each travelling in orbit”* (36:40), showing that sun, moon, night, day each have orbit and term, none overtaking other. The order is precise, not chaotic. The Qur'an says *“He made sun and moon subservient, each running for term appointed”* (13:2). Subservience, taskhīr, means they serve humans by providing light, heat, timekeeping. Sun's fixed term serves humans: it allows calculation of years and reckoning (10:5), determines prayer times, provides energy for grain and gardens (36:33-34). The sign is so both proof of power and provision for gratitude. The question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) applies to sun as well. Sun that runs for fixed term provides light without human effort, yet many do not give thanks.

**A Verse That Invites Reflection on Time**

Finally, sun's travel for fixed term invites reflection on time. The Qur'an says *“He made sun a radiance and moon a light and determined for it phases, that you may know number of years and reckoning”* (10:5). This Book is only Reminder and clear Qur'an”* (36:69), and Reminder includes reminding of time's passage and fixed terms. The reader who sees sun travelling for fixed term is invited to see his own life travelling for fixed term, to give thanks for light and time. To prepare for the Day when sun will be brought together with moon (75:9) and all will be brought before God (36:32), where *“no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) and where design of Almighty, All-Knowing will be fully manifest.

## Verse 36:39

> As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk

**Moon in Phases**

*“As for the moon, We have ordained precise phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk”* (36:39) follows sun's fixed term (36:38) with moon's measured stations. Qaddarnā, We have ordained, measured, decreed, indicates precise determination. Manāzil, phases, stations, are twenty-eight stations moon passes through in lunar month, each night in different position, allowing timekeeping. The Qur'an says *“He made sun a radiance and moon a light and determined for it phases, that you may know number of years and reckoning”* (10:5) and *“They ask you about new moons. Say, They are markers of time for people and for pilgrimage”* (2:189). Moon's phases are so mercy for calculation and worship. The comparison to old, curved palm stalk, ʿurjūn qadīm, describes crescent at end of month: thin, curved, yellowish, like dried date stalk that held cluster then withered, curved. The image is familiar to Arabs who saw palm stalks daily. Moon that was full becomes thin like old stalk, then disappears, then returns.

**Why Moon's Phases Are a Sign**

Moon's phases are sign because they show precise ordaining and transformation. Moon does not stay full; it waxes and wanes in fixed pattern, never skipping, never random. That precision indicates design by Almighty, All-Knowing (36:38). The transformation from full to thin stalk and back shows ability to bring from one state to opposite, which supports resurrection. Dead earth to grain (36:33), night to day (36:37), sun to fixed term (36:38), moon to old stalk — all are transformations that make resurrection reasonable. The sūrah later says *“He is the One who gives you fire from green trees, and behold, you kindle fire from them”* (36:80), another transformation of opposite from same. Moon's phases also show that decrease is not end; after becoming like old stalk, moon returns to new crescent. Similarly, human decrease to decayed bones is not end; return will come. The verse teaches that cycles in creation point to cycle of death and life.

**How Moon's Phases Relate to Timekeeping**

Moon's phases were essential for Arabs and for Islam for timekeeping, especially for pilgrimage and fasting. *“They are markers of time for people and for pilgrimage”* (2:189). Lunar calendar determines Ramadan, Hajj, etc. Ordaining precise phases is so mercy enabling worship. The sūrah says *“We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him. This Book is only Reminder and clear Qur'an”* (36:69), and Reminder includes reminding of times for worship through moon phases. The verse says We have ordained phases, attributing act to God, not to moon itself. Moon does not ordain its own phases; it is ordained.

**A Sign That Invites Gratitude and Reflection**

Finally, moon's phases invite gratitude and reflection. Gratitude because phases allow timekeeping, navigation, and beauty; reflection because phases show precise ordaining and ability to transform. The sūrah asks *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for provision. Moon's phases are provision for timekeeping, which enables religious rites, which are provision for soul. The verse invites reader to look at moon, see old palm stalk shape at end of month, and remember that just as moon returns after becoming thin, so will humans return after becoming dust. The One who ordains phases can ordain resurrection. The sūrah later says *“Can the One who created heavens and earth not easily resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can! For He is Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81). Moon is part of heavens, its phases part of creation, and its ordaining part of knowledge. Seeing moon as ordained, not random, prepares heart to see resurrection as ordained, not impossible.

## Verse 36:40

> It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own

**Order in Heavens**

*“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own”* (36:40) summarizes celestial order after detailing night (36:37), sun (36:38), moon (36:39). The verse denies two possibilities: sun overtaking moon, and night outrunning day. Lā yanbaghī, it is not for, it is not fitting, it does not behoove, indicates that such overtaking would be improper, contrary to design. Sun and moon have separate orbits, falak, and separate times; sun appears by day, moon by night, and they do not collide or overtake. Night and day also have order; night does not outrun day to come before its time, nor does day outrun night. The Qur'an says *“He made sun and moon subservient, each running for term appointed”* (13:2) and *“Each glides in an orbit”* (21:33). The image is of precise, non-colliding movement, like ships in sea each in course. The verse stresses that order is maintained by design, not chance.

**Each in Orbit as Sign of Design**

Each in orbit, kullun fī falakin, is phrase that appears in *“Each glides in an orbit”* (21:33) for sun and moon. Falak, orbit, originally means round, like whirl of spindle, indicating circular course. The verse says each is in orbit of its own, indicating individual course, not shared. Sun has orbit, moon has orbit, night and day have alternation that can be seen as orbit of light and darkness. The Qur'an says *“Sun and moon run for term appointed”* (13:2). The sūrah says *“Glory be to the One who created all things in pairs”* (36:36), and sun-moon, night-day are pairs that do not transgress bounds, unlike humans who transgress (36:19).

**How This Verse Supports Resurrection**

Order in heavens supports resurrection argument. If sun, moon, night, day each travel in orbit without overtaking, then universe is ordered, not chaotic, and its orderer can order resurrection. The sūrah later says *“Can the One who created heavens and earth not easily resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can! For He is Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81). Heavens and earth include sun, moon, night, day, and their orbits. If He can create and maintain such precise orbits, He can resurrect decayed bones. The verse also shows that fixed terms and orbits end. Sun runs for fixed term (36:38), moon has phases ending like old stalk (36:39), night and day alternate until Day when sun and moon are brought together (75:9). The orbits are not eternal; they have term, after which new order comes. That new order is resurrection, when all will be brought before God (36:32, 36:53). The present order is sign that orderer can bring new order.

**A Verse That Invites Trust in Divine Order**

Finally, this verse invites trust in divine order. The reader who sees sun not catching moon and night not outrunning day daily is invited to trust that his own life, with its night and day, has order too, and that God's promise of resurrection will not be overtaken or delayed. The sūrah says *“On that Day no soul will be wronged”* (36:54), indicating that judgment will be as precise as celestial orbits, with no overtaking or outrunning. The verse also invites gratitude: order that allows timekeeping, rest, work, navigation, is mercy. The question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) applies here too. Sun, moon, night, day travelling in orbits provide light, time, rest, all without human effort, yet many do not give thanks. Seeing their precise, non-colliding orbits should lead to glorification: *“Glory be to the One who created all things in pairs”* (36:36) and *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is authority over all things”* (36:83). The verse is short, but it holds a complete the order of the universe of order, design, and trust.

## Verse 36:41

> Another sign for them is that We carried their ancestors ˹with Noah˺ in the fully loaded Ark

**Ark as Sign for Those Who Travel**

*“Another sign for them is that We carried their ancestors with Noah in the fully loaded Ark”* (36:41) moves from celestial signs — night, sun, moon — to human history and travel. The sūrah says *“a sign for them”* (36:33, 36:37, 36:41) again, indicating this is another proof of God's power and mercy. The Ark, fulk mashḥūn, fully loaded Ark, refers to Noah's Ark, which carried believers, animals, and provision, preserving humanity. Most commentators identify ancestors as those saved with Noah, by whom all later humanity is descended. The Qur'an says *“We carried their progeny in the laden Ark”* in some readings, meaning descendants were carried as potential in loins of those saved. The Ark is sign because it shows salvation through obedience, carriage through water that drowned others, and preservation of life. The sūrah later says *“If We willed, We could drown them, then no one would respond to their cries, nor would they be rescued, except by mercy from Us”* (36:43-44), linking Ark to present ships that can also drown if God wills, but are saved by mercy.

**How Ark Relates to Resurrection**

Ark as sign supports resurrection argument. If God could carry ancestors in Ark through water that drowned all else, He can carry humans through death to life. The flood was like death — water covering earth, destroying — and Ark was like resurrection — preservation through water, emergence to new life. The Qur'an says *“We saved him and those with him in the laden Ark, and We made them successors and drowned those who denied Our signs”* (10:73). The sūrah's earlier sign of dead earth revived (36:33) is similar: earth drowned with water then revived with grain. The sūrah later says *“Can the One who created heavens and earth not easily resurrect these deniers?”* (36:81).

**The Relation to Ships and Modern Transport**

The next verse says *“and created for them similar things to ride in”* (36:42), which many interpret as ships similar to Ark, or camels called ships of desert, or all beasts of burden. The Ark is prototype of all transport that carries humans. The Qur'an says *“It is He who made for you ships and cattle to ride”* (43:12). Ships and cattle both carry, both can drown or stumble, both are saved by mercy. The verse invites reflection: just as ancestors were carried in Ark by divine mercy, you are carried in ships and on animals by same mercy. If God willed, He could drown you (36:43), but He allows enjoyment for a little while (36:44) out of mercy. The sign is so of dependence. Humans think they control ships, but carriage is by God's permission. The sūrah says *“If We willed, We could drown them”* (36:43), removing illusion of control. The Ark reminds that first carriage was purely divine rescue, not human technology, so all later carriage is also by permission.

**A Sign That Is Historical and Present**

Ark is historical sign — ancestors carried — and present sign — ships carrying now. The sūrah links past and present: ancestors carried, you are carried now in similar things. The link shows continuity of mercy. The same God who carried ancestors carries descendants. The verse also links to perfect record (36:12): carrying ancestors is recorded, and being carried now is recorded. The sūrah says *“Everything is listed in a perfect Record”* (36:12). The Ark's story is listed, and present voyages are listed. The sign invites gratitude: ancestors were carried, you are carried, will you not give thanks? The question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) applies here too. Carriage through water is favor that should produce thanks, not heedlessness. The town that was extinguished (36:29) failed to give thanks for messengers; those who travel in ships and do not give thanks for carriage are similar. The verse warns that carriage can be withdrawn — drowning — if thanks not given, but mercy allows enjoyment for a little while (36:44) to give chance to thank.

## Verse 36:42

> and created for them similar things to ride in

**Similar Things to Ride**

*“And created for them similar things to ride in”* (36:42) continues the sign of the Ark (36:41). After carrying ancestors in fully loaded Ark, God created for descendants similar things to ride: ships, camels, horses, and by extension all transport. The verse says We created for them, attributing creation of transport to God, not to human invention alone. Human ingenuity builds ships, but materials, buoyancy, wind, and guidance are from God. The phrase like of it, mithlahu, indicates similarity to Ark in function — carrying — not necessarily in form. The Qur'an says *“We made for you ships and cattle to ride”* (43:12) and *“He creates that which you do not know”* (16:8), indicating that transport includes what was known and what would be known later.

**Why Creation of Transport Is a Sign**

Creation of transport is sign because it shows mercy that enables movement, trade, and seeking provision, and power that can withdraw that ability. The sūrah says next that if divine will decreed sinking, no helper would answer and no deliverance would happen except through mercy (36:43-44). Ships that carry can also drown if God wills; camels that carry can stumble; vehicles that carry can crash. Carriage is by permission, not guarantee. The verse reminds that being carried is favor, not right. The Qur'an says *“Do they not see that We singlehandedly created for them cattle which are under their control?”* (36:71) about cattle, emphasizing singlehanded creation without partner. Similar things to ride are also singlehandedly created, subjected to humans, yet humans do not control their ultimate safety. The sign invites gratitude: you are carried, will you not give thanks? The question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) applies to transport as well.

**The Link to Ark and to Mercy**

Linking similar things to Ark shows continuity of mercy from ancestors to descendants. Ancestors carried in Ark through flood that drowned others; descendants carried in ships through sea that could drown. Both are mercy. The Ark was fully loaded, mashḥūn (36:41), indicating preparation and abundance; similar things are also loaded with people and goods. The sūrah stresses We for both: We carried ancestors, We created similar things. The same We that placed barriers (36:9) and resurrects dead (36:12) also carries. The verse also prepares for warning that carriage can end: drowning possible, rescue only by mercy (36:43-44). The Ark story already showed both drowning and rescue; present transport shows same duality. The reader who travels is invited to remember Ark and to see his own travel as continuation of that first rescue, and to give thanks rather than be heedless (36:6).

**A Sign That Is Seen Daily Yet Missed**

Transport is seen daily, yet many miss it as sign because heedlessness (36:6) makes them see only technology, not mercy. The verse says there is a sign for them in Ark and similar things, implying sign is present but may be missed. The sūrah says *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46). Ships and mounts come daily, yet many turn away from seeing them as signs. The verse's purpose is to point: look, We created for you similar things to ride. The pointing is mercy, teaching how to read creation. The Qur'an says *“Do they not see that We have made for them ships and cattle?”* (36:71) as question inviting seeing. Seeing transport as created and subjected leads to gratitude and to trust in One who carries, and to preparation for final carriage — being brought before God (36:32) and returned to Him (36:83). The One who carries through sea and land can carry through death to life.

## Verse 36:43

> If We willed, We could drown them: then no one would respond to their cries, nor would they be rescued—

**Power to Drown Those Carried**

*“If We willed, We could drown them: then no one would respond to their cries, nor would they be rescued”* (36:43) follows the sign of Ark and similar things to ride (36:41-42) with warning that carriage can be withdrawn. The verse says law shaʾnā, if We willed, indicating that drowning is not inevitable but possible by divine will. The same We that carried ancestors in Ark (36:41) and created similar things to ride (36:42) can drown those who ride if He wills. The conditional shows that safety in ships is not guarantee but permission. The Qur'an says *“When they board ships, they call upon God, sincere in religion, but when He delivers them to land, they associate others”* (29:65) and *“When harm touches you at sea, those you call upon besides Him vanish, except Him”* (17:67). Drowning is moment when false gods disappear and only God remains. The verse says then no one would respond to cries, fa-lā ṣarīkha lahum, no helper to answer scream, and nor would they be rescued, wa lā hum yunqadhūn, nor saved. Both help and rescue are denied except by mercy (36:44).

**The Relation to Mercy and Respite**

The next verse says *“except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a little while”* (36:44), showing that non-drowning is by mercy, not entitlement. The sūrah says *“That is design of Almighty, All-Knowing”* (36:38) for sun, indicating power and knowledge; here power to drown is restrained by mercy, allowing enjoyment for a little while. That little while, ḥīn, is worldly life, temporary enjoyment that is *“naught but enjoyment of delusion”* (3:185) compared to next life. The sūrah says *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for provision.

The Ark story (36:41) already showed drowning and rescue: flood drowned all except those in Ark. Present ships are same: they can drown, but mercy rescues. The verse says if We willed, We could drown them, echoing flood where God did will to drown disbelievers and did will to save believers in Ark. Both show that water, which is mercy — rain reviving dead earth (36:33), springs gushing (36:34) — can also be punishment — flood, drowning. Water is both mercy and might, like the pairing Almighty, Most Merciful (36:5). The town that threatened stoning (36:18) thought it had power to punish; this verse says true power to drown belongs to God, and He refrains out of mercy.

**The Link to Final Return**

Finally, drowning and lack of responder prefigure Day of Resurrection when *“no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) and *“hands will speak and feet testify”* (36:65). On that Day, there will also be no responder for those who denied, except mercy from God. The sūrah says *“They cannot help the pagans, even though they serve idols as dedicated guards”* (36:75) about idols, showing that false helpers will not respond. The verse about drowning is early version of that: at sea, no responder; on Day, no responder except mercy. The reader is invited to seek mercy now, while enjoyment for a little while (36:44) remains, by giving thanks for carriage and by following those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21). The One who could drown but instead grants mercy and enjoyment for a little while is Most Compassionate, whose greeting to Paradise residents will be *“Peace”* (36:58). Seeking that peace now is wise, before cry has no responder.

The Qur'an says *“It is He who enables you to travel on land and sea, until when you are in ships and they sail with them with good wind and they rejoice in it, there comes a stormy wind and waves come from every place and they think they are encompassed, they call upon God”* (10:22).

The Qur'an says *“He it is who made sea subservient that you may eat fresh meat therefrom and bring out ornaments to wear, and you see ships plowing in it”* (16:14).

## Verse 36:44

> except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a ˹little˺ while

**Mercy That Allows Enjoyment for a Little While**

*“Except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a little while”* (36:44) completes the warning about drowning (36:43). If God willed, He could drown those in ships with no responder and no rescue, except by mercy from Us, illā raḥmatan minnā, and enjoyment for a little while, wa matāʿan ilā ḥīn. The exception shows that non-drowning, safety at sea and on land, is by mercy, not entitlement. Raḥmah, mercy, is from Us, minnā, emphasizing divine source. The sūrah opened with revelation from Almighty, Most Merciful (36:5), pairing might and mercy; here might to drown is restrained by mercy to allow enjoyment. The enjoyment is for a little while, ilā ḥīn, temporary, not permanent. The Qur'an says worldly enjoyment is *“naught but enjoyment of delusion”* (3:185, 57:20) compared to Hereafter, and *“Whatever you have been given is enjoyment of worldly life, but what is with God is better and more lasting”* (28:60). Little while indicates that mercy gives time, not eternity in this world, time to repent, give thanks, and prepare for return (36:83).

**Why Mercy and Enjoyment Are Paired**

Mercy and enjoyment for a little while are paired because mercy manifests as allowing enjoyment. The sūrah says *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) after provision; allowing enjoyment for a little while is opportunity to answer that question with thanks. The Qur'an says *“Let not those who disbelieve think that respite We grant them is good for them. We only grant them respite that they may increase in sin, and theirs will be humiliating punishment”* (3:178) for those who use respite to increase sin, but for those who use respite to repent, it is good. The sūrah says *“Whenever a sign comes to them, they turn away”* (36:46), showing that many use respite to turn away.

**A Verse That Defines Worldly Life**

This verse defines worldly life as enjoyment for a little while by mercy. It is not ultimate purpose but temporary enjoyment. The Qur'an says *“Worldly life is but play and amusement, but Hereafter is better for those who are mindful”* (6:32). Little while, ḥīn, is indefinite but short compared to eternity. The sūrah later describes Paradise residents as busy enjoying themselves (36:55), having fruits and whatever they desire (36:57), greeted with peace from Merciful Lord (36:58) — enjoyment not for little while but forever. The contrast is intentional: worldly enjoyment for little while by mercy, otherworldly enjoyment forever by mercy. The first is test, second is reward. The sūrah says *“So do not let their words grieve you. Indeed, We fully know what they conceal and reveal”* (36:76), indicating that worldly words and enjoyments are temporary and known to God, while perfect record (36:12) preserves response.

**The Link to Return and Authority**

Finally, enjoyment for a little while ends with return to God (36:83), when authority over all things will be manifest. The sūrah ends *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is authority over all things, and to Whom you will all be returned”* (36:83). Return ends little while. The mercy that allows enjoyment for a little while is from the One to Whom return is, so using little while to prepare for return is wise. The verse is short, but it holds theology of time: worldly time is little while by mercy, given to prepare for eternal. Those who use little while to give thanks, follow Reminder (36:11), follow those who ask no reward (36:21), worship Originator (36:22), will find that mercy that allowed little while also allows entry into Paradise where enjoyment is not little but abundant and where greeting is peace from Merciful Lord (36:58). Those who use little while heedlessly will find that little while ends with single blast (36:49) and no chance to make gift (36:50).

The Qur'an says *“When they forgot what they were reminded of, We opened for them gates of everything, until when they rejoiced in what they were given, We seized them suddenly”* (6:44).

## Verse 36:45

> ˹Still they turn away˺ when it is said to them, “Beware of what is ahead of you ˹in the Hereafter˺ and what is behind you ˹of destroyed nations˺ so you may be shown mercy.”

**A Call to Beware of Before and Behind**

*“Still they turn away when it is said to them, Beware of what is ahead of you in the Hereafter and what is behind you of destroyed nations so you may be shown mercy”* (36:45) describes response to warning. The translation adds still they turn away, which is implied from next verse (36:46) where turning away is explicit. The call is ittaqū, be mindful, beware, fear, protect yourselves, of what is before you and behind you. The sūrah says *“Have they not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back?”* (36:31) — behind is destroyed nations. It says *“They will all be brought before Us”* (36:32) — before is Hereafter.

**Why Before and Behind Are Paired**

Before and behind are paired to indicate totality. A person should be mindful of what is ahead — Hereafter, judgment, return to God (36:83) — and what is behind — world, destroyed nations, past sins — so that he is aware in both directions. The Qur'an says *“Have they not considered what is before them and behind them of heaven and earth? If We willed, We could cause earth to swallow them”* (34:9). Awareness of before and behind produces humility and mercy-seeking. The sūrah says *“so you may be shown mercy”* (36:45), laʿallakum turḥamūn, indicating that mindfulness of before and behind leads to mercy. The town that was extinguished (36:29) was not mindful of before and behind; it threatened messengers without considering past destroyed or future judgment. The believing man was mindful — he worshiped Originator (36:22) and remembered return (36:22) — and was shown mercy: *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) and forgiveness and honor (36:27).

**The Meaning of Mercy Here**

Mercy, raḥmah, that may be shown if mindful, is same mercy that allows enjoyment for little while (36:44) and that greets Paradise residents with peace (36:58). Mercy is both respite now and reward later. The verse says so you may be shown mercy, implying that mindfulness is condition for receiving mercy, though mercy itself is gift. The Qur'an says *“My mercy encompasses all things, so I will decree it for those who are mindful”* (7:156). Mindfulness of before and behind is part of being mindful. The town that killed believer was not mindful, so it was not shown mercy but extinguished (36:29). The believer who was mindful was shown mercy: forgiveness and honor (36:27). The verse is so both warning and invitation: be mindful of what is ahead and behind, so mercy may be shown. The invitation is open, but response described is turning away, which is why next verse says whenever a sign comes, they turn away (36:46).

**A Verse That Defines Mindfulness**

Finally, this verse defines mindfulness as awareness of before and behind. Mindfulness is not only ritual but temporal awareness: remembering past destroyed nations as lesson, anticipating Hereafter as destination, and living present in light of both. The sūrah says *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and leave behind”* (36:12), indicating that what is before and behind is recorded. Being mindful of before and behind is being mindful of record. The perfect record contains past deeds and future recompense. The call to beware is call to live as if record is being written and will be read when all are brought before God (36:32). The verse invites reader to consider what is before — Hereafter, judgment, Paradise and Hell described later (36:55-64) — and what is behind — destroyed peoples, personal sins, worldly delusions — and to seek mercy now while enjoyment for little while remains. Those who turn away will have no responder when drowned (36:43) and no gift when blast seizes (36:50); those who are mindful will have forgiveness and honorable reward (36:11).

The Qur'an says *“Who does greater wrong than one who is reminded of signs of his Lord then turns away from them?”* (18:57, 32:22).

## Verse 36:46
> Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it

**The Habit of Turning Away**
The verse describes a settled habit: whenever a sign comes to these people from their Lord, they turn away from it. The Arabic word used for whenever carries repetition, so the point is not that they turned away once. It is that they turn away every single time.

The Book makes the same observation in almost the same words elsewhere, saying that no sign came to them from among the signs of their Lord but they turned away from it (6:4 states it). And it asks how many signs there are in the heavens and the earth that people pass by while turning away from them (12:105 asks it).

The word for turning away is not the word for not seeing. It describes a refusal to face something that is in front of you. The chapter had described a different condition earlier, where barriers were placed in front of people and behind them so that they could not see (36:9 describes them). This verse describes the active choice that keeps a person behind those barriers, which is why the chapter calls such people heedless (36:6 states it).

**The Signs They Were Turning From**
The chapter had just listed what was in front of them. The dead earth is revived and brings out grain (36:33 states it), and gardens of palms and vines stand among springs (36:34 states it). Everything is created in pairs (36:36 states it). The night is stripped of its day (36:37 states it). The sun runs to a fixed course (36:38 states it) and the moon is measured in phases (36:39 states it), and the two of them do not overtake each other (36:40 states it). Their ancestors were carried in the loaded ark, and other things like it were made for them to ride (36:41 gives the ark, and 36:42 the rest). And God could drown them, and nobody would answer their cry and nobody would rescue them except by His mercy (36:43 states the drowning, and 36:44 gives the exception and the time given to enjoy).

So the signs were not scarce. The response was the problem. The signs were themselves the mercy being offered (36:44 records the time granted), and the people who turn from them are the ones the chapter says the decree has been settled against (36:7 states it).

**The Warning and the Turning**
The verse before this one contains a call: be mindful of what is behind you and in front of you, so that mercy may be shown to you (36:45 gives the call). That warning is itself a sign, and the people turn away from it as well.

The story in the middle of the chapter makes the same point. A town received messengers who came to it (36:13 introduces them), and those messengers delivered their message clearly (36:17 records the delivery), and the town answered with threats and mockery instead. The town of Makkah, which the chapter says mocked the messenger sent to it (36:30 records the mocking), turned away from the wise Book (36:2 describes it).

The Book makes clear that the turning would continue even if the sign were extraordinary. It says that if a gate of heaven were opened and people kept climbing through it, they would still say that their eyes were dazzled and that they were under a spell (15:14 describes the climb, and 15:15 records their excuse).

**What the Turning Leads To**
The chapter then shows what follows, step by step (36:46 is the verse itself). When these people are told to give from what God has provided, they answer by asking why they should feed anyone God could have fed (36:47 records the answer). And when they are told about the threat of the Hour, they ask when it will come (36:48 records the question).

So turning away from the signs is the root, and the refusal to give and the mocking question are the fruit. The chapter says that everything they send ahead and everything they leave behind is written in a clear record (36:12 states it), and that on that day no soul will be wronged at all (36:54 states it).

**The Two Ends of the Two Responses**
The chapter sets the two responses side by side and shows where each one goes. Those who did not turn away are described as people who follow the reminder and fear the Most Merciful without seeing Him (36:11 describes them). They end up busy and happy in a garden, reclining, with fruit, greeted with peace from a merciful Lord (36:55 begins the scene, and 36:56, 36:57 and 36:58 complete it).

Those who turned away hear something very different. They are told to step aside on that day (36:59 records the command). They are asked whether they were not commanded not to worship Satan (36:60 asks it). They are told that this is the fire they were warned about (36:63 states it), and that they should burn in it today (36:64 gives the command).

Both outcomes grow from one thing: what a person did with the signs that kept arriving. So the verse invites its reader to look at the ordinary signs of a day, the grain, the gardens, the night, the sun, the moon and the ships, and to answer the question of what he does when they come.

## Verse 36:47

> And when it is said to them, “Donate from what Allah has provided for you,” the disbelievers say to the believers, “Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed if He wanted to? You are clearly astray!”

**Why This Argument Is Flawed**

The argument is flawed because it confuses divine ability with human responsibility. God can feed poor directly, but He has made feeding poor a means for wealthy to show gratitude and for poor to be tested with patience. The Qur'an says *“We have made some of you a trial for others, will you be patient?”* (25:20). The sūrah earlier said *“so that they may eat from its fruit, which they had no hand in making. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35) and *“They derive from them other benefits and drinks. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:73) about cattle. The disbelievers' argument also contradicts their own belief: they say Allah could feed if He wanted, acknowledging divine power, yet they do not worship Him alone but take other gods hoping to be helped (36:74).

**You Are Clearly Astray as Reversal**

They say to believers *“You are clearly astray”* (36:47), using same phrase believing man used for himself if he took other gods: *“Indeed, I would then be clearly astray”* (36:24). The disbelievers reverse label, calling believers astray for asking to feed poor. This reversal is common: those who are astray call guided astray. The sūrah says *“Yet he already misled great multitudes of you. Did you not have any sense?”* (36:62) about Satan misleading. Those misled think they are guided and call guided astray. The Qur'an says *“When it is said to them, Do not cause corruption on earth, they say We are only reformers. Unquestionably, it is they who are corrupters, but they perceive not”* (2:11-12). Calling believers clearly astray for enjoining charity shows corruption of values. Charity is not astray; it is straight path. The sūrah says worshiping God alone is straight path (36:61), and charity is part of worship.

**The Relation to Shackles and Barriers**

Refusal to donate relates to earlier image of shackles around necks up to chins (36:8), which prevents extending hands to give. Hands shackled to necks cannot donate. The disbelievers' argument — why should we feed those whom Allah could feed — is intellectual shackle that binds hands from giving. The sūrah says *“Do not shackle your hand to your neck”* (17:29) as warning against miserliness. The disbelievers have shackled hands by reasoning that excuses miserliness. The perfect record (36:12) records both provision and refusal to donate, and *“On that Day no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) will be based on that. The sūrah later says *“They cannot help the pagans, even though they serve idols as dedicated guards”* (36:75), showing that those who refuse to help poor end up serving idols that cannot help them. Refusal to help leads to serving what cannot help.

This verse follows *“except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a little while”* (36:44) and call to beware of before and behind so mercy may be shown (36:45). Donating from what Allah provided is way to show gratitude for enjoyment for little while and to seek mercy. Refusing to donate is using enjoyment heedlessly, which leads to being among those who turn away from signs (36:46). The disbelievers' refusal is ingratitude. The verse also shows that disbelievers understand provision comes from Allah — they say from what Allah has provided — yet they refuse to share. Acknowledging provision without sharing is incomplete thanks. The believing man who rushed from farthest end (36:20) gave his life, not just wealth, showing complete thanks. His reward was forgiveness and honor (36:27). Those who refuse to give will be told *“Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:64), showing that ingratitude has consequence.

The Qur'an says *“And in their wealth there is right for the beggar and deprived”* (51:19).

## Verse 36:48
> And they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”

**A Question That Is Not a Question**
The verse records what these people say: they ask when this promise will come about, if the messengers are telling the truth. The word used is the word for a promise, and it covers the resurrection, the Hour and the punishment.

The question is not an attempt to learn something. It is mockery. The people asking it are not waiting for an answer. They are making the warning look foolish. The same question appears in other sūrahs in the same mocking tone, put by people who have already decided not to believe (34:29 records one of them).

**Why the Question Misses the Point**
The question rests on a false expectation. They assume that a messenger who cannot name the exact time of the Hour must not be telling the truth, and the Book answers that the knowledge of the Hour belongs to God alone. It tells the Prophet that people ask him about the Hour and its time, and that he should say that knowledge of it is with his Lord, and that nobody but He will reveal its time (7:187 states all of that).

And the chapter has already said what a messenger's duty is: clear delivery of the message (36:17 states it). He is not sent to hand over the timetable of the unseen.

The Book also describes the difference between the two kinds of people in one sentence. Those who do not believe in the Hour try to hurry it on, while those who believe are afraid of it and know that it is the truth (42:18 states both).

**Why the Threat Was Delayed**
The threat was mocked because it had not arrived yet. The chapter says why: God allows people to enjoy themselves for a little while out of mercy (36:44 states it).

The delay was not evidence that the warning was false. It was time given, and the chapter shows what people did with it.

The answer to the mocking question comes in the very next verse. A single blast will seize them while they are still arguing about it (36:49 describes the blast). The town in the earlier story was destroyed in the same way, and the chapter says the blast took them while they disputed (36:29 describes that destruction). So the threat comes at the time God has set and not at the time the mockers choose.

**How This Verse Relates to the One Before It**
This verse follows the refusal to give charity (36:47 records that refusal), and the two belong together. Both are forms of turning away from the signs (36:46 describes that turning). Refusing to give turns away from the sign of provision, which is the food in front of them. Asking when the threat will come turns away from the sign of warning.

Both show the heedlessness the chapter named at its start (36:6 names it), and both sit behind the barriers the chapter described (36:9 describes them).

The sequence runs in order: the signs are shown (36:33 begins the list of them), the call to be mindful is given (36:45 gives it), the people turn away from the signs (36:46 describes it), then they refuse to give (36:47 records it), then they mock the threat (36:48 records it), and then the blast seizes them (36:49 describes it).

**Who Was Being Asked**
One detail is worth noticing. The mockers put their question to the believers, not only to the messenger (36:48 records the question as put to them). That happens because the believers also warn people, and because the messenger they follow asks for no payment and only wants them on the right road (36:21 states his position). The messenger's own call to worship is recorded in the chapter, along with the warning against taking other gods (36:22 gives the call, and 36:23 the warning).

So mocking the believers is simply an extension of mocking the messenger, and the chapter answers it by describing what happened to a town that treated the one believer among its own people the same way (36:20 describes him). The words of that believer are recorded in the chapter, where he called his people to follow the messengers and to fear a painful day (36:25 records his call). And the welcome he received is recorded too, since he was told to enter the garden (36:26 records it).

**The Right Way to Face an Unseen Threat**
The verse also shows, by contrast, what the right attitude looks like. It is belief, and fear, and preparation. The chapter names the people who take that attitude: they follow the reminder and fear the Most Merciful without seeing Him (36:11 describes them).

Preparation, in this chapter, has a shape. Give from what God has provided (36:47 states what was refused). Worship the One who created you, and to whom you will be returned (36:22 records the call). Do not take other gods beside Him (36:23 gives the warning). And remember that the record keeps everything (36:12 states it), while the age to come is the one that counts. The chapter describes its arrival as a single cry that seizes people while they are still arguing, with no time left to make a will or return home (36:49 describes the cry). And it says that no soul will be wronged by even the smallest weight on that day, since people are repaid only for what they used to do (36:54 states both).

The people who mocked the question will have no time to settle anything when that day arrives, since a single cry will take them while they are still arguing about it (36:50 describes the moment). The mockers wanted to talk about the timetable of the Hour. The chapter tells its reader to spend his time on the record instead of the date.

## Verse 36:49

> They must be awaiting a single Blast, which will seize them while they are ˹entrenched˺ in ˹worldly˺ disputes

**Single Blast Seizing While Disputing**

*“They must be awaiting a single Blast, which will seize them while they are entrenched in worldly disputes”* (36:49) answers the mocking question when will threat come (36:48). The answer is not date but description: threat will come as single blast, ṣayḥah wāḥidah, seizing them while they dispute, yakhiṣṣimūn, entrenched in worldly disputes. The same phrase single blast appears for town extinguished at once (36:29) and for resurrection when all will be brought before Us (36:53). One blast can destroy, another can resurrect. The blast here is first blast, ṣaʿqah, that ends worldly life, seizing people unexpectedly. The Book states *“It will not come upon you except suddenly”* (7:187) about Hour, and *“The Hour will come upon them suddenly while they perceive not”* (12:107). Seizing while disputing shows suddenness and heedlessness: they are disputing about provision, threat, messengers, while blast comes. The sūrah says *“Then no final will can be made nor return to family possible”* (36:50) next, showing that blast leaves no time.

**Why Disputes Are Mentioned**

Disputes, khusūmah, worldly disputes, indicate absorption in present argument — about wealth, status, provision, threat — that distracts from preparation. The disbelievers who said *“Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed?”* (36:47) and *“When will this threat come?”* (36:48) are examples of disputing. They dispute about donating and about timing of threat, while blast awaits. Scripture notes *“They await not but a single blast that will seize them while they dispute”* (36:49), implying that disputing is what they are doing when seized, so disputing does not prevent blast; it is context in which blast comes. The believing man rushed from farthest end (36:20) and declared belief (36:25) instead of disputing, so he was not among those seized while disputing; he was told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) before. The verse so contrasts two ways of spending little while allowed by mercy (36:44): disputing or rushing to truth. One leads to being seized, other to being honored.

**Single Blast as Divine Power**

Single blast shows divine power that does not need armies (36:28). One blast extinguished town (36:29); one blast will seize all while disputing (36:49); one blast will bring all before God (36:53). The same means — sound, cry — has different effects by divine will. It is reported *“All it takes, when He wills something to be, is simply to say to it: Be! And it is”* (36:82). Blast is like Be — command that creates effect instantly. The town's threat *“we will certainly stone you and you will be touched with painful punishment from us”* (36:18) required many stones and many people; God's blast requires one sound. The disproportion shows true power. The sūrah says *“If We willed, We could drown them”* (36:43) and *“Had We willed, We could have blinded their eyes”* (36:66) and *“transfigured them on the spot”* (36:67), all showing that God can punish in many ways, but chooses single blast as means that is sudden and all-encompassing.

**A Verse That Defines Urgency**

Finally, this verse defines urgency. They must be awaiting single blast seizing while disputing — that is what is actually coming, so urgency is needed. The Qur'an says *“The Hour is near”* (54:1) and *“What will make you know? Perhaps Hour is near”* (42:17). The verse invites reader to not be among those entrenched in worldly disputes when blast comes, but among those who follow those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21), who worship Originator (36:22), who declare belief (36:25), and who are told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26). The verse is short, but it holds complete warning: single blast awaits, it seizes while disputing, so stop disputing and start following, before no gift can be made (36:50).

## Verse 36:50

**No Time for Gift or Return**

*“Then they will not be able to make a last will, nor can they return to their own people”* (36:50) continues description of single blast seizing while disputing (36:49). The Qur'an says *“When death comes to one of them, he says My Lord, send me back, that I may do righteous deeds in what I left behind”* (23:99-100), but return is denied. The sūrah earlier said *“Have they not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back?”* (36:31) — no return to worldly life. The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they sent forth and left behind will be what remains; no new gift can add to it after blast.

**Why Gift and Return Are Mentioned**

Gift, waṣiyyah, is final act of disposing wealth, advising family, seeking forgiveness. Return to people, rajʿ ilā ahl, is final act of rejoining family, saying farewell, fixing relations. Both are things people think they will have time for at end. The verse says they will not, because blast seizes suddenly while disputing, not while preparing. The Qur'an says *“Death will seize you wherever you are, even if you are in lofty towers”* (4:78) and *“When their term comes, they cannot delay by an hour nor advance”* (16:61). The blast is term that cannot be delayed. The sūrah says *“They must be awaiting a single Blast, which will seize them while they are entrenched in worldly disputes”* (36:49) — disputing, not preparing, so no gift, no return. The believing man who rushed from farthest end (36:20) and declared belief (36:25) made his gift in advance — follow messengers (36:20-21) — and his return was to Paradise (36:26), not to people. He prepared before blast, so his gift remains.

**The Relation to Worldly Disputes**

Being unable to make gift or return is consequence of being entrenched in worldly disputes (36:49). Those absorbed in disputes about provision (36:47) and timing of threat (36:48) think they have time to continue disputing, but blast ends disputing and leaves no time for gift or return. The verse warns against absorption in disputes that distract from preparation. The Qur'an says *“Let not worldly life delude you”* (35:5). Disputing about worldly matters is delusion that there is time. The sūrah says *“except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a little while”* (36:44) — little while is time to prepare gift and return to God, not time to dispute. The verse says no gift, no return, showing that little while ends without warning. The perfect record (36:12) will show whether little while was used for disputing or for following Reminder (36:11) and giving thanks (36:35).

**A Verse That Teaches Urgency of Preparation**

This verse teaches urgency of preparation. Since blast can seize while disputing with no time for gift or return, preparation must be now. The Qur'an says *“O you who believe, fear God and let every soul look to what it has sent forth for tomorrow”* (59:18). What is sent forth (36:12) is what remains after blast, not gift made after. The believing man's gift — follow messengers who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21) — was made before his death, and it remains as example. His return was to Paradise (36:26), not to people, because he sought return to Originator (36:22). The verse invites reader to make gift now — donate from what Allah provided (36:47), follow Reminder (36:11), worship Originator (36:22) — and to return to God now, before forced return (36:83) when no gift or return to people possible.

## Verse 36:51

> The Trumpet will be blown ˹a second time˺, then—behold!—they will rush from the graves to their Lord

**Second Blast and Rushing From Graves**

*“The Trumpet will be blown a second time, then behold, they will rush from the graves to their Lord”* (36:51) describes resurrection after first blast that seized while disputing (36:49-50). The trumpet, ṣūr, is blown by angel Isrāfīl. First blow ends worldly life; second begins next life. The Qur'an says *“The Trumpet will be blown, and all who are in heavens and earth will swoon except whom God wills, then it will be blown again, and behold, they will stand looking”* (39:68). The interval between blows is said to be forty years in some reports. The verb yansilūn, rush, from nasala, to run quickly, indicates hasty movement, like rushing from farthest end of city (36:20) and rushing from graves. The sūrah says *“They will all be brought before Us”* (36:32, 36:53), and rushing from graves is how. The phrase to their Lord, ilā rabbihim, shows destination: return to Originator (36:22) and to One in Whose Hands is authority (36:83). Resurrection is not random scattering but directed rushing to Lord.

**Why Rushing Is Used**

Rushing indicates urgency and inevitability. The Qur'an says *“On Day when they will rush from graves as if rushing to a goal”* (70:43). The rushing also mirrors worldly rushing: the believing man rushed from farthest end to support messengers (36:20); now all rush from graves to Lord. The sūrah says *“Indeed, on that Day residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55), while others will rush in terror. The graves, ajdāth, are places of rest, marqad (36:52), from which they are raised. The raising is described as *“Who has raised us from our place of rest?”* (36:52), showing that graves were rest, now ended.

**How This Verse Supports Resurrection Argument**

This verse supports resurrection argument that runs through sūrah: dead earth revived (36:33), night stripped of day (36:37), sun fixed term (36:38), moon phases (36:39), fire from green trees (36:80), creation of heavens and earth (36:81). Rushing from graves is final proof that resurrection happens, not just possible. The sūrah earlier said *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and leave behind”* (36:12) as assertion; now it describes how — trumpet blown, rushing from graves. The description is vivid, meant to make resurrection imaginable. The Qur'an says *“They will come forth from graves as if they were scattered locusts, rushing to the caller”* (54:7-8). The image of rushing from graves is repeated to emphasize that resurrection is collective, swift, and directed. The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they sent forth will be basis for judgment when they rush to Lord.

**A Verse That Warns and Gives Hope**

**The Rush Out of the Graves**
The verse describes the moment the trumpet is blown and people come out of their graves and hurry toward their Lord. The picture is of a crowd moving toward the One who will judge them, and the chapter holds up both things that move them: fear of the account, and hope of mercy.

**The Fear**
The account is real, and the verse before this one states its terms: no soul will be wronged on that day, and people are repaid only for what they used to do (36:54 states both). Everyone without exception is brought before God, which the chapter states plainly (36:32 states it).

**The Hope**
The same rush can be a rush toward mercy, because the Lord being rushed to is the Most Merciful. The sūrah opened by naming the revelation as coming from the Almighty, the Most Merciful (36:5 names both). And the people who reach the garden are greeted with peace from a merciful Lord (36:58 gives the greeting).

The man in the middle of the story shows the hope side of it. He was told to enter the garden (36:26 records the welcome), and he is among the people who are busy and happy there (36:55 describes them).

**Everyone Rushes, With Different Outcomes**
The chapter says that all of them will be brought before God, believers and deniers together (36:32 states it), so the rush itself is not what decides anything. What decides is how a person spent the time before it, and the chapter has shown both ways of spending it.

The favored way is described at the start of the sūrah: following the reminder and fearing the Most Merciful without seeing Him (36:11 describes it). It is also described in the story, where the messengers asked for no payment and only wanted the people on the right road (36:21 states their position).

The other way is described just as clearly: turning away from the signs that keep arriving (36:46 describes it), refusing to give from what has been provided (36:47 records the refusal), and mocking the warning by asking when it will arrive (36:48 records the question).

**What the Verse Asks of Its Reader**
The rushing is not something a person can avoid. He can only decide what he will be carrying when it happens. So the verse invites its reader to look at the two endings the chapter has set out side by side: the garden where people are busy and happy (36:55 describes it), and the fire that the others are told to enter and burn in that day (36:64 gives the command).

The Qur'an says *“On Day when some signs of your Lord come, belief will not benefit soul that did not believe before”* (6:158). Raising from place of rest is sign whose belief after seeing does not benefit. The verse invites reader to recognize now, while enjoyment for little while remains (36:44), that Most Compassionate did warn and messengers told truth. That he will not be among those crying woe on Day, but among those busy enjoying in Paradise (36:55), reclining in shade (36:56), having fruits and whatever they desire (36:57), greeted with peace from Merciful Lord (36:58). The choice is between early recognition leading to Paradise and late recognition leading to woe.

Same gathering, different outcomes, based on what was sent forth and left behind (36:12) and recorded in perfect Record.

**A Verse That Defines Ease of Resurrection**

Finally, this verse defines ease of resurrection: one blast, at once, all brought. It is easy for God, hard for humans to imagine, but easy for Master Creator, All-Knowing (36:81). The verse invites reader to trust that resurrection will happen as easily as described. To prepare for being brought before God by following Reminder (36:11), following those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21), worshiping Originator (36:22), giving thanks for provision (36:35, 36:73), and seeking mercy while enjoyment for little while remains (36:44). The One who can bring all before Him with one blast can also forgive and honor with one word *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) and greet with *“Peace from Merciful Lord”* (36:58). The choice is between being brought to punishment or to peace, and preparation now determines outcome when one blast brings all before Us.

## Verse 36:57

> There they will have fruits and whatever they desire

**Fruits and Desires in Paradise**

*“There they will have fruits and whatever they desire”* (36:57) continues Paradise description after busy enjoying (36:55) and shade and couches with spouses (36:56). Fruits, fākihah, indicates delights, not just food but enjoyment. The sūrah earlier mentioned grain for eating (36:33), gardens of palms and grapevines and springs for fruit (36:34-35), and cattle benefits and drinks (36:72-73). Paradise fruits perfect those worldly fruits, without toil, without seasonal limit, without decay. The Qur'an says *“Every time they are provided with fruit therefrom as provision, they will say This is what we were provided before, and they will be given similar”* (2:25) — similar in name but superior in taste. Whatever they desire, mā yaddaʿūn, whatever they call for, claim, wish, indicates that Paradise provides not only fruits but anything desired. The Qur'an says *“They will have whatever they wish with their Lord”* (25:16, 39:34, 41:31, 42:22) and *“In it is whatever souls desire and eyes delight in”* (43:71). The verse promises abundance beyond fruits: whatever desired is granted.

**Why Fruits and Desires Are Paired**

Fruits and whatever they desire are paired to show that Paradise provides both specific — fruits — and general — whatever desired. Fruits are example of provision that is tangible, familiar, delightful; whatever they desire is general promise that covers all wishes. The pairing shows that Paradise is not limited to fruits; it includes all desires. The sūrah says *“Indeed, on that Day residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55) — busy enjoying includes fruits and desires. The verse says there, fīhā, in it, in Paradise, indicating location of fruits and desires. The worldly gardens and springs (36:34) were in earth, fīhā, in it, earth; Paradise fruits are in it, Paradise, showing parallel but superior location. The question *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for worldly fruits should lead to thanks that leads to heavenly fruits. Those who gave thanks for worldly fruits will have heavenly fruits and whatever they desire.

**How This Verse Describes Abundance**

This verse describes abundance: fruits and whatever they desire. Abundance is characteristic of Paradise, where there is no scarcity, no competition, no envy. The Qur'an says *“No soul knows what is hidden for them of comfort as reward for what they used to do”* (32:17). Fruits and whatever they desire is part of hidden comfort. The sūrah says *“On that Day no soul will be wronged, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do”* (36:54), so abundance is reward for deeds, not arbitrary. The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they sent forth determines abundance. Those who sent forth following Reminder (36:11), donating from provision (36:47), worshiping Originator (36:22) will have abundance; those who turned away (36:46) will have *“This is Hell you were warned of. Burn in it today”* (36:63-64), no fruits, no desires fulfilled, but barrier between them and what they desire (34:54).

**The Link to Peace From Merciful Lord**

Finally, fruits and whatever they desire are followed by ultimate greeting: *“Peace will be their greeting from Merciful Lord”* (36:58). Fruits and desires are provision; peace from Lord is greeting that makes provision enjoyable. Without peace, fruits would not be enjoyed; with peace, even simple fruit is delightful. The sūrah's progression from busy enjoying (36:55) to shade and couches (36:56) to fruits and desires (36:57) to peace from Lord (36:58) shows that Paradise enjoyment culminates in divine greeting, not just material. The verse invites reader to see fruits now as sign pointing to fruits then. To see whatever desired now as sign that true fulfillment is with Lord who can grant whatever desired in Paradise, where no soul wronged and reward is based on what was used to do, and where authority over all things ensures that fruits and desires are granted without limit.

A town received messengers who came to it (36:13 introduces them) and who told the people plainly that their only duty was clear delivery (36:17 records the words). The town ignored the warning and was destroyed at once (36:29 describes the end of it). The people of Makkah were warned by a wise Book (36:2 describes it) and by signs spread through the world around them (36:33 begins the list), and the chapter keeps pointing out that they turned away. The chapter also records that no messenger came to them without being mocked (36:30 states it) and that earlier peoples were destroyed as examples (36:31 states it). And when the call is finally believed, the Book records the confession that the messengers of the Lord had brought the truth (36:52 records it).

The warning itself is the mercy, and the record keeps what was done with it (36:12 states it). So the reader is invited to take the warning while it is still a warning. The choice is between the peace of the garden and being told to burn in the fire that this verse points at (36:64 records the command).

## Verse 36:64
> Burn in it Today for your disbelief.”

**The Command to Burn, and What It Answers**
The verse is the sentence spoken to the people who were shown the fire: burn in it today for what you used to reject (36:64 records the command). The verse before it says that this is the fire they were warned about (36:63 states it), and the command comes from God, in whose hand the authority over all things is held (36:83 states it).

The word "today" matters. It fixes the sentence at the moment of the reckoning, the day when no soul is wronged (36:54 states it), and it allows no delay of the kind they once asked about (36:48 records the question).

**What the Denial Was**
The chapter lists what the denial consisted of, and every item is a deed rather than an opinion. They turned away from the signs (36:46 describes it). They refused to give from what they had been provided (36:47 records it). They mocked the warning (36:48 records it). They took other gods in the hope of help (36:74 states the hope), and they followed the path of Satan instead of the command they had been given (36:60 records the question about it). So their rejection was a way of living, and the last words of the verse say exactly that: the burning answers what they kept doing.

The record is what the sentence is read from. Everything a person sends ahead and everything he leaves behind is written down (36:12 states it), so the judgment is not a matter of memory or of argument on that day.

**The Other Sentence Spoken in the Same Scene**
The sūrah sets the two endings side by side. The people of the garden are told nothing. They are greeted, and the greeting is peace from a merciful Lord (36:58 states it). They are busy enjoying themselves (36:55 states it), with their spouses in shade on raised couches (36:56 describes them), and with fruit and whatever else they ask for (36:57 states it).

So the same day hands down a sentence of burning and a word of peace, and it gives each to the people who chose it. Neither is unfair, because no soul is wronged and nobody is repaid for anything except what he used to do (36:54 states both).

**Where the Verse Points Its Reader**
The verse is short and final: burn, today, for what you kept doing. The chapter offers its reader the other sentence instead, and it names the people who hear it: the ones who follow the reminder and fear the Most Merciful without seeing Him (36:11 describes them), the messengers who asked for no payment and wanted only the right road (36:21 states their position), the man who called his people to worship the One who created them and to whom they return (36:22 records the call), and the same man declaring his belief in the Lord of the messengers (36:25 records his words). The time for that choice is the little while of ease that is still running (36:44 states it).

**Why the Punishment Is Real and Not a Picture**
The Book says of the fire that it waits in ambush and is a destination for the transgressors (78:21 states it). The town in the story was warned that it would be touched by a painful punishment (36:18 records the warning), and what came was God's punishment rather than the town's own threats. The people who mocked were seized while they were still arguing, with no time left to make a gift or a return (36:49 describes the seizure). And the town was destroyed by a single blast (36:29 describes it), which shows how quickly what people treat as far off arrives. The "today" of this verse is that same arrival, this time with no end to it.

## Verse 36:65
> On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit

**Mouths Sealed, Hands Speaking, Feet Testifying**
The verse describes the scene of judgment: mouths are sealed, hands speak to God, and feet give testimony about what their owners used to earn (36:65 states it). It comes right after the command given to the people of the fire (36:63 states it), and it belongs to the same day, the day when no soul is wronged (36:54 states it).

A sealed mouth is the end of argument. These were the people whose mouths did the talking in this world. They called the messengers liars (36:15 describes it), they mocked them (36:30 states it), they asked why they should feed people whom God could have fed (36:47 records the question), and they asked when the warning would arrive (36:48 records the question). On that day the mouth is closed and the case is made from somewhere else.

**The Witnesses Come From the Body Itself**
The hands and feet of a person are the parts that did the deeds, so they are the parts that speak. The people of the town had threatened the messengers with stoning, which is done by hand (36:18 records the threat), and those hands will be asked about it. The man who believed came running from the far end of the town (36:20 describes his coming), and the feet that carried him will speak on his side.

The Book describes the same scene in other places. It says that on the day their tongues, hands and feet will testify against them about what they used to do (24:24 states it), and it describes the day when people reach the fire and their hearing, sight and skins testify against them (41:20 describes it). Nothing in the scene is invented and nothing is left to memory, because what a person sent ahead and what he left behind stand in a clear record (36:12 states it).

**Why Partial Witnesses Are Enough**
A person being judged wants to control the story, and sealing the mouth removes that. What is left is the record together with the testimony of the body. Both belong to their owner: the record was written as he acted, and the limbs travelled with him wherever he went. So the sentence that follows cannot be called unjust. The chapter states the point twice over, that no soul is wronged and that people are repaid only for what they used to do (36:54 states both).

**The Same Power at Work All Through the Chapter**
The One who makes a hand speak or a mouth silent is the One who made the hand in the first place, and the chapter has spent its length listing what He made: everything created in pairs (36:36 states it), the night, the day, the sun and the moon moving by His order (36:37 describes them), the ark and the ships that carry people (36:41 states it), and the cattle that were made manageable for them (36:71 states it).

The two verses after this one carry the same theme, showing what could be done to these same bodies if He willed: taking away their sight so that they grope for the road (36:66 states it), or changing their shapes where they stand so that they can neither move on nor turn back (36:67 describes it). A body that can be reshaped by a word can also be made to speak. The whole scene rests on the authority of the One in whose hand everything is held (36:83 states it).

**What the Verse Asks of Its Reader**
The verse is a warning about hands and feet. They are used every day, they are being recorded, and a day is coming when their owner's mouth will be closed and they will do the talking. The invitation of the chapter is to use them now on the road that has its witnesses on its side: alongside the people who ask nothing in return and are themselves on the right road (36:21 states their position), in the worship of the One who created them (36:22 records the call), and away from the powers that were taken as helpers in the hope of rescue (36:74 describes their hope), together with the guards that will be brought out on that day (36:75 describes them). The other possibility is the moment when a person has no gift and no return left to offer (36:50 describes it).

## Verse 36:66
> Had We willed, We could have easily blinded their eyes, so they would struggle to find their way. How then could they see

> Had We willed, We could have easily blinded their eyes, so they would struggle to find their way. How then could they see

> Had We willed, We could have easily blinded their eyes, so they would struggle to find their way. How then could they see

**Why Blinding Eyes Is Mentioned**

**How Then Could They See as Rhetorical Question**

**A Verse That Defines Sight as Gift**

**The Gift of Sight, Named as a Gift**
The verse says that if God wished, He could take away the people's eyes, and then they would be stumbling about trying to find the road. It comes after a run of signs that are all seen with the eyes, which is why the verse uses eyes to make its point.

The chapter had listed those visible signs one after another. The dead earth is revived (36:33 states it), gardens and springs come out of it (36:34 states it), and people eat the fruit of it (36:35 states it). Everything is created in pairs (36:36 states it). The night is stripped of its day (36:37 states it), the sun runs to a fixed course (36:38 states it), the moon is measured in phases (36:39 states it), and the two of them move in their orbits (36:40 states it). The ark carries people and the ships do too (36:41 states it), and the cattle were created for them and made manageable (36:71 states both).

And still many of these people turn away from all of it (36:46 describes the turning).

**What the Verse Says About Seeing**
So the verse makes a simple point. Being able to see, and being able to find your way, are gifts rather than rights. The Book says the same thing in one sentence elsewhere: it is God who made for people hearing, sight and hearts, and little thanks do they give (23:78 states all three).

Sight exists so that a person can look at the signs and be grateful. The chapter keeps naming gratitude as the proper response: the people who eat the fruit of the earth are asked to give thanks for it (36:35 states it), and the same gratitude is asked again over the cattle (36:73 states it).

The question at the end of the run is the one this verse leaves with its reader. A person who could not see would not find the road at all. So sight was given for a purpose, and the purpose was not to walk past the signs with eyes open and never look. The Book states the same ownership in another sūrah, where it names God as the One who created people and gave them hearing, sight and hearts (67:23 states it).

**Where This Verse Sits in the Sūrah**
The verse comes in a run of statements about what God could do to these people if He wished. The same run says that He could place barriers in front of them and behind them so that they do not see (36:9 describes it). The chapter also says that the warning reaches all alike, so that it makes no difference whether a person is warned or not (36:10 states it). And it says that He could change their shapes where they stand, which is the power the chapter shows at work in the account of what happens to the guilty (36:61 records the account).

Taking away sight altogether is what this verse names, and the verses just after it carry the same point: the account will be settled, since mouths will be sealed and hands and feet will give testimony (36:65 describes it), and the fire is named for the people who chose the other road (36:64 gives the command).

The chapter also shows the opposite response. The people who follow the reminder and fear the Most Merciful without seeing Him are the ones who make good use of the sight they were given (36:11 describes them). The messengers in the story asked for no payment and wanted only the right road (36:21 states their position), and the man who believed called his people to worship the One who created them (36:22 records the call).

And the ending waiting for the people on the first road is a greeting of peace from a merciful Lord (36:58 gives it). Whatever eyes are given or taken, the saying of that day is the one the verse before this run states: no soul will be wronged at all (36:54 states it).

## Verse 36:67

> And had We willed, We could have transfigured them on the spot, so they could neither progress forward nor turn back

**Why Transfiguration on the Spot Is Mentioned**

Transfiguration on spot is mentioned because movement — progress forward and return — is gift that could be taken. The sūrah says *“They will not be able to make a last will, nor can they return to their own people”* (36:50) about those seized by blast while disputing — no return. Transfiguration would be similar: no progress, no return, frozen in place. The verse shows that ability to move, to progress, to return, is gift. The Qur'an says *“It is He who made earth tractable for you, so walk in its tracts and eat of His provision”* (67:15) — walking is by subjection. The sūrah says *“We have subjected these animals to them, so they may ride some and eat others”* (36:72) — riding is by subjection. Movement is gift. The verse asks to use movement to follow straight path (36:61), to rush from farthest end to support messengers (36:20), not to threaten stoning (36:18) or dispute (36:49). The believing man used feet to rush; his feet will testify for him (36:65). Those who use movement to transgress could be transfigured so they cannot move.

**The Relation to Barriers, Blinding, Sealing**

This verse relates to barriers before and behind (36:9) — barriers prevent progress and return; blinding eyes (36:66) prevents seeing way; sealing mouths (36:65) prevents speech; transfiguring on spot prevents movement altogether. The control is total, but restraint is mercy allowing enjoyment for little while (36:44). The perfect record (36:12) records how movement was used — to rush to truth or to threaten, to donate or refuse, to follow or turn away. On Day when no soul wronged (36:54), hands will speak and feet testify (36:65) about movement.

**A Verse That Defines Movement as Gift**

Finally, this verse defines movement as gift that could be taken, so it should be used for thanks and guidance. The sūrah says *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for provision; movement is provision. Walking is gift. The verse is short, but it holds complete theology of movement as gift, power to take gift. Inability to progress or return if taken, inviting to use movement now to follow those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21), to worship Originator (36:22), to rush from farthest end to support truth (36:20), before being among those who cannot progress or return, either in world by transfiguration or on Day when all brought before God (36:32, 36:53) with no soul wronged (36:54) and told either peace (36:58) or burn (36:64).

**Movement as Gift That Could Be Frozen**

Transfiguring on spot so they could neither progress nor turn back shows movement is gift that could be taken. The town extinguished at once (36:29) could neither progress nor return; those reversed in long life (36:68) lose ability to progress as before. The verse invites to use movement now to rush from farthest end to support truth (36:20), to follow those who ask no reward (36:21), to worship Originator (36:22), before inability, and to prepare for return to God (36:83) where authority over all things.

## Verse 36:68

> And whoever We grant a long life, We reverse them in development. Will they not then understand

*“And whoever We grant a long life, We reverse them in development. Will they not then understand?”* (36:68) follows power to blind eyes (36:66) and transfigure on spot (36:67) with natural reversal that happens in long life. Man nuʿammirhu nunakkishu fī al-khalq, whoever We grant long life, We reverse him in creation, development. The Qur'an says *“God creates you, then causes you to die, and among you is he who is brought back to most abject age, so that he knows nothing after having known”* (16:70) and *“Among you is he who is brought back to most abject age”* (22:5).

**Why Reversal Is a Sign**

Reversal in development is sign because it shows that creation has stages and that this world is not final. The sūrah earlier described dead earth revived (36:33), night stripped of day (36:37), sun fixed term (36:38), moon phases ending like old stalk (36:39) — all show transformation. Long life reversed in development is transformation of human himself, from strength to weakness, which should lead to understanding that just as strength reversed to weakness, weakness can be reversed to strength in resurrection. The sūrah says *“Can the One who created heavens and earth not easily resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can! For He is Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81). Creating human first time and reversing in development shows power to recreate again. The verse also shows that long life is not necessarily good; it can be reversal, so quality of life, not length, matters. The believing man who was killed and told *“Enter Paradise”* (36:26) had short life but honor; those granted long life but not understanding will be reversed and then brought before God (36:32).

**The Relation to Transfiguration and Blinding**

This verse relates to transfiguration on spot (36:67) and blinding eyes (36:66) as natural version of same. Transfiguration on spot would be sudden immobilization; reversal in long life is gradual immobilization through aging. Sealing mouths (36:65) could be sudden; reversal includes weakening of speech. The restraint from sudden taking is mercy allowing enjoyment for little while (36:44) and time to understand. Reversal in long life is itself sign that should lead to understanding: will they not then understand that life is temporary and return to God (36:83) is coming.

**A Verse That Defines Life as Stages**

Finally, this verse defines life as stages: grant long life, reverse in development. Life is not static but staged, with growth and reversal, both by divine will. The sūrah says *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and leave behind”* (36:12) — stages of life are recorded, and reversal is part of record. The verse is short, but it holds complete reflection on aging as sign that should lead to understanding that this world is temporary, that return to God is coming, that authority over all things is in His Hands (36:83). That true success is not long life but forgiveness and honor (36:27) and peace from Merciful Lord (36:58). The reader who sees old person reversed is invited to understand that he too will be reversed if granted long life. To use current strength to follow Reminder (36:11), follow those who ask no reward (36:21), worship Originator (36:22), give thanks (36:35, 36:73), donate (36:47), before reversal and before being brought before God with no soul wronged (36:54).

## Verse 36:69

> We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him. This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran

**Not Poetry, But Reminder and Clear Qur'an**

*“We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him. This Book is only a Reminder and a clear Qur'an”* (36:69) defends Prophet against accusation that Qur'an is poetry. The Quraysh said *“He is a poet”* and *“We will wait for him misfortune of time”* (52:30), and *“This is nothing but poetry”*. The verse says We have not taught him poetry, wa mā ʿallamnāhu al-shiʿr, and it is not fitting for him, wa mā yanbaghī lahu, it does not behoove him, it is not appropriate. Poetry, shiʿr, in Arab culture was art of imagination, exaggeration, sometimes falsehood, and poets were seen as inspired by jinn. The Qur'an says *“Poets are followed by the deviators. Do you not see that they wander in every valley and say what they do not do?”* (26:224-226). Teaching poetry would be teaching wandering and saying what not done, which is not fitting for messenger whose duty is clear delivery (36:17) and whose speech is truth. The verse says this Book is only Reminder, dhikr, and clear Qur'an, qurʾān mubīn, not poetry. Reminder, as earlier *“You can only warn those who follow Reminder”* (36:11), is what reminds of origin, return, signs. Clear Qur'an, mubīn, makes clear, as in *“Our duty is only to deliver message clearly”* (36:17).

**Why Poetry Is Denied**

Poetry is denied because Qur'an is not poetry in form or purpose. Form: poetry has meter, rhyme, and is constrained; Qur'an has rhythm but not bound by poetic meter, and its eloquence surpasses poetry. The sūrah opened with oath *“By the Quran, rich in wisdom”* (36:2) — wisdom, not poetic entertainment. The Qur'an says *“He does not speak from desire, it is nothing but revelation revealed”* (53:3-4). Teaching poetry would be teaching from desire and imagination; revelation is from Almighty, Most Merciful (36:5). If it were poetry, it could be matched by poets; since it is not, challenge *“Produce a surah like it”* (2:23) stands, and inability to match shows it is from God.

**This Book Is Only Reminder and Clear Qur'an**

This Book is only Reminder and clear Qur'an, in huwa illā dhikrun wa qurʾānun mubīn, defines Qur'an's nature. Only, illā, limits: it is nothing but Reminder and clear Qur'an, not poetry, not magic, not soothsaying. Reminder, dhikr, means it reminds of what is already known in fiṭrah — origin from God, return to God, signs in creation — that was forgotten through heedlessness (36:6). Clear Qur'an, mubīn, means it makes clear, clarifies, as in clear delivery (36:17). The two names together show function: Reminder reminds, clear Qur'an makes clear what is reminded. The sūrah says *“so that you may warn a people whose forefathers were not warned and so are heedless”* (36:6) — Reminder warns heedless. The sūrah says *“to warn whoever is truly alive and fulfil decree against disbelievers”* (36:70) next, showing purpose of Reminder and clear Qur'an: warning living and establishing proof against disbelievers.

This verse follows reversal in long life (36:68) — will they not then understand — with statement that Qur'an is Reminder and clear Qur'an that should make understand. The sūrah says *“We have placed in it gardens, caused springs to gush, so they may eat fruit which they had no hand in making. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:34-35) — will they not give thanks, will they not understand, are similar calls to intellect. The verse also connects to *“You can only warn those who follow Reminder”* (36:11) — Reminder is what is followed; clear Qur'an is what is followed. Those who follow Reminder and are in awe of Most Compassionate unseen (36:11) are those who benefit from Reminder and clear Qur'an.

## Verse 36:70

> to warn whoever is ˹truly˺ alive and fulfil the decree ˹of torment˺ against the disbelievers

**Warning the Living and Establishing Proof**

*“To warn whoever is truly alive and fulfil the decree of torment against the disbelievers”* (36:70) gives purpose of Reminder and clear Qur'an (36:69). Living, ḥayy, here means spiritually alive, whose heart is alive, not dead through heedlessness (36:6), barriers (36:9), turning away (36:46). The Qur'an says *“Is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him light by which he walks among people like one who is in darkness and cannot emerge?”* (6:122) — alive vs dead. The sūrah earlier said *“You can only warn those who follow Reminder and are in awe of Most Compassionate unseen”* (36:11) — those are truly alive. The sūrah said *“It is the same whether you warn them or not, they will never believe”* (36:10) — those are dead, and word fulfilled against them (36:7).

**Why Truly Alive Matters**

Truly alive, man kāna ḥayyan, indicates that not all who are biologically alive are spiritually alive. The sūrah says *“There is a sign for them in dead earth: We give it life”* (36:33) — dead earth revived is like dead heart revived by Reminder. The Qur'an says *“This Book is only Reminder and clear Qur'an, to warn whoever is truly alive”* (36:69-70) — Reminder revives dead hearts as water revives dead earth. The truly alive are those who follow Reminder (36:11), follow those who ask no reward and are rightly guided (36:21), worship Originator (36:22), give thanks (36:35, 36:73). The disbelievers are spiritually dead, like dead earth before revival, like those with barriers and covering so they fail to see truth (36:9). For them, word of torment fulfilled, yaḥiqqa al-qawl, same verb as *“The decree has already been justified against most of them”* (36:7) — word becomes true, binding, because they disbelieved.

**How Word Fulfilled Against Disbelievers**

Word, qawl, decree of torment, becomes fulfilled, justified, against disbelievers because they disbelieved after warning. The sūrah says *“The decree of torment has already been justified against most of them, for they will never believe”* (36:7) — justification comes from disbelief. The Reminder and clear Qur'an establishes proof, so that on Day when all brought before God (36:32, 36:53) and no soul wronged (36:54), disbelievers cannot say they were not warned. The Qur'an says *“We never punish until We have sent a messenger”* (17:15). Sending Reminder and clear Qur'an is sending messenger with message, so punishment after is just. The word fulfilled is *“I will surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together”* (32:13) and *“Likewise Word of your Lord has come due against those who disbelieve, that they are inhabitants of Fire”* (40:6). The fulfillment is not arbitrary but based on record that lists what they sent forth (36:12) — turning away, refusing to donate, mocking, taking other gods, following Satan.

**A Verse That Defines Life and Death**

Finally, this verse defines life and death spiritually: truly alive are those who can be warned by Reminder; dead are disbelievers against whom word fulfilled. The Qur'an says *“You cannot make the dead hear, nor make deaf hear call when they turn away”* (27:80) — dead cannot be warned. The sūrah invites reader to be among truly alive, not among dead against whom word fulfilled, by following Reminder, fearing Most Compassionate unseen (36:11), worshiping Originator (36:22), giving thanks (36:35, 36:73), donating (36:47), and seeking peace from Merciful Lord (36:58) while enjoyment for little while remains (36:44). The verse is short, but it holds complete purpose of revelation: warning living and fulfilling word against disbelievers. That authority over all things in God's Hands (36:83) is manifest as both mercy that warns and justice that fulfils word, and return to Him (36:83) is to either Paradise or Hell based on whether one was truly alive and warned or dead and word fulfilled.

## Verse 36:71

> Do they not see that We singlehandedly created for them, among other things, cattle which are under their control

*“Do they not see that We singlehandedly created for them, among other things, cattle which are under their control?”* (36:71) begins final section about cattle and resurrection, after defining Qur'an as Reminder and clear Qur'an to warn living and fulfil word against disbelievers (36:69-70). The sūrah earlier said *“We have placed in it gardens and caused springs to gush”* (36:34) and *“We created for them similar things to ride in”* (36:42) — all singlehandedly created. The Qur'an says next that these creatures were made tractable so some serve for transport and others for nourishment (36:72), showing subjection as divine act.

**Why Singlehandedly Matters**

Singlehandedly, from what Our Hands have made, matters because it shows that cattle, like all provision, are from God alone, not from idols or other gods. The disbelievers who have taken other gods besides Allah hoping to be helped (36:74) are reminded that cattle they control were created singlehandedly by God, not by idols. If idols cannot create cattle, they cannot help. Cattle that are under control were created by One who can help, so worship should be for Him alone, which is straight path (36:61). The phrase singlehandedly also shows that creation of cattle is easy for God, like creation of heavens and earth (36:81), and like giving fire from green trees (36:80), and like reviving dead earth (36:33). If He can create cattle singlehandedly, He can resurrect decayed bones (36:78-79).

**Do They Not See as Call to Consider**

Do they not see, a-lam yaraw, is call to consider, to look at cattle as sign, similar to *“There is a sign for them in dead earth”* (36:33) and *“There is also a sign for them in night”* (36:37) and *“Another sign for them is that We carried their ancestors in Ark”* (36:41). Cattle are sign for them, for deniers who doubt resurrection and neglect thanks. The sūrah says *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46) — cattle are sign that should not be turned away from. The verse asks do they not see that We created cattle for them, implying that seeing should lead to thanks and belief. The sūrah says *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for provision; cattle are provision that should lead to thanks. The question is rhetorical, expecting yes, they should see, and if they see, they should give thanks and worship Originator (36:22).

**Under Their Control as Subjection**

Under their control, mālikūn, indicates ownership and control, but ownership is by subjection from God, not by inherent power. The next verse says *“These creatures were made subservient so some serve for riding and others for food”* (36:72), showing that control is by subjection, taskhīr. The Qur'an says *“It is He who made cattle for you, that you may ride some and eat some”* (40:79) and *“We made for you cattle, that you may ride and eat”* (23:21). Subjection means cattle obey, allow riding, allow milking, allow slaughter, despite being larger and stronger. That obedience is not from cattle itself but from God who subjected. The verse invites to see control as gift, not entitlement, and to give thanks. The sūrah says *“They derive from them other benefits and drinks. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:73) — benefits and drinks from cattle should lead to thanks. Control that allows riding and eating is favor that should produce gratitude, not heedlessness (36:6).

## Verse 36:72

> And We have subjected these ˹animals˺ to them, so they may ride some and eat others

**Subjection of Cattle for Riding and Eating**

*“And We have subjected these animals to them, so they may ride some and eat others”* (36:72) explains how cattle under their control (36:71) are used. Dhallalnāhā lahum, We have subjected them to them, made them tractable, obedient, subservient, indicates divine act of subjection, taskhīr. The Qur'an says *“We made for you cattle, that you may ride some and eat some”* (40:79, 23:21) and *“It is He who made earth tractable for you”* (67:15) — tractable, subjected, is gift. Subjection means cattle, despite size and strength, allow humans to ride, milk, slaughter, use for benefits. The verse says so they may ride some and eat others, fa-minhā rakūbuhum wa minhā yaʾkulūn, some for riding, some for eating, showing variety of use. Camels and horses for riding, cows, sheep, goats for eating, plus benefits and drinks (36:73). The sūrah says *“Do they not see that We singlehandedly created for them cattle which are under their control?”* (36:71) — singlehandedly created and subjected, so riding and eating are by permission, not entitlement.

**Why Subjection Is Emphasized**

Subjection is emphasized because it shows mercy and power. Mercy, because subjection allows humans to benefit from animals without constant struggle; power, because only God can subject strong animals to weak humans. The Qur'an says *“Glory be to the One who has subjected this to us, and we could not have done it ourselves”* (43:13) about riding. Humans could not subject cattle by themselves; God subjected. The verse says We have subjected, attributing act to God, as with dead earth revived (36:33), gardens placed (36:34), night stripped (36:37), sun fixed term (36:38), moon phases ordained (36:39), Ark carried (36:41), similar things created to ride (36:42). All are We acts, showing tawḥīd: all provision and subjection from God alone, not idols. The disbelievers who have taken other gods hoping to be helped (36:74) are reminded that subjection that allows riding and eating is from God, not idols, so idols cannot help (36:75).

**Riding Some and Eating Others as Provision**

Riding some and eating others shows variety of provision from same cattle. Some for riding — transport, like Ark and similar things to ride (36:41-42) — some for eating — food, like grain and gardens and springs (36:33-35). The sūrah's catalogue of provision includes both transport and food, both by mercy for little while (36:44). Riding allows movement, seeking provision, trade, pilgrimage; eating allows survival, strength, enjoyment. Both should lead to thanks. The sūrah says *“From them come other uses and drinks, inviting gratitude?”* (36:73) next, adding benefits and drinks to riding and eating, and asking will they not give thanks. The question is repeated after provision, showing that thanks is expected response. The perfect record (36:12) records riding, eating, benefits, drinks, and thanks or ingratitude, and *“On that Day no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) will be based on that.

**A Verse That Defines Gratitude for Transport and Food**

This verse defines gratitude for transport and food: riding and eating are by subjection from God, so thanks due. The sūrah says *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35, 36:73) for provision; riding and eating are provision. The Qur'an says *“Eat of what God has provided for you, lawful and good, and give thanks for God's favor, if it is Him you worship”* (16:114). Eating and riding as worship includes saying bismillah, using in obedience, sharing with others. The disbelievers who said *“Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed?”* (36:47) refused to share provision from cattle and other sources; they will be among those told *“Burn in it today for your disbelief”* (36:64). Those who give thanks by riding to do good, eating with gratitude, donating from provision, will be among those busy enjoying in Paradise (36:55-58) with peace from Merciful Lord (36:58).

## Verse 36:73

> And they derive from them other benefits and drinks. Will they not then give thanks

**Other Benefits and Drinks From Cattle**

*“And they derive from them other benefits and drinks. Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:73) adds to riding some and eating others (36:72) other benefits, manāfiʿ, and drinks, mashārib. Benefits include wool, hair, leather, bones, manure, labor, etc., from cattle. Drinks include milk, which is pure, palatable, nutritious, coming from between blood and dung, as Qur'an says *“We give you drink from what is in their bellies, between excretion and blood, pure milk, palatable to drinkers”* (16:66). The sūrah earlier mentioned grain for eating (36:33), gardens and springs for fruit (36:34-35), night, sun, moon for timekeeping and light (36:37-40), Ark and ships for carriage (36:41-42), cattle for riding and eating (36:72) — now benefits and drinks complete picture of provision from cattle. All are from God singlehandedly created and subjected (36:71-72), so benefits and drinks are also from God. The question will they not then give thanks, a-fa-lā yashkurūn, is repeated from *“Will they not then give thanks?”* (36:35) after grain and gardens, showing that thanks is expected for all provision, including cattle benefits and drinks.

**Will They Not Then Give Thanks as Repeated Question**

Will they not then give thanks is repeated question that appears after grain and gardens (36:35) and now after cattle benefits and drinks (36:73), framing provision section with call to thanks. Repetition stresses that thanks is central response expected to all provision. Shukr, thanks, in Qur'anic usage is acknowledgment with heart, tongue, limbs, using provision in obedience. The sūrah says *“Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it”* (36:46) — turning away is opposite of thanks. Refusing to donate from what Allah provided (36:47) is opposite of thanks. The question will they not then give thanks is so both invitation and rebuke: provision given, benefits and drinks derived, will they not give thanks? The perfect record (36:12) records thanks or ingratitude, and *“On that Day no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) will be based on that. Those who gave thanks will be among those busy enjoying in Paradise (36:55-58) with fruits and whatever desired and peace from Merciful Lord; those who did not will be among those told burn in Hell today (36:63-64).

**How Benefits and Drinks Support Resurrection**

Benefits and drinks from cattle support resurrection argument. If God can produce milk, pure drink, from between blood and dung, from animal, He can produce life from decayed bones. The transformation from blood and dung to pure milk is similar to transformation from green tree to fire (36:80) and from dead earth to grain (36:33). The sūrah later says *“Can the One who created heavens and earth not easily resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can! For He is Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81). The verse also shows that benefits and drinks are for little while by mercy (36:44), not permanent, so use them for thanks before little while ends with blast seizing while disputing (36:49) and no gift or return (36:50).

**A Verse That Defines Gratitude as Using Benefits Rightly**

This verse defines gratitude as using benefits and drinks rightly: riding to do good, eating with bismillah and sharing, using wool and leather modestly, drinking milk with thanks, etc. The Qur'an says *“Eat and drink, but be not excessive”* (7:31) — moderation is part of thanks. The disbelievers who refused to donate (36:47) used benefits and drinks without thanks and without sharing. The believing man who rushed from farthest end (36:20) and declared belief (36:25) used his body, time, and life as benefits for truth, and was made among honoured (36:27). His benefits were used rightly, so he is among those having fruits and whatever desired (36:57) and peace (36:58). The verse invites reader to derive benefits and drinks from cattle and other provision, and to give thanks by using them in obedience, donating, worshiping Originator (36:22), following Reminder (36:11), before being brought before God (36:32, 36:53) with no soul wronged.

The Qur'an says *“And cattle, He created them for you, in them is warmth and benefits and you eat from them”* (16:5).

## Verse 36:74
> Still they have taken other gods besides Allah, hoping to be helped ˹by them˺

**Still Taking Other gods**
The verse states the response of the people after every sign the sūrah has listed: they have taken other gods besides God, hoping to be helped by them. The chapter has spent many verses showing what God has already done for them, and this sentence names what they do with it (36:74 states it).

**What the Chapter Had Put in Front of Them**
The chapter listed the signs in the world around them. The dead earth is revived and brings out grain, and gardens of date palms and vines stand among springs (36:33 names the earth), and the gardens and springs are named in the verse that follows (36:34 names them). All things are created in pairs (36:36 states it). The night is stripped of its day (36:37 states it). The sun runs to a fixed course and the moon is measured in phases (36:38 names the sun, and 36:39 the moon). Their ancestors were carried in the loaded ark, and God has made for them other things of the same kind to ride (36:41 gives the ark, and 36:42 the rest).

And if God wished, He could drown them, and then nobody would answer their cry and nobody would rescue them (36:43 states it). He created the cattle for them and made them manageable (36:71 states it), with benefits from them and something to drink (36:73 states it).

**What the People Did Instead**
The people took other gods anyway. The Book says about such worship that people call on beings that neither harm them nor benefit them (10:18 states it), and it says that if God brings harm, nobody can remove it but He (10:107 states it). So the hope placed in those gods has nothing under it.

The chapter explains the persistence. It says that most of them are heedless of this warning (36:6 states it), that barriers have been placed in front of them and behind them so that they do not see (36:9 states it), and that whenever a sign from their Lord comes to them, they turn away from it (36:46 describes them). Even in the middle of the story, when the believing man warned them, he asked how he could take other gods whose intercession would not help him and would not save him if the Most Merciful intended harm (36:23 records his question). He called that position clear error (36:24 gives the verdict). The men of the town had already refused the messengers on the ground that they were only human beings like themselves (36:15 records their words).

**What the Record Keeps**
The chapter says that God writes down what people send ahead and what they leave behind in a clear record (36:12 states it), and that on that day no soul will be wronged at all (36:54 states it). The record will include both the provision people received and the other gods they took.

So the verse gives its reader a plain diagnosis. Provision was given, gratitude was expected, and other gods were taken instead in the hope of help. The chapter itself calls that road following Satan rather than the straight path (36:60 records the command not to worship Satan), and it names the straight path as the worship of God alone (36:61 gives it). And the verse that follows says what those gods can actually do (36:75 states it).

## Verse 36:75
> They cannot help the pagans, even though they serve the idols as dedicated guards

**They Cannot Help Anyone**
The verse answers the hope named in the verse before it. Those gods cannot help the people who serve them, even though those people have become, as the Arabic puts it, an army standing guard for them. The verse before this one stated the hope (36:74 states it), and this verse gives the answer (36:75 gives it).

The picture is deliberately ironic. The worshippers serve as soldiers and guards for gods that cannot move, cannot hear, and cannot defend even themselves.

**What the Book Says About Such Gods**
The Book is consistent on this point. It says that people worship, besides God, beings that can neither harm them nor benefit them (10:18 states it). It says that if you call on them they do not hear your call, and that even if they heard they would not answer you (35:14 states both). And in this very chapter the believing man asked how he could take other gods whose intercession would not help him and would not save him if the Most Merciful intended harm (36:23 records his question).

The question of where help actually comes from is answered once for all elsewhere: if God helps you, nobody can overcome you, and if He leaves you, who is there to help you after Him (3:160 states both)? Those are the only two possibilities, and the idols have no share in either.

**The Futility Is Double**
The futility has two sides. No help is received, and service is given to something that was never able to receive it. The chapter says that on that day no soul will be wronged, and that people will be repaid only for what they used to do (36:54 states both). Serving those gods as guards is part of what they used to do, and it will be part of the record.

The chapter describes that record. It says that God writes down what people send ahead and what they leave behind (36:12 states it). And it describes the day when the record is opened, when hands speak and feet give testimony about what their owners used to do (36:65 describes it).

**What the Verse Invites Instead**
The verse invites its reader to seek help from the One who can actually give it. The chapter has listed what He has done: He created the cattle and made them manageable (36:71 states it), gives benefits from them and something to drink (36:73 states it), carries people in the ark and in ships (36:41 states it), revives the dead earth (36:33 states it), strips the night of its day (36:37 states it), sets the sun on its fixed course (36:38 states it), and ordains the phases of the moon (36:39 states it).

In His hand lies the authority over all things, and to Him everyone will be returned (36:83 states both).

The two roads are set out plainly in the chapter. Seeking help from any other power is clear error (36:24 calls it that), and that road ends with the guilty being told to step aside and with the fire they are driven into (36:59 records the command to step aside) and the fire itself (36:63 describes it). The other road ends with people busy and happy in a garden (36:55 describes the garden) and greeted with peace from a merciful Lord (36:58 gives the greeting). And the chapter says plainly that nobody will answer the cry of the drowning or rescue them (36:43 asks who would answer) except by mercy from God (36:44 gives the exception). The verse is short, and it holds a complete argument against putting hope in anything created. The chapter names the alternative plainly: worship God alone, for that is the straight path (36:61 states it).

## Verse 36:76
> So do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Indeed, We ˹fully˺ know what they conceal and what they reveal

**An Instruction Not to Grieve**
The verse speaks to the Prophet directly: do not let their words sadden you, because God knows fully what they hide and what they say openly (36:76 states both).

It comes at the end of a long stretch in which the people of Makkah have answered every sign with words. The chapter has recorded them turning away from the signs (36:46 describes it), refusing to give from what they have been provided (36:47 records the refusal), and asking mockingly when the threat will arrive (36:48 records the question). The verse before this one described them taking other gods in the hope of help (36:74 states the hope) and named what those gods can actually do (36:75 gives the answer).

**Why the Words Hurt**
The words hurt because of what they were. The chapter records that no messenger came to a people without their mocking him (36:30 states it). In the story it tells, the town answered the messengers by calling them liars (36:15 records the charge). In the earlier story, a town answered its messengers with threats of stoning (36:18 records the threat), and that same town killed the one man among them who believed (36:26 describes his entry into the garden after his death).

The people of Makkah used the same kind of speech about the Prophet and about the believers, calling the believers clearly astray (36:47 records that charge). So the grieving was natural. A person who cares for his people feels it when they answer him with contempt.

**Why He Is Told Not to Let It Settle**
The reason given is knowledge. God knows what they conceal and what they reveal, which means none of those words is lost and none of them goes unjudged. The chapter says that everything a person sends ahead and everything he leaves behind is written in a clear record (36:12 states it), and it describes the day when mouths are sealed and hands and feet give testimony about what their owners used to do (36:65 describes it).

So the words that were spoken in the street will be produced in the court. The chapter says that on that day no soul will be wronged at all, since people are repaid only for what they used to do (36:54 states both).

**What the Chapter Had Already Said About His Duty**
Earlier in the sūrah, the Prophet was told what his work is and what it is not. He warns only those who follow the reminder and fear the Most Merciful without seeing Him (36:11 describes them). Some people will not believe whether he warns them or not (36:10 states it). And the duty of a messenger is clear delivery of the message and nothing more (36:17 states it).

So the grieving in this verse is not over failure in his work, because the work was completed. It is grief over people he loves. And the Book answers the same feeling elsewhere, where it says that he is saddened by what they say, and that they do not deny him so much as they deny God's signs (6:33 states both).

**What Patience Looks Like Here**
The verse teaches patience that does not slide into despair, and it puts a model in front of the Prophet in the middle of the same sūrah. A man came running from the far end of the town, declared his belief (36:20 describes his coming, and his words of faith are recorded in the chapter, where he called his people to follow the messengers (36:25 records the call)), faced the threats of his own people, and was told to enter Paradise (36:26 records the welcome). He was then named among the honoured (36:27 states it).

The closing note of the sūrah is the same for both groups. Those who followed the reminder are greeted with peace from a merciful Lord (36:58 gives the greeting), and those who chose the other road are shown the fire they were warned about and told to burn in it (36:63 states it, and 36:64 gives the command). So the verse asks the Prophet to leave the judging where it belongs, and to keep doing the work he was given.

## Verse 36:77

> Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺

**Created From Sperm-Drop, Then Openly Challenges**

*“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then behold, they openly challenge Us?”* (36:77) begins final argument for resurrection after consolation not to grieve (36:76). The sūrah earlier said *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and leave behind”* (36:12) — resurrection asserted; now creation from sperm-drop is evidence that resurrection possible. The Qur'an says *“We created man from sperm-drop, then We made him hearing and seeing”* (76:2) and *“He created man from sperm-drop, yet he is open challenger”* (16:4). Sperm-drop, nuṭfah, is humble origin, tiny, fluid, yet from it comes human who challenges Creator about resurrection, saying *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78).

**Then Behold, They Openly Challenge as Suddenness**

Then behold, fa-idhā, indicates suddenness, surprise: created from humble fluid, then suddenly open challenger. The same fa-idhā appears for town extinguished at once (36:29), blast seizing while disputing (36:49), rushing from graves (36:51), all brought before Us at once (36:53) — sudden transformation. Here, creation from sperm-drop to open challenger is sudden in sense of unexpected ingratitude. The Qur'an says *“Does man not see that We created him from sperm-drop, then behold, he is open challenger”* (16:4, 36:77) — same phrasing, showing that challenging Creator after being created from humble origin is common human failing. The challenge here is about resurrection: *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78) — challenging ability to resurrect despite being created from sperm-drop, which is itself creation from near nothing. The verse invites to see that if creation from sperm-drop possible, resurrection from bones possible, so challenge is baseless.

This verse follows *“So do not let their words grieve you. Indeed, We fully know what they conceal and reveal”* (36:76) — words that grieve include open challenges about resurrection. The Prophet was grieved by those who said *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78), challenging resurrection. This verse says do people not see We created them from sperm-drop, then behold, they openly challenge Us — their challenge is known and is ingratitude, so do not grieve, because God knows hidden and open. The perfect record (36:12) records both creation from sperm-drop and open challenge, and *“On that Day no soul will be wronged”* (36:54) will be based on that. The verse also connects to *“Will they not then understand?”* (36:68) about reversal in long life — will they not see creation from sperm-drop and understand that resurrection possible.

**A Verse That Defines Ingratitude**

This verse defines ingratitude as being created from humble origin then openly challenging Creator. Ingratitude is not just refusing to give thanks for cattle benefits and drinks (36:73) or for grain and gardens (36:35), but using faculties given — intellect, speech — to challenge Giver. The Qur'an says *“Man is indeed ungrateful to his Lord”* (100:6). Open challenge, khaṣīm mubīn, is clear, evident disputation, not hidden doubt. The sūrah says *“They will cry, Woe to us! Who has raised us from our place of rest? This must be what Most Compassionate warned us of; messengers told truth”* (36:52) — those who challenged openly will cry woe when raised. The verse invites to not be open challenger but to be among those who follow Reminder (36:11), worship Originator (36:22), give thanks (36:35, 36:73), and seek peace from Merciful Lord (36:58).

## Verse 36:78
> And they argue with Us—forgetting they were created—saying, “Who will give life to decayed bones?”

**An Argument That Forgets Its Own Premise**
The verse describes what these people do: they argue with God about the resurrection, and they make an example out of decayed bones, asking who could possibly give life to them. And it adds the detail that makes the whole argument collapse: they forget their own creation in the process (36:78 states all of it).

The verse before this one introduces the man making the argument, after he was created from a drop of fluid and then came forward to dispute openly (36:77 describes him).

**What the Question Assumes**
The question is not a request for information. It implies that nobody can do it, because bones that have crumbled into dust have lost everything that could be put back together. It is the same challenge the Book records from other people, and it answers the same question elsewhere by asking whether a person does not remember that God created him before, when he was nothing at all (19:67 asks it).

And the answer, given in the next verse, is simply the One who produced them the first time (36:79 names Him). The Book states that He has full knowledge of everything He created, which is the guarantee that the remaking will be accurate.

**Why Forgetting Matters**
The man is standing in front of a fact that ruins his own objection. He was made from a drop of fluid and from nothing before that, and he has forgotten it. Bringing back bones that already exist is not harder than producing a person in the first place.

The chapter had already shown the same power in action across its earlier verses. The dead earth is revived and brings out grain (36:33 states it), and gardens and springs come from it (36:34 states it). The night is stripped of its day (36:37 states it). The sun runs on a fixed course (36:38 states it) and the moon is measured in phases (36:39 states it). The creation of the heavens and the earth is named as the largest act of all (36:81 states it).

The Book also gives an example from ordinary experience that is stranger than resurrection: the fire that people kindle comes from a green tree (36:80 states it). If something wet and green can produce fire, then dry bones producing life is no difficulty for the One who arranged both.

**Where the Chapter Puts This**
The chapter had already explained how a person reaches this state of mind. It asked whether people had no sense, after describing how Satan misled great numbers of them (36:62 records the question and the number). Not using the mind one has been given leads to forgetting one's own beginning, and forgetting one's own beginning makes an argument against the resurrection look reasonable to the person making it.

The chapter puts the opposite attitude in front of its reader in the middle of the sūrah. The believing man asked why he should not worship the One who originated him and to whom everyone will be returned (36:22 records his words), and he was told to enter the garden (36:26 records it) and was counted among the honoured (36:27 states it).

The chapter also names the people who take the sound attitude toward the reminder: they follow it and fear the Most Merciful without seeing Him (36:11 describes them). Against them it sets the announcement made to the others, that this is the fire they were warned about (36:63 states it).

So the verses around this one show two ways of looking at the same fact. One man remembers where he came from and believes, and the other forgets where he came from and argues, and the sūrah ends by handing all authority to the One in whose hand it rests and to whom everyone returns (36:83 states both).

## Verse 36:79
> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being

**The First Creation Proves the Second**
The verse answers a man who asked who could give life to bones that have crumbled away. The answer is the One who produced them the first time. The doubter is introduced in the verse before (36:77 introduces him), and his question is recorded in the verse itself (36:78 records it).

His question is answered by his own existence. The first creation began from a drop of fluid (36:77 states it), and the second creation would begin from bones that at least already exist, even if they have decayed. If God produced a human being from nothing at the start, bringing the same person back is not a harder thing for Him.

**What the Chapter Had Already Shown**
The chapter has been building the case all the way through. It revives the dead earth and brings grain out of it (36:33 states it), and it makes gardens and springs out of it (36:34 states it). It creates everything in pairs (36:36 states it). It strips the night of its day (36:37 states it), sets the sun on a fixed course (36:38 states it), and measures the moon in phases (36:39 states it). It brings fire out of the green tree (36:80 states it). And it creates the heavens and the earth (36:81 states it).

Every one of those is an act of creating and transforming, so reviving bones belongs to the same power. The doubter forgot his own creation, and the answer reminds him of it. The chapter says that its design shows the work of One who is Almighty and All-Knowing (36:38 states the pair), and it says of the Creator that He is the skilful maker who knows everything (36:81 states it).

**Perfect Knowledge Behind It**
The Book states the principle in a single sentence elsewhere: does the One who created not know, when He is subtle and fully aware (67:14 states both)?

That knowledge is the guarantee the verse rests on. Accurate re-creation requires knowing exactly what was made, and the chapter says the record already keeps what people send ahead and what they leave behind (36:12 states it). It also says that on that day no soul will be wronged at all (36:54 states it), because every deed and every person is known. And it says that God originates creation and then repeats it, and that this is easier for Him (30:27 states both). Elsewhere the Book asks whether a person does not remember that God created him before, when he was nothing at all (19:67 asks it).

**How the Answer Corrects the Doubter**
The doubter forgot; God did not forget. Forgetting is a human failing, and perfect knowledge is the divine attribute that answers it. The chapter even asks, a few verses earlier, whether such people will not understand, in a passage about being returned to the weakness of old age after strength (36:68 records the reversal and asks the question).

So the answer to the question about decayed bones is: the One who produced them the first time has perfect knowledge of them and will produce them again. The chapter states the ease of the act in one sentence later, saying that when God wills a thing, all He says to it is "Be" and it is (36:82 states it).

Against that, the reader is invited to remember his own beginning (36:77 states it), to give thanks for what he has been given (36:35 records the gratitude the chapter asks for), to follow the reminder (36:11 describes the people who take it), and to prepare for the return to God, where no soul is wronged and everyone is paid for what he used to do (36:54 states both). The chapter states the return in the words of the believing man (36:22 records them), and it records what happened to him when he entered his reward (36:26 describes it). The sūrah ends on the same note of return to the One who holds all authority (36:83 states both).

## Verse 36:80

> ˹He is the One˺ Who gives you fire from green trees, and—behold!—you kindle ˹fire˺ from them

**Fire From Green Trees**

*“He is the One who gives you fire from green trees, and behold, you kindle fire from them”* (36:80) gives another proof of ability to produce opposite from same, supporting resurrection. Green trees, shajar akhḍar, are wet, alive, full of water, yet fire, nār, comes from them, and you kindle fire from them, fa-idhā antum minhu tūqidūn, then behold, you kindle from them. The transformation is surprising: green, wet tree producing fire, which is hot, dry, opposite. Arabs knew two trees, markh and ʿafār, that when rubbed together produce fire even when green, or they knew that fire can be kindled from green wood by friction. The verse says He is the One who gives you fire from green trees, attributing transformation to God, not to nature alone. The sūrah earlier said dead earth revived with grain (36:33), night stripped of day (36:37), sun fixed term (36:38), moon phases like old stalk (36:39), cattle benefits and drinks from between blood and dung (36:73) — all transformations. Fire from green trees is similar: opposite from same, showing power to bring life from death and death from life.

**Why Fire From Green Trees Supports Resurrection**

Fire from green trees supports resurrection because it shows ability to produce one thing from its opposite. Green tree is wet, alive; fire is dry, consuming. If God can produce fire from green tree, He can produce life from decayed bones, which is also opposite — life from dead. The Qur'an says *“He brings living from dead and dead from living”* (3:27, 30:19). Fire from green trees is example of living (tree) producing something that consumes (fire), or wet producing dry, showing that opposites can come from same source by divine will. The sūrah says *“They will be revived by the One who produced them first time, for He has perfect knowledge”* (36:79) — producing first time and producing fire from green trees both show power to produce. The verse says behold, you kindle fire from them, fa-idhā, then at once, you kindle, showing immediacy and ease: green trees, then behold, fire kindled. Similarly, decayed bones, then behold, revived. The ease is same.

**The Relation to Previous Arguments**

This verse follows answer that they will be revived by One who produced first time and has perfect knowledge (36:79) with additional proof: fire from green trees. The sequence is: creation from sperm-drop (36:77), forgetting creation and asking who will give life to decayed bones (36:78), answer that One who produced first time will revive for He has perfect knowledge (36:79), then fire from green trees as additional proof (36:80), then creation of heavens and earth (36:81), then Be and it is (36:82), then glory be to One in Whose Hands is authority and to Whom all will be returned (36:83). The progression moves from human creation to fire from trees to cosmic creation to divine command to authority and return, each step showing greater power, making resurrection increasingly reasonable. Fire from green trees is middle step, showing transformation in familiar, daily experience — kindling fire — that points to greater transformation — reviving bones.

**A Verse That Defines Transformation**

This verse defines transformation as divine act: green trees to fire, dead earth to grain, night to day, sperm-drop to challenger, decayed bones to living. The sūrah says *“All it takes, when He wills something to be, is simply to say to it: Be! And it is”* (36:82) — Be transforms. The reader who kindles fire from wood is invited to see in that act a sign of resurrection, to remember that One who gives fire from green trees can give life to decayed bones, and to prepare for return to God (36:83) where no soul wronged (36:54).

The Qur'an says *“Have you seen the fire you kindle? Is it you who produce its tree, or are We the Producer?”* (56:71-72) — similar question about fire's origin.

## Verse 36:81

> Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺! For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing

**Creator of Heavens and Earth Can Resurrect**

*“Can the One who created the heavens and the earth not easily resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can! For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81) is climax of resurrection argument. After creation from sperm-drop (36:77), forgetting and arguing about decayed bones (36:78), answer that One who produced first time will revive for He has perfect knowledge (36:79), fire from green trees (36:80), now creation of heavens and earth is cited as greater than resurrection, so if greater possible, lesser possible. The answer is balā, yes, indeed, He can, wa huwa al-Khallāq al-ʿAlīm, for He is Master Creator, All-Knowing.

**Master Creator, All-Knowing as Names**

Master Creator, Khallāq, All-Knowing, ʿAlīm, are names that combine power and knowledge, like Almighty, All-Knowing (36:38) for sun. Khallāq indicates One who creates repeatedly, not just once, so resurrection is repetition of creation, which is characteristic of Master Creator. ʿAlīm indicates knowledge of every created being (36:79), so recreation will be accurate. The sūrah says *“They will be revived by the One who produced them first time, for He has perfect knowledge of every created being”* (36:79) — perfect knowledge now repeated as All-Knowing, showing consistency. The verse is so summary of previous: first creation, perfect knowledge, fire from green trees, heavens and earth — all point to Master Creator, All-Knowing who can resurrect. The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they sent forth is possible because All-Knowing knows.

**How This Verse Answers Doubt**

This verse answers doubt *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78) with all the more: if heavens and earth created, then bones can be revived. The disputer forgot his own creation (36:78), but heavens and earth are visible creation that he cannot deny, so argument from greater to smaller is compelling. The Qur'an says *“Do they not see that God who created heavens and earth and was not wearied by their creation is able to give life to dead? Yes, indeed, He is able to do all things”* (46:33). Not wearied indicates ease. The sūrah says *“When He wills a thine wills something to be, is simply to say to it: Be! And it is”* (36:82) next, showing ease. The verse invites to look at heavens and earth — sun traveling fixed term (36:38), moon phases (36:39), night stripped of day (36:37), dead earth revived (36:33) — and to conclude that Creator of all that can easily resurrect deniers.

**A Verse That Defines Ease of Resurrection**

This verse defines ease of resurrection: if heavens and earth created, then resurrecting deniers easy, because Master Creator, All-Knowing can do. The verse says can He not easily resurrect, indicating that resurrection is easy, not difficult, for such Creator. The sūrah says *“It is certainly We who resurrect the dead”* (36:12) as assertion; now ease explained. The verse is short, but it holds complete all the more argument that is central to Qur'anic resurrection discourse. The reader who sees heavens and earth daily is invited to see them as proof that resurrection is easy, to not be among those who argue forgetting creation, but among those who follow Reminder (36:11) and are busy enjoying in Paradise (36:55-58) with peace from Merciful Lord (36:58).

The Qur'an says *“Creation of heavens and earth is greater than creation of mankind, but most of mankind do not know”* (40:57).

## Verse 36:82

> All it takes, when He wills something ˹to be˺, is simply to say to it: “Be!” And it is

**Be and It Is**

*“All it takes, when He wills something to be, is simply to say to it: Be! And it is”* (36:82) describes divine command that makes resurrection and all creation easy. Innama amruhu idhā arāda shayʾan a yaqūla lahu kun fa-yakūn, His command when He wills something is only to say to it Be and it is. The verse stresses ease and immediacy: will, say Be, and it is. No process, no delay, no difficulty. The sūrah earlier said single blast extinguished town at once (36:29), single blast seizes while disputing (36:49), single blast brings all before God at once (36:53) — all are examples of Be and it is. The verse says all it takes, innamā, only, indicating exclusivity: only saying Be is needed. The Qur'an says similar in *“When We will something, Our only word is Be and it is”* (16:40) and *“His command is only Be and it is”* (2:117, 3:47, 19:35, 40:68). The command Be is kun, imperative, and its result fa-yakūn, and it is, shows immediate fulfillment.

**Why Be and It Is Supports Resurrection**

Be and it is supports resurrection because it shows that resurrection does not require assembling bones gradually, but only divine command Be. The disputer asked *“Who will give life to decayed bones?”* (36:78) thinking revival requires difficult reassembly; answer is One who produced first time and has perfect knowledge (36:79) and gives fire from green trees (36:80) and created heavens and earth (36:81) can simply say Be and bones revived. The sūrah says *“Can the One who created heavens and earth not easily resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can! For He is Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81) — Master Creator's way is Be. The perfect record (36:12) that lists what they sent forth is not needed for God to know how to reconstruct; Be reconstructs.

**The Relation to Previous Blasts**

The sūrah says *“All it took was one mighty blast, and they were extinguished at once”* (36:29) — blast as Be that extinguishes. *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us”* (36:53) — blast as Be that gathers. Be and it is is the principle behind blasts: divine command produces immediate result. The verse also relates to *“We did not send soldiers from heavens against his people after his death, nor did We need to”* (36:28) — no soldiers needed because Be suffices. The town that threatened painful punishment from itself (36:18) needed many people and stones; God needs only Be.

**A Verse That Defines Divine Command**

This verse defines divine command as Be and it is, showing that God's will and speech are effective, not mere wish. The sūrah says *“So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is authority over all things, and to Whom you will all be returned”* (36:83) next, indicating that authority over all things includes authority to say Be and it is, and return is to One who says Be. The verse is short, but it holds complete theology of divine command: will, say Be, and it is, immediate, easy, effective. The reader who hears Be now as reminder is invited to respond before forced Be that brings all before God. The Qur'an says *“The matter of Hour is but as blinking of eye, or nearer”* (16:77) — the divine "Be, and it is" is as swift as blinking. The verse invites to prepare now, while enjoyment for little while remains (36:44), by following Reminder (36:11), worshiping Originator (36:22), giving thanks (36:35, 36:73), seeking peace from Merciful Lord (36:58), before Be brings all before Him.

The Qur'an says *“Creation of you all and resurrection of you all is but as a single soul”* (31:28) — easy as one.

## Verse 36:83
> So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is the authority over all things, and to Whom ˹alone˺ you will ˹all˺ be returned

**The Sūrah's Closing Words**
The sūrah ends with praise: glory be to the One in whose hand lies the authority over all things, and to Him you will all be returned (36:83 states both). The verse has two halves, and between them they say everything the sūrah has been saying.

The first half gives God the authority over everything. The word used for authority is the intensive form of the word for a kingdom, which means complete control rather than a share of it. The second half states where everyone is going: back to the One who holds that authority.

**The Authority the Sūrah Has Shown**
The chapter has been displaying that authority from its first pages. It placed barriers in front of the people and behind them (36:9 states it). It raises the dead and writes down what people send ahead and what they leave behind (36:12 states both). It carried their ancestors in the loaded ark (36:41 gives the ark), and it made other things of the same kind for them to ride (36:42 gives the rest). It could drown them, and nobody would answer their cry and nobody would rescue them except by God's mercy (36:43 states the drowning, and 36:44 gives the exception). It could blind their eyes or fix them in place (36:66 names the first power), and it could change their very shapes where they stood (36:67 names the second). And it turns a person back to weakness after strength, so that he returns to the helplessness he came from (36:68 describes the reversal). It created them from a drop of fluid (36:77 states it). It brings fire out of the green tree (36:80 states it). And when it wills a thing, it only says to it "Be" and it is (36:82 states it).

Every one of those is an exercise of authority, which is why the final verse hands the praise to the One who holds it.

**What Has No Authority**
The verse also tells the reader where authority is not. It is not with the gods the people took up, since those cannot help the people who serve them even while those people stand guard for them. The hope is stated in the verse before (36:74 states it), and the answer is given in the next (36:75 gives it). It is not with the town that threatened its own punishment against the messengers sent to it (36:18 records the threat). And it is not with Satan, who misled great numbers of people (36:62 states it).

The Book states the same ownership elsewhere: authority belongs to the One in whose hand is all dominion (67:1 states it).

The phrase about things being in God's hand is the language of possession and control, and it is the reason the idols can offer nothing. They did not create, they do not provide, and they do not decide anything.

**The Return**
The second half of the verse states the return, and the sūrah has been naming it all along. The believing man said that he worshipped the One who originated him and to whom everyone will be returned (36:22 records his words). The chapter says that all people will be brought before God (36:32 states it).

Return is to the One who holds authority, which means return is to a judge who can reward and punish, and who wrongs nobody: on that day no soul will be wronged at all, and people will be repaid only for what they used to do (36:54 states both). The account they are repaid from is the record that keeps what they sent ahead and what they left behind (36:12 states it), and the day will come when hands speak and feet give testimony about what their owners used to do (36:65 describes it).

The two roads have already been shown in the story the sūrah tells. The believing man returned to a garden and a greeting of peace from a merciful Lord (36:26 describes his entry, and 36:58 gives the greeting), while the people who killed him were driven to the fire (36:63 describes the driving) and were told to enter it that day (36:64 records the command).

**How the Verse Completes the Sūrah**
The sūrah opens with its disconnected letters, whose meaning is left with God (36:1 gives them), and it closes with glory to the One who holds all authority and to whom all return. The sūrah also used the word glory earlier in its middle, when it praised the One who created all things in pairs (36:36 states it), so the chapter begins and ends with praise and keeps its signs and scenes inside that frame.

So the last verse of the sūrah asks two things of its reader. Give the authority to the One who actually holds it, and get ready for the return. The chapter has already named the way to do that: worship God alone, for that is the straight path (36:61 names it), rather than following Satan as the earlier generations were told not to do (36:60 gives the command). That path leads to peace from a merciful Lord (36:58 gives it), and the other road leads to the fire (36:63 describes it). The reader is left with a little time to enjoy (36:44 mentions it), a recorded account (36:12 states it), and a return he cannot avoid (36:83 states it).
