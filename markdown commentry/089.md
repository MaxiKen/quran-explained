# Sūrah al-Fajr (Chapter 89) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Break of Dawn and the Serene Soul**

Al-Fajr, "The Dawn," is the eighty-ninth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 30 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with five solemn, mysterious chronological oaths: *“By the dawn, and by ten nights, and by the even and the odd, and by the night when it passes—is there in that an oath for one possessed of intellect?”* (89:1–5). The "ten nights" are universally interpreted in classical hadith literature (including Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 969) as the sacred first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah—the pinnacle of the spiritual and pilgrimage year. Sūrah al-Fajr is celebrated for its sweeping historical warnings about the ruined civilizations of ancient Arabia (Iram of the Pillars, Thamūd, and Pharaoh), its searing socio-economic critique of orphan neglect and greed, and its peerless, immortal conclusion in verses 27–30, which welcomes the tranquil, purified spirit into the Divine Presence: *“O tranquil soul (*Yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭma'innah*)! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him]!”*

**The Structural Architecture and the Four Movements**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Fajr unfolds across four dramatic movements:

1. The Oaths of Time and the Ruins of Tyranny (1–14): Swears by the dawn, the ten nights, the even and odd, and the passing night, reviewing the sudden destructions of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh to show that God is ever watchful over human tyranny.

2. The False Calculus of Wealth and the Neglect of Orphans (15–20): Delivers a profound psychological diagnosis of human arrogance: when God tests man with honor and wealth, he boasts: *“My Lord has honored me!”*, but when He tests him with restricted provision, he complains: *“My Lord has humiliated me!”* (89:15–16). It strikes at the heart of their societal cruelty: *“No! But you do not honor the orphan, and you do not encourage one another to feed the impoverished, and you consume inheritance devouringly, and you love wealth with immense love!”* (89:17–20).

3. The Shattering of the Earth and the Regret of Life (21–26): The apocalyptic climax: when the earth is leveled, pounded powder upon powder (*dakkan dakkā*), and your Lord comes with the angels rank upon rank, and Hell is brought near on that Day! He will cry the tragic, immortal regret of verse 24: *“Oh, I wish I had put forward [good deeds] for my true life (*Yā laytanī qaddamtu li-ḥayātī*)!”*

4. The Serene Soul: Al-Nafs al-Muṭma'innah (27–30): The sūrah concludes with the gentlest, most exquisite invitation in all of sacred scripture: *“O tranquil soul (*Yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭma'innah*)! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him]! And enter among My [righteous] servants! And enter My Paradise!”*

All transgressed through corruption and tyranny, yet *“your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment; indeed, your Lord is ever watchful (*inna Rabbaka la-bi-l-mirṣād*)!”* (89:13–14).

An-Nasāʾī recorded the report that keeps the sūrah in the prayer's daily bread. Jābir narrated that a man joined Muʿādh in prayer; Muʿādh made it long, so the man went aside, prayed alone, and left to feed his she-camel. Muʿādh called him a hypocrite and brought the matter to the Prophet ﷺ, who questioned the young man and heard his answer — the prayer was too long for him — and then said: "Are you causing trouble, O Muʿādh? Why do you not recite 'Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High,' 'By the sun and its brightness,' 'By the dawn,' 'By the night when it envelops'?" The sūrah of the dawn stands in that list of the Prophet's ﷺ own choices: short sūrahs for the congregation, the length a community can carry, and the rebuke of the zealous imam is part of the sūrah's history.

The oath swears by the day's oldest mercy either way — the light that returns on schedule, whether the schedule is the night's or the year's.

**The Ten Nights of the Fasting**

The third reading, the reading of the ten nights of the month of the fasting, is the reading that the sūrah's context favors, and the context is the context of the revelation of the Book. The ten nights are the last ten nights of the month, and the last ten nights are the nights of the Night of the Decree. The sūrah's ten nights, on this reading, are the ten nights of the seeking, and the seeking is the seeking of the Night of the Decree in the odd of the ten. The reading is so the reading that the sūrah's oath carries in its fullest form, and the fullest form is the form of the last ten nights of the month of the fasting.

The reading the tradition leans on names the ten as the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah — Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn az-Zubayr, Mujāhid and others among the early and the later generations — and the reading has its hadith: al-Bukhārī recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, "There are no days in which righteous deeds are more beloved to Allah than these days." His hearers asked: not even fighting in the way of Allah? He answered: "Not even fighting in the way of Allah — except for a man who goes out with his own self and his own wealth and returns with neither." The oath by the ten is therefore an oath by the year's summit, the days that outrank even battle in the book of beloved deeds.

## Verse 89:5
> Is all this ˹not˺ a sufficient oath for those who have sense

**The Sufficiency of the Oaths**

The question of the sūrah is the question of the sufficiency, and the sufficiency is the sufficiency of the four oaths for the one who has sense. The sūrah does not ask whether the oaths are true; the sūrah asks whether the oaths suffice, and the sufficing is the sufficing of the witness for the one who reflects. The sūrah's method is the method of the Book at its moments of the oath: the oaths are the witnesses, and the witnesses are enough for the one who has sense, and the one who has sense is the one whose intellect prevents him from the refusal of the witness. The sūrah's question is so the question that states the weight of the oaths, and the weight is the weight that suffices for the one who reflects.

**The Word for the Mind That Prevents**

Ibn Kathir pauses on the verse's last word, because the word is a small lesson in itself. The intellect is called ḥijr, he explains, because it prevents: it stops a person from the actions and words that do not befit him. The same root names the ḥijr of the Kaʿbah, the enclosure that stops the circling pilgrim from crossing to the far wall; the ḥijr of the pigeon, the cage that stops the bird; and the judge's ḥajara, the ruling that stops a man from squandering what he owns. The mind, on this showing, is not first a calculator but a fence — the thing in a human being that says no — and the verse asks whether the four sworn objects are not oath enough for anyone whose inner fence is standing. The oath is gathered, Ibn Kathir adds, over the times and acts of worship — the pilgrimage, the prayer — which is why the question is aimed at sense and not at learning.

**The Named Readings of Iram**

The verse's city of legend, in other words, has its sober early readings: a people, a dynasty, and a wind — not gold and jewels, but pillars and pride.

## Verse 89:30

**The Sūrah Complete**

The sūrah is complete in this verse, and the completeness is the completeness of the five movements. The sūrah's five movements are the movement of the oaths in the five verses, the movement of the three peoples in the nine verses, the movement of the vacillation in the two verses, the movement of the rebuke in the four verses. The movement of the Day and the address in the ten verses. The sūrah's structure is the structure of the five movements, and the five movements are the sūrah's complete form. The sūrah that opened on the oath of the dawn closes on the entry to the Garden of the Lord, and the closing is the sūrah's way of placing its argument in the two ends of the Event. The sūrah's last word is the word of the Garden, and the Garden is the Garden that the sūrah's first oath promised in its dawn.

**The Word Given to Abū Bakr**

Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that this address was revealed while Abū Bakr was sitting with the Prophet ﷺ — and Abū Bakr said: O Messenger of Allah, there is nothing better than this! The Prophet ﷺ answered him: "This will indeed be said to you." The report makes the sūrah's last verses a promise already delivered, spoken into the sitting room of the first of the Companions while the words were still new. And the address itself, Ibn Kathir notes, is spoken twice — at the death and at the rising — just as the angels give the believer their glad tidings at his death and again when he stands from his grave. The soul that spent its life being drawn toward its Lord hears the call that names it tranquil, hears it once at the closing of the eyes and once at the opening of the grave, and the sūrah of the dawn ends where the light is: at the entry into a Garden that was His before it was theirs.
