# Sūrah al-Fajr (Chapter 89) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Break of Dawn and the Serene Soul**

Al-Fajr, "The Dawn," is the eighty-ninth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 30 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with five solemn, mysterious chronological oaths: *“By the dawn, and by ten nights, and by the even and the odd, and by the night when it passes—is there in that an oath for one possessed of intellect?”* (89:1–5). The "ten nights" are universally interpreted in classical hadith literature (including Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 969) as the sacred first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah—the pinnacle of the spiritual and pilgrimage year. Sūrah al-Fajr is celebrated for its sweeping historical warnings regarding the ruined civilizations of ancient Arabia (Iram of the Pillars, Thamūd, and Pharaoh), its searing socio-economic critique of orphan neglect and greed, and its peerless, immortal conclusion in verses 27–30, which welcomes the tranquil, purified spirit into the Divine Presence: *“O tranquil soul (*Yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭma'innah*)! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him]!”*

**The Pillars of Iram and the Slaves of Makkah**

The historical context of al-Fajr is the arrogance of the Qurayshi oligarchy, who walked the streets of Makkah boasting of their wealth, while systematically disinheriting vulnerable orphans, mistreating widows, and ignoring the starving poor. Sūrah al-Fajr shatters their pride by invoking the ghosts of ancient Arabian superpowers whose architectural genius dwarfed Makkah: Iram of the Pillars (*Dhāti al-ʿImād*), whose likes had never been created in the land; Thamūd, who carved monolithic palaces from the living rock of the valleys; and Pharaoh, the lord of iron stakes and imperial fortresses. All transgressed through corruption and tyranny, yet *“your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment; indeed, your Lord is ever watchful (*inna Rabbaka la-bi-l-mirṣād*)!”* (89:13–14).

**The Structural Architecture and the Four Movements**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Fajr unfolds across four dramatic movements:
1. The Oaths of Time and the Ruins of Tyranny (1–14): Swears by the dawn, the ten nights, the even and odd, and the passing night, reviewing the sudden destructions of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh to demonstrate that God is ever watchful over human tyranny.
2. The False Calculus of Wealth and the Neglect of Orphans (15–20): Delivers a profound psychological diagnosis of human arrogance: when God tests man with honor and wealth, he boasts: *“My Lord has honored me!”*, but when He tests him with restricted provision, he complains: *“My Lord has humiliated me!”* (89:15–16). The sūrah corrects this false theology: wealth is not divine approval; poverty is not divine rejection; both are tests of moral character! It strikes at the heart of their societal cruelty: *“No! But you do not honor the orphan, and you do not encourage one another to feed the impoverished, and you consume inheritance devouringly, and you love wealth with immense love!”* (89:17–20).
3. The Shattering of the Earth and the Regret of Life (21–26): The apocalyptic climax: when the earth is leveled, pounded powder upon powder (*dakkan dakkā*), and your Lord comes with the angels rank upon rank, and Hell is brought near on that Day! On that Day man will remember, but what will remembrance avail him? He will cry the tragic, immortal regret of verse 24: *“Oh, I wish I had put forward [good deeds] for my true life (*Yā laytanī qaddamtu li-ḥayātī*)!”*
4. The Serene Soul: Al-Nafs al-Muṭma'innah (27–30): The sūrah concludes with the gentlest, most exquisite invitation in all of sacred scripture: *“O tranquil soul (*Yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭma'innah*)! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him]! And enter among My [righteous] servants! And enter My Paradise!”*

**The True Life and the Tranquil Soul**

The supreme philosophical jewel of Sūrah al-Fajr is the phrase *Li-ḥayātī* ("for my true life"). The human being lives on earth assuming that this physical, biological existence is his real life; only on the Day of Judgment does he realize with agonizing clarity that this worldly existence was merely a fleeting shadow, and that the Hereafter is the only *true life* for which he should have prepared. In contrast to the turbulent, greedy soul that panics over restricted wealth, the *Nafs al-Muṭma'innah* is the soul that achieved tranquility through surrender to God—anchored in contentment, honored in death, and welcomed into the eternal presence of its Lord.
