# Sūrah al-Tawbah (Chapter 9) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Severing of Alliances and the Ultimatum of Sincerity**

Al-Tawbah, "The Repentance"—also widely designated in classical exegesis as *Barā'ah* (The Immunity or Repudiation)—is the ninth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 129 verses. It is universally distinguished by a striking, unique feature: it is the only sūrah in the entire Qur'anic corpus that does not open with the *Basmalah* (*“In the Name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*). When the caliph ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān was questioned about this omission, he noted that al-Tawbah was revealed near the very end of the prophetic era, that its themes were intimately intertwined with Sūrah al-Anfāl, and that the Prophet ﷺ passed away without explicitly indicating whether it constituted an independent chapter or a continuation. The Companion ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib provided the classical spiritual rationale: *“The Basmalah is a guarantee of security and mercy, whereas Barā'ah was revealed with the sword, stripping away security from treaty-breaking polytheists.”* It is also named *al-Fāḍiḥah* (The Exposer) by ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās, because it mercilessly exposed the clandestine plots, psychological cowardice, and treacherous cynicism of the hypocrites, leaving no facade intact.

**The Structure and Thematic Movements**

The architecture of al-Tawbah unfolds in two colossal, sweeping movements. The first movement (1–37) announces the definitive end of idolatrous treaties (*Barā'ah*), sets out the treatment of honorable versus treacherous tribes, commands the defense of monotheistic sanctuaries, and expresses the principles governing warfare with hostile polytheists and scriptural factions who refused peaceful submission. It contains the celebrated decree of safe passage (*istijārah*, 9:6), mandating that any polytheist seeking asylum must be protected, allowed to hear the Word of God, and escorted to his place of safety. The second and larger movement (38–129) conducts an exhaustive psychological autopsy of the Tabūk mobilization. It rebukes the spiritual paralysis that struck some believers when commanded to march (*“What is the matter with you that when you are told to go forth, you cling heavily to the earth?”*, 9:38), immortalizes the divine rescue of the Prophet ﷺ and Abū Bakr in the cave during the migration (*“Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us,”* 9:40), and dissects the rationalizations of the hypocrites who built the subversive “Mosque of Harm” (*Masjid al-Ḍirār*) to fracture the community. It codifies the eight definitive categories of Zakat beneficiaries (9:60), contrasts the mutual loyalty of true believing men and women against the mutual cynicism of hypocrites, and narrates the poignant story of the three sincere believers who stayed behind from Tabūk without excuse—whose social ostracization ended in triumphant divine forgiveness when *“the earth, despite its vastness, seemed to close in on them”* (9:118). The sūrah concludes (128–129) with a luminous tribute to the character of the Messenger ﷺ: *“There has certainly come to you a Messenger from among yourselves; grievous to him is what you suffer; [he is] concerned over you and to the believers is kind and merciful.”*

## Verse 9:1

> ˹This is˺ a discharge from all obligations, by Allah and His Messenger, to the polytheists you ˹believers˺ have entered into treaties with

**A Public Release From a Public Obligation**

The opening noun is barāʾah, a declaration that one party is no longer bound by a former connection. It is stronger than private disappointment and more exact than a general warning. A treaty had created obligations between the Muslim community and certain polytheist tribes; this verse announces that those obligations have been discharged. The wording also makes the authority clear: the release is from Allah and His Messenger. It is not a local commander improvising a policy, nor a crowd abandoning a promise in anger. A covenant of public consequence is ended by a public authority and in a form that can be heard by those affected.

The verse also shows that covenants are not decorative language. Allah commands, Keep the covenant of Allah when you have pledged it, and do not break oaths after confirming them (16:91).

**The Messenger as Herald of the Notice**

The proclamation was delivered during the pilgrimage season, when representatives of the Arabian tribes could hear the new policy directly. Abū Bakr led the pilgrimage in the ninth year after the Hijrah, and ʿAlī was sent to proclaim the relevant terms. This arrangement gave the announcement a public character and removed uncertainty about who spoke for the Muslim authority. Reports preserved in the major hadith collections describe the announcement as including the end of pagan pilgrimage practices and the protection of treaties that had not expired through breach.

## Verse 9:2

> “You ˹polytheists˺ may travel freely through the land for four months, but know that you will have no escape from Allah, and that Allah will disgrace the disbelievers.”

**The Land Is Wide, But It Is Not an Escape**

The command to travel is followed by a warning: know that you cannot thwart Allah. The contrast is deliberate. The people may move across the land, but no distance places them beyond divine knowledge or judgment. Human geography gives them room to act; it does not give them a place outside accountability. The verse holds mobility and helplessness together without contradiction. They have a genuine choice about what to do during the four months, yet they cannot turn the existence of that choice into immunity from its consequences.

The Qur'an uses the same idea elsewhere when it says that no one can escape Allah in the earth or in the sky (29:22). That cross-reference clarifies the warning here: it is not a prediction that every road will be physically blocked. It is a denial that flight can defeat the moral reality revealed by God. A person may cross a border, alter an alliance, or find a temporary refuge, but none of these changes the truth of a broken covenant. The notice therefore calls for action rather than panic. Travel is permitted; evasion is impossible.

**Disgrace Is the Exposure of a False Claim**

Disgrace should not be confused with humiliation for its own sake. The verse connects it to a moral disclosure. What was hidden in private dealings becomes visible in the outcome. The same Qur'an that warns of disgrace also says that Allah does not let the deeds of the virtuous go to waste (9:120). The contrast is between conduct and its revealed worth. A people who spend the notice period in repentance can change their standing; a people who treat the notice as another opportunity for manipulation move toward the exposure named here.

**A Notice Period Is Also a Mercy**

The Qur'an often gives warning before punishment. Noah warned his people, Moses was sent to Pharaoh, and the Messenger was commanded to make the message clear. The principle is stated plainly: We never punish until We have sent a messenger (17:15). Verse 2 applies that principle to a political transition. A community informed of its changed status is more capable of making a responsible choice than one left to guess. Grace does not mean that every outcome is removed. It means that the decision is made with the necessary knowledge.

## Verse 9:3

> A declaration from Allah and His Messenger ˹is made˺ to all people on the day of the greater pilgrimage that Allah and His Messenger are free of the polytheists. So if you ˹pagans˺ repent, it will be better for you. But if you turn away, then know that you will have no escape from Allah. And give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to the disbelievers of a painful punishment

**The Announcement Belongs in the Open**

This verse names a declaration made to all people on the day of the greater pilgrimage. Its setting is not incidental. Pilgrimage gathered tribes that otherwise lived far apart, and the sacred season provided a recognized period in which public speech could travel without being mistaken for a private raid. A change in relations affecting many communities required a message that many communities could hear. The verse therefore presents the policy as an open announcement, not as a secret instruction passed from one faction to another.

**The Irony of “Good News”**

The irony also reveals two kinds of hearing. Someone who still wants truth may hear the warning as a reason to repent. Someone committed to denial may hear only an insult. The message itself has not changed; the condition of the listener determines whether the warning becomes rescue or evidence against him. The Qur'an says that the same revelation increases believers in faith and increases diseased hearts in defilement (9:124–125). Verse 3 begins that distinction in the public square.

---

The declaration was carried to the pilgrims at that year's ḥajj by ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, whom the Prophet ﷺ sent to proclaim it, and the reports in both Ṣaḥīḥ collections describe him announcing to the crowds on the day of the sacrifice that no polytheist would perform the pilgrimage after that year and that no one would circle the House naked. The verse's purpose is the abolition of the old arrangement rather than the beginning of hostilities: the respite of the preceding verse (9:2) is allowed to run out, and from that year the sanctuary is closed to those who associate others with God.

**Who Read It Out and Where**

The announcement was made at the pilgrimage of the ninth year, and the man who read it to the crowd at Minā was ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, sent by the Prophet for that purpose. Abū Bakr led that pilgrimage as the commander of the caravan, and the declaration was given to the Prophet's own household to deliver, as the reports in Bukhārī describe. The choice of a man from the family of the Prophet to carry a message to the tribes of Arabia was deliberate: the terms were being imposed on people who measured words by the standing of the speaker, and the Book gives the message public delivery by a named hand.
**The Four Terms in the Sentence**

The verse states them plainly: God and His Messenger are free of obligation to the idolaters, and the declaration covers the whole body of them. The verses that follow set out how the command was to be applied. Those with whom a treaty had been made and who had not broken it were to have their term completed to its last day (9:4). Those who broke it were given four months to move or to make their choice (9:2-5). And a treaty that was found to be unsound was thrown back to the other party on equal terms, with the Muslims forbidden to act against a tribe while a treaty still stood (8:58). The law of treaties in the schools comes out of this passage: an agreement is binding while the other side honours it, and a public declaration is required before relations change.
**What the Verse Refuses**

The declaration ends with a sentence about the disbelievers and a call to the believers to give the good news. The Book does not permit the community to go on with a double arrangement in which the sanctuary is shared and the treaties are ambiguous, and it says so openly at the largest gathering of the year. The verse is therefore not a notice of war. It is an announcement that the terms of coexistence had changed, and it was given at the season when a message could travel to every tribe without being mistaken for a raid.

## Verse 9:4

> As for the polytheists who have honoured every term of their treaty with you and have not supported an enemy against you, honour your treaty with them until the end of its term. Surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺

**The Exception Is the Rule’s Moral Center**

This verse prevents the opening proclamation from being read as a command to abandon every treaty with every polytheist. It identifies the protected group by conduct: they have honored the agreement and have not aided an enemy. Their religious identity does not erase the obligation created by their faithful behavior. The passage is therefore not sorting people only by creed. It is also sorting political relationships by trustworthiness.

**Complete the Agreement to Its End**

This principle echoes the Qur'anic command, Fulfill the covenant of Allah when you have pledged it (16:91). It also accords with the Prophet's public proclamation during the pilgrimage, which preserved the agreements of those who had not violated them. The verse gives a concrete test for piety. A person may appear religious in worship and speech, yet the truth of his God-consciousness is tested when keeping his word costs political advantage. The faithful treaty partner receives protection because fidelity itself has moral weight.

---

**The Command to Finish What They Started**

The verse does not end with the exception; it ends with an order: *so complete for them their treaty up to its term* — not to the treaty's first convenient exit, but to the end of the agreed period. Then comes the sentence that elevates the whole passage from policy to worship: *indeed, God loves those who are mindful of Him*. The verse says that the man who keeps faith with a polytheist who kept faith with him is doing more than honouring a contract; he is being *taqwā* — showing the awareness of God that the passage claims to look for. The sūrah opened with a declaration that dissolved the treaties that had been broken; it takes care in its fourth verse to say that the treaties kept are protected by the same God the declaration invoked.
**What the Test Was**

The first listeners were men who had been betrayed by allies and had a case for treating every treaty as void. The four-verse opening asks them to sort their allies one by one, by conduct rather than by name, and to protect the man who had done nothing wrong. That is the harder discipline. Anger wants to generalise; the verse refuses to let it, and makes the refusal a matter of the fear of God.

## Verse 9:5

> But once the Sacred Months have passed, kill the polytheists ˹who violated their treaties˺ wherever you find them, capture them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them on every way. But if they repent, perform prayers, and pay alms-tax, then set them free. Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**The Command Has a Defined Setting**

This is the passage's hardest sentence, and it must be read with every condition the verse itself supplies. The command concerns the polytheists who violated their treaties, and it follows a public notice and a period of security. It is not a free-standing order directed at every non-Muslim in every place and time. Verse 4 has just protected treaty keepers, and verse 6 will require asylum and safe escort for one who asks to hear the message. Those neighboring verses are not optional commentary added later; they are part of the same legal unit.

**The Hostile Case Is Defined by Breach**

Some jurists did read the verse more broadly, and their reasoning must be acknowledged rather than hidden. They focused on the force of the command and on the political transformation of Arabia after years of conflict. Other jurists emphasized the explicit exceptions and the prophetic practice of honoring treaties and granting protection. The stronger contextual reading sees a war order directed at a hostile political configuration, not a permission to kill any person whose creed differs. Verse 5 itself gives the terms of release: when the hostile alignment is abandoned through repentance, prayer, and alms, the person is to be set free.

---

**The Verses That Frame It**

The sentence stands inside a legal unit, and the unit is easy to trace. Verse 1 declares the dissolution of the treaties with the polytheists who broke them, and verse 2 gives them four months to move about the land. Verse 4 protects those who kept faith and gave no help against the Muslims, and commands the community to complete their term. Our verse follows with its command, and verse 6 immediately requires that any polytheist who asks for protection be granted it and escorted to safety "so that he may hear the word of God". Then verse 7 asks the question that settles how the whole passage is to be read: how can there be a treaty with those who, whenever they get the upper hand, respect neither kinship nor agreement?
**What the Companions Understood It to Cover**

The passage was revealed about a particular set of Arab tribes that had repeatedly broken their agreements with the community, at a moment when the Muslims had the strength to settle the matter. The jurists drew two things from the sequence that are agreed across the schools: a treaty with those who honour it must be observed to its term, and the person who seeks to hear the message must be protected while he listens. The harsher general reading of the sentence that some jurists recorded is answered by the conditions in the same unit, which the sūrah put there deliberately: an order that comes with an exception for treaty-keepers, a notice period, and a guaranteed safe conduct for anyone who asks for one is not a licence to attack people for their beliefs, and the chapter's own arrangement of the verses says so.

## Verse 9:6

> And if anyone from the polytheists asks for your protection ˹O Prophet˺, grant it to them so they may hear the Word of Allah, then escort them to a place of safety, for they are a people who have no knowledge

**The Person Who Asks Is Protected**

Verse 6 places a striking limit beside the fighting command of the previous verse. If a polytheist asks for protection, the response is not suspicion or detention. Protection must be granted. The Arabic command is direct and leaves little room for the commander to make safety depend on a prior promise of conversion. The request itself begins a duty. In a setting of war, the person who approaches under a plea for security is moved from the category of active threat to the category of one whose case must be heard.

**Ignorance Calls for Explanation Before Judgment**

The verse explains the command by saying that these people have no knowledge. The statement identifies a condition, not a permanent defect. Their hostility may be sustained by ignorance of the actual revelation and of the reasons for the Muslim community's actions. If they have not heard the message clearly, allowing them to hear it is part of justice. The Qur'an says, We never punish until We have sent a messenger (17:15). Verse 6 gives that principle a practical form: knowledge is not presumed when a person has a genuine opportunity to receive it.

**What the Verse Commands**

The verse tells the Prophet that if any of the men against whom the fighting command was given asks for protection, he is to be granted it, and then brought to a place where he can hear the words of God, and then delivered to safety. The order matters: protection, hearing, escort. The jurists built the rules of the safe conduct on this verse, and they held that an enemy who asks for a hearing may not be refused, and that it is the leader who grants the guarantee, though a single member of the community can bind the whole of it by the Prophet's own word in Bukhārī that the believers are one hand against the rest and the protection given by the least of them is binding.
**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**

The versés before it contain the hardest commands of the chapter, and the verse in front of us stands between them and the command to fulfil the treaties. The Book's arrangement is deliberate: it states the severity of the war and then immediately states the door that stays open in it. The Sūrah of the Spoils gives the same balance in its own passage, telling the Prophet that if they turn to peace he should turn to it and trust God (8:61), and the Sūrah of the Cow forbids compulsion in religion and states that the right is distinct from the wrong (2:256).
**The Rule and the Community's Practice**

The sūrah closes this verse by saying that the reason for the protection is so that the man may hear the words of God, since some of the people do not know. The command was carried out in the early years: emissaries were received, hostages were treated as guests, and the records of the conquests show terms negotiated by single Muslims on their own word. The Book's own explanation of the protection is the sentence that best summarises its policy, and the community's jurists quoted it whenever the subject of safe conduct and diplomatic immunity was raised.

## Verse 9:7

> How can such polytheists have a treaty with Allah and His Messenger, except those you have made a treaty with at the Sacred Mosque? So, as long as they are true to you, be true to them. Indeed Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺

**A Covenant With God Has a Human Form**

The verse speaks of a treaty with Allah and His Messenger. This wording lifts a political agreement above ordinary convenience. When the Messenger makes a binding arrangement, the believers do not regard it as a private contract that can be adjusted whenever public opinion changes. Allah is named as the ultimate witness and guarantor. Breaking such an agreement is therefore not merely a failed negotiation; it is a violation of an obligation made under divine oversight.

The Qur'an uses similar language for allegiance: Those who pledge allegiance to you pledge allegiance only to Allah; Allah's Hand is above their hands (48:10). That verse does not turn human beings into gods. It teaches that a promise made to the Messenger carries a higher accountability because the Messenger's authority is entrusted by Allah. Verse 7 applies the same principle to a treaty. The parties deal with one another, but the covenant is recorded in a court larger than both parties.

**The Exception Is Based on Steadfast Conduct**

The commentators differed over the historical group identified by the exception. Some associated it with members of Banū Bakr who did not participate in the breach connected to Ḥudaybiyah. Others mentioned Banū Khuzāʿah or particular Qurashī groups who received a period of notice and then changed their position. The reports vary, but the rule that unites them is stated in the verse itself: as long as they remain true, remain true to them.

**What the Verse Is Dealing With**

The verse asks how the idolaters could have a treaty with God and His Messenger, and it then gives the exception: the people with whom the community had made the treaty at the Sacred Mosque, since they had kept faith as long as the others did. The Book gives the rule for the whole matter in the sentence that follows: as long as they keep to it, keep to it with them, for God loves those who are mindful of Him. The passage is one of the sūrah's most practical, and it names the treaties that the community was bound by among the tribes of Arabia.
**The Law of Treaties in the Sūrah**

The Sūrah of the Spoils states the same rule from the other side, telling the community to return to a tribe its agreement on equal terms when treachery is feared (8:58), and the sūrah in front of us orders the carrying out of the treaties with the tribes that had not broken them. The jurists built the law of the truce and its term on these verses, and they held that the obligation of an agreement outlives the convenience of the moment. The verse's own reason is stated in the last clause, which names the love of God as the ground of the keeping.
**Where the Passage Goes**

The verses that follow give the four months' notice for the tribes that had broken their agreements, and the passage names the men who kept faith as a separate case. The Book's arrangement gives the reader the two kinds of treaty in the same chapter, so that the community's own conduct might show which kind it kept. The sūrah is dealing with the year of the delegations, and the verse in front of us states the standard the community was to be held to after the fighting ended.

## Verse 9:8

> How ˹can they have a treaty˺? If they were to have the upper hand over you, they would have no respect for kinship or treaty. They only flatter you with their tongues, but their hearts are in denial, and most of them are rebellious

**Power Reveals Whether a Treaty Is Real**

Verse 8 answers the question raised in the previous verse by proposing a test: what would these people do if they gained the upper hand? A treaty that exists only while one side lacks the strength to break it is not secure. The verse does not praise weakness, and it does not condemn every strong party. It exposes a specific character: the party that uses polite words while waiting for the chance to disregard every obligation.

This test reaches beyond the original tribes. Many people appear honest when external pressure makes honesty easy. The deeper question is what they do when enforcement disappears. A promise honored only because betrayal would be costly reveals fear of consequence, not necessarily faithfulness. The verse asks the reader to look at a pattern of conduct under advantage, because power exposes the values that polite circumstances can conceal.

**Mouth and Heart Are Put in Conflict**

The Qur'an often treats the heart as the center of settled purpose, not as a private organ that excuses public behavior. Words still matter because they create promises; what makes these words blameworthy is that they are used without the intention to honor them. The verse warns believers not to evaluate trust by charm alone. Pleasant speech can be a sign of good manners, but it can also be a tool of manipulation. Conduct over time reveals which one it is.

---

The treaty the verse has in view is the agreement of Ḥudaybiyah (48:1) and its collapse through the Quraysh's backing of an attack on their allies. The Book states the same test elsewhere in the sūrah: these are the people who, if they prevailed, would respect neither kinship nor covenant with the believers (9:10), and the same verse's description of flattery rather than faith is what the next verses set out — the sale of God's signs for a small price (9:9). The point of the test is practical: a treaty is worth what the other party does when it ceases to need it.

## Verse 9:9

> They chose a fleeting gain over Allah’s revelations, hindering ˹others˺ from His Way. Evil indeed is what they have done

**Revelation Was Treated as a Commodity**

The verse portrays the hostile group as choosing a small worldly return in exchange for Allah's revelations. The image is a transaction, but its moral force lies in the terrible imbalance of the price. Revelation carries truth, guidance, and a person's final direction. The gain that replaces it is brief and limited. The verse does not say that the gain had no attraction. It says that the attraction was too small to justify what was surrendered.

The Qur'an often uses the language of buying and selling to describe moral decisions. People exchange something lasting for something immediate, or they give what cannot be retained to receive what cannot be lost. In 9:111, believers are described as selling their lives and wealth for Paradise. Verse 9 presents the counterfeit transaction: revelation is exchanged for influence, security, profit, or the comfort of remaining in charge. Both groups are trading. The difference is what each regards as valuable.

**The Market Metaphor Includes a Better Trade**

The same Qur'an that criticizes the bad sale invites a better one. It asks, Who will lend Allah a goodly loan? He will multiply it for him, and he will have a generous reward (57:11). It also announces a commerce that saves from painful punishment: faith in Allah and His Messenger and striving in His way (61:10–11). These passages are not separate decorations. They clarify verse 9 by showing that human life always involves preference and exchange. The question is whether the person trades truth for a short gain or trades temporary possessions for lasting nearness.

## Verse 9:10

> They do not honour the bonds of kinship or treaties with the believers. It is they who are the transgressors

**Transgression Means Crossing a Limit**

That boundary may be a legal term, a promise, or the limit set by justice in conflict. The Qur'an can authorize resistance to aggression while still condemning the person who exceeds the reason and limit of that resistance.

This is why the verse is connected to the rule, Whoever transgresses against you, then transgress against him in the same measure in which he transgressed against you (2:194). The instruction is not a celebration of revenge. It is a demand for proportion. The hostile group in verse 10 has crossed multiple lines at once: family protection, treaty obligation, and the protection owed to believers. The label transgressors describes a settled disregard for limits, not simply the fact that a conflict exists.

**A General Rule for Public Trust**

The same rule governs family power. Parents must not use kinship as a license for abuse, and children must not use religious language to cancel every duty toward parents. Verse 10 does not turn every bond into an absolute command; it teaches that bonds have weight and cannot be discarded for convenience. When a higher command requires a boundary, the boundary must be explained honestly rather than disguised as though no bond existed.

## Verse 9:11

> But if they repent, perform prayer, and pay alms-tax, then they are your brothers in faith. This is how We make the revelations clear for people of knowledge

**Repentance Changes the Social Category**

Verse 11 returns to the condition stated in verse 5, but it now explains the consequence in the language of belonging. Those who repent, establish prayer, and pay the alms-tax become brothers in faith. The verse does not describe a temporary ceasefire or a probationary status. It names a new relationship. The people who were addressed as participants in a hostile order are received into the community once they abandon that order and enter the obligations of Islam.

**Brotherhood Is an Obligation, Not a Mood**

The word brothers in faith carries legal and moral weight. It does not merely say that believers should feel warmly toward one another. Brotherhood creates duties of protection, honesty, support, and restraint from harm. The Qur'an elsewhere says that believers are guardians of one another, encouraging right and restraining wrong (9:71). Verse 11 establishes the foundation for that mutual care: when a person joins the faith through repentance and its public duties, he is not placed at the edge of the community forever.

**The Qiblah Community and the Protection of Life**

Classical jurists drew a major principle from this verse: the people who face the qiblah and practice the public marks of Islam are treated as members of the protected Muslim community. The exact legal discussions became complex, especially when political divisions arose among Muslims, but the verse's central social effect is clear. A person who has entered the covenant of faith is not to be treated as an outsider merely because his previous life was opposed to the believers.

The Prophet's saying, Islam is built upon five, names the testimony, prayer, alms, fasting, and pilgrimage as the visible pillars of the religion (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 8; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 16). Verse 11 highlights prayer and alms because the immediate context concerns the public transition from hostile idolatry to the Muslim community. It does not reduce Islam to two acts; it shows how those acts signify the new shared order.

## Verse 9:12

> But if they break their oaths after making a pledge and attack your faith, then fight the champions of disbelief—who never honour their oaths—so perhaps they will desist

**A Breach Can Become an Organized Threat**

Verse 12 addresses a more advanced stage of betrayal. The hostile party has not merely failed to keep a promise; it has broken oaths after making a binding pledge and has attacked the religion of the believers. The two features belong together in the verse. A political breach becomes especially dangerous when it is joined to a campaign aimed at weakening the community's faith and resolve.

The Arabic verb for breaking an oath can evoke the untying of a firmly twisted cord. That picture explains the moral force of the verse. An oath is a bond deliberately strengthened through words. To break it after confirming it is to pull apart something one had just claimed to secure. The addition of attacking the religion shows that the breach is not an accidental failure or a private dispute. It has become an active effort against the community's identity and public order.

**Attack on Faith Is Not Mere Offense**

This is why jurists disagreed over cases involving treaty holders who insulted Islam or the Prophet. Some held that a grave insult joined to breach removed protection; Abū Ḥanīfah's reasoning, as reported in the commentaries, distinguished disbelief as a belief from public speech that threatened political peace. The disagreement shows that the legal tradition did not simply treat every offensive word as the same act. It asked what the speech did within the treaty and whether it formed part of an aggressive campaign.

## Verse 9:13

> Will you not fight those who have broken their oaths, conspired to expel the Messenger ˹from Mecca˺, and attacked you first? Do you fear them? Allah is more deserving of your fear, if you are ˹true˺ believers

**The Verse Supplies the Reasons**

Verse 13 asks whether the believers will fight a people and then lists three reasons: broken oaths, a plan to expel the Messenger, and first aggression. The list is the chapter's clearest explanation of the hostile case introduced earlier. The fighting is not left hanging on a vague charge of difference. The verse presents a record of actions and intentions that have made continued submission to the old treaty arrangement impossible.

The first reason points to the destruction of a pledged bond. The second concerns an attack on the Messenger's presence and leadership. The third establishes who started the hostilities. Together they describe a threat to both covenant and community. Even if exegetes differed over whether the tried expulsion refers to the Hijrah plot, later efforts against the Prophet in Madinah, or both, the principle remains: an intention to remove the Messenger by force is itself part of the case.

**First Aggression Matters**

The phrase attacked you first places the community's response in a moral sequence. The believers are not being asked to pretend that all sides began at the same time. The attack on Banū Khuzāʿah by Banū Bakr, with Quraysh support according to the historical reports, is the immediate example connected with the broken Ḥudaybiyah order. Qur'an 22:39 gives the wider principle that permission to fight was given to those who were fought because they were wronged.

**Fear Is Reordered, Not Denied**

The same principle appears in the command, Do not fear them; fear Me (5:44), and in the reassurance that Satan frightens his allies, so do not fear them but fear Allah (3:175). These verses do not praise recklessness. They teach that fear must not become the authority that decides whether a duty is obeyed. A believer may feel afraid and still choose the right action. Faith is shown by which fear governs the choice.

---

## Verse 9:14

> ˹So˺ fight them and Allah will punish them at your hands, put them to shame, help you overcome them, and soothe the hearts of the believers—

**Human Hands Can Be Instruments of Divine Justice**

Verse 14 attributes several results to Allah while describing the believers' hands as the means through which those results occur. The balance is deliberate. The community must act, but it must not imagine that its action makes it the owner of history. Allah punishes through their hands, puts the aggressors to shame, grants victory, and heals the hearts of the believers. Human agency is real, yet it remains subordinate to divine judgment.

The same relation appears in 8:17: You did not kill them, but Allah killed them; you did not throw when you threw, but Allah threw. That verse does not deny that people struck or threw. It denies that their actions were independent of Allah's will and support. Verse 14 applies the same theology to a campaign. The believers are responsible for obeying the command and for the manner of their conduct; the final outcome belongs to Allah.

**Shame Exposes a Failed Moral Order**

The promise is not a command for believers to enjoy humiliation as a personal pleasure. It is a statement about the end of injustice. Shame belongs to the aggressor's false account of himself, not to the believer's appetite for revenge. The believer should want the threat ended and the truth revealed, while remembering that Allah can also turn the heart of the former enemy. Verse 15 will make that possibility explicit.

---

## Verse 9:15

> removing rage from their hearts. And Allah pardons whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Anger Can Point to Justice Without Becoming Its Master**

A community that feels no anger at betrayal may have lost its sense of moral reality. The verse's promise to remove rage implies that the emotion arose in response to something that mattered. The wronged people need not call the attack harmless to become peaceful. At the same time, anger is a poor ruler. It exaggerates, seeks new targets, and makes the original injury a reason for fresh injury. Divine healing is not the suppression of moral concern. It is the restoration of proportion.

This distinction matters for the believer's personal life. A person can be right about a wrong and still be damaged by the way he continues to carry it. The Qur'an's prayer, Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and do not place in our hearts resentment toward those who believe (59:10), shows that the heart's purification is a separate need from the judgment of an act. One can name injustice accurately and still ask Allah to remove its poison.

This relationship is visible in the chapter's opening title, al-Tawbah, and in the repeated invitations to repent.

---

## Verse 9:16

> Do you ˹believers˺ think that you will be left without Allah proving who among you ˹truly˺ struggles ˹in His cause˺ and never takes trusted allies other than Allah, His Messenger, or the believers? And Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**Faith Is Not Left Untested**

The verse opens with a question that challenges a false expectation: do believers think they will be left alone? The question is not about abandonment by Allah. It is about being left without a trial that reveals what the claim of faith means in practice. A community may speak of loyalty, sacrifice, and reliance on Allah while circumstances remain easy. The test shows whether those words govern real choices.

The Qur'an states the same principle in 29:2–3: Do people think they will be left to say, We believe, and they will not be tested? Testing does not give Allah information He lacked. It brings hidden commitments into visible action. A person learns what he trusts when competing demands arrive, and a community learns its true priorities when comfort, family, money, or safety pull against obedience.

**How the Verse Prevents Complacency**

The answer will not be measured by slogans. Allah sees what is done. The community should therefore avoid judging hidden hearts while still taking public actions seriously. Someone may be weak yet sincere; another may be eloquent yet disloyal. Verse 16 places both the test and the final knowledge with Allah. The believer's task is to respond honestly when the test arrives, not to perform a religious identity for the crowd. The unseen loyalty becomes visible through the choices that pressure produces, especially when comfort and obedience point in different directions. The test separates a sincere claim from a convenient association. Allah makes the hidden loyalty visible through choices, especially when obedience costs friendship, safety, or reputation.

---

## Verse 9:17

> It is not for the polytheists to maintain the mosques of Allah while they openly profess disbelief. Their deeds are void, and they will be in the Fire forever

**Who Maintains the Mosques**

The verse says that the men who worship other beings alongside God have no business maintaining the mosques of God while they openly profess their disbelief, and it says that their deeds will come to nothing and that they will be in the fire. The verse is not a statement about repairing buildings; it is a statement about who may claim the house of God as theirs.

**What the Verse Is About**

The house in Makkah was in the hands of the idolaters before the community returned to it, and the rulers among them had taken charge of its upkeep while they filled the place with the idols the verse before this one names. The chapter's point is that the service of the house belongs to the service of the One the house stands for. A man who has taken God's house as a centre for the worship of others is not maintaining it; he is using it, whatever he spends on it. The sūrah says the same thing in its own words a few verses later, when it states that the men who believe in God and the last day and keep the prayer and give the alms and fear God are the ones who should maintain the places of worship (9:18 gives the full list).

**What Happens to Deeds Done Without Faith**

The verse says that the deeds of those men will come to nothing. The Book does not teach that kindness and repair are worthless in this world; a man may build something that helps people, and it remains a help. What the verse teaches is that deeds done in the name of worship while the worship itself is given to someone else cannot carry the weight of a religious claim. The Book says in another place that work can be lost when a person joins partners with God in it (39:65 states the principle), and it says in the sūrah of the Cave that the deeds of such people are like dust scattered by the wind (18:103 and the verses after it describe what they find). The verse in front of the reader says what a person's work is worth when the foundation under it is wrong.

**The Community's Own House**

The chapter will go on to name the men who are entitled to the houses of God, and it will do so in the plainest terms: faith in God and the last day, prayer, alms and the fear of God (9:18 gives all four). The sūrah then warns the whole community against a different kind of misuse of the same house: it describes the mosque that was built to cause harm and division among the believers, and it tells the Prophet to stand in it only if he chooses to (9:107 sets out the accusations against its builders). The same chapter tells the community that the true maintenance of the house is the remembrance of God's name in it, and it will say that the men who keep it alive are the ones who believe.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader**

The verse leaves the reader with a question about why he does the things he does. A person can repair a building, give a gift, or serve a guest, and the same act can be done for God or for something else. The chapter says the same thing about giving and about fighting in the verses that follow: it tells the believers to fight in God's way and names the men who would not go (9:38 and the verses after it describe the men who preferred to stay), and it says that the men who give and the men who do not are not the same (9:79 describes the men who criticized the givers). The verse in front of the reader tells the community that a house belongs to whoever it is dedicated to, and that the deed matches the dedication.

---

## Verse 9:18

> The mosques of Allah should only be maintained by those who believe in Allah and the Last Day, establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and fear none but Allah. It is right to hope that they will be among the ˹truly˺ guided

**The Positive Standard for Custody**

Verse 18 answers the boundary established in verse 17 by describing who should maintain the mosques of Allah. Four qualities are named. The custodian believes in Allah and the Last Day, establishes prayer, pays alms-tax, and fears none but Allah. The list moves from conviction to practice, from worship to social responsibility, and from both to moral courage. A mosque's true maintenance requires all four dimensions.

A mosque may be wealthy, crowded, and admired while its decisions are driven by fear of losing status. Verse 18 asks a deeper question.

**Fear None but Allah Protects the Message**

This condition links the verse with 9:13, where the believers are told that Allah is more deserving of fear than the hostile army. The same fear-audit applies inside a mosque. A custodian who speaks firmly to enemies but changes the religion to keep a wealthy supporter has not met the verse's standard. Courage is not loudness. It is the freedom to do what Allah requires when another person can punish or reward you.

---

The verse was read against the claims of those who inherited the care of the sanctuaries as a family privilege — the descendants of Ismāʿīl who held the keys of the Kaʿbah, and the notables who claimed the right to maintain the Prophet's mosque. The Book puts the custodianship on the four qualities named, which is the same standard it applies to the sacrifices of the pilgrimage: it is not the flesh or the blood that reaches God but the mindfulness of the worshipper (22:37). When the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah in the conquest, he took the keys of the Kaʿbah from ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah and then returned them to him, with the house kept in the care of its traditional custodians — a decision the jurists read as confirming that custody is a trust, not a badge of religious standing.

## Verse 9:19

> Do you ˹pagans˺ consider providing the pilgrims with water and maintaining the Sacred Mosque as equal to believing in Allah and the Last Day and struggling in the cause of Allah? They are not equal in Allah’s sight. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people

**The Historical Dispute Behind the Comparison**

Early reports connect the verse with a dispute about which act deserved the highest merit. Al-ʿAbbās, who was associated with providing water to pilgrims, and ʿAlī, who had taken part in early struggles for Islam, are mentioned in one account. Another report describes a broader discussion among Muslims near the Prophet's pulpit. The details differ, and al-Qurṭubī allowed that a verse already revealed may have been applied to a later dispute. The common issue is clear: people were ranking visible religious offices against costly commitment.
**Allah’s Scale Is Not the Crowd’s Scale**

The Qur'an's language of scales appears elsewhere: Are those who know equal to those who do not know? (39:9). Equality is not assumed merely because two acts look religious from outside. The divine scale considers proportion, foundation, and consequence. That knowledge should make believers humble. They can admire useful service without declaring the servant superior to the person who carried a heavier burden for Allah.

**The Question and Who Was Asking It**

The verse answers a dispute about honour. Men from the leading families of Makkah were comparing their services to the sanctuary — giving water to the pilgrims and maintaining the mosque — with the record of the believers who had left their homes and fought for the faith. The Book's answer is a ranking: the two are not equal. The service had value, and the sūrah says so in the verses around it, but a man's standing before God rests on faith in God and the Last Day and on what he does with his life for the sake of the faith. The next verse states the ranks and the reward more fully (9:20).
**Where the Sūrah Draws the Line**

The sūrah of Repentance is hard on claims. It has already told the tribes that the maintenance of the mosques belongs to the people who believe and pray and give, not to the people who built them for prestige (9:17-18), and it deals with the wealthy who stayed behind and with the men who claimed credit for a service while avoiding its cost. The verse therefore is not a dismissal of good work. It is a statement about what a person is measured by, and the Book uses the same standard elsewhere. The Sūrah of Women ranks those who struggle above those who sit at home (4:95), and the Sūrah of the Bee tells the emigrants after their trials that their patience and struggle are what the reward is attached to (16:110).

## Verse 9:20

> Those who have believed, emigrated, and strived in the cause of Allah with their wealth and their lives are greater in rank in the sight of Allah. It is they who will triumph

**Three Movements Define the Higher Rank**

Verse 20 names the people who stand above the merely respected religious office described in the previous verse. They believe, emigrate, and strive in Allah's cause with wealth and lives. The sequence describes a movement from conviction to separation and then to expenditure. Faith gives the direction. Emigration shows that the direction has become more important than inherited comfort. Striving shows that the new commitment continues to cost something after the move.

**Wealth and Life Are Both Spent**

The pairing also creates a safeguard against spiritual boasting. A person cannot claim that his financial gift excuses him from moral courage, nor that his courage excuses him from generosity. The believers at the center of the sūrah made their resources serve their convictions. That pattern continues in the Qur'an's description of the good trade: faith and striving with wealth and selves are mentioned together (61:10–11).

**Rank Is Assigned by Allah**

The Qur'an elsewhere speaks of degrees for those who strive with wealth and selves (4:95–96). Degrees do not mean that every believer without the same opportunity is worthless. They mean that Allah recognizes differences in effort, sacrifice, and circumstance. A person who gives much from much may not be equal to one who gives little from little, and a person who bears a costly duty may not be equal to one who chooses the easy path. The scale is exact and merciful because it knows what each person could actually give.

---

## Verse 9:21

> Their Lord gives them good news of His mercy, pleasure, and Gardens with everlasting bliss

**Divine Pleasure Is Greater Than Possession**

The second gift is pleasure, riḍwān. This word points to Allah's approval and contentment with the servant. It is more personal than a reward placed before a person. A person can receive a gift from someone while remaining distant from the giver. Riḍwān means that the giver's relationship with the servant has itself become a source of joy.

The Qur'an later says that Allah's pleasure is greater than the Gardens and their pleasures (9:72). Verse 21 introduces that hierarchy here. The believer wants Paradise because it is Allah's promised home, but the highest desire is not the landscape without the Lord. The heart that has learned to sacrifice for Allah wants to be received by Him. The Garden is blissful because Allah's pleasure surrounds it. Without that pleasure, abundance would still leave the deepest hunger untouched.

**A Reward That Reverses the World’s Valuation**

For believers today, the verse gives a way to understand costly obedience without romanticizing pain. Hardship is not automatically proof of righteousness. The action must be connected to belief and Allah's cause. But when a person loses a worldly benefit because he refuses what Allah forbids, the loss is not the final measure. The Lord who sees the sacrifice gives good news that the world could not give: mercy, pleasure, and a bliss that does not expire. The believer should therefore welcome the transformation of fear into hope. Allah's mercy does not erase the seriousness of the journey; it gives the traveler strength to continue toward Him.

---

## Verse 9:22

> to stay there for ever and ever. Surely with Allah is a great reward

**Eternity Completes the Promise**

Verse 22 completes the previous promise by adding the quality that earthly reward cannot provide: permanence. The believers will stay in the Gardens for ever and ever. The repeated expression closes the door on the anxiety that follows every worldly pleasure. A person may have something beautiful and still fear its loss. The Garden promised here is not held on a temporary lease. It is a home whose enjoyment does not move toward an ending.

The Qur'an often pairs Paradise with words of abiding. Created beings are aware that time takes everything from them.

**The Great Reward Is Kept With Allah**

Greatness here also resists calculation. A person can count wealth, land, or years, but he cannot measure the reward that Allah has prepared. The Qur'an uses the same phrase in other passages, including 4:95 and 48:29, after describing faith and sacrifice. The recurring formula teaches that the reward's scale matches the Lord's generosity, not the narrow scale by which people assess a believer's worldly standing.

**Forever Changes the Meaning of Loss**

That contrast appears throughout the Qur'an. The enjoyment of this life is described as little compared with the Hereafter (9:38), not because created blessings have no value, but because their duration is limited. Verse 22 gives the missing term in the calculation. When the reward is everlasting, a temporary sacrifice can be understood without being trivialized. The believer may grieve what was lost while still knowing that Allah's replacement is not only equal. It is beyond the reach of loss.

**The Promise Guards Against Empty Heroism**

The context also prevents a false glorification of sacrifice. People can suffer for nationalism, pride, revenge, or the desire to be remembered. Verse 22 does not promise eternal reward to every person who undergoes hardship. It follows the description of those who believed, emigrated, and strove in Allah's cause. The reward is tied to faith and purpose. Pain alone is not a path to Paradise.

---

## Verse 9:23
> O believers! Do not take your parents and siblings as trusted allies if they choose disbelief over belief. And whoever of you does so, they are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers

**When Faith Divides a Family**

The verse addresses believers with a command that reaches the deepest human bonds. It speaks of parents and siblings, and those are not casual friendships that can be set aside without pain. Family is where a person's identity, affection, shelter and earliest sense of belonging come from. So the command does not pretend that choosing faith over a hostile family preference is easy.

What the verse forbids is a specific thing. It is not ordinary love, and it is not kindness, and it is not the duty of care that the Book commands toward parents elsewhere. It is the role of trusted alliance, the relationship in which a person places his loyalty and his safety with someone who is pulling him away from God. The verse says that no created bond may hold that place.

**Why This Verse Was Sent**

The passage belongs to the sūrah's call to go out and to leave what had to be left. The verses around this one name the things people were hiding behind: fathers, sons, brothers, wives, clans, and the wealth and trade they were afraid of losing, and they end by warning that anyone who prefers these over God, His Messenger and striving in His way should wait until God's decision arrives (9:24 lists them).

That is the situation the verse is addressing. The community was being asked to leave Makkah, or to set out on a hard expedition, and the pressure against going came mostly from inside the family. So the verse draws a line that people needed in that specific moment: the refusal to disobey God is not negotiable, whatever face it wears at home.

**Kindness Is Not Cancelled**

Another passage sets the distinction clearly. If parents try to make a person join others with God in worship, that person must not obey them, and he must still keep their company in this world with kindness (31:15 states both halves). This verse and 31:15 belong together. Disobedience to a parent in a matter of faith does not authorize cruelty, insult or neglect. The believer refuses the false demand while keeping whatever kindness remains lawful, and the Book commands that kindness as firmly as it forbids the obedience.

The same sūrah goes on to state the limits on the other side. God does not forbid the community from being kind and fair to those who did not fight them and did not drive them out of their homes, and He forbids only the alliance with those who fought them and drove them out and helped in expelling them (60:8 and 60:9 give the two rulings). So the line in this verse is about allegiance, and even for enemies the Book keeps a door of fairness open.

**The Standard Kept Elsewhere**

The Book states the same principle in another sūrah in one sentence: a people will not be found who believe in God and the last day and still love those who oppose God and His Messenger, not even if they are their fathers, their sons, their brothers or their clan (58:22 states it). Read with the verse in front of the reader, the Book is consistent. Faith comes first when a bond asks to be first, and everything short of that demand remains an obligation of mercy, patience and good treatment.

---
## Verse 9:24

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If your parents and children and siblings and spouses and extended family and the wealth you have acquired and the trade you fear will decline and the homes you cherish—˹if all these˺ are more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and struggling in His Way, then wait until Allah brings about His Will. Allah does not guide the rebellious people.”

**The Verse Inventories the Heart’s Strongest Loves**

Verse 24 expands the family warning into a complete audit of attachment. It names parents, children, siblings, spouses, extended family, acquired wealth, trade, and beloved homes. The list moves through the whole human world: origin, future, companionship, clan, property, livelihood, and shelter. Nothing important is left outside it. The verse asks whether these goods are more beloved than Allah, His Messenger, and striving in His way.

**Allah and the Messenger Are the Fixed Center**

The counterweight to the eight loves is Allah, His Messenger, and striving in His way. Allah is the source and owner of every good listed. The Messenger teaches how to love Allah in concrete life. Striving proves that the love has become action rather than sentiment. The three belong together. Love of Allah without following the Messenger becomes a private claim with no revealed shape; following without sacrifice becomes a theory that has never been tested.

The Prophet taught that faith is not complete until he is more beloved than one's father, child, and all people (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 15; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 44). That teaching does not compete with filial kindness. It sets the Messenger's guidance above every human preference. The believer loves family more truly when he loves them within the truth, not when he helps them destroy their souls to preserve peace.

---

The list covers the whole outward world: family by every degree, wealth by acquisition, trade by its risks, and homes by their comfort. The verse's sentence then holds them all under a single condition — that they are loved more than God, His Messenger and striving in His way — and names the consequence, waiting until God's command comes. The Book repeats the warning about family in the same terms: wealth and children are a test (8:28), and neither will avail on the Day the ties of kinship are cut (23:101). The verse closes with the statement that God does not guide the defiantly corrupt, which places the choice in the will rather than in the strength of the affections.

**What the Verse Lists**

The verse names the objects of a person's attachment one by one: fathers, sons, brothers, spouses, clan, wealth earned, trade that might slow, and dwellings that please, and it says that if these are dearer than God and His Messenger and the struggle in His way, then wait until God brings His command. The list is one of the Book's most complete audits of the things a person loves, and the jurists and preachers used it as the test of where a heart is actually placed, since it does not name one category but the whole range of human ties.
**The Report Behind It**

The verse is connected with the migration, and the reports describe men who were unwilling to leave their families and their property behind. The Prophet's word in the collections about the love of the homeland and about the migration that ends only with repentance belongs with the passage, and the sūrah has been dealing with the campaign to Tabūk and with the men who stayed behind since its middle. The verse's sentence about waiting for the command of God is read by the commentators as the warning of the punishment or of the loss that comes to a community which puts its households first.
**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The Book does not forbid love of family or of a home, and the sūrah commands kindness to parents in the same book of the community's law. What it forbids is the preference, and the measure it gives is the comparison with God and His Messenger and the struggle. The Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states the same test in the verse about the men who love God before all (3:31), and the sūrah of the Cave has the same warning about the household and the children who may become an enemy. The verse in front of us puts the whole inventory in front of the reader and asks him to sort it.

## Verse 9:25

> Indeed Allah has given you ˹believers˺ victory on many battlefields, even at the Battle of Ḥunain when you took pride in your great numbers, but they proved of no advantage to you. The earth, despite its vastness, seemed to close in on you, then you turned back in retreat

**A Victory Can Become a Test of the Victor**

Verse 25 recalls Ḥunain not simply as a battle report but as a correction to the believers' confidence. Allah had already granted victory on many battlefields. The word many establishes a history of help. Then the verse singles out Ḥunain, where the believers were impressed by their great number. The problem was not having a large army. The problem was allowing the number to become the source of confidence.

The Muslim force after the conquest of Makkah was larger than any force the community had earlier fielded. New Muslims from Makkah joined the veterans of Madinah, and the size of the army created a feeling that defeat was no longer possible. The valley of Ḥunain exposed that assumption quickly. A numerical advantage can be useful, but it cannot replace dependence on Allah. When a gift becomes an object of trust, the gift itself becomes a trial.

**The Valley Made Inner Overconfidence Visible**

The earth then seemed to close in despite its vastness. The physical valley had not literally become smaller, but retreating soldiers experienced a loss of room. Narrow ground, surprise, noise, and confusion turned breadth into constriction. The image also describes panic. Fear can make a person feel trapped inside a world that has not changed. Qur'an 8:45 links steadfastness and remembrance with strength in battle; Ḥunain showed the opposite when confidence broke.

**What Happened at Ḥunayn**

The verse recalls a battle fought two years before this chapter was given, and it names the fact that made it worth recalling: the community had numbered twelve thousand and had looked at its own size. The tribes of Hawāzin and Thaqīf had gathered their men and their property, and the Muslim army took the field with confidence, and then the first volley broke part of the line and the men began to fall back, and the Prophet stood in the middle of the retreat calling his people. The report in Bukhārī and Muslim describes the moment, with the Prophet on a white mule calling out that he was the prophet of God and the son of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib. The verse gives the reason for the setback in its own words: their great number did not save them, and the earth became narrow for them in spite of its breadth, and then they turned and fled.
**What the Verse Adds After the Account**

The next sentence records what came then: God sent down His calm upon the Messenger and upon the believers, and sent soldiers that were not seen, and punished the disbelievers. The word for the calm is the same *sakīnah* that appears in the Sūrah of the Cow and the Sūrah of Victory, and it names a quietness given to men who had lost their nerve. The verse does not leave the community with a defeat to explain. It gives the failure, the reason for it and the recovery, all in two sentences.
**Why It Belongs in This Sūrah**

The chapter is addressed to a community that had just grown stronger and wealthier, and the verse is its warning about what strength does to men. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states the same lesson after the setback at Uḥud, and the Sūrah of the Spoils gives it as a rule, telling the believers that they were few and despised before and that God gave them shelter and support (8:26). The verse in front of us is placed among the sūrah's commands about the treaties and the money, and it tells the men who were being asked to fund a campaign what the last one had cost them.

---

## Verse 9:26

> Then Allah sent down His reassurance upon His Messenger and the believers, and sent down forces you could not see, and punished those who disbelieved. Such was the reward of the disbelievers

**Reassurance Restored the Scattered Army**

Verse 26 describes the reversal of Ḥunain by beginning with reassurance from Allah. The Arabic term sakīnah is related to stillness and settling. It names a calm that descends into a heart when fear has made it unstable. The verse places this reassurance upon the Messenger and the believers. The Prophet was not excluded from the need for divine support, and the ordinary believers were not left outside the gift.

The sequence matters. The army's first need was not a new number but recovered composure. Panic had made a large force ineffective. Reassurance restored the ability to hear, gather, and act. Reports about the battle describe the Prophet calling the believers back while those around him helped spread the call, but the verse gives the deepest explanation: Allah settled His Messenger and the believers. Human effort became useful again because the heart was no longer ruled by confusion.

**Visible Defeat Does Not End the Story**

Verse 25 ended with retreat. Verse 26 shows that retreat was not the final state. The believers could return to the field after recognizing their dependence. This pattern is valuable for anyone who has failed under pressure. A collapse can reveal pride, but it need not define the rest of the life. Repentance, remembrance, and renewed action can restore what panic interrupted.

---

The battle named in the previous verse is Ḥunayn, fought in the year of the conquest of Makkah against the tribes of Hawāzin and Thaqīf, and the scene the Qur'an records shows the community scattered in the first stage of the fighting. The calm that was sent down is the same *sakīnah* the sūrah names in other places for other moments: in the cave with Abū Bakr (9:40), at Ḥudaybiyah (48:4), and on the day of the pledge of Ridwān (48:18). The army that appeared to be broken was not restored by numbers — the sūrah says the multitude did not avail them — and so the chapter's account of the reversal belongs with its teaching that victory is given rather than produced.

## Verse 9:27

> Then afterwards Allah will turn in grace to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Mercy Follows the Hardest Judgment**

Verse 27 follows the defeat of the enemy at Ḥunain with a statement about Allah's grace. The order matters. The passage has spoken of fighting, punishment, and the consequences of disbelief, but it refuses to make severity the last word. Afterwards Allah turns in grace to whoever He wills. The people who were on the opposing side are not treated as a single permanent block. Some may leave the hostility, accept truth, and enter the reach of forgiveness.

**The Chapter’s Name Returns to the Battlefield**

Al-Tawbah is often remembered for repentance as a human act, but verse 27 highlights Allah's turn toward the repentant. The chapter's hard passages and its mercy passages are one argument. A community must be able to confront betrayal and still believe that the betrayer can become different. If it cannot, its justice has become a form of despair. If it cannot confront betrayal, its mercy has become indifference.

The turn of Allah also gives hope to believers who fear that a serious failure has removed them forever. The verse does not say that grace belongs only to those who never opposed the truth. It says Allah can turn to whoever He wills. The conditions of repentance remain real, but the range of mercy is wider than the believer's imagination. A person should not call himself beyond return while the Lord of the sūrah continues to announce His forgiveness.

---

## Verse 9:28

> O believers! Indeed, the polytheists are ˹spiritually˺ impure, so they should not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year. If you fear poverty, Allah will enrich you out of His bounty, if He wills. Surely, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Impurity Here Is a Religious Description**

The verse calls the polytheists spiritually impure and then gives a practical ruling about the Sacred Mosque. The wording does not simply describe dirt on the body. Classical jurists discussed whether najasah here means a ritual condition, the pollution of polytheistic belief, or a status that requires distance from the sanctuary. The translation's addition of spiritually captures the main point: the verse is addressing the contradiction between the worship of many gods and a sanctuary dedicated to Allah's oneness.
**The End of Pagan Pilgrimage Changes the Economy**

The verse anticipates a practical fear: if polytheists no longer approach the sanctuary, will Makkah lose income? Pilgrims brought trade, gifts, and commercial activity. The believers may have wondered whether religious purification would damage the city's material life. Allah answers that He can enrich them from His bounty. The promise does not deny the possibility of a short-term economic loss. It denies that obedience leaves Allah unable to provide.
**Purification Is Not Contempt**

The believer must hold the ruling without adopting contempt for people. The Qur'an commands justice and kindness toward those who do not fight the Muslims (60:8). Verse 6 of this chapter protects an idolater who asks to hear the Word and requires safe escort. These commands stand beside verse 28. The sanctuary can be protected from a religious practice while the person outside it is treated with fairness.

**The Command and the Fear Behind It**

The verse forbids the idolaters to come near the Sacred Mosque, and it does so because the pilgrims were the market. Makkah lived on the caravans and the trade that the pilgrimage season brought, and the community was being told to close the door on the people who had just been its customers. The verse answers the objection before it is raised: if you fear poverty, God will enrich you from His bounty if He wills. The economy of the town was about to be rebuilt on a different base, and the conquests that followed in the next generation were the fulfilment the first hearers could not have imagined.
**What the Word for Impurity Means**

The word here is *najas* — unclean — and the jurists read it as a religious uncleanliness, not a physical one. It describes a person whose worship is built on setting partners beside God, and the command concerns the approach to the mosque of the sanctuary. The schools differed over how far the ban reaches: some confined it to the Sacred Mosque itself, others extended it to mosques in general, and the Ḥanafī school permitted entry where there is a need or a contract or a service. The verse's own context is public worship in the sanctuary rather than the private dealings of a Muslim with his neighbour, and the sūrah commands kindness and justice towards those who are not fighting the community a few pages later (60:8).
**The Year the Command Belongs To**

The year mentioned is the ninth, after the treaty arrangements with the Arab tribes and before the pilgrimage led by Abū Bakr. The chapter has spent its earlier verses sorting out the treaties, and this verse is one of its decisions: the polytheists would no longer perform the rites alongside the Muslims. The new community was being built on a separation of worship rather than a separation of commerce, and the trade and the treaties continued.

## Verse 9:29

> Fight those who do not believe in Allah and the Last Day, nor comply with what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, nor embrace the religion of truth from among those who were given the Scripture, until they pay the tax, willingly submitting, fully humbled

**The Verse Addresses a Political Order**

Verse 29 introduces the command about certain People of the Book after the passage on Arabian polytheists. It is a legal and political verse, not a statement that every Jew or Christian is identical. The wording itself says from among those who were given the Book, and the Qur'an elsewhere praises upright People of the Book who believe, worship, and hasten to good (3:113–115). The group in this verse is defined by the political and religious conditions named here and by the campaign connected with the northern frontier.
**The Description Is Not a Blanket Statement About All Scripture People**

The verse lists failure to believe in Allah and the Last Day, failure to forbid what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, and failure to follow the religion of truth. The Qur'an recognizes that Jews and Christians claim belief in God and the Hereafter, so exegetes debated how the description applies. Some understood it as a legal description of their non-Muslim status and rejection of the Prophet. Others emphasized that the verse targets those whose conduct and political allegiance make them part of the hostile case.

**What the Verse Ordered and on What Ground**

The command to fight the group described here belongs to the northern campaign of Tabūk in the ninth year, and it was directed at a political order, not at a faith. The verse names three things the group failed to observe, and the jurists read them as the description of a hostile power rather than a judgement on every person who reads the Torah or the Gospel. The command that follows in the same verse — that they pay the tribute and are then left in peace — is what made the arrangement practical, and the Book elsewhere insists that there is no compulsion in religion (2:256) and orders that monasteries, churches and mosques be protected (22:40).
**How the Ruling Was Applied**

The Prophet took the tribute from the Magians of Hajar, which the jurists cited as the basis for extending the same arrangement to communities of other faiths under Muslim rule, and the early caliphs kept the same practice with the people of the territories they opened. The word describing them at the end of the verse was read in more than one way: as a state of submission to the law, as a payment made willingly, or as a loss of the great power they once held. The reading that made it a licence to humiliate contradicts the rest of the sūrah, which states plainly that God does not forbid Muslims from dealing kindly and justly with those who are not fighting them (60:8).
**Where It Sits in the Sūrah**

The verse stands in a passage that moves from the treaty obligations of the Arab tribes to the money and the frontier, and the sūrah is as much about internal discipline as about war. It has already dealt with the believers who stayed behind, and it deals with the public treasury a few verses later. The one sentence about a protected community and a tax is part of a whole argument about a community that had just become responsible for other people, which is the part of the sūrah that is hardest to hold and easiest to forget.

## Verse 9:30

> The Jews say, “Ezra is the son of Allah,” while the Christians say, “The Messiah is the son of Allah.” Such are their baseless assertions, only parroting the words of earlier disbelievers. May Allah condemn them! How can they be deluded ˹from the truth˺

**The Claim About Ezra and the Claim About the Messiah**

The verse reports what both communities said: the Jews said that Ezra is the son of God, and the Christians said that the Messiah is the son of God. Then it passes the Book's judgement on the claim: these are words out of their mouths, repeating what the disbelievers before them said, and the Book calls down condemnation on them for the way they have been turned away from the truth.

**What the Verse Records**

The Book records the claim as the groups made it, and it treats both claims as one thing: the use of the word son for a creature who stands under God. The chapter has already named the two communities in this passage and dealt with the beliefs they held, and the verse before this one answers the claim that God has taken a son by saying that He is far above that and that whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him and serves Him (9:30 is this verse, and the chapter's earlier verses on the same subject are at 9:29 and around it). The claim about Ezra was held by a part of the Jewish community of Madinah and those who came after them, and the verse records it as the Book records the claims it rejects: it states them and lets the argument stand on what God says about Himself.

**Why the Book Rejects the Language**

The Book refuses the word because it changes the distance between God and the created world. A son is a part of a family, and a family has members who depend on one another, and the Book says in the plainest of its sūrahs that God has no equal and that nobody is born from Him (112:3 states the whole matter in one line). The Book says elsewhere that those who were called sons were creatures who ate food and walked in the markets (the same sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān names the Messiah and his mother and says they both ate food at 5:75). The verse in front of the reader says that the words are only words that come out of mouths, which is the Book's way of saying that the claim has nothing behind it.

**The Sūrah's Instruction About Dealing With the People**

The chapter does not leave the community with a grudge; it gives it a method. The Book tells the community to argue with the People of the Book in the best way, and to mention what is common between the two sides: the belief in what was sent to the community and what was sent to them, and the fact that both sides serve one God (29:46 states the instruction, and the sūrah in front of the reader says the same thing when it tells the community that many of the men of the Book are honest and believe at 9:33's wider passage). The Book also tells the community to deal fairly with those who do not fight them or drive them out of their homes (60:8 states that rule). The verse in front of the reader names the claim it rejects, and the sūrah's wider instruction tells the reader how to live beside the people who made it.

**What the Chapter Does With the Claim**

The chapter goes on to speak about the men who take their scholars and their monks as lords beside God, and it brings the same charge: the claim grows when people hand to creatures what belongs to God (9:31 gives the third example). It then tells the community not to be like the men who took a scholar's word where God's word had already spoken. The sūrah in front of the reader keeps the two things together — the claim it rejects and the community's duty to live with the people of the claim in justice and mercy — which is how the whole passage is built. The report of the Prophet's words on the same claim is preserved in the collections, and Muslim carries the one in which he is described as saying that God curse the men who said such things.

---

## Verse 9:31
> They have taken their rabbis and monks as well as the Messiah, son of Mary, as lords besides Allah, even though they were commanded to worship none but One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. Glorified is He above what they associate ˹with Him˺

**Obedience Can Become Worship**

This verse describes people who took their rabbis, their monks and the Messiah as lords besides God. It does not mean that every Jew or Christian bowed down physically to a scholar. Its point is that obedience can become worship when a created authority is given the right to change what God has commanded.

The difference is exact. A teacher who is obeyed as a teacher remains a servant. An authority whose permission makes a forbidden thing lawful, and whose prohibition makes a lawful thing forbidden, has been given a share of divine authority. That is the line the verse is drawing.

**The Report That Explains It**

The report behind the verse comes from ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, who asked the Prophet ﷺ whether his people had actually worshipped their rabbis and monks. The answer was that they had made lawful what God had forbidden and forbidden what He had made lawful, and were followed in it, and that this was their worship.

The report fixes the meaning of the verse. It describes obedience taken to the level of legislating against God, and it puts the boundary at the point where a human ruling is treated as a divine one. The verse's closing phrase, that they were commanded to worship none but one God, gives the standard their practice had broken.

**Legitimate Authority Is Not Cancelled**

This does not remove human authority. Parents have rights, judges have duties, teachers and leaders have their place. The Qur'an instructs believers to obey those in authority in what is lawful, and to refer any dispute back to God and the Messenger (4:59 gives the instruction).

So the correction here is not anarchy, and it is not a command to despise scholars. It is the restoration of proper order, where every authority in the world sits below the Book and answers to it. A scholar who teaches within that order is performing a service. A scholar whose words are treated as making a thing lawful or unlawful on their own is being placed where only God belongs.

**The Prophets Themselves Taught the Same**

The verse names the Messiah in the same breath, and that is deliberate. Even a prophet does not hold legislative authority of his own. The Book says that prophets were commanded to teach the worship of the One who created them, and that they were not to be taken as lords besides God (3:79 and 3:80 state both).

That is why the verse puts the Messiah, the rabbis and the monks into one sentence. The error it corrects is not respect for a holy person. It is the habit of giving a person the right to define good and evil.

**What the Sūrah Is Doing in This Passage**

The sūrah is arguing with the People of the Book on the ground of their own scriptures. The verse after this one shows the shape of the argument. God says that these men invented their own teachings and that He sent His light with His messenger (9:32 states both). The passage is not a denunciation of learning or of lawful leadership. It is a correction of the one step that turns a legitimate authority into a rival, and the community facing the same temptation is warned by watching it happen to neighbours who had good books in their hands.

## Verse 9:32

> They wish to extinguish Allah’s light with their mouths, but Allah will only allow His light to be perfected, even to the dismay of the disbelievers

**Speech Cannot Extinguish Revelation**

Verse 32 pictures opponents trying to extinguish Allah's light with their mouths. The image is intentionally disproportionate. A mouth can produce denial, ridicule, propaganda, and false argument, but it cannot put out divine guidance. The verse does not deny that speech can harm people or delay acceptance. It denies that human speech can finally cancel what Allah has sent.

The light may refer to revelation, knowledge of Allah, the Qur'an, or the religion carried by the Messenger. These meanings overlap. Guidance illuminates the mind, revelation exposes the path, and faith gives life to what was earlier hidden. The opponents' mouths try to turn that light into darkness by calling truth false, suppressing it, or making people afraid to hear it. Their effort can create noise, but noise is not the same as extinction.

**Truth Survives Its Opponents**

The Qur'an's wider history confirms the verse's pattern without requiring every generation to repeat the same political shape. Empires, schools, and media campaigns have tried to define Islam out of public life, yet the message has continued to be recited, studied, and embodied in lands far from its first audience. That continuity is not a reason for complacency. It is a reason to trust Allah while accepting responsibility for honest transmission.

---

## Verse 9:33

> He is the One Who has sent His Messenger with ˹true˺ guidance and the religion of truth, making it prevail over all others, even to the dismay of the polytheists

**“Prevail” Has More Than One Dimension**

The Arabic expression translated making it prevail can indicate elevation, manifestation, victory, or making something known above competing systems. Exegetes differed over which sense is primary. Some connected it to the political spread of Islam in Arabia and beyond. Others emphasized the superiority of the proof and the public clarity of the religion. Some associated the fullest completion with the end of time.

These readings address different aspects of the same promise. A religion can prevail by becoming visible and intellectually established even while its political followers are weak. It can also gain political authority without every person accepting its truth. The verse's wording should not be reduced to a crude claim that historical Muslim states always represented the religion perfectly. The promise belongs to Allah's truth; the behavior of its followers remains accountable.

**The Qur’an Interprets the Promise Elsewhere**

A closely related passage says that Allah sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth so that He may make it prevail over every religion (61:9). The repetition shows that the verse is a major statement of prophetic mission, not a local boast. Other passages describe the Muslim community as a witness over humanity (2:143) and command it to uphold justice. Manifestation therefore includes proof, witness, and moral practice.

**What Prevail Means in a Plural World**

For modern believers, the verse does not authorize forcing belief or suppressing every other community. The Qur'an says there is no compulsion in religion (2:256), and verse 6 of this chapter protects a seeker who comes to listen and then returns safely. The religion can be manifest in a plural society through its truth, institutions, worship, ethics, and public witness without making every neighbor submit to its creed.

---

## Verse 9:34

> O believers! Indeed, many rabbis and monks consume people’s wealth wrongfully and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah. Give good news of a painful torment to those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in Allah’s cause

**Religious Authority Can Become Financial Exploitation**

Verse 34 warns believers about many rabbis and monks who consume people's wealth wrongfully and turn people away from Allah's way. The word many is important. The verse does not condemn every religious leader in the communities of scripture. It identifies a recurring abuse: sacred authority is used to collect wealth, protect privilege, or create dependence while the leader himself departs from the path he claims to represent.

The warning then turns to those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend in Allah's cause. The two groups are connected by misuse of wealth. Religious leaders may take money from people falsely; wealthy people may keep money from the purposes Allah has commanded. In both cases wealth ceases to be a trust and becomes an instrument of control. The verse speaks to leaders and donors together because corruption often needs both.

**Hoarding Blocks the Way Too**

The wealthy hoarder can also hinder Allah's way without speaking against religion. When essential resources are locked away, the poor are weakened, institutions cannot serve, and social resentment grows. The Qur'an treats spending in Allah's cause as part of the public life of faith. Wealth withheld from obligatory or necessary good becomes a barrier through which people cannot reach the support and dignity Allah intended.

---

The warning continues into the next verse, which describes what will be done with what the hoarders kept: the gold and silver will be heated in the fire of Hell and pressed to their foreheads, flanks and backs, with the words "this is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste what you used to hoard" (9:35). The Book states the principle positively in the same sūrah: the believers are those who give in ease and hardship (3:134), and the poor have a right in the wealth of the giver (70:24–25). What the verse adds here is the special gravity of it in teachers of religion, whose office gave them the trust of the poor.

## Verse 9:35

> The Day ˹will come˺ when their treasure will be heated up in the Fire of Hell, and their foreheads, sides, and backs branded with it. ˹It will be said to them,˺ “This is the treasure you hoarded for yourselves. Now taste what you hoarded!”

**The Hoarded Wealth Returns as Evidence**

Verse 35 describes the consequence of the hoarding condemned in the previous verse. Gold and silver are heated in the Fire and used to brand the very people who refused to spend them for Allah's cause. The image is morally exact: the treasure is not replaced by an unrelated punishment. The thing guarded as a source of safety becomes part of the exposure of the person's failure.

**The Hereafter Does Not Invent the Moral Meaning**

The same principle appears in the Qur'an's warning that those who withhold what Allah gave them will find it a collar around their necks (3:180). The object is transformed into a symbol of the person's refusal. The believer should not read these images as an invitation to envy. They are an invitation to freedom. Money can be useful without becoming the thing that defines the owner.

**Spending Is a Form of Purification**

The previous verse linked spending with Allah's cause. Giving away wealth does not merely help the recipient. It purifies the giver from the illusion of self-sufficiency. Every act of charity says that the future does not depend completely on the amount held in one's hand. This is why the Qur'an praises spending repeatedly and describes it as a loan to Allah that He multiplies (57:11).

**What the Verse Adds to the One Before It**

The verse before it describes the men who hoard gold and silver and do not spend them in the path of God, and it gives them the news of a painful punishment (9:34). The verse in front of us takes the picture and completes it: on the day when the metal is heated in the fire of Hell and the men's foreheads and sides and backs are branded with it, and the sentence spoken over them is that this is what they hoarded for themselves, so taste what you used to hoard. The two verses are read together in every book of law that deals with the zakāt, because the offence named is the withholding of the due, and the punishment is drawn from the object itself.
**What the Commentators Explained**

The older commentators explained the brand on the three places as the parts of a person that were used to gather and keep: the forehead for the face a man showed, the side for the direction he turned, the back for what he carried. The Book states the same principle in the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, where the men who stored up what God gave them are warned that their gold will be a collar around their necks on the Day (3:180). The images belong together, and the legal ruling the sūrah draws is that a person's wealth is a trust with a claim on it.
**The Context of the Passage**

The verse stands inside the sūrah's long address about the campaign and the spending, and it follows the passage about the scholars of the earlier communities who take money for their words (9:34). The Book puts the two errors in the same paragraph: the men who sell the religion for money and the men who lock the money away from the religion, and it names the punishment of both with the same picture, which is the wealth itself turned into a brand.

## Verse 9:36

> Indeed, the number of months ordained by Allah is twelve—in Allah’s Record since the day He created the heavens and the earth—of which four are sacred. That is the Right Way. So do not wrong one another during these months. And together fight the polytheists as they fight against you together. And know that Allah is with those mindful ˹of Him˺

**Time Belongs to Allah Before It Belongs to People**

Verse 36 establishes the calendar as part of Allah's created order. There are twelve months in Allah's Record from the day He created the heavens and the earth. The statement is not only a calculation for scheduling. It says that time has an order given by the Creator, and human communities must not rearrange sacred distinctions to serve their desires.

The four sacred months are traditionally identified as Dhū al-Qaʿdah, Dhū al-Ḥijjah, Muḥarram, and Rajab. Three are consecutive and one is separated. The arrangement was known in Arabian practice, though its meaning had been manipulated. The verse restores the months to the order Allah appointed. Sacred time creates a pause in which violence, injustice, and reckless desire are restrained. It is a public reminder that even conflict must not consume every season.

**A Calendar for the Inner Life**

For modern believers, verse 36 invites attention to the moral use of time. Sacred seasons can interrupt habits and create space for worship, reconciliation, and restraint. The believer should not treat Ramadan, the pilgrimage months, Fridays, or other appointed times as cultural decorations. They train the soul by giving it rhythms that do not come from the market.

---

The Prophet ﷺ stated the same lesson in his sermon at the farewell pilgrimage: time had come back to its original state as it was on the day God created the heavens and the earth, with twelve months, four of them sacred — Dhū al-Qaʿdah, Dhū al-Ḥijjah, Muḥarram and Rajab. The Book had already protected those months in the rules of fighting: the sacred month is not to be violated in it (2:194), and the sūrah of the Cow names the prohibition on fighting in them (2:217). The closing words of this verse, that fighting is prescribed as a whole, are the answer to the penchant for observing one month's sanctity while neglecting the wider law — the reckoning is with God, who created the year and set the months.

**The Postponement the Verse Sets Up to Condemn**

The next verse of the sūrah condemns a practice that had grown up around the four sacred months: the postponement of a month, in which a tribe moved the sanctity of one month onto another when fighting suited them, and the Book calls that an addition to denial (9:37). The Arabs kept the four names but shifted the dates. The result was a thing the verse in front of us closes off: a calendar with a month marked as God's in the text and a different month, chosen by men, treated as sacred in practice. The sentence that the months belong to God who created the heavens and the earth is therefore not a lesson in arithmetic. It states that the order of the year is given with the order of the world, and that whoever rearranges the sacred seasons to suit the fighting season has taken a decision about time that belongs to its Maker.

**Wronging One Another in the Sacred Months**

The command not to wrong one another during these months was read by the commentators in two ways. One reading holds the sacred months above all from bloodshed, so that a war started in them is a graver injustice than the same war started outside them, and the sūrah of the Cow treats the violation of the month as serious while insisting that the greater wrong is to block the way to God and drive people from the Sacred House (2:217). The other reading treats the sentence as a general instruction: the months of God carry a heavier weight for acts of injustice done to people, so the man who has wronged his brother should settle it before the season passes. The jurists differed on whether fighting in the four months remains forbidden at all: most held that the ban was lifted by the commands to fight when the enemy attacks (2:194, and 9:5 read with this verse), while others kept the sanctity standing, since nothing in the text revokes it. What the verse does not allow any reader to do is to keep the calendar's sanctity and neglect its law. The message the Farewell Sermon pressed on the same months is the one this verse states: the sanctity of God's months, like the sanctity of life, blood and property, was given for the protection of people, and it is broken by wronging them, whether or not a weapon is drawn.

## Verse 9:37

> Reallocating the sanctity of ˹these˺ months is an increase in disbelief, by which the disbelievers are led ˹far˺ astray. They adjust the sanctity one year and uphold it in another, only to maintain the number of months sanctified by Allah, violating the very months Allah has made sacred. Their evil deeds have been made appealing to them. And Allah does not guide the disbelieving people

**Nasīʾ Preserves the Form and Destroys the Rule**

Verse 37 names the manipulation of sacred months as nasīʾ and calls it an increase in disbelief. The exact historical form of the practice was debated. It can mean postponing a sacred month, or adding time to keep the lunar calendar aligned with the seasons. The dominant interpretive discussion focuses on moving the sacred status from one month to another so that the number four was preserved while the restriction itself could be avoided.

The moral problem is clear in either case. A community keeps the outward number demanded by revelation while changing the actual burden of obedience. It can then say that it honors the command even as it chooses when the command will apply. The verse exposes this as more than a calendar error. It is a refusal to let Allah's order govern human interest.

**Legal Appearance Can Hide Moral Rebellion**

The verse says they make a month lawful one year and sacred another. This is a picture of rules being moved whenever they interfere with political or economic plans. A month that should restrain fighting becomes available for attack; a month that should protect travel becomes convenient for settling a score. The community can preserve the language of sanctity while turning sanctity into a tool it controls.

---

The practice the verse condemns is *nasīʾ*, postponing the sanctity of a month to another month of the same year, which allowed a tribe to keep fighting through the season it had declared sacred and to declare a different month sacred in its place. The reports in the sīrah trace the custom to ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy, who introduced it among the Arabs, and the Qur'an's objection is that it keeps the form of the sacred month while destroying its value: the practice is made to look respectable while the number is juggled to suit the fighting season. The verse ends by stating what the disbelievers' deeds have cost them and that God does not guide a people who deny — the same standard applied to manipulating the calendar as to rejecting the revelation itself.

**What the Practice Was**

The verse names the practice of postponing the sacred months and calls it an increase in disbelief, by which the men who disbelieved were led astray: they make it lawful one year and unlawful another, so as to match the number God made sacred while making lawful what God had forbidden. The books of history describe the practice of the men who kept count of the year by the moon and shifted the months to fit their wars and their markets, and the Book condemns the manipulation rather than the calendar. The passage is one of the sūrah's clearest statements about the difference between keeping a form and keeping the law.
**What the Verse Teaches About Law and Form**

The Book's sentence about matching the number while changing the rule is the whole of the argument, and it applies to any community that keeps the appearances of a rule while rearranging its content. The Prophet's farewell sermon named the sanctity of the months and the inviolability of the lands and the blood as things that would not be disturbed, and the reports put the sanctity of the four months at the head of the arrangements the community inherited. The verse in front of us states the principle in one line, and the jurists used it in the chapters on the religious practices whose timing cannot be moved.
**Where the Passage Goes**

The verse follows the passage on the four months and the treaties, and it precedes the commands about fighting in the sacred month and the sanctity of the sanctuary (9:36 in the same passage). The sūrah's argument has been that the community's arrangements with God cannot be used as instruments, and the practice of the men of the ignorance is given as the example of the thing the community is forbidden to do. The passage ends by saying that God does not guide the men who disbelieve, and the sūrah then turns to the campaign.

## Verse 9:38
> O believers! What is the matter with you that when you are asked to march forth in the cause of Allah, you cling firmly to ˹your˺ land? Do you prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter? The enjoyment of this worldly life is insignificant compared to that of the Hereafter

**The Body Can Cling Before the Mind Admits It**

Verse 38 asks what has happened to believers who are called to march in Allah's cause and cling heavily to the land. The image is physical: a person who should rise remains pressed down, as if the ground has become a weight. The body exposes an attachment the tongue may still deny. They may say they believe in sacrifice, yet the summons reveals that comfort has become their true master.

The immediate setting is the expedition to Tabūk, with its heat, distance, cost, and fear of the Byzantine frontier. The command was not a demand that every person ignore genuine incapacity. Later verses exempt the weak, sick, and those without means. Verse 38 addresses those capable of responding who prefer the settled life because the march threatens their ease. The distinction between inability and reluctance is central.

**The Command Has Legitimate Exceptions**

This distinction is useful in every religious duty. A person must separate genuine limitation from reluctance dressed as limitation. Illness may require rest; fear may require counsel; poverty may restrict a person's contribution. But a person with strength, wealth, and opportunity who repeatedly chooses ease over a clear duty is hearing verse 38. Allah judges the actual capacity and intention of each person, not a comparison with someone else's capacity.

---

The expedition in view is Tabūk, in Rajab of the ninth year, when the community was called out at the worst time for it: the heat at its height, the harvest ripe, and the distance long. The verses that follow draw the portrait of the men who stayed: they rejoiced at remaining behind (9:81), hated to strive with their wealth and lives (9:81), and came with excuses (9:90).

The comparison the verse makes is between the goods of this life and the goods of the next, and the Book states the same scale elsewhere: what is with God is better and more lasting (42:36), and the life of this world is no more than a passing enjoyment (57:20).

---
## Verse 9:39

> If you do not march forth, He will afflict you with a painful torment and replace you with other people. You are not harming Him in the least. And Allah is Most Capable of everything

**Refusal Does Not Weaken Allah**

Verse 39 answers the hesitation exposed in the previous verse. If the believers refuse to march when the duty applies to them, Allah can punish them and replace them with another people. The threat is not that Allah's mission will fail because one generation refuses service. The threat is that the generation itself will lose the honor and be removed from the work.

This is a recurring Qur'anic principle. Allah is never dependent on a particular group of servants. People need the opportunity to obey; Allah does not need their obedience to remain Lord. The warning protects believers from imagining that religious identity guarantees permanent favor. A community can inherit a noble mission and then abandon it through comfort. Allah can raise others who are more willing.

**Replacement Is a Warning to Institutions**

A mosque, charity, school, or movement can also become attached to the belief that its current leaders are irreplaceable. Verse 39 challenges that belief. If a group stops serving truth and begins protecting its own comfort, Allah can bring forward people with fewer resources but greater sincerity. Numbers and history cannot force Allah to keep a trust in the hands of those who abuse it.

---

The warning that God will replace them is repeated in the sūrah of the Table: if the community turns back from its religion, God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him, humble toward the believers and firm toward those who deny (5:54). The same sentence in this verse protects the two sides of the matter: the refusal harms no one but the refuser, and it does not diminish God in the least, who has power over all things. The chapter's later verses show the promise kept in the other direction too: the community that did march was given the victory and the spoils (9:25–27).

## Verse 9:40

> ˹It does not matter˺ if you ˹believers˺ do not support him, for Allah did in fact support him when the disbelievers drove him out ˹of Mecca˺ and he was only one of two. While they both were in the cave, he reassured his companion, “Do not worry; Allah is certainly with us.” So Allah sent down His serenity upon the Prophet, supported him with forces you ˹believers˺ did not see, and made the word of the disbelievers lowest, while the Word of Allah is supreme. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise

**The Messenger’s Mission Does Not Depend on Us**

Verse 40 answers the people who hesitate to support the Messenger by recalling a moment when human support seemed almost absent. If the believers do not help him, Allah has already helped him. The statement is not permission to abandon the Messenger. It is a correction to the illusion that Allah's plan needs a particular group to keep it alive. The community is honored by supporting the Messenger, but the Messenger's Lord is not dependent on the community.

**The Two Words Are Put in Opposition**

The verse contrasts the word of the disbelievers with the Word of Allah. The first is lowered; the second is supreme. The word of the disbelievers can mean their plan, claim, or intention to extinguish the Messenger's mission. The Word of Allah is His decree, promise, and declaration of truth. The contrast is not simply between two speeches. It is between a human plan limited by time and a divine command that controls the final outcome.

This does not mean that every human statement from a Muslim is automatically the Word of Allah. The distinction belongs to revelation and divine decree. Believers serve that Word by speaking truthfully and living by it, but they must not confuse their opinions with Allah's authority. The humility of the cave belongs beside the confidence of the promise.

---

The scene is the cave of Thawr, where the Prophet ﷺ and Abū Bakr sheltered on the migration, and the words the verse preserves — "do not grieve, indeed Allah is with us" — are given in the report of Abū Bakr himself, who described his fear when the pursuers' feet appeared above them and received that answer. The verse then states the promise fulfilled: God sent down His tranquillity on him, supported him with forces unseen, and made the word of the disbelievers the lowest. The report is in both Ṣaḥīḥ collections, and the episode shaped the community's memory of Abū Bakr, who is the second of the two the verse names.

**The Hour in the Cave**

The verse reaches back to the migration and names the event that the whole sūrah is built on. The Prophet left Makkah with one companion, and the two of them spent three nights in the cave of Thawr while the search went past the mouth of it. The Book records the state he was in: he was saying to his companion, do not grieve, God is with us. Bukhārī preserves the exchange in Abū Bakr's own words, including his answer that he feared for the Prophet and not for himself. The Book then names what came down on the two men: a quietness from God, and troops the eye could not see. The sūrah of the Cow uses the same word for the quietness that settled on the believers in the day of Ḥunayn, where the numbers had not helped them (9:26).
**The Merit of the Companion**

The verse calls Abū Bakr the second of the two, and the phrase became one of the proofs the scholars of the community used for his standing. A man who is named in the Book as the companion of the messenger in the cave has been given a rank that later events did not add to. The verse's own purpose, though, is not to honour a man. It is to answer the community in Madinah who were being told to carry the burden of the campaign, and it does so by pointing at what God had already done without an army.
**The Troops Nobody Saw**

The Book says that God supported the messenger with soldiers that were not visible, and the commentators read them as the angels who came down at Badr, where the Qur'an names a thousand and then three thousand (3:123-125). The lesson is placed at the end of the verse: God is mighty and wise. What the community is being told is that the outcome of the whole matter had been settled in a cave in the dark, by two men and a promise, and that the men who were now being asked to fund a campaign were being asked to join something already decided.

## Verse 9:41

> ˹O believers!˺ March forth whether it is easy or difficult for you, and strive with your wealth and your lives in the cause of Allah. That is best for you, if only you knew

**The Command Does Not Wait for Perfect Conditions**

This verse removes the excuse that obedience must wait until circumstances become comfortable. Easy and difficult cover more than two moods. They include abundance and shortage, youth and age, good equipment and poor equipment, eagerness and reluctance. The command reaches the believer in both states because the value of an obligation is not decided by convenience. A person who serves only when the road is smooth has not yet learned whether he serves Allah or his own comfort.
That distinction matters. A wealthy person is not permitted to imitate poverty when wealth is what the cause needs.
**The Better Choice May Be Hidden at First**

The Qur'an uses the same moral pattern when it teaches that a disliked thing may contain good and a beloved thing may contain harm (2:216). This verse does not make every dangerous choice virtuous. It places action inside knowledge of Allah's command and the servant's ability. Courage is obedience with judgment, not a desire to prove fearlessness.

**What the Verse Does Not Excuse**

The command is to go out, and the phrase *light or heavy* — *khifāfan wa-thiqālan* — is broad enough to cover every condition the commentators listed: young and old, rich and poor, mounted and on foot, eager and reluctant, in health and in difficulty. The verse is placed at the head of a passage about the expedition to Tabūk, a march to the far north in the heat of summer at the end of a drought, when the harvest was standing and the journey was long. It removes, in advance, the two excuses the community used: the season and the distance. It does not remove the ones the Book itself grants elsewhere — the sick, the weak, and those who cannot find the means are exempted a few chapters into the same sūrah (9:91), which is where the mercy of the law is stated. The verse is a claim on the able, not on the incapacitated.
**What Is Offered in Place of Comfort**

The reason the verse gives is not victory: it is that striving with wealth and life is the better bargain, *if they only knew*. The same argument runs through the sūrah — the trade the believers are invited into, the gardens named at the end of the sūrah for those who spend and fight — and it is pitched against the visible arithmetic a man does when he weighs a hot road against a standing crop. The book elsewhere states the same principle for the man who finds it hard: a thing may be hateful to you while it holds your good, and a thing you love may hold your harm (2:216). The verse asks a person to trust the account over his own read of it.

## Verse 9:42

> Had the gain been within reach and the journey shorter, they would have followed you, but the distance seemed too long for them. And they will swear by Allah, “Had we been able, we would have certainly joined you.” They are ruining themselves. And Allah knows that they are surely lying

**A Short Profit Would Have Exposed a Long Weakness**

The verse identifies the hidden condition behind their obedience. If the expedition had promised an easy and nearby gain, they would have joined. Their problem was not lack of movement but the absence of a reward that could be quickly seen. The phrase “within reach” points to a fleeting worldly advantage, while the long journey represents a cost whose return cannot be guaranteed in the present.

**They Destroy Themselves Before Anyone Else Can**

The judgment “they are ruining themselves” changes the focus from social embarrassment to spiritual damage. Their lie may persuade some listeners for a time, but it cannot protect the soul from the effects of choosing falsehood. Each excuse makes the next excuse easier. The heart learns to defend the decision rather than to repent of it, and eventually a person can no longer distinguish fear from principle.

Self-destruction here is gradual. It includes the loss of truthfulness, the weakening of trust, and the habit of treating divine commands as negotiable whenever they are costly. A community may lose the immediate benefit of one person's service, but the liar loses something deeper: the ability to meet his own weakness honestly. Repentance begins when that inner defense is abandoned.

---

The expedition's difficulty is named in the verses around this one: the distance (9:42), the heat and lack of provision (9:81), and the ripening harvest that kept men at home (9:86). The verse's test is the one the Book applies to sincerity throughout: whether a person follows when the cost is low or only when the gain is near. The same measure appears in the sūrah of the Cow in the command to strive even when it is disliked (2:216), and the Prophet ﷺ stated the standard plainly in speaking of the one whose migration is for God and His Messenger rather than for gain or marriage, the reward following the intention.

## Verse 9:43

> May Allah pardon you ˹O Prophet˺! Why did you give them permission ˹to stay behind˺ before those who told the truth were distinguished from those who were lying

**Pardon Opens a Question of Judgment**

The verse begins with pardon before it asks the question. That order is part of its mercy and part of its instruction. The Prophet is not addressed as a rejected leader whose whole decision was condemned. He is addressed with divine forgiveness while being taught that permission had been granted before the sincerity of the applicants became visible. Correction can therefore be direct without becoming humiliation.

The opening also protects the community from a crude reading of leadership. A sound leader may make a choice based on the information available and later receive a better direction. The existence of correction does not prove corruption. It proves that human judgment remains human, even when exercised by the best of people. Divine guidance refines the decision and teaches those who follow how to respond to correction with respect.

**Permission Allowed Motives to Remain Hidden**

The central issue is not that everyone who stayed behind was exposed as a liar. The issue is that permission was given before the truthful and the false had been distinguished. A request may sound reasonable, and granting it may seem compassionate, yet the request itself can hide a heart's real relation to duty. Action would have disclosed what words concealed.

---

## Verse 9:44
> Those who believe in Allah and the Last Day do not ask for exemption from striving with their wealth and their lives. And Allah has perfect knowledge of those who are mindful ˹of Him˺

**Faith Makes Duty Worth More Than Comfort**

The verse describes a settled response from believers: they do not ask to be excused from striving with their wealth and their lives. This is not a claim that every believer feels no fear or has endless strength. It identifies the direction of the heart.

Faith in God gives the command its authority, and faith in the last day gives sacrifice its horizon. A cost that looks final in this world is not final in the account of what comes after it. The verse is placed in a sūrah that has just been listing the excuses people offered, so it is describing the opposite group by their one defining quality. They hear the call and they do not look for a way out.

**What the Pair of Wealth and Life Covers**

The Book uses wealth and life together as the fullest description of sacrifice, because between them they cover everything a person has. Wealth means funding the expedition, feeding the poor, equipping the fighters and keeping the community standing. Life means going out yourself and putting your body where the risk is. The pair also appears in the promises made to this group in the same sūrah: the highest rank with God, and mercy, pleasure and gardens of lasting bliss (9:20 and 9:21 give the reward).

**The Last Day Corrects the Short View**

Without the last day, sacrifice can look like a bad bargain. A believer may lose money, safety and standing and receive nothing visible in return. Faith changes the calculation without turning obedience into a business deal. God is not in debt to anyone, and nothing done for His sake is lost.

That certainty also keeps a believer from measuring success by visible victory alone. A sincere effort may fail in its immediate aim and still be accepted, since what God weighs includes the intention, the endurance and the service nobody saw. The same standard cuts the other way as well. Cowardice that looked profitable at the time is weighed in the same account, and the Book keeps the day of account in front of its readers for exactly this reason.

**The Contrast the Verse Draws**

The verses around this one describe the people who came asking for permission to stay behind. The sūrah records them asking that they be allowed to remain with those who sat at home, and it says that their hearts were sealed by what they had earned, so that they did not understand (9:87 states both). Then it says that the Messenger and the believers with him strive with their wealth and their lives, and that they are the ones who will have all the best (9:88 gives it, and this verse states the principle behind that company).

So the verse is not describing an unusual level of devotion reserved for a few. It is naming the ordinary response of people who believe in the last day, set beside the response of people who do not, and it leaves the reader to decide which way of calculating he is using when a cost comes due.

---
## Verse 9:45

> No one would ask for exemption except those who have no faith in Allah or the Last Day, and whose hearts are in doubt, so they are torn by their doubts

**The Excuse Reveals a Deeper Absence**

This verse explains the pattern identified around it. The problem is not a single request for relief considered in isolation. The problem is the heart that uses relief to avoid every costly demand. Such a person has no settled faith in Allah's authority and no firm expectation of meeting Him. When those two convictions weaken, immediate comfort becomes the strongest power in the decision.

The statement must be read with the exemptions Allah grants to the genuinely unable. Scripture does not condemn illness, incapacity, or necessary care. It diagnoses a settled evasiveness that disguises itself as prudence. A believer may ask what duty remains when circumstances change. The doubtful person asks how to escape the duty while preserving an appearance of loyalty.

**Doubt Tears the Heart in Two Directions**

This inner division produces unstable speech. The tongue offers an oath, the body avoids preparation, and the mind invents a reason after the decision has already been made. Such a person may think he is preserving options, but he is losing integrity. A heart cannot remain permanently neutral when truth requires a response. Avoidance is itself a choice, and repeated avoidance shapes the soul.

---

The doubt the verse names is not the honest uncertainty of someone seeking an answer but the condition the Book calls being "torn" — thrown back and forth with no direction. The same diagnosis is given of the waverers elsewhere: their hearts are sick (2:10), and they are of two minds, not wholly with the believers nor wholly with their people (4:143). What the verse denies them is the excuse of hardship: the exemption they seek is the visible sign of a heart that has not settled on the duty, which is why only the waverers ask for it and the steadfast do not.

## Verse 9:46

> Had they ˹really˺ intended to march forth, they would have made preparations for it. But Allah disliked that they should go, so He let them lag behind, and it was said ˹to them˺, “Stay with those ˹helpless˺ who remain behind.”

**Divine Holding Back Works Through Human Choices**

Allah's dislike that they should go does not mean they were innocent victims of an arbitrary force. The passage describes them as hypocrites whose own reluctance and corruption made their presence harmful. They were held back through the very choices by which they held themselves back. Divine decree and human responsibility meet here: Allah allowed the hidden character to become an outward separation.

This protects two truths. Nothing escapes Allah's will, and no sinner can use that will as an excuse. The person cannot say, “I was prevented, therefore I bear no blame,” when his lack of preparation arose from his own refusal. At the same time, the believers do not imagine that their safety depended on their ability to control every person's intention. Allah can protect a community by excluding a danger it does not yet recognize.

**The Sentence Turns an Excuse Into a Verdict**

“Stay with those who remain behind” can be heard as a permission, but in this setting it becomes exposure. The people who wanted exemption receive the position they preferred, yet the position no longer carries the honor they sought. They are not counted among those who sacrifice; they are placed with those whose absence has a different meaning.

---

The preparation the verse says they would have made is the ordinary work of going out: provision, mounts, arrangements. Their failure to make any of it is the outward evidence of an inner refusal. The statement that God disliked their setting out is stated in the same breath as their own reluctance, which the commentators read as one event seen from two sides: God's knowledge of what they would have brought with them — the sūrah says elsewhere that had they gone out with the army they would have added nothing but trouble (9:47) — and their own decision to stay. The Book works the same way in the sūrah of the Cow, where the disbelievers are turned back by what their hands earned (2:10).

## Verse 9:47

> Had they gone forth with you ˹believers˺, they would have been nothing but trouble for you, and would have scrambled around, seeking to spread discord in your midst. And some of you would have eagerly listened to them. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers

**Presence Is Not Always Support**

The verse corrects the assumption that a larger number automatically strengthens a cause. These people would have gone with the believers, but their presence would have produced trouble rather than help. Khubāl includes confusion, weakness, disquiet, and the disruption of unity. A person can stand inside a formation while working against its purpose.

**Loyalty Includes the Discipline of Verification**

The practical cure for discord is careful verification. Before passing on a report, ask who spoke, what was actually said, and what action the claim is meant to provoke. Before forwarding a grievance, ask whether it is complete and whether the absent person has a right to answer. These habits are not bureaucratic decoration. They protect hearts from being moved by falsehood.

The Qur'an's wider teaching about verifying news from an unreliable person (49:6) gives this verse a clear application. A believer does not become loyal by believing every message that flatters his resentment. He becomes loyal by seeking what Allah approves, even when the truth is less exciting than the rumour.

**What Their Going Would Have Cost**

The verse says that the men would have added nothing but confusion, and the word used, *khubāl*, is the word for a thing that eats away from inside. The next verse states what they had already been doing before this: they had been spreading trouble and turning things upside down from the moment the community arrived, until the truth came and God's command was made clear (9:48). The sūrah is therefore not describing a first offence. The court of Madinah had watched a group work on the community's nerves for years, and the report of the campaign at Tabūk is the moment the Book names the mechanism: it is not the enemy outside that such men damage, it is the march, the supply, the line, the confidence.
**The Ruling About Screening**

The practical instruction that the community drew from the passage belongs to leadership in general: a mission that requires trust should not be handed to people whose record shows they will use it against its purpose. The Book's own terms are exact — a person can stand inside a formation while working on its fractures — and the sūrah later deals with the men who made excuses, the men who stayed behind, and the men who built a mosque as a base for the division of the believers (9:107). The verse in front of us states the principle the whole passage applies, and the words the Prophet is given elsewhere — that he should take counsel and then trust God (3:159) — sit beside it without cancelling it.
**The Verification the Book Teaches**

The Sūrah of the Private Apartments gives the general rule about news, instructing the believers to verify what a trouble-maker brings them before they act on it (49:6), and the Sūrah of the Night Journey treats the ear the eye and the heart as things a person will be asked about (17:36). The verse in front of us was given for a specific group in a specific campaign, and the habit it teaches holds anywhere: the report that arrives with a name attached, the grievance that has not been checked, the claim about an absent man who has not been given room to answer. The sūrah puts the rule inside a war chapter because that is where an unchecked rumour costs lives.

---

## Verse 9:48

> They had already sought to spread discord before and devised every ˹possible˺ plot against you ˹O Prophet˺, until the truth came and Allah’s Will prevailed—much to their dismay

**The Present Plot Has a History**

The verse refuses to treat the troublemakers' conduct as a sudden accident. They had sought discord before and had considered plots against the Prophet. A repeated pattern gives meaning to a present act. This does not mean that every past error proves permanent guilt, but it does mean that serious decisions should include a person's history of loyalty, repair, and truthfulness.

People often judge an incident as if it stands alone because a single event is easier to discuss than a pattern. The Qur'an looks at continuity. The earlier tries to weaken the community explain why their later presence would have been dangerous. Memory, used justly, is a form of wisdom. It helps a leader distinguish an accidental failure from a settled method of harm.

**Discord Is a Goal, Not Merely a Side Effect**

Some conflict arises from confusion or honest disagreement. This verse describes something more deliberate: seeking discord. The aim is to make the community less able to recognize truth, trust one another, and act together. A plot may use competing loyalties, private promises, or selective information. Its success is measured by what it does to relationships.

---

The earlier discord the verse refers to belongs to the years before Tabūk, when the hypocrites worked against the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah — the reports mention their role in the affair of the slander, their withdrawal at Uḥud, and their attempts to break the alliances around the city. The phrase "until the truth came and the command of Allah prevailed" frames the whole pattern: their plots did not stop the mission, and what is described here is not a new conspiracy but the same conduct in a new setting, which is why the verses that follow describe the mosque they built to divide the believers (9:107).

## Verse 9:49

> There are some of them who say, “Exempt me and do not expose me to temptation.” They have already fallen into temptation. And Hell will surely engulf the disbelievers

**“They Have Already Fallen” Is a Severe Diagnosis**

The statement is brief because the contradiction is decisive. They ask not to be tested, but the asking has become their test. They fear the hardship of obedience more than the moral damage of disobedience. Their plea therefore does not postpone temptation; it proves that temptation has already gained influence over them.

**The Honest Way Through Fear**

Fear should be brought into truthful speech. A person may say that he is weak, ask what he can do, and accept guidance about a lawful limit. He may not manufacture a pious story to conceal that he simply dislikes sacrifice. Honesty preserves the possibility of repentance because it names the actual disease.

This is why the servant should test a claimed fear by its fruits. Does it lead to a lawful alternative, a request for guidance, and greater honesty? Or does it lead to secrecy, resentment, and relief that others will carry the burden? The first may be caution; the second is the temptation already at work. No one should rush to pronounce on another person's unseen state, but everyone can examine the pattern of his own choices. Allah's warning is an invitation to stop defending the excuse and to return before the heart becomes comfortable calling disobedience protection.

The safest response is to name the weakness before it becomes a creed. Allah can turn a honest admission into repentance, but a polished excuse only gives the weakness more authority.

---

## Verse 9:50

> If a blessing befalls you ˹O Prophet˺, they grieve, but if a disaster befalls you, they say, “We took our precaution in advance,” and turn away, rejoicing

**Victory Can Be a Trial for the Opponent**

The blessing is often understood as victory, gain, or good fortune for the believers. Instead of receiving it as a shared good, the hypocrites experience it as a personal loss. This shows how envy changes the moral meaning of another person's success. The blessing is no longer seen as justice or mercy; it becomes an insult to the ego that wanted the other side diminished.

**Disaster Gives Cowardice a False Certificate**

When harm reaches the Prophet, they say they had taken precautions in advance. Their statement turns disaster into evidence of their own wisdom. Because they refused to join, they can pretend that the loss proves their decision was intelligent rather than disloyal. Hindsight becomes a tool for avoiding repentance.

Precaution itself is not condemned. The believer plans, protects lives, and learns from danger. The falsehood lies in claiming that every bad outcome validates the person who stayed away. A wise plan can fail, and a fearful refusal can appear fortunate by accident. Outcomes alone cannot tell the moral quality of the original choice. Intention, means, and obedience remain part of the account.

---

## Verse 9:51

> Say, “Nothing will ever befall us except what Allah has destined for us. He is our Protector.” So in Allah let the believers put their trust

**What Is Written Is Known to Allah**

The Arabic idea behind “destined” is connected with what Allah has prescribed or written. Muslim scholars have related this to the divine record and to Allah's knowledge beyond time. The point of the verse is not to invite speculation about the unseen record. It is to establish that events are not random accidents escaping the Lord's attention.

**A Public Reply to Private Anxiety**

His confidence is not arrogance toward danger. It is loyalty to Allah. He takes wise precautions, refuses cowardice, and accepts that victory and loss are both within the Lord's knowledge. When believers place their trust in Allah together, fear loses its power to scatter them. They can act with care because the Protector is greater than the threat.

The teaching about decree also protects the believer from post-event worship of his own decisions. He may have planned well and succeeded, yet he knows that success was not produced by his intelligence alone. He may have failed despite care, yet he knows that failure does not prove that Allah abandoned him. He reviews the means, learns the lesson, and then returns to reliance. This balance is especially important after public loss, when people search for someone to blame. Accountability for mistakes remains real, but blame does not become a rival to trust. Allah is the Protector before the event, during it, and after it.

Trust is therefore a discipline practiced before the result, not a phrase spoken after it. The servant works, prays, and accepts that Allah's wisdom surrounds both effort and outcome.

---

## Verse 9:52

> Say, “Are you awaiting anything to befall us except one of the two best things: ˹victory or martyrdom˺? But We are awaiting Allah to afflict you with torment either from Him or at our hands. So keep waiting! We too are waiting with you.”

**Courage Is Not a Desire for Death**

A careless reading could turn the verse into a celebration of danger for its own sake. Its balance lies in the phrase “the two best things,” not in a command to seek death. The believer seeks Allah's cause, uses wisdom, protects life where protection is required, and accepts martyrdom only if it comes through faithful action. He does not manufacture peril to prove his courage.

**The Opponent Waits for a Different Judgment**

The reply then turns the expectation back on the opponents. While the believers await either victory or a blessed death, they await punishment from Allah or through the believers' hands. “By our hands” does not mean that believers become independent agents of divine anger. It means that consequences may arrive in this world through the lawful defeat of wrongdoing.

**The Answer Is Firm Without Being Frantic**

The command “keep waiting” carries a calm challenge. The believers do not chase every rumor or beg their enemies to understand. They state the moral reality and continue. Firm speech is appropriate when false confidence is being used to intimidate the sincere.

---

The two outcomes are named in the same way in the sūrah of Āl ʿImrān, where the believers are asked whether they expected to enter Paradise without the like of what came to those before them, and are told that their petitions were answered with forgiveness and a good end (3:193–195); the Book states the same two goods in the promise of the purchased bargain, that the believers fight and are killed and theirs is the Garden (9:111). The verse's balance is in the phrase itself: the believer prepares for one of the two, and the courage it calls for is confidence in the outcome rather than a wish for death. The Book forbids the throwing of oneself into destruction (2:195) and permits the shortening of prayer and the breaking of the fast in hardship.

## Verse 9:53

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹Whether you˺ donate willingly or unwillingly, it will never be accepted from you, for you have been a rebellious people.”

**Charity Begins With Allegiance**

The verse does not reduce Islam to a private feeling. Faith produces obligations toward people, including the proper use of wealth. But charity cannot be separated from allegiance to Allah. When belief is present, giving becomes one expression of a life already directed to Him. When belief is rejected, giving may remain socially useful but loses the character of accepted worship.

The honest question is therefore larger than, “How much did I give?” It is, “What does my wealth say about what I serve?” A gift that follows repentance, prayer, and justice is a sign of return. A gift that hides rebellion is only a covering, and no covering can conceal the heart from Allah.

The donor must also remember that public usefulness does not erase private rebellion. Good effects should be acknowledged, but worship requires the heart to return to Allah rather than merely improve its image among people.

---

## Verse 9:54

> And what prevented their donations from being accepted is that they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger, they never come to prayer except half-heartedly, and they never donate except resentfully

**Repair Begins at the Root**

A person who recognizes this pattern should not begin by polishing the public gift while ignoring faith and prayer. He begins with repentance and renewed certainty. He learns the meaning of the prayer, protects its times, and asks Allah to remove the bitterness that makes giving feel like loss. As worship becomes real, charity can become an expression rather than a performance.

The process may be gradual, but it must be honest. A servant can say, “I am struggling,” without saying, “My struggle is the standard.” He can give a small amount with a sincere heart, then build discipline. Acceptance belongs to Allah, yet the path toward acceptance is visible: belief, prayer, generosity, and a heart that wants obedience rather than merely its reputation.

The three defects reinforce one another. Weak faith makes prayer feel burdensome; neglected prayer makes the heart less ready to surrender; resentment toward giving then appears natural because the servant has forgotten that all provision comes from Allah. Repair must therefore be connected. Renew belief, protect the prayer, and give in a way that trains gratitude. The servant may begin with a hidden act so that people cannot reward the performance. He may then increase the act as sincerity grows. Allah does not need the display, but the servant needs the discipline that turns display into worship.

---

The verse states its reasons in a list: they disbelieve in God and His Messenger, they come to prayer only lazily, and they spend only reluctantly. Each answers a part of the same problem — the acts are performed but not meant. The Book describes the same reluctance in the sūrah of the Women, where the hypocrites stand for prayer indolent and display before people, remembering God but little (4:142). The remedy the verse implies is not a better gift but a changed root; the sūrah's later portrait of the believers, given at the end of the chapter, starts from the opposite place: those who repent, worship, praise, fast, bow and prostrate (9:112).

## Verse 9:55

> So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers

**Visible Prosperity Is Not Divine Approval**

The verse tells the Prophet not to be impressed by wealth or children had by the hypocrites. These are natural blessings, but they become dangerous when observers treat them as proof that Allah approves of the owner's faith. Richness can coexist with rebellion, and a large family can coexist with a heart that refuses gratitude. Numbers and possessions do not reveal the final moral condition of a person.

**Do Not Envy the Burden You Cannot See**

The command is addressed to the Prophet because even a righteous observer sees the surface before the hidden cost. The community may envy someone who has money, heirs, or social security without knowing the fear that controls him. Envy becomes less reasonable when the apparent advantage may be carrying a severe spiritual burden.

This does not mean the believer should refuse lawful prosperity. He may seek wealth to support family, pay debts, give charity, and protect dignity. He simply refuses to admire prosperity detached from obedience. The right prayer is not, “Give me what made that person proud,” but, “Give me what helps me remember You and spend Your gift correctly.”

---

The same warning is repeated in the sūrah at verse 85 for the same reason, and the Book gives the doctrine on which it rests elsewhere: do not let their wealth and children impress you, for God intends to test them by these in this life (20:131), and the delay granted to those who deny is a gradual drawing on rather than a favour (3:178). The verse's point is aimed at the observer rather than the owner: prosperity is a test for the one who has it and for the one who watches it, and the community is being trained not to read a person's standing with God off his bank and his household.

## Verse 9:56

> They swear by Allah that they are part of you, but they are not. They only say so out of fear

**Fear Can Produce Speech Without Faith**

They say they belong because they are afraid. The fear may be fear of the believers' strength, fear of losing social protection, or fear of the consequences of open opposition. Fear can make a person imitate the language of the group without accepting its truth. Outward conformity then becomes a survival strategy rather than a confession of conviction.

**Political Loyalty Is Not the Same as Faith**

A community may include people who cooperate for worldly reasons. Such cooperation can be managed through clear agreements, but it should not be confused with spiritual fellowship. The verse exposes the danger of assuming that a public alliance has already produced a shared moral center.

**Belonging Carries Responsibility**

Every believer should examine his own use of communal language. Does he say “we” only when there is victory and say “they” when there is sacrifice? Does he want the honor of a group while criticizing every obligation it carries? Genuine belonging includes correction. It does not require agreement with every human decision, but it requires loyalty to the truth and honest repair when one is wrong.

---

## Verse 9:57

> If only they could find a refuge, or a cave, or any hiding-place, they would rush headlong towards it

**The Places of Hiding Become Moral Images**

The words for refuge and cave carry the sense of a place into which a person slips away. The picture is physical, but its deeper force is moral. A person can hide in a room, a faction, a private grievance, or an online identity. Any shelter can become a cave if it is used to avoid honest responsibility.

**Rushing Headlong Means Loss of Deliberation**

The verse describes them as rushing headlong. They do not carefully seek the safest lawful course. They run toward any opening that promises escape. Fear has taken control of judgment, so the first available shelter appears better than every obligation outside it.

This is how panic behaves in ordinary life. A person leaves a duty through an impulsive message, abandons a relationship without a honest conversation, or joins a harmful group because it offers immediate protection from criticism. Speed can feel like decisiveness while actually being surrender to fear. The believer slows down enough to ask Allah for guidance, consult sound people, and choose a refuge that does not require falsehood.

---

## Verse 9:58

> There are some of them who are critical of your distribution of alms ˹O Prophet˺. If they are given some of it they are pleased, but if not they are enraged

**Their Standard of Justice Is Personal Gain**

The verse exposes people who criticize the Prophet's distribution of alms according to what they receive. A gift makes them pleased; withholding makes them enraged. Their reaction reveals that the language of justice is being used to demand a personal advantage. They are not asking first whether the distribution is lawful, wise, or directed to genuine need.
**Contentment Cannot Depend on Being Chosen**

The pleased person who receives and the enraged person who does not receive are governed by the same desire. Neither has placed the matter under Allah's wisdom. This makes contentment a test of the heart. A person may hope for provision and ask for his right, but he should not make his happiness depend completely on being selected over someone else.
Contentment does not mean refusing to report a genuine wrong. It means that a just decision remains just even when it is personally disappointing. The believer can say, “I was not given this, but I will not accuse without evidence.” He can pursue a lawful claim without feeding resentment. The heart's discipline is shown by what happens after the answer is no.

**What the Men Did With the Word Justice**

The verse describes men who measure the community's fairness by their own receipts. The alms were being distributed, and the same man is described twice: pleased when he is given from them, angry when he is not. The sūrah has already explained what the distribution is for — the eight categories named a few verses later (9:60) — and the verse's opening word, *and among them are those who find fault with you concerning the alms*, locates the fault in the fault-finders rather than in the distribution. Their standard is their own share, which they call justice when it pays them.
**What the Verse Offers as the Alternative**

The next verse states the position the first men held and the position the complaint deserves: if they had been content with what God and His Messenger gave them, and said, God is enough for us, God will give us of His bounty and His Messenger also, we turn to God — that would have been better for them (9:59). The sentence is a correction of the tone as much as the claim. The believer is not commanded to be indifferent about his rights and the sūrah does not tell the poor to stop asking; the society the Prophet built had a treasury with rules. What the verse asks is that the answer "no" not produce the response recorded here, and the difference between a claim pressed lawfully and a demand backed by anger is what the passage sets out to teach a community that was learning how to live with its own money.

## Verse 9:59

> If only they had been content with what Allah and His Messenger had given them and said, “Allah is sufficient for us! Allah will grant us out of His bounty, and so will His Messenger. To Allah ˹alone˺ we turn with hope.”

**Contentment Begins by Recognizing the Giver**

The verse offers the response that would have saved them from anger: contentment with what Allah and His Messenger had given. Contentment does not mean that every person receives the same amount or that legitimate need should never be presented. It means that the heart sees provision as a gift under Allah's command rather than as a debt owed to the loudest claimant.

This recognition changes the tone of asking. A needy person may request help without shame, and an administrator may review a decision without fear. Neither turns the request into an accusation against Allah. The believer knows that human distribution can be mistaken and can be corrected, but he does not make one disappointing decision proof that divine care has failed.

**Allah Is Sufficient Without Making Means Useless**

“Allah is sufficient for us” is a declaration of reliance, not a rejection of lawful means. Allah may provide through work, family, charity, public funds, or an unexpected opening. The believer thanks the channel without confusing the channel with the Provider. This protects him from both ingratitude toward people and dependence on people as though they controlled the unseen.

---

The response the verse asks for is stated in full: contentment with what God and His Messenger have given, the declaration that God is sufficient, and the expectation that He will grant out of His bounty. The Book states the same sufficiency elsewhere — "is Allah not sufficient for His servant?" (39:36) — and attaches the reward to it: whoever is content, God will enrich. The words are the answer to the grumbling of the previous verse, whose subject is the division of alms and spoils, so the contentment being taught is not resignation to poverty but trust in the arrangement God has made for the community's wealth.

## Verse 9:60

> Alms-tax is only for the poor and the needy, for those employed to administer it, for those whose hearts are attracted ˹to the faith˺, for ˹freeing˺ slaves, for those in debt, for Allah’s cause, and for ˹needy˺ travellers. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Charity Has Defined Recipients**

This verse places charitable funds within eight recognized categories. The limitation protects the money from becoming a private pool for whoever has influence. The poor and needy receive direct care. Administrators may receive for the work of collecting and distributing. Those whose hearts are attracted to faith may receive support that strengthens a fragile bond. Funds may free enslaved people, relieve debt, serve Allah's cause, and help travelers cut off from their resources.

The list does not reduce compassion. It gives compassion a disciplined form. A fund entrusted for alms cannot be redirected merely because another project is fashionable or because a powerful person requests it. Need must be examined, and the category must be real. The verse turns generosity into an accountable institution rather than leaving it to personal mood.

**Allah's Cause and the Stranded Traveler**

“For Allah's cause” is broad in language, so it requires responsible interpretation. It refers to work that genuinely serves Allah's religion and public good under sound legal guidance, not to every project that adopts a religious slogan. The category must not become a way for administrators to spend without the limits placed on the other recipients.

---

The verse is the sūrah's charter for the use of alms, and the jurists built the law of zakāh on its eight categories: the poor, the needy, those employed to collect it, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, the freeing of those in bondage, the debtors, the cause of God, and the traveller. The categories were read as a closed list, which is why the schools differ over whether all eight must be represented or whether any one of them may be given to. The closing words, that it is an obligation from God who is All-Knowing and All-Wise, put the distribution on the same footing as the obligation itself: the wealth is God's, and the categories are His arrangement of it.

## Verse 9:61

> And there are others who hurt the Prophet by saying, “He listens to anyone.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He listens to what is best for you. He believes in Allah, has faith in the believers, and is a mercy for those who believe among you.” Those who hurt Allah’s Messenger will suffer a painful punishment

**Listening Is Not Gullibility**

The insult calls the Prophet an “ear,” suggesting that he accepts whatever reaches him and can be swayed by any speaker. The accusation confuses a virtue with a weakness. A leader who listens carefully is not necessarily a leader without judgment. The real question is what he listens to, how he tests it, and what he does after hearing it.

The reply gives the answer: he listens to what is best for the community. He receives truth, sincere counsel, and the words that lead to repair. He does not treat every report as equal merely because it has been spoken. The insult is defeated not by claiming that the Prophet hears nothing, but by showing that his hearing is directed toward good. Attentive leadership can be both compassionate and discerning.

**Hearing the Best Requires Rejecting Harm**

This does not justify silencing uncomfortable truth. Beneficial speech can be painful when it corrects a beloved person. The test is its relation to truth, justice, and repair. A believer listens to the critic who wants the wrong corrected and resists the speaker who wants an audience divided. Good hearing is an ethical act, not passive consumption.

---

The offence the verse names — hurting the Messenger and harbouring disbelief — is the subject of the passage that runs from 9:59 to 9:70, and the Book attaches the same warning to it elsewhere: those who hurt God and His Messenger are cursed in this world and the next (33:57). The verse's mention of gathering the disbelievers in Hell together with the hypocrites matches the sūrah's closing picture of the two groups in the same place (9:68), and the believers are told in the same chapter what their own speech should be: a word that is recognised and obeyed rather than complaint (9:61).

**The Report Behind the Insult**

The verse answers a remark that the sūrah's reports attach to the hypocrites of Madinah: that the Prophet was an ear, hearing whatever anyone said and believing it. The commentators record the talk of the men who said it, and the sūrah places it in the sequence of offences it lists against the same group, alongside the oaths they used as cover (9:62) and the mockery that the Book answers with the sentence that a people should not apologize when they have denied after believing (9:65-66). The offence is not a slip of the tongue. The Book treats the honour of the Messenger as part of the message itself: blessed are those who lower their voices in his presence and speak to him as they would not speak to one another (49:2; 24:63), and those who hurt God and His Messenger are named as cursed in this world and the next (33:57). The answer the verse gives — he listens to what is best for you — is the Book's way of putting the insult into its proper size. What the men were calling gullibility, the Book calls trust and mercy (21:107).

## Verse 9:62

> They swear by Allah to you ˹believers˺ in order to please you, while it is the pleasure of Allah and His Messenger they should seek, if they are ˹true˺ believers

**Pleasing People Is a Small Goal**

The hypocrites swear to the believers to win their approval. Their oaths are aimed at the immediate audience, not at the Lord before whom every oath will be judged. The problem is not that believers should never care about one another's feelings. Good manners, reconciliation, and kindness are part of faith. The problem is allowing public approval to become the highest goal.
A person who lives for applause changes his words whenever the audience changes. He says what calms the powerful, what flatters the group, or what protects his reputation. Such speech may produce peace for a moment, but it cannot produce integrity. The verse moves the believer from “How will they receive this?” to “What does Allah require me to say and do?” That question makes human approval smaller without making human dignity irrelevant.
**Oaths Cannot Repair a False Motive**

An oath invokes Allah, but invoking His name does not turn a false motive into sincerity. The hypocrites use solemn speech to please the believers, yet the reason for the oath exposes its weakness. Their concern is not truth before Allah; it is acceptance before people. A sacred form can be used for an unsacred purpose.

**The Oath and the Priority**

The verse is part of the sūrah's exposure of the men in Madinah who said one thing and meant another. They would swear to the Prophet to keep him content (9:61), and this verse answers the swearing rather than the claim: God and His Messenger are more deserving of being pleased, if they are believers. The sentence turns the whole transaction around. A person who swears to keep a man happy has put the man in the place of the One who sees the oath being taken, and the sūrah states the correction in one line.
**Where the Oaths Are Dealt With**

The chapter returns to the same behaviour repeatedly. The hypocrites swear that they belong to the community when they do not (9:56), and later they swear that they said nothing when they had insulted the Prophet (9:74). The Sūrah of the Hypocrites states the pattern at the level of an opening line, where their oaths are described as a shield behind which they turn away from the truth (63:1-2). The Book treats this kind of oath as a specific vice, because it uses the name of God to buy protection while the heart stays where it was. The verse in front of us gives the remedy in six words: please God first.
**What Follows the Verse**

The next verse states what is prepared for the people who are content to oppose God and His Messenger: the Fire of Hell, to remain in it, and that is the great disgrace (9:63). The sūrah is therefore not asking for a small thing. It is telling a community that the men who swore loyalty in the mosque were being weighed, and that the weighing does not stop at an oath. The verse's last phrase, "if they are believers", is the doorway the whole passage leaves open, and the Book's habit is to leave it open even here.

## Verse 9:63

> Do they not know that whoever opposes Allah and His Messenger will be in the Fire of Hell forever? That is the ultimate disgrace

**The Greatest Disgrace Is Misplaced Honor**

People often fear losing status more than losing faith. The verse reverses that scale. The person may keep a reputation among his peers while opposing Allah, but that reputation is temporary and powerless before the final account. True honor is not being admired by people who reward defiance. It is standing with what Allah approves.

The believer therefore measures success by obedience, not by applause. He does not seek disgrace for opponents, because he wants guidance and justice. He does not hide the seriousness of opposition, because mercy without truth deceives. The warning stands as a boundary: the path that rejects Allah and His Messenger ends not in freedom, but in the Fire and the exposure of a life that chose the wrong object of honor.

The believer should therefore choose humility before the command rather than waiting for a public defeat to teach it. He asks when his resistance is principled and when it is only pride defending itself. He asks whether criticism seeks understanding or enjoys disrespect. These questions keep disagreement within the limits of faith. Allah's Messenger can be honored without making human interpretations infallible, and Allah can be obeyed without turning obedience into a license to mistreat people. The Fire and the disgrace remain a warning against the final form of opposition: knowing the truth, despising it, and refusing to return.

---

## Verse 9:64

> The hypocrites fear that a sûrah should be revealed about them, exposing what is in their hearts. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Keep mocking! Allah will definitely bring to light what you fear.”

**Mockery Cannot Control Revelation**

Their mocking speech tries to reduce sacred truth to entertainment. Mockery can create the feeling that the mocker is in command: if everything is a joke, nothing can judge him. The verse removes that illusion. Allah can bring to light what the mocker tries to keep hidden, and no performance can prevent the truth from being disclosed.

The command to “keep mocking” is not approval of mockery. It is a challenge that exposes its futility. The mocker is allowed to continue for a time, but the permission is not protection. This pattern appears throughout the Qur'an: delay can give the false person room to repent, yet it can also allow his own words to accumulate as evidence against him.

**Envy Can Blind a Person to Plain Truth**

Envy is not only wanting what another has. It is resenting the good that Allah placed in another person. When envy governs, guidance from that person becomes unbearable, even if the guidance is sound. The believer asks Allah to protect him from this blindness. He can learn from someone without feeling that learning diminishes him, because honor comes from following truth rather than from being its first discoverer.

---

## Verse 9:65

> If you question them, they will certainly say, “We were only talking idly and joking around.” Say, “Was it Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger that you ridiculed?”

**A Joke Cannot Erase Its Target**

The hypocrites defend themselves by calling their words idle talk and jokes. The answer does not reject humor as such. It asks what was made the object of the humor: Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger. A joke is not morally empty simply because the speaker laughs or claims not to mean it. Its target, meaning, and effect still matter.

**An Excuse After Exposure Reveals Character**

The response “we were only joking” comes after they have been questioned. It is not offered as a sincere explanation before harm occurs; it is used as a shield after the words have been exposed. This teaches the community to examine how a person responds to correction. Repentance accepts the wrong. Evasion argues about the audience's sensitivity.

A person who has caused harm should be able to say, “That was wrong, and I will not repeat it.” Such an admission may feel humiliating, but it preserves the soul. A person who keeps refining the excuse protects the image while deepening the defect. Allah values truthfulness more than a clever defense.

---

## Verse 9:66

> Make no excuses! You have lost faith after your belief. If We pardon a group of you, We will punish others for their wickedness

**The Excuse Has Reached Its Limit**

The verse refuses the defense offered by those who mocked sacred things. “Make no excuses” does not mean that repentance is impossible. It means that explanations designed to erase the act will not be accepted. The speakers want to turn deliberate contempt into harmless entertainment, but the command names the seriousness of what they chose.

**Repentance Requires More Than a Better Story**

The passage leaves a door open through the possibility of pardon. Pardon is not gained by finding a more acceptable description of the offense. It requires regret, abandonment of the wrong, and a return to reverence. If the mockery harmed people, repair may also require an apology or a public correction.

A servant should not delay repentance while waiting to feel dignified. The feeling of humiliation is often the ego losing control of the story. Better to bear that feeling than to meet Allah with a defense built from lies. A truthful confession does not make the offense good; it makes change possible.

---

The mockery the previous verse records was spoken at Tabūk, where a group of the waverers made light of the reciters and of the revelation, and the sūrah answers with the statement that they should not try to justify themselves: they had said what they said, and their excuse-making is part of the offence. The Book states the rule for speech about God in the same sūrah: when the hypocrites and the sick of heart see a sūrah revealed they take it as a jest (9:64), and the verses that follow complete the exchange. The severity is proportionate to the object: the ridicule was aimed at revelation itself, not at a person's opinions.

## Verse 9:67

> The hypocrites, both men and women, are all alike: they encourage what is evil, forbid what is good, and withhold ˹what is in˺ their hands. They neglected Allah, so He neglected them. Surely the hypocrites are the rebellious

**Men and Women Share the Same Moral Pattern**

The verse places hypocritical men and women together. Their gender differs, but their moral pattern is the same: they encourage evil, resist good, and close their hands. This is a statement of equal accountability. Neither men nor women are given a special exemption from the duties of faith or the consequences of betrayal.

**Forgetting Allah Produces Divine Neglect**

“They neglected Allah, so He neglected them” (9:67) is not a claim that Allah forgets in the human sense. It describes a reciprocal judgment: they abandoned remembrance, guidance, and obedience, so Allah left them without the special care and direction they had rejected. The language makes the moral consequence vivid.

A person can forget Allah while still speaking religious words. Forgetfulness appears when prayer is postponed without concern, commands are treated as obstacles, and gratitude disappears from the use of blessings. Divine neglect is therefore not arbitrary abandonment. It is the painful result of choosing to live as though Allah's guidance were irrelevant. The servant should seek refuge from this state by remembering Allah before the heart becomes comfortable without Him.

---

The verse draws the portrait of the hypocrites by placing it against the portrait of the believers given a few verses later: here the men and women of the hypocrites command what is wrong, forbid what is right and close their hands; there the believers and believing women command good, forbid wrong, keep up prayer, give alms and obey God and His Messenger (9:71). The two pictures are exact opposites in every clause, which is how the sūrah defines hypocrisy — not as weak faith but as a reversed direction. The verse's closing words state the consequence, and the name given to the group is the same one the chapter has used throughout: it is they who are the losers.

## Verse 9:68

> Allah has promised the hypocrites, both men and women, and the disbelievers an everlasting stay in the Fire of Hell—it is sufficient for them. Allah has condemned them, and they will suffer a never-ending punishment

**The Warning Includes Men and Women Together**

The verse repeats the pairing of hypocritical men and women before naming the punishment. The repetition makes accountability unmistakably universal. Hypocrisy is not a defect tied to one sex, class, or public position. Anyone who hides rejection behind belonging can fall under the warning, and anyone who returns sincerely can seek Allah's mercy before the condition becomes fixed.

**Everlasting Punishment Follows a Settled End**

The never-ending punishment must be understood with the distinction between settled disbelief and a sinful believer who repents or dies with faith. Allah does not confuse a passing weakness with a final rejection. The verse addresses those whose disbelief and hypocrisy have become their enduring state.

This distinction protects justice and encourages repentance. A sinner must not say, “My weakness is harmless because I still claim faith.” Nor should he despair as though one failure has made return impossible. The right response to the warning is immediate reform. Delay is dangerous because habits can become identities and identities can become the condition in which a person meets Allah.

---

God's promise to the hypocrites, the disbelievers and the men and women of both together is stated in one sentence: the fire of Hell, abiding in it, and the curse of God. The same verdict is given at the end of the sūrah of the Hypocrites, where their men and women are of one another, commanding wrong and forbidding right (63:7–8), so that the two groups are treated as one party in this world in their conduct and in the next in their destination. The verse's phrase "it is sufficient for them" answers any thought that the punishment might be lightened, and the words that follow — "and theirs is a lasting torment" — are the sūrah's standard formula for the final state of those who die on that path.

## Verse 9:69

> ˹You hypocrites are˺ like those ˹disbelievers˺ before you. They were far superior to you in might and more abundant in wealth and children. They enjoyed their share in this life. You have enjoyed your share, just as they did. And you have engaged in idle talk, just as they did. Their deeds have become void in this world and the Hereafter. And it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers

**Power Does Not Change the Moral Pattern**

The hypocrites are compared with earlier peoples who had greater might, wealth, and children. The comparison removes their excuse that their present behavior is justified by circumstance. The earlier people had more visible strength, yet strength did not save them from the consequences of rejecting guidance. The hypocrites have less power and still repeat the same moral pattern.

This is a warning against measuring truth by the scale of worldly success. A large army, prosperous family, or influential network can make a community feel secure. None of these proves that Allah approves its direction. The question is what power serves. If strength protects justice and gratitude, it can be a blessing. If it protects arrogance and idle talk, it becomes evidence against its owner.

**Enjoyment Is Not the Same as Gratitude**

Enjoyment becomes dangerous when it teaches the heart that the world is all there is. The person begins to defend the lifestyle that produced the forgetfulness. He does not ask whether the wealth is lawful, whether the family is being guided, or whether the comfort is making him generous. He asks only how to keep the share growing. Gratitude measures the effect of a blessing; appetite measures only its size.

---

**What the Earlier Peoples Had**

The verse lists what the earlier generations possessed — greater strength, more wealth, more children — and the list is chosen because it names the three things the hypocrites of Madinah were counting. Strength was the alliance they could call on, wealth was the trade and the estates, children were the men who carried a family's name into the next generation. Each of them is a real good, and the verse does not sneer at any of them; it says that none of them functioned as a protection. The men of ʿĀd could not have built their towers without the strength, and the towers are gone.
**The Law of Enjoyment**

The verse's second half gives the sentence in the sūrah's own terms: they enjoyed their share, and you enjoyed your share as those before you enjoyed theirs, and you talked as they talked. The three clauses build one diagnosis — a life organized around using what has been given rather than answering for it. The point is not that enjoyment is forbidden; it is that enjoyment is not the same as gratitude, and a generation raised inside a blessing can inherit the blessing and lose the reason for it. The verse ends by naming what that costs: the works of such people are lost in this world and the next, and they are the losers.

## Verse 9:70

> Have they not received the stories of those ˹destroyed˺ before them: the people of Noah, ’Âd, and Thamûd, the people of Abraham, the residents of Midian, and the overturned cities ˹of Lot˺? Their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves

**History Is a Moral Witness**

The verse asks whether the hypocrites have received the accounts of earlier peoples. These stories are not offered as decoration or as a list of ancient names. They are evidence that communities can have strength, culture, and time yet still destroy themselves by refusing clear guidance. History becomes a witness against the claim that consequences are too distant to matter.

The named peoples include Noah's community, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Abraham's people, Midian, and the overturned cities often associated with Lot. The range of stories prevents the listener from reducing judgment to one kind of sin or one region. Different societies received messengers, faced different tests, and shared the choice to reject clear proof. Their variety makes the warning harder to dismiss as someone else's unique problem.

**The Overturned Cities Carry a Double Image**

The expression “overturned cities” points to a destruction that changed the order of a place. Some interpreters connect it specifically with the cities of Lot; others see the phrase as broader. Both readings preserve the central lesson: a society can be turned from apparent stability to ruin when its moral corruption is defended as normal.

---

**What the Verse Actually Says**

The verse gives the list in full and then closes it with a sentence that removes the excuse of ignorance from every generation: *their messengers came to them with clear proofs, and God was not unjust to them, but they were wronging themselves*. That last clause is the one to hold on to. The Book never presents the destruction of a people as a verdict on people who had no chance; in every case the messenger came first and was heard, and what followed was the consequence of a choice that was made in the open. The wrong was done to the wrongdoers before it was done to them.
**Why the List Is So Long**

The peoples named here come from every corner of the map the first listeners knew: the flood in Mesopotamia, ʿĀd in the south of Arabia, Thamūd in the north, the towns of Abraham, Madyan on the trade road, and the cities that were turned upside down. Some were destroyed by water, some by a wind, some by a shout, some by the earth opening. The variety is the point of the list — the same refusal received different answers in different countries, so that nobody hearing the sūrah could file the warning under one tribe's bad luck. And the men being addressed were traders who knew these routes and knew the ruins on them.

## Verse 9:71

> The believers, both men and women, are guardians of one another. They encourage good and forbid evil, establish prayer and pay alms-tax, and obey Allah and His Messenger. It is they who will be shown Allah’s mercy. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise

**Believers Protect One Another**

The verse describes believing men and women as guardians, allies, and responsible friends to one another. Faith is not presented as a private arrangement between an individual and Allah. It creates bonds of care, counsel, protection, and shared duty. A believer watches over another person's dignity, safety, and faith without claiming ownership of the other person.

The pairing of men and women is important. Both belong to the moral community, both carry obligations, and both can help preserve what is good. Guardianship does not mean that every person has the same role in every setting. It means that no sex is excluded from spiritual agency. A woman can advise, protect, teach, and resist wrong; a man can do the same. The community's health depends on the virtue of both.

**Goodness Needs Encouragement**

Believers encourage good rather than waiting for goodness to appear without support. Encouragement includes teaching, making lawful obedience easier, praising honest effort, and arranging institutions that help people keep their duties. A person may know what is right and still need companionship, resources, or a clear example before he can act on it.

---

**What the Guardianship Is For**

The verse does not leave the mutual guardianship as a feeling; it gives the guardianship's content in the same sentence — *they enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong* — and then the two practices that sustain it, the prayer established and the zakāh given, and finally the obedience that holds the whole structure together: God and His Messenger. The close names the two attributes that make the promise safe: God is Almighty and All-Wise, able to carry out what He has undertaken and wise in how He does it. And what the obedience leads to is named in the next verse: gardens and mercy, and a great attainment (9:72).
**The Mirror Image in the Same Sūrah**

Four verses before this one, the sūrah described the other party, and the description is built to the same shape with every clause reversed: the hypocrite men and women are allies of one another, *they enjoin what is wrong and forbid what is right*, and they close their hands. The two verses are meant to be read side by side, because the difference between the two communities is not the quantity of their religious talk but the direction of the influence they exert. A community whose members call one another to good and stop one another from harm is doing the work this verse describes, and the verse names it as the mark that separates the two groups the sūrah has been contrasting from its first pages.

## Verse 9:72

> Allah has promised the believers, both men and women, Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and splendid homes in the Gardens of Eternity, and—above all—the pleasure of Allah. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph

**The Promise Includes Men and Women Equally**

The promise is addressed to believing men and women together. The reward is not divided by a lesser spiritual status assigned to one sex. Both receive Allah's promise of the Garden because both were called to faith, obedience, sacrifice, and guardianship. This is the other side of the moral equality stated in the preceding verse: shared responsibility is joined to shared hope.

**The Gardens Answer Human Longing Without Reducing It**

The imagery of gardens with flowing rivers speaks to life, beauty, refreshment, and permanence. Water that flows beneath the gardens suggests abundance without the fear that the source will dry up. The homes are splendid because the reward includes belonging, safety, and a dwelling worthy of rest. These descriptions address the body and the imagination in language people can understand.

The promise should not be reduced to material appetite. The Garden is a created reward from Allah, and its beauty is part of His generosity. The believer is allowed to hope for its pleasures without being ashamed of that hope. Yet the images also point beyond the limits of worldly pleasure: no earthly garden removes death, loss, or fear. The promised Garden does.

---

The promise is put in the plural and describes a dwelling: gardens under which rivers flow, abiding in them for ever, and dwellings of lasting bliss. The same promise is given to the believers after their description at the end of this chapter (9:100, 9:111), and the sūrah of the Ranks states it with the exchange it rests on: those who believe and strive are given gardens and forgiveness (61:11–12). The closing words of the verse name the whole thing the greatest success — *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* — which is the Book's usual way of putting the reward beyond comparison with anything the world offers.

## Verse 9:73

> O Prophet! Struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Hell will be their home. What an evil destination

**Struggle Against the Disbelievers and the Hypocrites**

The verse tells the Prophet to struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites and to be firm with them, and it says that their home is the fire. The command joins two kinds of opposition in one sentence, and the sūrah spends much of its length explaining how the two differ.

**Struggle Is More Than One Thing**

The word the verse uses for struggle covers every serious effort made for the truth, and the teachers of the community applied it according to the situation. Where a war existed, the community fought; where there was no war, the effort was argument, evidence, patience and refusal to give in. The verse's breadth is deliberate: it does not tell the community to treat every opponent in the same way. A man who reads it as a licence to fight everyone has misread it, and a man who reads it as a soft word for doing nothing when people are being harmed has misread it too.

**Why the Two Groups Are Named Together**

The two groups opposed the community in different ways. The disbelievers opposed it openly: they fought it in the field, blocked its road and drove its people out. The hypocrites lived inside it, appearing to belong while working against it from within, and the sūrah names their habits in detail, from the men who stayed behind when the army marched to the men who built a mosque to split the community. The sūrah of the Prohibition gives the same command in the same words (66:9 states it), and the reason is the same in both places: the community had to deal with an open enemy and a hidden one at the same time.

**What Firmness Means Here**

The firmness the verse asks for is not loudness; it is the quality of not being moved from the position. The Prophet was told to be firm with men who made excuses to his face, and the sūrah shows him answering them with clear words rather than with anger. Firmness in this verse means that the community does not soften its position to keep a difficult neighbour happy, and it does not pretend that the harm done inside the community was not harm.

**What the Verse Promises**

The verse ends by saying that their home is the fire, and the sūrah had already said the same about both groups where it describes the men who do not believe in God and the last day and the men who were given over to a lasting punishment (9:68 states it). The warning is placed at the end so that the reader understands where the road of each group leads. The chapter is not written to make the community angry; it is written to tell it the truth about what it faces and where the truth leaves.

---

## Verse 9:74

> They swear by Allah that they never said anything ˹blasphemous˺, while they did in fact utter a blasphemy, lost faith after accepting Islam, and plotted what they could not carry out. It is only through resentment that they pay Allah and His Messenger back for enriching them out of His bounty! If they repent, it will be better for them. But if they turn away, Allah will torment them with a painful punishment in this world and the Hereafter, and they will have no one on earth to protect or help them

**Denial Cannot Erase a Spoken Wrong**

The verse describes people who swear that they did not say what they in fact said. Their denial is not a search for the truth; it is a try to control the record. They want the benefits of innocence without accepting the responsibility of speech. A false oath may deceive listeners, but it does not change the event before Allah.
This teaches the believer to treat speech as morally real. A careless word can wound, mislead, or expose a hidden contempt. If challenged, the honest response is to clarify what was meant and admit what was wrong. Reconstructing the story to protect reputation adds a second sin to the first. Truthful confession may lower a person's standing before people, but false denial places him under the judgment of the One who heard the original words.
**Blasphemy Is Not a Casual Category**

The verse calls their speech a word of disbelief because it attacked the foundations of faith. This seriousness should not be used to label every awkward phrase or ignorant question as blasphemy. The passage concerns deliberate words joined to mockery, denial, and hypocrisy. Legal and religious judgment requires knowledge, evidence, and proper authority.
The verse concerns men who swore they had said nothing and were shown that they had — the report attaches it to the waverers at Tabūk, where a threat against the Prophet's life was discussed and the men swore they had intended nothing. The chapter treats the denial as part of the pattern: their oaths are false (9:95–96), they were warned by their own words (9:65), and the sūrah says that after their scheme failed, they still hoped for change. The closing clause, that God will bring their deeds to light, is the same principle the verse of action states later in the chapter: whatever is done will be seen by God, His Messenger and the believers (9:105).

**What They Denied and What They Said**

The verse reports a group swearing by God that they had said nothing when the sūrah says plainly that they had said the word of disbelief. The reports of the occasion place it in the campaign to Tabūk, where a man at the gathering spoke against the Prophet, and the sūrah had already said a few verses earlier that the hypocrites feared a chapter being sent down that would reveal what was in their hearts (9:64). The verse then adds the sentence that turns the matter from talk to action: they aimed at what they could not reach. The commentators read that as the plot against the Prophet on the road back from the campaign, when a group tried to frighten his mount. The chapter records the plan and closes it with one word.
**The Strange Thing They Resented**

The verse says they hated nothing but that God and His Messenger had enriched them from His bounty. That line is one of the most cutting in the sūrah. The reason for their bitterness was the community's success, because the success made the message harder to dismiss and left them inside a rising city that they had bet against. The Book then states the two roads in one sentence: if they repent, it is better for them; if they turn away, the punishment comes in this world and the next. The door is offered in the middle of the sentence and the warning closes it.
**Where the Passage Stands in the Sūrah**

This is the chapter that deals with the men who stayed behind, the men who made excuses, and the men who built a mosque as a base for division. The verses after this one describe the mosque of Ḍirār and the men who built it, and the two passages belong together: the talk, the plot, the building. The sūrah's answer is in the verse in front of us, and it is the answer the whole chapter keeps repeating: the community is not held together by cleverness, and the men who tried to work against it from the inside were known, watched, and dealt with by name in the revelation they were pretending to accept.

## Verse 9:75

> And there are some who had made a vow to Allah: “If He gives us from His bounty, we will surely spend in charity and be of the righteous.”

**The Vow Sounds Righteous Before the Test Arrives**

The people described make a promise to Allah: if bounty reaches them, they will give charity and become righteous. Their words identify a condition that should not need to be there. Worship is presented as a payment for receiving wealth, as though the servant will begin obeying only after Allah proves that He can satisfy the servant's request.

A person may ask Allah for provision and promise to use it well. The danger lies in making the promise a bargain that excuses present disobedience. Righteousness is not reserved for the day of abundance. A poor person can be grateful, honest, and generous with time. If a vow is made, it increases responsibility; it does not make obedience optional until the condition arrives.

**A Conditional Promise Reveals a Conditional Allegiance**

The phrase “if He gives us” can reveal a hidden complaint: the speaker will serve only after Allah serves him in the exact way requested. True faith accepts that Allah's bounty may arrive as money, opportunity, restraint, or guidance. The servant may ask for a specific good, but he does not make obedience depend on receiving it.

---

The report most often cited for these verses concerns a man who pledged to give alms if God gave him wealth, then withheld what he had promised, and the commentators differed over the report's chain and over whether the verses were revealed about him or about the category in general. The Qur'an's rule does not depend on the story: an undertaking made to God is a covenant, and breaking it is not merely a lapse in giving. The Book states the consequence in the same terms elsewhere, that those who sell God's covenant and their oaths for a small price have no share in the Hereafter (3:77), and this verse gives the inner effect: hypocrisy planted in the heart until the day they meet Him.

## Verse 9:76

> But when He gave them out of His bounty, they withheld it and turned away indifferently

**The Gift Exposed the Promise**

The promised condition arrived: Allah gave them from His bounty. The event should have released the charity they had pledged. Instead, they became miserly and turned away. Their problem was therefore not inability. It was the change that occurred when imagined wealth became actual possession. The gift exposed whether their earlier vow had been sincere.

**Miserliness Is More Than Keeping Money**

Withholding includes refusing the financial obligation, but the moral defect can spread wider. A person may hold back time, knowledge, forgiveness, or protection even when Allah has given him the ability to share. The closed hand is an outward sign of a heart that treats bounty as a private wall.

Careful spending is not miserliness. A servant must provide for dependents, avoid waste, and plan for real needs. Miserliness begins when fear of loss makes a person deny a rightful claim or forget gratitude. The wealthy person can be generous without extravagance, and the poor person can be miserly with a small amount. The measure is willingness to fulfill Allah's claim.

---

## Verse 9:77

> So He caused hypocrisy to plague their hearts until the Day they will meet Him, for breaking their promise to Allah and for their lies

**Divine Requital and Human Choice Meet**

Many exegetes understand the subject of the punishment to be Allah, while another reading stresses that their own miserliness produced the hypocrisy. These readings are not enemies. Allah judges and allows the consequence, while the people choose the conduct that makes the consequence fitting. Divine judgment does not cancel human responsibility.

This balance protects the believer from two errors. He cannot say that his hypocrisy was forced on him and therefore blame no one. He also cannot imagine that his self-repair alone controls the final result apart from Allah's mercy. He must repent, tell the truth, and ask Allah to heal the heart. Guidance is sought through action and received as grace.

**The Promise and the Lie Stand Together**

The verse names two causes: breaking the promise to Allah and lying. The breach is not only financial. It is a refusal to let one's word bind one's conduct. The lie then protects that refusal. Together they create a person whose statements point one way while his choices travel another.

---

## Verse 9:78

> Do they not know that Allah ˹fully˺ knows their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks, and that Allah is the Knower of all unseen

**Private Speech Is Still Within Allah's Knowledge**

The verse asks whether the hypocrites know that Allah knows their thoughts, secret conversations, and all unseen things. Their public oaths are not the full record. The private discussion in which they mock, plan, or encourage one another is also known. No locked room, whispered message, or hidden motive lies outside Allah's awareness.

This knowledge is a warning to the dishonest and a comfort to the sincere. The hypocrite cannot protect himself by controlling the public version of events. The sincere person who is falsely accused can trust that Allah knows what happened even when no human witness is available. Divine knowledge is not selective attention; it includes the words people conceal and the intentions beneath the words they reveal.

**The Unseen Includes What Has Not Yet Appeared**

This does not mean that human beings have the unseen. They must not claim to know another person's final state or invent hidden motives without evidence. The verse belongs to Allah's knowledge. The believer acts on what is clear and leaves the unseen judgment to Him. That humility prevents suspicion from becoming a religious virtue.

---

## Verse 9:79

> ˹There are˺ those who slander ˹some of˺ the believers for donating liberally and mock others for giving only the little they can afford. Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment

**The Mockery Attacks Both Abundance and Scarcity**

The mockers criticize two opposite donors. They slander the person who gives generously and ridicule the person who can afford only a little. Their target is not the amount itself. Their target is the act of giving, because generosity threatens the selfish order they prefer. If a person gives much, they accuse him of showing off; if he gives little, they call the gift worthless.

**A Large Gift Is Not Automatically Proud**

A generous donation can come from wealth, gratitude, and a sincere desire to relieve need. It can also be mixed with a desire for recognition. Human beings should not claim certainty about the unseen motive merely because the amount is large. They can protect the fund from vanity and still honor the possibility of sincere sacrifice.

The donor should examine himself. Does he prefer that people know, or would he still give if his name were hidden? If public acknowledgment serves accountability, it may be lawful; if it becomes the purpose, the gift is in danger. The correct response to suspicion is neither self-display nor paralysis. Give responsibly, purify the motive, and leave the final acceptance to Allah.

---

The verse answers the men who made a target of the givers, mocking those who gave freely and those who gave little out of their own labour. The Book treats mockery of the believers as an offence against the mockers themselves: the sūrah of the Chambers names it among the things that ruin a person's standing (49:11), and the Prophet ﷺ warned in a well-known report that a man is dragged into the Fire for a word he did not think important. What the verse adds is the specific cruelty of the offence: the mockery was aimed at the poor and at the willing, and it was meant to discourage giving altogether.

## Verse 9:80

> ˹It does not matter˺ whether you ˹O Prophet˺ pray for them to be forgiven or not. Even if you pray for their forgiveness seventy times, Allah will never forgive them. That is because they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger. And Allah does not guide the rebellious people

**Prayer for Forgiveness and the Hard Case**

The verse tells the Prophet that it makes no difference whether he asks forgiveness for these men or not: even if he asked seventy times, God would not forgive them, because they have lost their faith in God and His Messenger, and God does not guide a people who refuse Him.

**What the Verse Is Not Saying**

The Book does not tell the Prophet that prayer has no value or that asking for others is useless. The same Prophet is told elsewhere to ask forgiveness for the men and women who believe, and he is described as praying for them, because prayer is part of what a messenger does (the sūrah of Muhammad tells him to ask forgiveness for the believers and names the prayer that brings them calm at 9:103, and the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān describes his prayer for them at 3:159). What the verse says is that no one can force forgiveness on someone who has settled into rejection, and that the number of the requests does not change the case. The teachers of the community noted the point the wording makes: the limit is in the person being prayed for, not in the one praying.

**Who the Verse Is About**

The men the chapter has been describing are the men whose belief was a public language while their actions served a different end. The verses before this one name them: the men who were pleased with what they had done and wanted to be praised for what they had not done (9:80 is this verse, and the passage that precedes it describes the men who stayed behind and came with excuses at 9:81's own verses). The chapter draws the line between a believer who is weak and a man who has made refusal his position: the first may fall and come back, and the second has settled. The verse in front of the reader states that settled position in the plainest terms, and the next verses explain the way the community should treat such men.

**The Meaning of the Number Seventy**

The number in the verse means the limit of what a person would ever try, which is a form of speech the Arabic uses for a great many. The Book does not give a ceiling on God's mercy; it states that the case has moved past the point where it can be fixed by asking, because the men in question had been shown the truth and turned away from it. The sūrah of the Heights makes the same statement about the men who refuse after the road has been made clear to them, and the sūrah of the Bee says that God does not guide a people who choose refusal (16:107 states it). The verse in front of the reader therefore gives the community an honest answer, which is more useful to them than a false hope.

**Why the Verse Is Still in the Record**

The chapter does not hide the hard cases, and the community was meant to know them. The Book tells the Prophet that some men will not be guided even if he asks for them (the sūrah of Yūsuf reports the prophet's own words to that effect at 12:103), and it leaves the matter with God rather than with the pulpit. The verses around this one tell the community how to act towards the men the verse describes, and they warn the community against being deceived by the public words of such men (9:83 and the verses after it tell the Prophet not to go out with them and not to stand at their graves). The sūrah keeps the mercy of God for the people who turn, and it names the men who will not, so that the community can live with the truth rather than with an illusion. The sūrah of the Hypocrites gives the same answer when it tells the Prophet that it makes no difference whether he asks forgiveness for such men or not (63:6 states it), and the sūrah in front of the reader adds that the Prophet himself was told not to pray for the men who had settled in their refusal (9:113 records the instruction).

---

## Verse 9:81

> Those ˹hypocrites˺ who remained behind rejoiced for doing so in defiance of the Messenger of Allah and hated ˹the prospect of˺ striving with their wealth and their lives in the cause of Allah. They said ˹to one another˺, “Do not march forth in the heat.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “The Fire of Hell is far hotter!” If only they could comprehend

**Relief at Disobedience Reveals the Heart**

The hypocrites rejoice because they remained behind. Their joy is not the relief of people who were genuinely unable to travel. It is satisfaction at having avoided a duty and at having opposed the Messenger by avoiding it. The same outward absence can therefore carry different meanings. One person stays through a valid weakness and grieves that he cannot serve; another stays through rebellion and celebrates the escape.

**Understanding Must Reach Conduct**

The final phrase, “if only they could comprehend,” points to a failure of moral understanding. They know the words and feel the heat, but they do not connect immediate choices to final consequences. Knowledge that never changes action remains a possession of the tongue rather than wisdom of the heart.

The same failure can appear in a believer who hears warnings but keeps protecting a habit. He may understand that wealth is a trust and still refuse to give. He may know that truth is required and still hide behind a convenient story. Comprehension is shown when knowledge begins to govern preference. The servant asks Allah to make the unseen consequence more real than the visible excuse.

---

The rejoicing the verse records is at Tabūk, where those who stayed behind were glad to have sat out the campaign and disliked being asked to spend and to fight. The sentence that follows, "so let them laugh a little, and let them weep much", is one of the sūrah's sharpest formulations of the exchange the Book makes between this life and the next: the laugh is measured in years and the weeping in what has no end. The same balance is used elsewhere in the chapter's warnings, and the verse closes with the words that they return to God and the accounting of what they used to do.

**What the Verse Records**

The verse says that the men who stayed behind rejoiced in their sitting and were unwilling to strive with their wealth and their lives, and it records what they said to one another: do not go out in the heat. The Book answers in one line: say that the fire of Hell is hotter, if only they understood. The verse before it has the men reassuring each other about the campaign, and the verses after state the reward they will not receive, and the contrast the sūrah draws runs through the whole passage.
**The Word for the Joy**

The Book uses the word for a satisfaction that comes from a decision rather than a circumstance, and it places it beside the grief of the men in tears who could not go (9:92). The sūrah has already stated the rule that the believers' comfort is not in their houses and that the campaign came first, and the Prophet's own word about the heat of the fire, reported in Bukhārī and Muslim in the passage on the two gardens, sits over this verse. The men who were speaking about the heat of the road were being answered by a fire they were not talking about.
**Where the Sūrah Turns Next**

The passage after this one gives the reward that the men missed, and it turns to the believers who went out and names the gardens God has prepared for them (9:89). The sūrah then deals with the men who asked to be excused from the campaign and the men who came to be given mounts (9:90-93). The verse in front of us is the first of the three groups the sūrah sorts out, and its place is between the excuse and the answer.

## Verse 9:82

> So let them laugh a little—they will weep much as a reward for what they have committed

**The Present Laugh Is Brief**

The verse contrasts a little laughter with much weeping. The contrast is not a ban on happiness or a demand that believers live without humor. It exposes the short duration of the hypocrites' enjoyment and the long consequence of what they chose. They laugh because they avoided sacrifice, but the pleasure is brief when measured against the account that follows.

**Weeping Can Belong to Faith or Punishment**

The source tradition distinguishes the weeping of believers in this life from the weeping of the condemned in the Hereafter. A believer may weep from fear of Allah, hope for mercy, grief over sin, or compassion for people. That weeping can purify the heart and lead to action. The hypocrites' weeping is the result of discovering that their laughter was built on rebellion.

This distinction prevents the verse from praising misery. Tears are not automatically signs of holiness, and laughter is not automatically signs of sin. The moral question is what produces them and what follows them. A joyful believer can be grateful and obedient. A sorrowful believer can repent and repair. The condemned person weeps because the chance to change has been lost.

---

## Verse 9:83

> If Allah returns you ˹O Prophet˺ to a group of them and they ask to go forth with you, say, “You will not ever go forth or fight an enemy along with me. You preferred to stay behind the first time, so stay with those ˹helpless˺ who remain behind.”

**A Previous Refusal Can Change a Future Trust**

The verse directs the Prophet not to accept the later request of people who had chosen to remain behind earlier. Their new request may sound like a change of heart, but a serious trust cannot be restored by words alone. Their earlier refusal revealed that they were willing to abandon the community when sacrifice was required. The community must therefore consider reliability before assigning a dangerous responsibility.

This is not a claim that repentance never restores a person. Repentance can restore the person's relationship with Allah and may eventually restore public trust. But public trust often requires time, evidence, and a role suited to the level of repair. Forgiveness and unrestricted access are not identical. A leader can pardon a person while still refusing to place others at risk.

**Leadership Must Protect the Mission**

The prohibition is connected to the harm these hypocrites could cause if they joined later. Their presence was not neutral. They might spread discord, weaken resolve, or sabotage the effort. Leadership therefore includes the duty to say no to a request that would endanger the people being led.

**The Scene After the Return**

The verse belongs to the passage about the men who stayed behind from the Tabūk expedition. When the Prophet returned to Madinah and the next call to go out came, those same men asked to be allowed to come along, and the answer in the verse is the refusal: you stayed the first time, so stay now with those who stay behind. The commentators explain the reference as the people who were legitimately excused — the women, the children, the elderly and the sick — and the sentence therefore puts the men who had chosen comfort in the company of those who had no choice. The Book's way of stating a penalty is often to place a person where his behaviour has put him, and this is one of its clearest examples.
**What the Sūrah Had Already Said to Them**

The verses before this one have the men making excuses, and the answer the Prophet is given is that God has told him about them and that their conduct will be seen by God and His Messenger and then they will return to the Knower of the unseen (9:94). The sūrah has already stated the rule about men who prefer their comfort: they are the ones who are content to be with those who stay behind, and their hearts have been sealed (9:87). The verse in front of us is the follow-through. The door of repentance is stated elsewhere in the sūrah for those who turn back (9:104), and the door of public trust is closed here for the purposes of a campaign that had to be carried out by people who would not run.
**The Lesson About Restoration**

The verse teaches the difference between being forgiven and being relied on. The Book does not say the men were abandoned or that their faith was declared void; it says the messenger would not take them out again. The community's leaders in every age have to make the same distinction, and the sūrah makes it for them: a person can mend his record with God, and a community can still decline to place other people's lives in his hands until the record is mended in public.

---

## Verse 9:84

> And do not ever offer ˹funeral˺ prayers for any of their dead, nor stand by their grave ˹at burial˺, for they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger and died rebellious

**Funeral Prayer Publicly Affirms Belonging**

The prohibition concerns funeral prayer and standing by the grave, acts that carry public religious meaning. Funeral prayer is not only a private request for mercy; it also represents the deceased as belonging to the community whose prayer is being performed. The verse forbids that public affirmation for people who died in settled hypocrisy and rejection.

This boundary does not mean that believers should become indifferent to death. It distinguishes compassion from religious endorsement. A person can grieve the harm caused by a death, protect the family from humiliation, and still refuse a ritual that would falsely present the deceased as a sincere believer. Clear symbols matter because they teach the living what the community honors.

**The Prohibition Is About the Final State**

The verse speaks of those who lost faith and died rebellious. It is not a command to refuse every ordinary funeral because someone committed a sin or had a troubled life. Human beings do not know every hidden state. The ruling concerns a clear condition of rejection that persisted until death and had been established through the person's conduct.

---

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse names a group that has been identified throughout the sūrah by their conduct, and it addresses a specific act — praying over a dead man, standing at his grave. The prohibition is not a verdict on the private soul of every sinner who dies, and the Qur'an says elsewhere that the inner record is God's business. What the community is told to withhold is its public ritual, which is itself a statement of faith: a funeral prayer performed by the believers says, in front of everyone who attends, that this man belongs to us and died as one of us. The Book refuses to let the community say that about a man who died as he had lived.
**The Two Duties It Leaves Standing**

The rule separates the ritual from the person's family. Nothing in the passage tells the Muslims to humiliate the household, refuse them help, or treat their grief as the crime. The help owed to a widow or a child of a man whose politics were poison is still owed; the line drawn here is drawn around one act of worship, not around mercy. And the ruling is tied to a condition the community could actually see: a life that ended in open rejection of what it knew to be true. The sūrah has spent many verses showing the difference between weakness and settled enmity, and this ruling belongs to the second category, where the end of a life is known.

## Verse 9:85

> And let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this world, and ˹then˺ their souls will depart while they are disbelievers

**The Repeated Warning Corrects a Strong Attraction**

The warning about wealth and children is repeated because these are among the strongest attractions in worldly life. People easily treat prosperity, family size, and social influence as proof of divine favor. The repetition breaks that reflex. A possession can be good in itself while still becoming a means of punishment for the person who uses it against Allah.

The Prophet is told not to be impressed, not because wealth has no value, but because appearance cannot reveal the final account. A wealthy person may be grateful and just; a poor person may be proud and cruel. Children may strengthen a family in worship, or they may become objects of vanity. The meaning of the gift lies in its moral use.

**The Gift Can Become a Chain**

Wealth and children can punish a person through the fear of losing them, the pressure of maintaining status, and the duties he refuses to perform. A person may become a servant of his possessions. He works without rest, protects his image, and compromises truth because he believes that security depends on keeping what Allah gave him.

---

The warning is repeated in the sūrah using the same words as at 9:55, and the repetition is itself the teaching: the Book repeats what the community is most likely to misunderstand, and the wealth and children of the waverers were exactly that. The verse's clause, "so that God may torment them through them in this world, and their souls may perish while they disbelieve", describes a prosperity that works against its owner. That is the same reversal the sūrah of the Cave describes in the parable of the man with the two gardens, whose harvest was destroyed around him while he stood helpless (18:42).

## Verse 9:86

> Whenever a sûrah is revealed stating, “Believe in Allah and struggle along with His Messenger,” the rich among them would ask to be exempt, saying, “Leave us with those who remain behind.”

**The Command Reaches the Able First**

When revelation calls people to belief and struggle, the affluent among the hypocrites ask to be left behind. Their wealth matters because they have the means to prepare and participate. The request is not the plea of people whose bodies or resources make the task impossible. It is the refusal of those who have ability but do not want the cost.

**Revelation Interrupts Comfortable Religion**

The revealed command does not allow them to keep a faith made only of words. Belief in Allah is joined to action with the Messenger. A person may prefer a religious identity that costs nothing, but revelation repeatedly connects conviction to sacrifice, prayer, justice, and care for people.

**The Request to Stay Behind Is a Moral Choice**

“Leave us with those who remain behind” (9:86) sounds modest, but it contains a decision. They do not ask how they can support the mission from another place. They ask to be placed among the people whose absence has already become a sign of refusal. The location they seek carries a meaning, and they choose that meaning willingly.

**The Sincere Who Could Not Go**

The same passage carries the other side of the comparison, and the contrast is the sūrah's way of judging the men who asked to stay. The Book exempts the weak, the sick and those who find nothing to spend, provided they are sincere towards God and His Messenger (9:91), and it records the men who came to the Prophet asking to be carried and who turned away with tears running down their faces because there was nothing to mount them on (9:92). The two groups wanted opposite things from the same expedition, and the sūrah makes the difference plain: one asked for an excuse from a position of strength, the other asked for a place and was given none. The verses after this one finish the account of the men who stayed: they are content to be left with those who remain behind, their hearts are sealed, and the Messenger and the believers who are with him strive with their wealth and their lives, and the reward the Book names for them is the gardens (9:87-88). The verse in front of us is one entry in that reckoning, and the honesty of the Book is that it records both kinds of men and names which kind the request to stay puts a person among.

---

## Verse 9:87

> They preferred to stay behind with the helpless, and their hearts have been sealed so they do not comprehend

**Preference Reveals the Direction of the Heart**

The hypocrites preferred to remain with those who were not required to fight. The problem is not that the helpless stayed behind. The problem is that the able chose the same position to escape a duty. A lawful category becomes a disguise when a person enters it by preference rather than by genuine limitation.

The verse makes preference central. They were not merely overwhelmed by circumstances. They wanted the position of those who could not be blamed. This shows how the heart seeks not only comfort but a respectable explanation for comfort. The person does not want to say, “I refuse.” He wants to say, “I belong among those for whom refusal is not blameworthy.”

**The Helpless Deserve Honor and Care**

The reference to the helpless should never be read as contempt for them. The weak, sick, elderly, and dependent can have great faith and great moral worth. Their exemption protects them from blame, while their presence in the community remains valuable. They may teach, advise, care for families, pray, and support the mission within their ability.

---

## Verse 9:88
> But the Messenger and the believers with him strived with their wealth and their lives. They will have all the best, and it is they who will be successful

**The Messenger Leads By Sharing the Cost**

The verse puts the Messenger ﷺ and the believers who stayed with him on one side: they strive with their wealth and their lives. Leadership here is not a command shouted from safety while others carry the weight. The Messenger spends his money, his strength, his time and his own safety alongside the community. That is what makes the command credible, because he is doing what he asks of others.

This came in a hard season. The community was being called out on a long expedition in the heat, at a time of scarcity, when the distance was great and the harvest was at hand. The verses around this one describe the people who found excuses, the ones who asked to be excused, and the ones who were glad to stay behind. Against all of that, this verse records the answer of the Prophet ﷺ and those with him: they went, and they paid for it out of their own possessions.

**What Striving With Wealth and Life Means**

The Book uses the pairing of wealth and life as the fullest description of sacrifice, because between them they cover everything a person has. Striving with wealth means funding the journey, supplying the fighters, feeding the poor and keeping the community standing. Striving with life means going out yourself and putting your body where the risk is.

The same pair appears in the promises made to this group. Earlier in the sūrah, God promises those who believe, migrate and strive in His way that they will have the highest rank, and that they are the successful ones (9:20 states the rank). The verse after that names what they are given: good news of mercy from God, His pleasure, and gardens of lasting bliss (9:21 states the three). This verse's phrase, that they will have all the best, is that whole list summed up.

**All the Best Includes Both Worlds**

The promise of all the best is wide. The commentators understood it to cover good in this life and reward in the next. Honest effort can produce a good name, a useful example, a stronger community and inward dignity, and its greatest return still stays with God.

The promise does not mean that every effort ends in visible success. A believer may strive and still meet loss, criticism or death. The good may appear as a faith kept, a wrong resisted, or a later generation made stronger. God's measure takes in effects that the person himself cannot see, and the best is not always the outcome he first asked for.

**What the Verse Says About Following Leaders**

One more thing follows from the verse's pairing. The community is told the standard their leaders are held to. Someone who calls others to sacrifice while keeping himself out of it is not following this pattern, and the Book is careful to show that the leader here carried the same burden he asked the people to carry. That is why the verse names the Messenger and the believers together, in one sentence, with one verb.

---
## Verse 9:89

> Allah has prepared for them Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph

**The Reward Is Prepared Before the Servant Arrives**

The verse says Allah has prepared the Gardens for the Messenger and believers who strive. Preparation suggests care, certainty, and a reward beyond what the servant can build for himself. The Garden is not an accidental result of human effort. It is Allah's generosity, promised to those who receive it through faith and obedience.

This wording gives courage during sacrifice. A servant may not see the result of his work in the world, and he may not have the means to create lasting security. Allah has prepared a home that no enemy can seize and no time can decay. The believer acts without demanding a visible monument because the reward is already known to Allah.

**Forever Answers the Fear of Losing Home**

The phrase “to stay there forever” answers the instability of worldly homes. A person may lose a house through poverty, war, illness, or death. Even a secure dwelling eventually passes to someone else. The eternal Garden offers a home whose possession is not threatened by age, conflict, or inheritance disputes.

---

## Verse 9:90

> Some nomadic Arabs ˹also˺ came with excuses, seeking exemption. And those who were untrue to Allah and His Messenger remained behind ˹with no excuse˺. The unfaithful among them will be afflicted with a painful punishment

**Not Every Request for Exemption Has the Same Meaning**

The verse distinguishes between nomadic Arabs who came seeking exemption and others who stayed behind while being untrue to Allah and His Messenger. Exegetes differed over the reading of the phrase: it can describe people making excuses without a valid reason, or people who came with genuine excuses. The surrounding principles require this distinction to be handled carefully rather than treating every late request as hypocrisy.

**The Nomadic Setting Does Not Remove Responsibility**

The mention of nomadic Arabs does not make geography a moral defect. People living outside the city may have less access to teaching, fewer resources, and different social pressures. These conditions can affect knowledge and ability. The verse addresses those among them who were untrue, not every person from a particular way of life.

This guards the believer from class contempt. Urban status does not guarantee faith, and rural distance does not prove ignorance or hypocrisy. Allah judges knowledge, opportunity, intention, and action. Communities should teach people who have less access to guidance rather than using distance as an excuse to dismiss them.

---

The verse distinguishes the men of the desert who came with excuses from those who simply stayed behind, and the two groups receive different treatment in the verses that follow: this group is included in the general warning (9:91–92), while those who lied to God and His Messenger are named as the ones the punishment will reach. The commentators noted that the excuse-makers at least presented themselves and asked, which is a lesser degree than those who simply defaulted. The sūrah's handling of the two groups is the same graduated treatment it applies to the three who stayed behind and then told the truth (9:118): the honesty of the account matters.

## Verse 9:91

> There is no blame on the weak, the sick, or those lacking the means ˹if they stay behind˺, as long as they are true to Allah and His Messenger. There is no blame on the good-doers. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Truthfulness Is the Condition of Exemption**

The verse adds that they must be true to Allah and His Messenger. Exemption protects the sincere person, not the person who uses weakness as a costume. Truthfulness means that the person acknowledges the actual limit, remains loyal to the mission, and does not turn absence into contempt for those who serve.

A sick believer may grieve that he cannot go and still be free of blame. A healthy person who invents an illness to avoid sacrifice is in a different position. The outward result is the same, but the inward direction differs. Leaders therefore ask for honest information without demanding that the weak prove their worth through unnecessary pain.

**Naṣīḥah Is Sincere Loyalty**

The Arabic idea behind being true includes sincere counsel and faithful concern. It is more than a claim that one believes. A servant can be unable to participate physically and still show loyalty by wanting good for the community, warning it about danger, and supporting the work according to ability.

**Who Is Excused and Why**

The verse names the blind, the lame and the sick, and it lifts the obligation of going out to fight from them. The Sūrah of the Victory states the same three in the same breath, and it puts the whole rule in one line: there is no blame on the blind, the lame and the sick (48:17). The jurists took the passage as the base of a wider principle: an obligation that a person cannot physically carry out is lifted from him, and the burden falls on those who can. The Sūrah of the Cow closes its longest passage of prayer with the appeal that God should not burden the community beyond its strength (2:286), and the Sūrah of the Women states that God has preferred the men who strive above those who sit at home, while promising good to both (4:95).
**The Men Who Wanted to Fight Anyway**

The reports of the sīrah show what the excuse was worth in the wrong hands. ʿAmr ibn al-Jamūḥ was a lame man of Madinah who insisted on going to Uḥud with his sons and was killed there, and the Prophet's word about him was that God had excused him. The following verse records a different group, the men who came to be carried to the front and went away with their eyes full of tears because nothing was found for them to ride (9:92). The two scenes belong to the same chapter and are placed next to each other, and the arrangement tells the reader where the line runs: the exemption is for the man who cannot, and the grief is for the man who can and was left behind.
**The Condition Attached**

The verse adds a condition to the exemption: that they are truthful to God and His Messenger. The words mean that the excuse has to be the real state of affairs and not a costume, and the sūrah has just spent several verses dealing with men who produced illness, property and family as reasons when nothing was wrong with them. The Book's own way of naming the difference is to say that a person's loyalty shows in what he does when he is not required to do anything.

---

## Verse 9:92

> Nor ˹is there any blame on˺ those who came to you ˹O Prophet˺ for mounts, then when you said, “I can find no mounts for you,” they left with eyes overflowing with tears out of grief that they had nothing to contribute

**Desire to Serve Can Exist Without the Means**

The people described came to the Prophet seeking mounts so that they could join the campaign. They did not stay away because they preferred comfort. They wanted to go, but the means were unavailable. When they were told that no mounts could be found, they left grieving because they had nothing to contribute.

Their tears show the difference between inability and indifference. The hypocrites celebrated when they remained behind; these believers grieved when they were unable to go. Emotion alone does not prove sincerity, but here it agrees with the action: they came forward, asked for help, and accepted the answer rather than inventing an excuse. Allah knows the difference between a person prevented from service and a person who avoids it.

**The Mount Represents Access to Duty**

A person may want to study but lack access to a teacher, want to work but lack transport, or want to help but lack the equipment. Leaders should not interpret every absence as laziness before asking what means are missing. Sometimes a small provision unlocks a large act of service. The community becomes generous when it looks for those barriers instead of judging only the visible result.

**Who They Were**

The verse deals with the men who came to the Prophet to be given mounts for the campaign and were turned away because there was nothing to ride, and it says that they went away with their eyes full of tears because there was nothing for them to spend. The reports name them among the poorer companions, including members of the family of the Helpers, and the verse is placed after the exemption for the blind, the lame and the sick and before the passage on the men who stayed behind with their excuses. The Book records their tears and grants them their good intention, which is a statement about where the account of a deed is kept.
**What the Verse Settles About Intention**

The Book has already said, in the verses just before, that there is no blame on the weak, and now it names the grief of the men who had no means as something deserving of the same treatment. The Prophet's report in Bukhārī, that if a person intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it as a good deed for him, is the community's key to the whole passage, and the sūrah's own use of the word for what is written in the record is the base of it. The men who wept did everything that was in their power and were given the same rank as the men who rode.
**Where It Sits in the Chapter**

The sūrah is dealing with the campaign to Tabūk, and it has already given the excuses of the men who stayed behind while able to go. This verse interrupts them, and the arrangement tells the reader the difference the Book is drawing: the excuse is not the test, the truth about a person is. The verses that follow return to the hypocrites, and the contrast the sūrah sets up between the men in tears and the men who were comfortable is one of its sharpest portraits of a divided community.

---

## Verse 9:93

> Blame is only on those who seek exemption from you although they have the means. They preferred to stay behind with the helpless, and Allah has sealed their hearts so they do not realize ˹the consequences˺

**A Sealed Heart Loses Sight of Consequences**

The seal on their hearts is linked to their inability to realize the consequences. Some interpretations present the seal as a result of their actions; others emphasize how it blocks further understanding. The order of the passage supports a warning: repeated refusal can make a person less able to recognize why he is wrong.
This is why small compromises deserve attention. A servant who lies once can repent. If he defends the lie, repeats it, and rejects every correction, he is training the heart to resist truth. The seal is not a reason to delay repentance. It is a reason to return while understanding remains alive.
**Consequences Include Harm to Others**

A believer asks what his refusal makes others carry. Sometimes withdrawal is necessary and lawful. Sometimes it is a selfish transfer of cost. The difference can be discussed honestly when people are willing to explain their ability and accept a fair assignment. Allah's law protects the unable and exposes the person who knowingly shifts his duty to others.

**Who Is Exempt and Who Is Not**

The verse comes directly after a ruling about those who were excused from the march — the weak, the sick, the poor who had nothing to spend (9:91) — and this verse draws the other line: the ones who had the means, the strength, and the invitation, and stayed anyway. The Book does not leave the question of blame hanging; it says the blame is *upon those who ask you for exemption while they are rich*, who were content to be with the ones who stay behind, and whose hearts God sealed so that they do not understand. The comparison the passage draws is not between the sick and the healthy but between two kinds of unable: the man whose body stops him and the man whose preference does.
**What the Seal Says and Does Not Say**

The seal is the hardest part of the verse, and the Book treats it as an outcome rather than a verdict from outside. The message has run through the sūrah, the invitation has been given repeatedly, and the men in question have made a habit of staying home. What is being described is a settled condition, reached by a road they walked themselves; the previous verse notes that they preferred to be with those who remained, and this one names the end of the road. It is not a statement that a given man was created unable to answer — the passage is addressed to men asked to explain themselves, which would be meaningless if they had no capacity to answer. It is a warning about where repeated refusal goes, and the verse before it offers the door: turn to God, and the whole account changes.

## Verse 9:94

> They will make excuses to you ˹believers˺ when you return to them. Say, “Make no excuses, ˹for˺ we will not believe you. Allah has already informed us about your ˹true˺ state ˹of faith˺. Your ˹future˺ deeds will be observed by Allah and His Messenger as well. And you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen, then He will inform you of what you used to do.”

**A Late Explanation Cannot Undo an Earlier Choice**

The hypocrites offer excuses after the believers return. Their timing matters. They did not speak honestly before the mission, when an accurate report could have helped planning. They speak afterward, when the immediate danger has passed and the community is more likely to be pressured into accepting a convenient story.

The instruction to make no excuses rejects this try to manage the record. It does not teach believers to refuse every explanation. It teaches them not to accept explanations from people whose conduct has already exposed a settled pattern of deceit. Truthful accountability occurs before the duty when possible, not after the result has made the excuse necessary.

**Trust Can Be Lost Through Conduct**

The believers are told that they will not believe the excuses because Allah has informed them about the hypocrites' true state. This does not mean ordinary people have the unseen. It means that revelation and conduct have provided enough evidence for the community to stop treating these speakers as reliable.

---

The excuse offered on the believers' return is answered by an argument the sūrah has already established: God informed the community of these men's news before the march, and the report of their conduct arrives before their own words do (9:94). The Book's rule here is that the moment for an explanation has passed — a person who could have spoken honestly before the campaign and chose not to cannot repair the matter with a story afterwards. The next verse completes the answer with the command to leave them alone, and the chapter's final word on the group is given at 9:96, that God is not pleased with them.

**What Comes Next in the Sūrah**

The verses after this one complete the picture, and they are the answer to the excuses: the men will swear to the community so that it may be pleased with them, and the Book says that if the believers are pleased with them, God is not pleased with a people who have left the faith (9:96). The passage then turns and addresses the Prophet with a sentence about the men and women of the hypocrites (9:97-98) and moves on to the Bedouin whose excuses were sincere and those who were lying. The four verses on the return from Tabūk are one of the sūrah's most detailed treatments of a single episode, and the Book spends them on the difference between a community that forgives and a community that is fooled.
**The Ruling About Oaths**

The jurists drew a rule from the passage about accepting an oath from a person whose character is known: the oath is taken as a legal device to settle a case, and it is not a statement about what God knows. The Sūrah of the Cow states the principle behind the rule at the end of its passage on debt, where the believers are told that if one of them trusts another, the trusted one should hand back what he holds and fear God (2:283). The verse in front of us warns the community against letting the volume of an apology stand in for the truth, and it gives the reason in its own words: God is well informed of what they do.
**The Sentence the Verse Leaves**

The words that God and His Messenger will see the deeds and then the people will return to the Knower of the unseen is the whole answer, and it is addressed to the men who thought that a campaign they had skipped was a private matter. The Book recasts it as something the community and its messenger were still going to see. A person who spends the campaign in Madinah does not keep it hidden by staying off the road; the record follows him into the council of the returning army, and the verse gives him the only response the Book ever recommends for that situation, which is the truth.

## Verse 9:95

> When you return, they will swear to you by Allah so that you may leave them alone. So leave them alone—they are truly evil. Hell will be their home as a reward for what they have committed

**Turning Away Can Be a Boundary**

The command to leave them alone is not permission to admire their conduct or to pretend that no wrong occurred. It is a direction to withdraw trust and social approval from people whose presence is spiritually defiling. Continued argument may give their excuses more space than their conduct deserves.

**Defilement Describes Moral Contagion**

This is why leaders must consider who is given access to public trust. A person may be welcomed as a human being without being given control of funds, information, or vulnerable people. Distance from a corrupt influence can protect the community while leaving a path for the person to repent. Boundaries need not become hatred.

**Mercy Must Be Guided by Knowledge**

A believer can say, “I will not continue this conversation,” without saying, “You can never return.” He can protect a community while refusing to delight in the person's downfall. This balance keeps the boundary clean. It prevents mercy from becoming gullibility and firmness from becoming revenge.

---

The command to leave them alone is the political result of the verses: they have sworn to be left alone and the community is told to do exactly that, without the trust or the standing that belonged to the believers. The chapter has already stated the separation in doctrinal terms — the men are unclean and their home is Hell (9:95) — and the closing words, "as a recompense for what they used to earn", put the loss where the sūrah consistently places it. The instruction to the Prophet not to pray over them at their death is given in the same sūrah (9:84), and the two rulings stand together: no civic trust and no share in the community's worship for those who betrayed it.

## Verse 9:96

> They will swear to you in order to please you. And even if you are pleased with them, Allah will never be pleased with the rebellious people

**Human Approval Cannot Repair Rebellion**

The hypocrites swear to please the believers. Their purpose is the audience's satisfaction, not Allah's satisfaction. The verse does not deny the value of reconciliation between people. It exposes reconciliation built on a false story. A community may feel calmer after hearing an oath, but calm is not the same as truth.

**A Leader's Contentment Has a Limit**

The verse says that even if the believers become pleased, Allah will not be pleased with the rebellious. This limits human authority. Leaders may forgive, reconcile, and decide not to pursue a matter, but they cannot declare rebellion acceptable before Allah. Human contentment can remove a worldly consequence; it cannot rewrite divine judgment.

This also protects people from becoming dependent on human approval. A servant can be forgiven by a leader and still need to repent before Allah. Conversely, a leader can remain displeased while Allah accepts a sincere servant's repentance. The two forms of contentment must not be confused. Human judgment matters in social dealings, but Allah's pleasure is the final measure.

**The Oaths of the Men of Tabūk**

The verse belongs to the passage the sūrah gives to the men who stayed behind from the expedition to Tabūk, and the oaths it describes have their own place in the account. The verses before it say that they will swear to you when you return to them, so that you may leave them alone (9:95), and the Book tells the Prophet not to accept it: they are a defilement, and their resting place is Hell. The same sureness is stated at the head of the sūrah when the Book says that whether you ask forgiveness for them or not, God will not forgive them (9:80), and the sūrah of the Hypocrites records their oaths offered as a screen (63:2). The Book is not telling the community to distrust every apology it hears. It is naming a case where the oath was manufactured to manage an audience, and it draws the line the verse states: the pleasure of men can end the argument in the street, and it cannot change the answer the men are walking towards.

**A Limit on the Community's Mercy**

The verse leaves a lesson for the men who hold authority in the community, because the Prophet is addressed here and told that even his own satisfaction would not reach the matter. The Book's practice is to separate the two accounts without confusing them. A wrong that touches the rights of people can be settled between them, and the sūrah tells the believers that the way back for a wrongdoer is to repent and make amends (9:104), and it holds out forgiveness for the men who come honestly (9:106; 9:117-118). What it refuses is the forgiveness that treats the wrong against God as a social inconvenience to be smoothed over. The men of this verse were not asking to be forgiven; they were asking to be believed, and the verse says that the community's goodwill is not the thing they should be counting on.

---

## Verse 9:97

> The nomadic Arabs ˹around Medina˺ are far worse in disbelief and hypocrisy, and less likely to know the laws revealed by Allah to His Messenger. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Distance From Teaching Can Increase Vulnerability**

The verse describes nomadic Arabs as more severe in disbelief and hypocrisy and less likely to know the limits revealed to the Messenger. The setting matters. People living far from the city and from regular instruction may have less access to learning, communal worship, and correction. Distance can make ignorance easier to preserve.

**Severity Is a Moral Description, Not a Tribal Insult**

The passage speaks about hypocrites among the nomads, not about ancestry as a fixed defect. A person's origin does not determine faith. The same Qur'an recognizes sincere nomadic believers and distinguishes them from those who treat giving as loss or wait for the community's misfortune.

This prevents the believer from turning a contextual warning into racism or class pride. City dwellers can be hypocritical and ignorant of Allah's limits, while a person far from public institutions can be deeply sincere. The measure is knowledge, opportunity, intention, and conduct. Allah's knowledge and wisdom ensure that no person is judged merely for geography.

---

The verse's judgement on the desert Arabs is comparative rather than absolute, and the next verses show why: the ones near Madinah had more opportunity to see the revelation's working and less excuse. The exceptions are named twice in the same passage, at 9:99 for the believing nomads and at 9:101 for the hypocrites among the tribes and in the city itself, so the sūrah refuses a blanket verdict on any group. The Book's general principle is stated in the sūrah of the Chambers, that the most noble in God's sight is the most mindful (49:13), which makes nearness to the source of teaching an advantage only when it is used.

## Verse 9:98

> And among the nomads are those who consider what they donate to be a loss and await your misfortune. May ill-fortune befall them! And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**A Donation Can Feel Like Loss to a Selfish Heart**

Some nomads regard what they spend as a loss. The problem is not that giving reduces a balance on paper. The problem is the belief that nothing is gained through obedience. They see only the object leaving their hand and do not see gratitude, social repair, spiritual reward, or Allah's promise. Their account has become so narrow that charity appears irrational.

A believer also notices the cost of giving. He may need to plan, reduce a desire, or postpone a purchase. Faith does not require pretending that sacrifice has no material effect. It teaches the servant to place the material cost inside a larger account. What is lost from immediate possession may be gained as protection for a person, dignity for a family, or nearness to Allah.

**They Wait for the Believers to Fail**

The believer should examine whether he carries a smaller form of this waiting. Does another person's success make him secretly wish for reversal? Does he give support only while the group is strong? Loyalty is tested when the cause is vulnerable. A sincere servant may correct mistakes, but he does not enjoy the collapse of people who are trying to obey Allah.

---

## Verse 9:99

> However, among the nomadic Arabs are those who believe in Allah and the Last Day, and consider what they donate as a means of coming closer to Allah and ˹receiving˺ the prayers of the Messenger. It will certainly bring them closer. Allah will admit them into His mercy. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**The Same Setting Contains Sincere Believers**

After criticizing some nomadic Arabs, the verse identifies others who believe in Allah and the Last Day. This correction is essential. A social setting may create certain pressures, but it does not determine every person's heart. The Qur'an refuses to turn a contextual warning into an universal condemnation.

**Giving Can Bring the Servant Near**

The verse describes charity as a means of approaching Allah. Nearness is not physical distance. It is a change in the servant's relation to the Lord: gratitude deepens, attachment weakens, and the heart becomes more willing to obey. A donation can therefore be an act of inner travel even when the giver never leaves his home.

This nearness is not purchased by an amount. A small gift can be spiritually significant when it is given with sincerity and lawful intention. A large gift can be empty if it seeks status. The believer gives what he can, asks Allah to accept it, and remembers that Allah's mercy is the true source of nearness.

---

The verse's mention of the Messenger's prayer for the givers is the answer to a ruling question the community raised: the Prophet ﷺ prayed for those who brought their alms, and the verse states that his prayer is a source of comfort for them — the commentators called this one of the special privileges attached to his person. The same sufficiency of prayer is stated in the sūrah of the Repentance's earlier passage on the alms of the Bedouin, and the Book's general law is that prayer benefits the believers (9:103). What distinguishes these men from the others is that they gave with the intention of nearness, which is the standard the whole passage has been applying.

**What the Verse Names**

The verse names the believers among the desert people and describes them as believing in God and the Last Day and taking what they spend as a means of nearness to God and of the Prophet's prayers, and it says that it will bring them near and that God will admit them to His mercy. The passage comes after the verses on the men of the desert who took their spending as a loss and waited for a turn in the community's fortune (9:98), and the Book's arrangement puts the two kinds of men in the same stretch so that no reader takes the general criticism for the whole class.
**Why the Sūrah Makes the Distinction**

The Book's manner with the tribes was to distinguish a group from the people it belonged to, and the sūrah does it in several places: the men of the desert who made excuses and the men of the desert who were sincere (9:90-99), the Emigrants and the Helpers together at the front of the line (9:100). The Prophet's own word about the Bedouin in several reports and the practice of sending messengers to the tribes belong with the passage, and the community's leaders used the verse whenever the question of the nomad tribes was raised in the early years.
**What the Verse Says About Spending**

The sentence about taking the spending as a means of nearness is the part the jurists used, since it names the intention behind a gift as a matter that God accepts and that the community prays over. The verse says that the men's giving and the Prophet's prayer for them will bring them near to God, and it ends with the promise of mercy and forgiveness. The Book's answer to the men who saw their own spending as a loss is stated in one sentence, and the chapter then goes on to the men who were the first of the community in belief.

## Verse 9:100

> As for the foremost—the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers—and those who follow them in goodness, Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. And He has prepared for them Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. That is the ultimate triumph

**Following Them Requires Goodness**

The verse includes those who follow the foremost with goodness. It does not ask later generations to copy every outward circumstance. Later believers may not migrate from the same city or face the same danger. They follow through sincere faith, support for truth, generosity, courage, and the refusal to exploit the community they inherited.
**Gardens Confirm the Value of Early Faith**

The prepared Gardens show that the first sacrifices are remembered. People who act early can feel forgotten when later generations enjoy the benefits of their work. Allah's promise corrects that feeling. The comfort enjoyed by later believers does not erase the cost paid by those who opened the path.
This also teaches later believers gratitude. They should not treat inherited guidance, institutions, or communal safety as though they appeared without labor. They receive a trust built by others and must preserve it with goodness. The Garden is promised to those who follow faithfully, not to those who consume an inheritance while weakening its foundations.

**Who the Three Groups Are**

The verse names three: *al-sābiqūn al-awwalūn* from among the Emigrants and the Helpers — the first of those who made the move and those who took them in — and then *those who followed them with goodness*. The first two are the founding generation, and the third is everyone after them who follows the same path in the same spirit. The verse does not make the later generations' standing depend on birth or on having been present at the founding; it makes it depend on the following, which is why it says *with goodness* rather than *afterwards*.
**What the Promise Covers**

God is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him — the sentence runs in both directions — and what is prepared is gardens with rivers beneath them, to remain in them forever, and the closing words call it the great attainment. The verse is the sūrah's answer to a question the community could not avoid asking once the founding generation began to die: whether the ones who came later had any share in what the first men died for. The answer given here is the strongest form the Book uses, the mutual pleasure of God and the servant, and it is attached to the following rather than to the lineage.
**What the Following Requires**

The sūrah has spent a hundred verses describing who belongs to the community and who does not, and this verse comes at the end of a section on the hypocrites and the Bedouin who stayed behind. The contrast is deliberate. The followers are not asked to reproduce the same circumstances — they will not emigrate from the same city or face the same siege — they are asked for sincere faith, support for the truth, generosity, courage, and a refusal to eat an inheritance while weakening what they inherited. The men who opened the road are remembered by the promise, and the men who walk it are promised the same gardens.

## Verse 9:101

> Some of the nomads around you ˹believers˺ are hypocrites, as are some of the people of Medina. They have mastered hypocrisy. They are not known to you ˹O Prophet˺; they are known to Us. We will punish them twice ˹in this world˺, then they will be brought back ˹to their Lord˺ for a tremendous punishment

**Hypocrisy Can Live Close to the Believers**

The verse warns that hypocrisy exists among nomads around the believers and among the people of Medina. Distance from the city does not create hypocrisy, and nearness to the Prophet does not prevent it. A person can hear teaching, attend gatherings, and use the language of faith while protecting a different allegiance in the heart.

**They Have Mastered the Art of Concealment**

The expression that they have mastered hypocrisy suggests persistence and skill. Their falsehood is not a momentary fear. They know how to present loyalty, when to swear, and how to remain close enough to benefit from the community while avoiding its sacrifices. Hypocrisy becomes an art when the person spends more effort managing appearances than repairing the heart.

A servant should watch for this craft in himself. He may learn which words make him look sincere, which causes win praise, and which confessions make people stop asking questions. Those skills are not repentance. They are only better concealment. Truthful faith becomes simpler over time because the servant no longer needs a separate public and private story.

---

The verse names hypocrisy as a condition that can be practised so regularly that it becomes a skill — "they have grown accustomed to hypocrisy" — and it distinguishes the two populations, the tribes around Madinah and the people of the city itself. The reports tell of men who appeared among the believers while keeping contact with their opponents, and the sūrah has already described their conduct in detail: mocking (9:65), plotting (9:74), and building a base against the community (9:107). The closing words, that God will punish them twice and then they return to a great punishment, are the chapter's formula for a double reckoning, once in this world's exposure and once in the next.

The verse names two groups in the same sentence: the hypocrites among the desert people around the community, and the men of Madinah itself who persist in hypocrisy, and it says that the community is not aware of them but that God knows them, and that He will punish them twice and then they will be returned to a great punishment. The Book's arrangement puts the two together because the passage before this one names the men who came with excuses and the men who were sincere, and the verse in front of us states that the same towns and the same desert held both kinds of people.

**What the Commentators Said**

The older commentators read the double punishment differently: some took it as the punishment of this life and the punishment of the grave, some as the shame of exposure among the community and the torment after death, and others as the decline and the loss that came to the men as their plots failed. The Sūrah of the Parties gives the same account of the men who were known and the men who were not (33:60), and the Prophet's own words about the three signs of the hypocrite are read with the passage. The Book's sentence about God knowing them is the answer to the question of why they were tolerated.

**Where the Passage Stands**

The sūrah has been dealing with the campaign to Tabūk since its middle, and the passage in front of us is part of its sorting of the community: the men who stayed behind, the men in tears, the sincere of the desert, the hypocrites of both places, and the first believers who went ahead. The verses after it name the men of the community who were the first in belief and promise them the gardens (9:100), and the arrangement gives the reader the two lists and leaves him to find himself in one of them. The sūrah's purpose is the purification of a community that had just been tested by a long march, and the verse in front of us is one of its sharpest warnings.

## Verse 9:102
> Some others have confessed their wrongdoing: they have mixed goodness with evil. It is right to hope that Allah will turn to them in mercy. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Good and Evil Mixed in One Life**

The verse describes a mixed record. These people have done some good and some wrong, and it puts them in a third category, between the firm believers and the open hypocrites. Such a mixture is a realistic picture of human life. It is also not an excuse for complacency.

Two false calculations have to be resisted here. The first says that a person does enough good that his wrong does not matter. The second says that a person has done so much wrong that his good is pointless. The Book rules out both. Each act stands in its own place before God, and the response the verse is looking for is not despair and not self-satisfaction. It is to keep what is good, leave what is wrong, and ask God to purify what has been mixed.

**What Is Said About This Group**

The sūrah tells the reader what happens to a confession like this. The verses around it describe people who admitted their wrong and mixed a good deed with another that was evil, and then say that God may turn to them, since He is forgiving and merciful (9:102 and the following verse carry both). The mercy here is not a blanket covering that leaves the lives unchanged. It is attached to the admission and to the turn that follows.

The Prophet ﷺ was asked about one group who tied themselves to the mosque pillars until he released them, and the report shows what the community's response looked like in practice. Those who confessed were dealt with as people who had a way back, and the restoration was made in the open, within the community, rather than by silencing the matter.

**What the Community Learns From It**

The passage teaches a community how to hold firmness and hope together. Name the wrong. Ask for change. Leave the door of return open. A community that only accuses produces despair, and a community that only excuses produces corruption. The verse's own group is the proof that both errors can be avoided at once.

It also protects the person who has failed from the two traps that follow failure. He is not told that his good deeds are worthless, which would push him toward abandoning them. He is not told that his bad deeds are covered, which would let him keep doing them. He is told that the mixture can be purified, which is the only instruction that leaves a person with something to do.

**How Repentance Works Here**

The Book's pattern is consistent in this sūrah. Repentance reorganizes a life rather than adding one more religious phrase to it. The sūrah has already described the people who turn, set their conduct right, and hold fast to God (9:104 and the verses around it describe the pattern of return). It has also warned that excuses offered instead of action do not change anything.

So the verse in front of the reader gives the reader a way to read his own record without flinching and without giving up. The mixture is acknowledged. The two false exits are closed. And the turn back is left standing open before the person is asked for anything else.

---
## Verse 9:103

> Take from their wealth ˹O Prophet˺ charity to purify and bless them, and pray for them—surely your prayer is a source of comfort for them. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**Charity Can Purify a Moral Debt**

The verse directs the Prophet to take charity from the wealth of those who confessed their wrongdoing. The gift is not a bribe that purchases forgiveness. It is a practical sign that repentance has reached the person's possessions. Wrongdoing often leaves traces in what a person owns, withholds, or has used selfishly. Releasing wealth for Allah helps undo the grip that the wrong had on the heart.

This is why charity can purify. It trains the servant to acknowledge that wealth is not an untouchable extension of the ego. He gives from what he wants to keep and learns that obedience can be expressed through the hand as well as the tongue. The act does not erase every obligation, but it turns repentance into a visible change.

**Purification Is More Than Financial Transfer**

Purification requires lawful wealth and sound distribution. A person cannot use a gift of doubtful origin to declare himself clean while refusing to return what belongs to another. Restitution comes first when a specific person's right was taken. Charity then expresses general repentance and gratitude. The believer asks a knowledgeable guide what form of repair is required rather than using generosity to avoid a direct obligation.

---

The charity described here is attached to the men who had confessed their failure and asked what they could do; the report states that the Prophet ﷺ took a third of their wealth as alms and prayed for them, and the verse establishes the purpose of the payment: it purifies them and blesses them. The jurists took the words as a statement of the inner work of zakāh, and the Book states the same double effect in the sūrah of the Cow, where alms purify the owner's wealth and heart (2:261, 2:264). The instruction to pray for the givers is applied in the community's practice afterwards: the one who collects the alms is to supplicate for the one who paid it.

## Verse 9:104

> Do they not know that Allah alone accepts the repentance of His servants and receives ˹their˺ charity, and that Allah alone is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful

**Repentance Is Received, Not Performed**

A servant may perform the outward steps of repentance and still need sincerity. Turning away from the wrong, regretting it, and resolving not to return are not a performance for an audience. They are a return of the whole person toward Allah. The verse's language of acceptance reminds the servant that repentance is a gift he asks Allah to receive.

This prevents pride after reform. A person may have apologized, repaid, or changed his habits, yet he still hopes rather than boasts. He does not claim that his own effort forced Allah's acceptance. He does what Allah loves and asks Allah to complete the mercy. Humility keeps repentance alive after the first emotion fades.

**Charity Is Received Beyond Its Visible Channel**

When Allah receives charity, the gift's material route does not exhaust its meaning. The money may go to a poor person, a public need, or a lawful cause. Allah receives the intention, sacrifice, and obedience behind it. This is why a small gift can be accepted while a large gift offered for pride may be spiritually empty.

---

## Verse 9:105

> Tell ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Do as you will. Your deeds will be observed by Allah, His Messenger, and the believers. And you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen, then He will inform you of what you used to do.”

**The Messenger and Believers Observe Conduct**

The verse also mentions the Messenger and the believers observing deeds. This can refer to public conduct in this world or to its disclosure in the Hereafter. Either way, it teaches that communal life needs evidence. Repentance is inward, but its effects reach other people. Trust, leadership, and responsibility are assigned according to what conduct shows.

The servant will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen. This return prevents action from being treated as a temporary performance. A person may escape criticism today, but he cannot escape the final account. The unseen motive, the private conversation, and the unrecorded harm all belong to the knowledge of Allah.

**The Command Encourages Rather Than Abandons**

“Do as you will” can sound severe, but in context it places responsibility in the servant's hands. Allah has made the path clear, and the person now chooses what his deeds will testify. The command does not abandon him to meaninglessness. It warns that freedom is accountable.

---

The command to "do as you will" is an exposure rather than a permission: it states that the conduct of the waverers is already visible and will be recorded. The sūrah has established the same standard twice in the passage, in the observation of the companions' deeds (9:94) and in the warning to those who mocked (9:65), and the Book applies it to the whole community in the sūrah of the Cave, where every person's work is what will be shown on the Day (18:49). The closing words return the matter to the One who knows the seen and the unseen, and the verse that follows makes the point practical: the believers are told to act, and their action will be seen.

## Verse 9:106

> And some others are left for Allah’s decision, either to punish them or turn to them in mercy. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Not Every Case Is Immediately Resolved**

The verse describes people whose case is left for Allah's decision. They are not placed among the clear hypocrites who defended falsehood, and they are not yet described with the open confession and repair of those who repented. Their position remains unresolved in the community's immediate judgment.

This teaches patience with cases that do not fit easy labels. A person may have failed seriously without lying about the failure, yet may not have taken the steps needed for repair. Leaders may need to limit access and wait for evidence rather than announce a final verdict. The pause is not indecision without principle. It is an acknowledgment that Allah knows more than people know and that time can reveal whether a heart turns toward repentance or persists in avoidance.

**Waiting Can Be a Discipline**

The community may be commanded to wait because immediate action would be unjust or premature. Waiting does not mean ignoring the harm. It can include social boundaries, careful observation, private counsel, and prayer. The person under review experiences uncertainty, but uncertainty can make a hidden intention visible.

A servant should not demand that every painful situation receive instant closure. Some judgments need facts, repentance, and time. He can protect people without pretending that he knows the final state of the one who failed. Wise waiting is active: it preserves safety while leaving room for Allah to open a path of return.

---

## Verse 9:107

> There are also those ˹hypocrites˺ who set up a mosque ˹only˺ to cause harm, promote disbelief, divide the believers, and as a base for those who had previously fought against Allah and His Messenger. They will definitely swear, “We intended nothing but good,” but Allah bears witness that they are surely liars

**A Former Enemy Can Use an Internal Base**

The mosque is also described as a base for those who had fought Allah and His Messenger. This shows how internal betrayal can connect to external hostility. The problem is not cooperation with every non-Muslim; lawful agreements and peaceful relationships remain distinct. The problem is giving a hostile force a platform from which to attack the believing community.

**Religious Trust Requires Moral Foundations**

This warning should not make believers cynical about institutions. It should make them careful stewards. Build places of worship, but build them on truth. Welcome people, but do not hand the trust to those who use it for division. Allah knows the real purpose, and a community that seeks His pleasure will prefer a modest truthful space to an impressive building founded on harm.

The phrase “we intended nothing but good” also teaches that claimed intention must be compared with the whole design. A project that consistently divides believers cannot be defended by pointing to one charitable event. A leader who says he wants unity but rewards slander has revealed the real purpose through the reward. The believer does not read hearts, but he can read patterns. He asks what the institution funds, whom it excludes, and what kind of speech becomes normal inside it. Truthful projects welcome such questions because accountability strengthens them; harmful projects call accountability an attack because secrecy is part of their foundation.

---

The mosque the verse describes is the one built by the hypocrites at Qubāʾ, and the reports name the man behind it, Abū ʿĀmir, who had gone over to the enemy at the conquest of Makkah and asked that a mosque be prepared for him. The four purposes the verse lists — harm, disbelief, division of the believers, and a base for those who had fought God and His Messenger — are the tests the community is given for distinguishing a place of worship from a piece of strategy. After the return from Tabūk the Prophet ﷺ sent men who dismantled and burned the structure, and the sūrah's next verse states the standard applied to it: a mosque founded on righteousness from the first day.

## Verse 9:108

> Do not ˹O Prophet˺ ever pray in it. Certainly, a mosque founded on righteousness from the first day is more worthy of your prayers. In it are men who love to be purified. And Allah loves those who purify themselves

**A Sacred Act Must Stand on a Sound Foundation**

The Prophet is told not to pray in the mosque built for harm. Prayer is not used to bless a corrupt purpose merely because the place carries the name of mosque. The command establishes a principle: worship must be joined to the moral foundation of the institution in which it is publicly performed.

**Righteousness Begins Before the Building Opens**

The worthy mosque is founded on righteousness from the first day. Its purpose is set before the walls rise. The builders ask whom the place will serve, how it will be funded, what teaching it will support, and whether it will bring people toward Allah or toward factional pride.

Foundations are moral before they are architectural. A building raised through injustice carries a question that decoration cannot answer. A modest place built through lawful means, sincere prayer, and service may be more valuable than a grand institution designed to display influence. The first intention shapes what later activity can become.

---

The mosque the verse points to is the one founded on righteousness from the beginning — the reports identify it with the mosque of Qubāʾ, the first mosque the Prophet ﷺ built on the migration, or with his own mosque in Madinah — and the men praised in it are those who love to purify themselves. The verse closes with the principle the chapter has been arguing in the preceding verses: God loves those who purify themselves, which makes the intention of the builder and the worship of the people, rather than the structure's name, the measure of a place of prayer.

## Verse 9:109

> Which is better: those who laid the foundation of their building on the fear and pleasure of Allah, or those who did so on the edge of a crumbling cliff that tumbled down with them into the Fire of Hell? And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people

**Every Building Rests on a Moral Foundation**

The verse compares two builders. One lays a foundation on reverence for Allah and the desire for His pleasure. The other builds on the edge of a crumbling cliff and falls with it into the Fire. The image asks the reader to evaluate not the height of the building but the ground beneath it.

A project may be large, efficient, and admired while resting on falsehood. A family, charity, school, or public movement can look strong yet depend on deception, injustice, or factional hatred. The first question is therefore not “How impressive is it?” but “What will remain when pressure tests the foundation?” Reverence gives an institution a reason to serve beyond its own survival.

**Fear and Pleasure Belong Together**

The foundation is built on fear and pleasure of Allah. Fear keeps the builder from crossing limits, while desire for Allah's pleasure gives the work a positive aim. Fear without hope can produce paralysis or harshness. Hope without fear can produce careless claims of good intention.

**The Better Building Is Chosen by Its Ground**

The question “which is better?” expects an obvious answer, but it also challenges the viewer's priorities. People often compare size, visibility, attendance, and income. Allah compares reverence and purpose. A small place where people pray, learn, and purify themselves may be better than a large place that feeds division.

**The Two Foundations and the Ruling**

The verse describes two buildings, and the two are the masjid built for division, which the sūrah names in the verse before it, and the mosque founded on the fear of God. The Book asks which is better established, and it answers with the picture: the one that stands on the edge of a crumbling cliff is about to fall with whoever built it into the fire of Hell. The jurists read the passage as a rule about the moral ground of a building rather than about its stones, and they used it to argue that a house of worship built for a corrupt purpose cannot be made sound by its dedication.
**What Was Built and What Was Demolished**

The mosque at Ḍirār was built by men who had come back from a campaign with excuses and who wanted a base to talk business in without the Prophet present, and the sūrah says that they built it to divide the believers and to make a place for the man who had been working against the community. The Prophet was told not to stand in it, and after the campaign he ordered it taken down, and the report is in the collections. The verse in front of us gives the reason the demolition was ordered, and the next verse states that God guides the wrongdoers and the verse after that carries the sūrah forward to the reward of the men who bought their faith with their property and their lives.
**The Lesson the Community Carried**

The passage became one of the community's first lessons about institutions and intention, and the reports about it were read in every generation as a warning about projects that are raised for a purpose other than what they claim. The Book's test is the one stated in the verse: a foundation of reverence and the desire for God's pleasure, or a building on a cliff. The sūrah puts the sentence at the end of a record of years of prevarication, and it names the ground rather than the structure, which is where the Book usually puts the question.

---

## Verse 9:110

> The building which they erected will never cease to fuel hypocrisy in their hearts until their hearts are torn apart. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**A Harmful Institution Keeps Working on the Heart**

The building they erected does not end with the moment of construction. It continues to fuel hypocrisy in their hearts. Institutions shape the people who maintain them. Every meeting, message, and public act can reinforce a false story until the members become more committed to protecting the institution than to seeking truth.

**Hypocrisy Thrives on Repeated Reinforcement**

Hypocrisy rarely survives through one dramatic statement alone. It is reinforced by repetition. People repeat the institution's explanation, defend its leaders, and reinterpret each harmful result as proof that outsiders are hostile. The heart becomes accustomed to double speech: public claims of good and private knowledge of harm.

The believer must interrupt this cycle early. Ask whether the project's facts agree with its stated purpose. Invite independent review. Listen to those harmed by the work instead of treating them as enemies by definition. A structure that cannot bear honest questions is not protected by its sacred language. It is revealing the weakness of its foundation.

---

## Verse 9:111

> Allah has indeed purchased from the believers their lives and wealth in exchange for Paradise. They fight in the cause of Allah and kill or are killed. This is a true promise binding on Him in the Torah, the Gospel, and the Quran. And whose promise is truer than Allah’s? So rejoice in the exchange you have made with Him. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph

**Life and Wealth Cover the Whole Person**

The verse names wealth and life together. Wealth includes property, skill, time, and the means by which a person supports others. Life includes the body, courage, attention, reputation, and the years available for obedience. The contract therefore reaches the whole person without requiring every believer to sacrifice in the same form.

**The Promise Crosses Sacred History**

The verse says that this promise appears in the Torah, the Gospel, and the Quran. The detail places the Muslim community within a wider history of revelation. The idea that faith requires sacrifice is not an invention of one generation. Earlier communities also received guidance that called them to give themselves to Allah.

This continuity honors the original revelations without erasing the distinct form of the Quran's message. It also teaches believers that truth is not validated by novelty. Allah's promises have a consistent moral direction across scripture: trust Him, give up false masters, and expect a reward greater than the present cost. The believer reads his sacrifice as part of that long covenantal pattern.

---

The verse states the exchange in commercial language — God buys the lives and wealth of the believers with the Garden — and it adds that the promise is written in the Torah, the Gospel and the Qur'an, which makes it the common ground of every revealed book. The reports connect the pledge to the allegiance given to the Prophet ﷺ, at ʿAqabah and at the pledge of Ridwān, where the Companions placed their hands in his hands on that condition. The closing words, "and who is more faithful to his covenant than Allah?", are the sūrah's answer to the excuse of the waverers, and the verse ends with the call to rejoice in the bargain, since that is the great success.

**The Transaction the Verse States**

The Book states the bargain in the plainest commercial terms: God has bought from the believers their persons and their wealth in return for the garden. It is a sale, and the sellers are the ones who accept the terms, which is why the verse is placed among the verses about spending in the path of God and about the men who stayed behind. The Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān gives the same sell in another form, where God promises those who fight and are killed forgiveness and mercy (3:157), and the Sūrah of the Cow warns the believers not to trade away their covenant for a small price when they are called to believe in what has been sent down (2:41).
**What the Verse Asks and What It Gives**

The verse names the two things the buyer receives: they fight in God's way and they kill and are killed. The Book does not promise that both outcomes will be reached by every person; it promises that the man on the field is either the one who kills or the one who is killed. The words used for the two fates belong to the register of exchange, and the readers of the verse are told that they have entered it deliberately.
**The Promise Recorded in the Two Books**

The clause that the promise is written in the Torah, the Gospel and the Qur'an is the verse's answer to the claim that the arrangement was invented by the new community. The Book makes the same claim about the covenant of the earlier communities in the Sūrah of the Cow (2:40) and about the disciples of Jesus in the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān (3:52), and it states here that the arrangement is the same line. The verse closes by naming the bargain as the great success, which is the same word the sūrah of the Table uses for the reward of the truthful (5:119).

## Verse 9:112

> ˹It is the believers˺ who repent, who are devoted to worship, who praise ˹their Lord˺, who fast, who bow down and prostrate themselves, who encourage good and forbid evil, and who observe the limits set by Allah. And give good news to the believers

**Repentance Is the First Quality Named**

The verse begins its list with repentance. The believer is not defined as someone who never fails. He is defined as someone who returns when he fails and refuses to make sin his permanent direction. Repentance keeps the other qualities alive because it removes the pride that would turn worship into a claim of superiority.

**Worship Gives the Life Its Center**

The worshippers devote themselves to Allah, and the list then names praise, fasting, bowing, and prostration. These forms join the heart, tongue, body, and appetite in obedience. Worship is not a private mood. It trains the servant to remember Allah in different conditions and to let remembrance direct daily conduct.

Fasting is described by commentators as a journey because it requires leaving ordinary enjoyments for a period and learning that desire does not command the person. Bowing and prostration teach bodily humility. Praise teaches gratitude in ease and hardship. Together the practices form a servant who can receive blessing without pride and endure difficulty without rebellion.

---

The verse's list is the sūrah's portrait of the believing community, and it is given here to answer the pictures of the hypocrites that have filled the chapter: they repent, worship, praise, fast, bow and prostrate, enjoin right, forbid wrong and keep the limits of God. The scholars drew the character of the believer from the accumulation: the qualities that follow one another in the list are all inward states expressed in action, and the chapter's closing verses attach the good news to them. The list appears in a similar form in the sūrah of the Ranks, where the same traits are named in the same order before the command to strive.

## Verse 9:113

> It is not ˹proper˺ for the Prophet and the believers to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they were close relatives, after it has become clear to the believers that they are bound for the Hellfire

**Prayer for Forgiveness Has a Boundary**

The verse establishes a boundary around seeking forgiveness for those who died in settled polytheism after their final state became clear. It is not a command to hate every non-Muslim, abandon kindness, or refuse guidance to a living person. It concerns a religious prayer after death when the person has no longer chosen faith and the truth of his condition is known.

Prayer is not an empty gesture. It expresses hope that Allah will forgive, and this hope must agree with what Allah has revealed. The Prophet and believers are instructed not to ask for what Allah has ruled out. This protects worship from being directed by emotion alone, even when the deceased was beloved.

**Clarity Matters in Applying the Rule**

The phrase “after it has become clear” limits careless application. Human beings do not know every person's final inner state. They cannot declare someone condemned merely because he was distant, confused, or described by rumor. Before death, the question of faith remains open. After death, public and established facts may make the religious status clear, but ordinary people must still avoid claiming knowledge beyond evidence.

---

The verse forbids seeking forgiveness for those who died in polytheism, and it makes the exception of the living explicit elsewhere in the Book: the Prophet ﷺ prayed for the man who had not yet believed and was told that it was for God to guide whom He wills (28:56). The chapter's rule was applied in the community's practice as a distinction between the living and the dead: mercy for the living by calling them to faith, and no petition for those who died in the state the Book has stated to be unforgivable except by repentance (4:48). The verse's closing words place the whole matter in God's knowledge of those who keep their duty.

## Verse 9:114

> As for Abraham’s prayer for his father’s forgiveness, it was only in fulfilment of a promise he had made to him. But when it became clear to Abraham that his father was an enemy of Allah, he broke ties with him. Abraham was truly tender-hearted, forbearing

**Compassion Can Exist Before the Final Clarity**

Abraham prayed for his father's forgiveness because of a promise he had made. The prayer arose from compassion and from hope that the relationship might change. It was not a claim that idolatry was acceptable. While a person is alive, the possibility of guidance remains, and a loving relative may pray for that guidance and forgiveness.

**Clarity Can Require a Change in Relationship**

When it became clear that the father was an enemy of Allah, Abraham broke ties with him in the religious sense. The change was not caused by a passing disagreement or personal irritation. It followed the recognition of a settled opposition to Allah. The verse teaches that affection has a boundary when a relationship is being used to fight truth.

Breaking ties does not require cruelty or revenge. It means refusing to treat the person's hostility as a harmless family difference. The believer may continue to act justly, fulfill ordinary rights, and avoid oppression while withdrawing religious solidarity and the forms of loyalty that strengthen hostility. Truth can alter a relationship without erasing the duty of justice.

---

The verse refers to Abraham's prayer for his father Āzar, which the Book records in the sūrah of Maryam, where he promises to ask forgiveness for him and says that he will not remain silent before his Lord (19:47), and in the sūrah of the Poets, where he prays for his father among those for whom he asks pardon (26:86). The disavowal is stated in the sūrah of the Tested Woman, where Abraham and those with him say to their people that they have nothing to do with them until they believe in God alone, with the single exception of the promise made to the father (60:4). The verse's conclusion — that he ceased when it became clear the man was an enemy of God — is the boundary that the whole passage has in view.

## Verse 9:115

> Allah would never consider a people deviant after He has guided them, until He makes clear to them what they must avoid. Surely Allah has ˹full˺ knowledge of everything

**Guidance Comes Before Blame**

The verse says that God would not leave a people to go astray after He had guided them until He made clear to them what they must avoid, and it closes by saying that God knows all things. The verse protects the reader from thinking that he can be held to account for a rule he never met.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The Book teaches that responsibility follows knowledge. A person is not punished for what he did before a command reached him, and a community is not held to a ruling that was never delivered to it. The same principle appears when the Book says that God does not charge a soul beyond what it can bear (2:286 states it at the close of the sūrah of the Cow) and when it names the men who were left with no path and no messenger to guide them (4:98 names the helpless who are excused). The verse states the rule with the word avoid, which means the thing a person is told to keep away from, and the sentence therefore covers both the command and the prohibition that came to the community.

**Why This Verse Was Given**

The passage around it deals with the men whose loyalty had been split and with the awkward questions the community kept asking. Some of the believers were troubled about the things they had done before the rulings arrived, and the verse answers their anxiety with a rule rather than a scolding. The Book had already made the point about the community's situation in this chapter: it told the Prophet that the men who came with excuses were not the same as the men who believed (9:81 describes the men who stayed behind, and 9:94 recounts the excuses they brought later). The verse in front of the reader makes the line clear: the accounting starts when the message arrives.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader Once the Message Arrives**

The other half of the rule is that knowledge ends the excuse. The sūrah had told the community to go out and learn what they needed to know, and it names the men who could be sent to gain understanding and warn their people when they returned (9:122 gives the instruction). A person who has been told what to avoid and then does it cannot claim afterwards that nobody told him. The Book makes the point with its usual plainness in the sūrah of the Heights, where the men are asked whether their messengers did not come to them and warn them (7:53 records the question and the answer). The verse in front of the reader tells the reader that his accounting begins the day the ruling reaches him, which is a comfort for what is past and a responsibility for what is coming.

**The Name at the End**

The verse closes by saying that God knows all things, which answers the question the reader might be left with: what about the cases nobody discussed, and the people whose position in the middle of the road was never clear? The Book says that God knows what is in the hearts and what people make public, and the sūrah in front of the reader keeps stating it (9:105 says that God sees what people do and that the matter returns to Him). The verse in front of the reader leaves the judgment with the One who knows what a person was able to know, and it leaves the reader with the duty to learn what has been sent to him.

---

## Verse 9:116

> Indeed, to Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And besides Allah you have no guardian or helper

**All Authority Returns to Allah**

The verse places the heavens and earth under Allah's kingdom. This is not only a statement about ownership. It is a statement about authority, judgment, and the right to command. Every human institution, family, and political power exists within Allah's kingdom and remains accountable to Him.

**Life and Death Are Divine Signs**

Allah gives life and causes death. The pair covers the beginning and end of bodily existence and points to the servant's dependence at every moment between them. No person creates his own life, and no person can command death to remain away when its appointed time arrives.

This knowledge produces humility without passivity. The believer protects life, seeks treatment, and avoids reckless danger because life is a trust. He also accepts that death is not a failure of Allah's control. Life and death belong to the same Lord, so both are placed within a meaningful account rather than left to chance.

The verse denies that anyone besides Allah can be the ultimate guardian or helper. Human beings can help one another, and Islam commands cooperation. The denial concerns independence. A friend, government, physician, or institution may be a means of protection, but none can guarantee the final outcome apart from Allah.

---

## Verse 9:117

> Allah has certainly turned in mercy to the Prophet as well as the Emigrants and the Helpers who stood by him in the time of hardship, after the hearts of a group of them had almost faltered. He then accepted their repentance. Surely He is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to them

**Divine Mercy Reaches the Whole Community**

The verse says Allah turned in mercy to the Prophet, the Emigrants, and the Helpers. Mercy is not limited to people who never felt pressure. A group of believers nearly faltered in a difficult hour, yet Allah accepted their repentance and renewed their bond with Him.
The inclusion of the Prophet alongside the two groups shows that divine turning encompasses the leadership and the community. It does not imply the same kind of fault for every person. It shows that Allah's mercy surrounds the whole effort and that correction can reach every level. A community should therefore not treat a moment of strain as proof that the entire mission has failed.
**The Difficult Hour Tests Real Commitment**

This gives believers a realistic account of courage. Courage is not the absence of a faltering heart. It is the return of the heart to obedience after faltering. A servant can admit that he was near retreat and still become faithful through repentance and renewed action. The community should support such return rather than demanding a false story of constant strength.

**The Hour of Difficulty**

The verse names the setting in two words: the hard time. It refers to the expedition to Tabūk in the ninth year, the longest march the community had made, in extreme heat and with the harvest waiting and supplies scarce. Some of the men wavered, and the verse says so plainly — the hearts of a group of them nearly turned aside — and then states the outcome for all of them together: God turned back to the Prophet and to the Emigrants and the Helpers, and He is kind and merciful to them. The verse after it turns to the three who stayed behind, whose case is described at the end of the same passage and whose relief is recorded as a mercy shown to them as well (9:118).
**Why the Honesty Matters**

The sūrah does not present a community of heroes. It says the pressure was so heavy that hearts nearly slipped, and it names the mercy that stopped them. The Book treats that moment as worth recording permanently, which is a comfort to every believer who has come near to a mistake and turned back from it. The sūrah's reader is meant to notice how the passage is put together: the waiver is stated, the mercy is stated, and the rank of those who went out for the sake of God is not withdrawn from them because of a night of hesitation (9:20).

## Verse 9:118

> And ˹Allah has also turned in mercy to˺ the three who had remained behind, ˹whose guilt distressed them˺ until the earth, despite its vastness, seemed to close in on them, and their souls were torn in anguish. They knew there was no refuge from Allah except in Him. Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent. Surely Allah ˹alone˺ is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful

**Honesty Can Be Painful Before It Becomes Liberating**

The three had remained behind without inventing an excuse. Their honesty did not spare them immediate distress. They waited under a public decision while the earth's vastness felt closed and their own souls pressed in upon them. This is the pain of a conscience that knows it failed and refuses to escape through a lie.

Their anguish should not be confused with despair. They did not conclude that Allah's mercy was impossible. They knew there was no refuge from Allah except in Allah. The same Lord before whom they were ashamed was the only Lord who could accept them. True repentance often begins in this tension: the servant feels the seriousness of the wrong but still turns toward the One who can forgive.

**Social Isolation Can Reveal the Heart**

The community's separation from the three was a severe discipline. It removed ordinary comfort and forced them to face the consequence without using social conversation to manage it. Isolation can be cruel when imposed carelessly, but in this case it served a clear moral purpose under prophetic guidance.

---

The three men are named in the report of Kaʿb ibn Mālik, who told the story of how he and his two companions stayed behind at Tabūk, told the truth when the others made excuses, and were boycotted by the community for fifty days until the verse of their pardon was revealed. The report is long and is preserved in both Ṣaḥīḥ collections, and it records the severity of the isolation: no one spoke to them, the earth seemed to close in on them, and the turning to them in mercy came at the point they had given up hope of it. The verse's closing words name God as the one who turns in mercy, and the story is kept in the community's memory as the model of an honest account being accepted.

**Who the Three Were**

The verse names the three men who stayed behind, and the reports name them: Kaʿb ibn Mālik, Murārah ibn al-Rabīʿ and Hilāl ibn Umayyah. Kaʿb's account, which is in Bukhārī at length, tells the story from the inside. He had the means to go and did not, and when the army returned he found that everyone who had an excuse had one ready, and he told the truth and was turned away along with the other two. The Prophet forbade the community to speak to them, and they were left in that state for fifty nights, during which the earth felt narrow to them in the words of the verse itself.
**What Happened During the Waiting**

Kaʿb's report describes the day the waiting ended, when he climbed onto the roof after the dawn prayer and heard the call that the men had been forgiven, and the community ran out to meet them. The verse calls that relief a mercy and describes the men as turning to God, and it says that God is the one who accepts the return of the repentant and is merciful. The Book gives the men their names and their honour in the revelation, and the verses that follow are addressed to the believers with the commands that suit a community that had just passed through the test (9:119).
**What the Verse Teaches**

The passage is the Book's clearest account of a public discipline imposed by a community on its own members out of loyalty to the mission, and it shows the community carrying it without bitterness and the men submitting to it without fleeing. The Prophet was told not to beg forgiveness for the hypocrites, and he was given no instruction to lift the ban from the three, and the release came through the verse itself. The lesson the sūrah draws is that the same mercy that lifted them is what a person should hope for rather than count on, and the words that close it name God as the One who turns to the repentant.

## Verse 9:119

> O believers! Be mindful of Allah and be with the truthful

**Mindfulness Needs Truthful Company**

The verse joins mindfulness of Allah to being with the truthful. Taqwā is not sustained by private intention alone. Companions shape what a person considers normal, admirable, and possible. If he stays among people who lie to protect comfort, he will find truth increasingly awkward. If he stays with truthful people, their conduct helps him return when he drifts.

Being with the truthful includes friendship, counsel, shared work, and moral allegiance. It does not require pretending that truthful people never make mistakes. It requires choosing people who admit mistakes, keep promises, and prefer correction to image. Their company makes repentance practical because they do not help the servant build a false story about himself.

**Company Tests the Heart's Direction**

Being with truthful people can be uncomfortable because they do not always flatter. They may expose an excuse that a more agreeable friend would protect. The believer values this discomfort because it is safer than the comfort of a lie. He also tries to become such a companion for others, offering counsel without humiliation and honesty without cruelty.

---

## Verse 9:120

> It was not ˹proper˺ for the people of Medina and the nomadic Arabs around them to avoid marching with the Messenger of Allah or to prefer their own lives above his. That is because whenever they suffer from thirst, fatigue, or hunger in the cause of Allah; or tread on a territory, unnerving the disbelievers; or inflict any loss on an enemy—it is written to their credit as a good deed. Surely Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers

**The Messenger's Mission Changes the Meaning of Sacrifice**

The verse says that the people of Medina and the nomadic Arabs around them should not prefer their lives over the Messenger's when following him is required. The command belongs to the Prophet's public leadership and to the conditions of that community. It is not a general permission for private individuals to abandon lawful authority or to force others into danger.
The principle is that a community cannot claim loyalty while treating its messenger's mission as someone else's burden. The believers share the cost according to ability. They do not place the Prophet in danger while preserving every personal comfort. In later settings, the same principle appears as readiness to support truth and legitimate communal responsibility rather than as blind obedience to every political leader.
**Movement Can Frighten Wrongdoing**

The verse includes stepping into territory that unnerves the disbelievers. This describes the moral effect of the believers' presence. They do not need to boast or commit injustice to weaken false power. Their organized obedience, courage, and refusal to submit can make wrongdoing uncertain.
The verse gives the doctrine behind the duty: the effort of the campaign itself is written down — no thirst, no fatigue, no hunger, no step that angers the enemy, and no blow struck — and the reward is attached to the hardship as much as to the outcome. The Book states the same accounting in the sūrah of the Cow, where God says He does not waste the reward of those who do good (3:195), and the Prophet ﷺ gave the rule in his own words when he said that the deeds of a traveller continue to be recorded. The passage also explains why the community was told not to prefer their lives to the Messenger's: the hardship of the road is part of the record the verse describes.

**What the Verse Counts**

The verse is part of the account of the expedition to Tabūk, and it sets a rule for the people of Madinah and the tribes around it: it is not for them to stay behind the Messenger of God or to put their own safety before his. Then the verse lists what gets written down. Thirst, and weariness, and hunger in the way of God. A road that angers the disbelievers. A blow struck against an enemy. Each one is recorded as a good deed, and the sūrah adds that God does not waste the reward of those who do good. The verse after it completes the accounting: nothing small or great that they spend, and no valley they cross, but it is written for them, so that God may repay them for the best of what they did (9:121).
**Why It Is Written This Way**

The Book is telling a community that hardship is not wasted when the outcome is not what was hoped for. The expedition was long, the heat was severe, and the fighting was minimal; nothing about it looked like a victory to a person counting results, and the verse says the thirst and the road and the hunger were the record. The same principle is stated in the Sūrah of the Cow, where a grain of corn grows into seven ears and God multiplies as He wills (2:261), and in the Sūrah of Women, where those who strive are ranked above those who sit at home (4:95).
**The Obligation Behind the Sentence**

The rule that opens the verse is that the community and its messenger share the same risk. The Book does not allow a division where one group carries the danger and another keeps its comfort, and the passage that follows deals with the men who stayed behind and their reasons. The verse stands at the point in the sūrah where the accounting of the whole expedition is taking place, and it makes the point that the hardship of a single difficult journey is itself a written reward — which is why the men who grieved over missing it grieved so much.

## Verse 9:121

> And whenever they make a donation, small or large, or cross a valley ˹in Allah’s cause˺—it is written to their credit, so that Allah may grant them the best reward for what they used to do

**Nothing Given or Travelled Is Lost**

The verse says that whenever these men spend anything, small or large, or cross a valley, it is written down for them, so that God may give them the best reward for what they did. The verse follows a passage about the men who gave and went out, and it answers the fear that a person's quiet effort goes unnoticed.

**What the Verse Is Recording**

The Book describes the deeds of the believers as items in a register kept by the One who misses nothing. The sūrah has already told the community that the men who gave in hard times and went out were the ones whose effort counted (9:120 names the men of Madinah and the men of the desert who followed the Prophet and says that neither hunger nor thirst nor a journey to the enemy went unwritten), and the verse in front of the reader sums up the same list in one sentence. The point is that the small gift and the long road are the same kind of deed in the record, and both are written.

**Why the Book Names Crossing a Valley**

The words describe the ordinary march of a group going out on a journey, and the Book raises it to the level of an act of worship. The sūrah had told the Prophet that the men who refused to go out would be asked about it (9:81 describes their rejoicing at staying behind), and it told the believers that going out with the Prophet was a duty they could not avoid (9:38-39 give the order and the warning). The verse in front of the reader tells the community what happens to the ordinary discomforts of that journey: they are recorded as deeds. The Book says in the same sūrah that the men who gave and the men who refused are not the same in the accounting (9:79 names the men who criticized the poor man's small gift), and it answers a complaint that was made about the size of a gift before the verse was given.

**What the Promise Means for the Giver**

The verse's phrase about the best reward teaches the reader to stop calculating. A person who gives can never know how far his gift travels or how long its effect lasts, and the verse tells him that the value is not what he can see. The Book says the same in another sūrah when it promises that whatever is spent in God's way will be paid back in full and more (2:261 gives the image of the seed that grows into seven ears, and 2:262 promises the givers their reward with their Lord). The verse in front of the reader keeps the community's small efforts in view and tells the givers that the account is not closed when the coins leave their hands.

**What the Sūrah Does With the Same Idea**

The chapter has been describing the campaigns of that period and the spread of the community, and it keeps returning to the fact that everything is recorded. It says that the men who stayed behind will be told what they did on the day when the records are opened (9:94 recounts their excuses and the answer they get), and it tells the Prophet that the deeds of the men and the women who believe are seen by God (9:105 states it). The verse in front of the reader is the community's comfort in the middle of a long and tiring campaign: the reward is not measured by the size of the gift or by the length of the road, but by the fact that it was done for God.

---

## Verse 9:122

> ˹However,˺ it is not necessary for the believers to march forth all at once. Only a party from each group should march forth, leaving the rest to gain religious knowledge then enlighten their people when they return to them, so that they ˹too˺ may beware ˹of evil˺

**The Community Needs More Than One Kind of Service**

The verse corrects the idea that every believer must be physically present in every campaign. Some march, while others remain to gain religious knowledge and teach their people. Both roles serve the community. Courage in the field and patient learning in the gathering are complementary responsibilities rather than competing claims to virtue.

This balance prevents a community from losing its teachers whenever a crisis appears. It also prevents scholars from treating study as a refuge from every communal duty. The division of labor is deliberate: some protect the community's immediate safety, while others protect its understanding and long-term direction.

The verse presents learning as a responsibility that must be carried by a group. Not everyone can master every field, but every community needs people who devote themselves to sound knowledge. These people learn deeply, distinguish revelation from custom, and return to explain what they have understood.

This is not permission for a small class to monopolize religion. The knowledgeable teach, and the wider community listens, asks, and acts. Learning becomes a trust rather than a badge. A scholar who refuses to communicate has not completed the responsibility described here, and a listener who refuses to learn cannot shift all responsibility to the scholar.

---

The verse is the sūrah's rule for the community's division of labour, and it was read as the foundation of the duty to seek religious knowledge: a party marches and a party remains to learn and to teach, so that when the others return they are warned and may take care. The scholars built the discipline of teaching and study on the phrase "to gain understanding in the religion", which gives the pursuit of knowledge the standing of a service to the community rather than a private accomplishment. The verse's closing words — that they may beware — make the purpose practical: knowledge exists to change conduct, which is the standard the whole chapter has been applying to the waverers.

## Verse 9:123

> O believers! Fight the disbelievers around you and let them find firmness in you. And know that Allah is with those mindful ˹of Him˺

**The Command Addresses an Organized Community**

The verse speaks to believers as a community under the Messenger's leadership. Its command to fight is not a license for private violence or for harming neighbors because of identity. It concerns an organized conflict in which authority, cause, and moral limits matter. The believer reads the command alongside the prohibition of transgression and the duty to protect those not engaged in fighting.

The wording about those around the believers teaches strategic responsibility. A community should not chase distant glory while ignoring danger at its own door. It protects neighbors, families, and institutions from immediate aggression before making grand claims about remote problems.

The principle applies to moral reform as well. A person should confront the clear fault nearest to his own responsibility before performing outrage about the faults of strangers. He begins with his household, conduct, and community. Nearness creates a duty of attention, not permission to treat every nearby person as an enemy.

---

## Verse 9:124

> Whenever a sûrah is revealed, some of them ask ˹mockingly˺, “Which of you has this increased in faith?” As for the believers, it has increased them in faith and they rejoice

**The Same Revelation Reveals Different Hearts**

The verse contrasts the mockery of hypocrites with the joy of believers. Revelation does not act like a neutral object producing the same response in every person. It meets hearts that are open, resistant, proud, fearful, or sincere. The words remain true, but the listener's condition shapes whether the truth becomes guidance or an occasion for ridicule.

This contrast calls the believer to examine the heart before blaming the message. If a passage unsettles him, he asks what attachment it is exposing. Discomfort can be a doorway to repentance. The hypocrite instead turns the discomfort into a joke and gathers others around the joke so that nobody has to face what the revelation demands.

The believers' faith increases when a new sūrah is revealed. Faith is not treated as a fixed possession that cannot deepen. Understanding, certainty, love, humility, and obedience can all grow through sincere engagement with Allah's words.

Increase requires more than hearing sounds. The believer listens, reflects, asks how the command applies, and acts upon what he understands. A person may know many passages while remaining unchanged if knowledge never reaches conduct. The increase is visible when revelation produces trust, patience, worship, and moral courage.

---

## Verse 9:125

> But as for those with sickness in their hearts, it has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness, and they die as disbelievers

**The Sickness Is Moral as Well as Intellectual**

The verse speaks of sickness in the heart. This includes doubt and false belief, but it also includes greed, jealousy, guile, and the desire to resist truth. The heart in Quranic language is the center of understanding, intention, and moral choice. A person can have information while his heart remains sick because he uses knowledge to avoid surrender.

The sūrah increases wickedness in those whose hearts are sick, but the revelation is not the cause of their sickness. It reveals and provokes what was already present. A mirror does not create a blemish by showing it. The Quran's clarity makes resistance visible and gives the person an opportunity to repent.

**Wickedness Can Accumulate**

The phrase “wickedness upon wickedness” describes a process. A person doubts, then protects the doubt with mockery. He envies, then invents a reason to discredit the envied person. He lies, then adds another lie to preserve the first. Each refusal makes the next refusal easier.

---

## Verse 9:126

> Do they not see that they are tried once or twice every year? Yet they neither repent nor do they learn a lesson

**Mercy Appears in the Chance to Notice**

Repeated trials can be understood as mercy because Allah gives the person more than one opportunity to wake up. The warning arrives before the final outcome. A person who sees the pattern can still turn, and a community can still repair its institutions.

“Do they not see?” asks whether the problem is vision rather than evidence. The trials are visible, but the hypocrites refuse to interpret them. The believer trains himself to notice moral meaning without pretending to know the exact divine reason for every event.

He says, “What can I learn?” rather than, “Whom can I blame?” That question turns disruption into guidance. It leads to repentance, wiser preparation, and greater compassion for others who are being tested. A trial becomes a sign of mercy when it returns the servant to Allah.

Taking heed also includes compassion for people whose trials differ from one's own. The believer does not use another person's illness or loss to announce a simple verdict about his faith. He asks how to help, what justice requires, and what lesson applies to himself. Allah may be teaching the whole community through one person's difficulty. When people respond with mercy and honest reflection, the trial produces more than survival. It produces a community less proud, better prepared, and more attentive to the Lord.

---

## Verse 9:127

> Whenever a sûrah is revealed, they look at one another, ˹saying,˺ “Is anyone watching you?” Then they slip away. ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has turned their hearts away because they are a people who do not comprehend

**Their First Concern Is Exposure**

When revelation is recited, the hypocrites look at one another and ask whether anyone is watching. Their concern is not what Allah is saying but whether their reaction has been noticed. This is the psychology of a person who manages appearances while resisting truth.

The question can also be understood as mockery among themselves. Either way, the group is united by avoidance. They monitor the audience instead of examining the message. The believer reverses this priority: he is more concerned with Allah seeing his heart than with people seeing his face.

They leave when the revelation demands attention. Physical departure represents an inward refusal. A person may remain seated while mentally leaving, changing the subject, checking who agrees, or finding a reason to delay obedience. The body can be present and the heart absent.

The verse does not condemn every need to leave a gathering. People have legitimate duties, health needs, and responsibilities. The issue is deliberate escape from guidance because it threatens a preferred identity. The believer distinguishes practical departure from the habit of fleeing correction.

**What the Chapter Says They Were Watching For**

The verse describes a council in which a chapter comes down and the men in it look at one another, and the question they ask is whether anyone sees them, and then they leave. The sūrah has said earlier that some of the Bedouin take what they spend as a loss and wait for a turn in the community's fortune (9:98), and the verses before this one describe the same men and their oaths (9:124-126). The Book's picture is of men who sit in a gathering where the revelation is being recited and treat the whole of it as a risk to be managed.
**The Sentence the Book Adds**

The verse ends by saying that God has turned their hearts away, since they are a people who do not understand. The Book uses the same turning in the Sūrah of the Spoils, where the believers are told that God comes between a person and his own heart (8:24), and it states the same consequence for the men who persist in the Sūrah of the Cow (2:7). The commentary tradition read the sentence as a description of the outcome rather than a decree that fell on them from outside: the men who spent years sliding out of the room ended by having nothing left to understand with.
**Where the Passage Sits**

The sūrah has been dealing with the campaign to Tabūk since its middle, and the verse stands at the end of a stretch on the men who stayed behind. The verses after it bring the sūrah to the messenger and the religion, and the Book's arrangement is that the record of the community's worst days comes before the summons. The men in front of us left the gathering when the chapter was recited, and the chapter recorded them leaving.

---

## Verse 9:128

> There certainly has come to you a messenger from among yourselves. He is concerned by your suffering, anxious for your well-being, and gracious and merciful to the believers

**The Messenger Is Near to the Human Audience**

The verse reminds the people that a messenger has come from among themselves. He speaks their language, knows their social world, and shares the conditions of human life. Guidance is not delivered by a distant abstraction. It arrives through a person whose example can be seen and whose concern can be recognized.

This nearness is a blessing, not a reason to take the messenger lightly. Familiarity can make people forget the extraordinary gift of receiving revelation through one of their own. The believer honors both dimensions: the Messenger is human and therefore an accessible model, and he is commissioned by Allah and therefore entitled to obedience.

The Messenger is concerned by what the people suffer. His mission is not indifferent to their pain. He wants them protected from disbelief, injustice, confusion, and the Fire. His concern may lead him to warn them, correct them, or require sacrifice, but the severity of a warning does not cancel its mercy.

---

The verse closes the sūrah with the Messenger's bond to the community: a messenger from among them, distressed by their suffering, eager for their good, gentle and merciful to the believers. The two divine names used at its end — the Compassionate, the Merciful — are applied in the Qur'an to the Prophet only here, and the commentators took that as a mark of the closeness of his relation to the community. The verses were among the last of the revelation in the reports of the Companions, and the Prophet's own instruction to recite the sūrah's closing verses in the evening prayer is preserved in the collections of the Sunan, which gives 9:128–129 a place in the community's daily worship.

## Verse 9:129

> But if they turn away, then say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is sufficient for me. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. In Him I put my trust. And He is the Lord of the Mighty Throne.”

**Trust Is Responsible Reliance**

The Prophet says he trusts Allah. Tawakkul does not mean abandoning effort or refusing counsel. The Messenger warned, taught, planned, and endured. Trust means that after using the means, he leaves the outcome to Allah and remains obedient whatever the outcome may be.
A believer who trusts Allah still seeks treatment, earns lawfully, protects dependents, and addresses injustice. He does not call passivity reliance. Nor does he treat planning as a rival to faith. Trust purifies effort by removing the illusion that the servant controls results independently.
**The Mighty Throne Signals Absolute Sovereignty**

Allah is the Lord of the Mighty Throne. The image points to the summit of the created order and therefore to a sovereignty greater than every human authority. If the Lord of the Throne supports the Messenger, rejection by a group of people cannot make the truth small.
This freedom does not make a person cold. He still values friendship and grieves rejection. It prevents grief from becoming worship of approval. The heart returns to Allah after every human response and asks what obedience requires next.

**Who the Verse Is Spoken To and What It Uses as a Ground**

The sūrah closes with a short declaration: if they turn away, say, God is enough for me; there is no god but He; in Him I put my trust, and He is the Lord of the Mighty Throne. The historical reports place it near the end of the Prophet's life, and the sūrah that has spent a hundred verses sorting the community, threatening the waverers, and sending out the expedition names the one thing the man carrying it all can rely on. The verse gives three clauses in order — sufficiency, the oneness of God, and trust — and closes by naming the Throne whose owner holds everything above the kings and the armies the Messenger was dealing with.
**What the Last Name Adds**

The sūrah has used this closing before and uses it again: the Lord of the Throne, the vastest created thing the Book names and the image the tradition uses for the summit of creation, with everything below it under one hand. The point of naming it at the end of a book about a hard year is not decoration. The Prophet had letters to write to emperors, a city to hold, a coalition to watch, and men around him whose faith had already been tested; and the verse ends not with a promise of victory but with an identification of the One to whom the whole matter belongs. That is what the man says when they turn away: it is not information offered to the opponent, but the ground he is standing on while they decide.
