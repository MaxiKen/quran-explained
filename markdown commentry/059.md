# Sūrah al-Ḥashr (Chapter 59) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Gathering of the Banished and the Mountain of Humility**

Al-Ḥashr, "The Gathering" or "The Banishment," is the fifty-ninth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 24 verses of Medinan revelation. In the Companion tradition, ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās referred to this chapter without hesitation as *Sūrat Banī al-Naḍīr* (The Chapter of Banū al-Naḍīr), because its historical core (verses 2–17) chronicles the bloodless siege, surrender, and expulsion of the heavily fortified Jewish tribe of Banū al-Naḍīr from the environs of Madinah in 4 AH (625 CE). Positioned as the second of the five *Musabbiḥāt* chapters, Sūrah al-Ḥashr is universally celebrated for its breathtaking, peerless climax in verses 21–24: the majestic, awe-inspiring parable of the Qur'an descending upon a mountain, causing the granite crags to shatter in humility from the fear of God, followed by the grand, liturgical litany of the Most Beautiful Names of God (*al-Asmā' al-Ḥusnā*).

**The Structural Architecture and the Names of Glory**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Ḥashr moves across three magnificent movements:

1. The Fall of the Fortresses and the Charter of Wealth (1–10): Opens with the cosmic glorification of God, recounts the expulsion of Banū al-Naḍīr whose stone fortresses could not shield them from God's decree, and codifies the revolutionary economic law of *al-Fay'* (war gains acquired without battle). Verses 7–8 decree that this wealth belongs to Allah, the Messenger, near relatives, orphans, the destitute, and the wayfarer, establishing the immortal socio-economic maxim of Islam: *“So that it may not be a perpetual circulation among the rich from among you (*kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna al-aghniyā'i minkum*).”* It delivers the foundational constitutional principle of prophetic authority: *“And whatever the Messenger has given you—take; and what he has forbidden you—refrain from”* (59:7). It honors the impoverished Emigrants (*Muhājirūn*) who sacrificed their homes and wealth, and celebrates the Helpers (*Anṣār*) who loved those who migrated to them, harbored no jealousy in their breasts, and preferred others over themselves even when they were in dire poverty (*wa-yu'thirūna ʿalā anfusihim wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah*, 59:9 — *“They give ˹the emigrants˺ preference over themselves even though they may be in need.”*).

2. The Psychology of Betrayal and the Parable of Satan (11–17): Dissects the hollow promises of the hypocrites, exposing them as liars who never aid their allies in battle because fear of the believers is greater in their breasts than fear of God. It presents the striking military diagnosis: they will never fight you together except within fortified towns or from behind stone walls, their inner hatred among themselves is severe, and you imagine they are united, yet their hearts are utterly divided (59:14 — *“Even united, they would not ˹dare˺ fight against you except ˹from˺ within fortified strongholds or from behind walls.”*). It compares their deception to the parable of Satan, who commands man to disbelieve, but when man disbelieves, Satan says: *“Indeed, I am disassociated from you; indeed, I fear Allah, Lord of the worlds”* (59:16).

3. The Shattered Mountain and the Litany of Divine Names (18–24): Summons the believers to mindfulness (*Taqwā*), commanding every soul to look to what it has put forward for tomorrow, and warning them not to be like those who forgot God, so God made them forget their own souls (*fa-ansāhum anfusahum*, 59:19 — *“And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. It is they who are ˹truly˺ rebellious”*). It delivers the breathtaking image of verse 21: *“If We had sent down this Qur'an upon a mountain, you would have seen it humbled and split apart from the fear of Allah. And these examples We present to the people that perhaps they will give thought.”* The chapter concludes with the sublime, three-verse litany of the Most Beautiful Names of God: Allah, beside Whom there is no deity; the Knower of the unseen and seen; *al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm*; *al-Malik* (The Sovereign), *al-Quddūs* (The Pure/Holy), *al-Salām* (The Source of Peace), *al-Mu'min* (The Bestower of Security), *al-Muhaymin* (The Guardian), *al-ʿAzīz* (The Almighty), *al-Jabbār* (The Compeller), *al-Mutakabbir* (The Majestic); *al-Khāliq* (The Creator), *al-Bāri'* (The Originator), *al-Muṣawwir* (The Fashioner), to Whom belong the Most Beautiful Names, glorified by all in the heavens and earth.

## Verse 59:1

> Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah. For He is the Almighty, All-Wise

**The Mode of Praise Need Not Be Familiar to Us**

The declaration is wider than our ability to describe how each creature glorifies Allah. It gives the fact of praise without presenting a catalogue of its forms. One should not fill that silence with imagined sounds or claims about the hidden language of particular objects. Nor should the absence of ordinary human speech be treated as proof that the declaration has no reality. Our familiarity with one mode of expression is not a measure of every possible relation between creatures and their Lord.

Two ways of understanding created praise should be distinguished. A thing's ordered existence can indicate its Maker's perfection, and a creature can glorify in a manner known to Allah even when people cannot understand it. The first explains how creation serves as a sign; the second takes seriously the declaration of praise beyond human comprehension. These need not be enemies, but reducing every instance to what people can infer would narrow the verse unnecessarily. Ibn Kathīr's commentary on this verse explicitly treats the whole creation as praising and revering Allah and connects that reading to 17:44 — *“There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification.”*. The latter passage places a limit on human understanding of such praise. The stronger approach therefore affirms the declaration without pretending to know all its mechanisms. Recognizing a limit of knowledge is different from denying what the verse affirms.

**Might Means No Creature Can Secure Independence**

Ibn Kathīr explains this name here in majesty that cannot be overcome. That interpretive addition sharpens the point: might is not simply the ability to win a contest after meeting resistance on equal terms (Ibn Kathīr on 59:1 — *“Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah. For He is the Almighty, All-Wise”*). Created powers do not become independent by resisting the obligations laid upon them. Their resistance remains the action of dependent beings. This does not make their choices unreal, but it prevents their choices from being mistaken for a successful escape from lordship. For worship, the consequence is clear. Praise is not flattery offered to a vulnerable ruler who needs reassurance. It recognizes an authority whose reality neither increases through applause nor diminishes through refusal.

## Verse 59:2

> He is the One Who expelled the disbelievers of the People of the Book from their homes for ˹their˺ first banishment ˹ever˺. You never thought they would go. And they thought their strongholds would put them out of Allah’s reach. But ˹the decree of˺ Allah came upon them from where they never expected. And He cast horror into their hearts so they destroyed their houses with their own hands and the hands of the believers. So take a lesson ˹from this˺, O people of insight

**The Expulsion Concerns a Particular Community**

The verse describes the removal of a particular group from its homes. Ibn Kathīr identifies that group as Banū al-Naḍīr, a Jewish tribe in the region of Madinah, and explains the event within the history of their broken agreement with the Prophet (Ibn Kathīr on 59:2 — *“He is the One Who expelled the disbelievers of the People of the Book from their homes for ˹their˺ first banishment ˹ever˺.”*). This identification is necessary because the phrase People of the Book is broader than the group involved in this event. The passage is not a report that all scriptural communities were expelled, nor does it make every member of such a community responsible for the conduct of these participants.

**The Unexpected Opening Was Inside Their Hearts**

Ibn Kathīr connects the unexpected reversal with 16:26 — *“Indeed, those before them had plotted, but Allah struck at the ˹very˺ foundation of their structure, so the roof collapsed on top of them, and the torment came upon them from where they did not expect”*, where a scheme is undone from a direction its makers did not foresee. The cross-reference adds a useful distinction. The problem is not only that one calculation happened to be inaccurate. Human planning can fail at the very point its makers consider settled. Neither passage warrants claiming that every fearful person is being punished or that every failed defense reveals a person's moral standing. Here the explanation is given by revelation for this event. Outside such an identified case, fear may arise in many circumstances, and human observers do not have Allah's complete judgment. The lesson is humility about the reach of one's plans, not an unrestricted right to label other people's suffering. The text describes a particular terror and gives its meaning within a particular judgment.

---

**What the Verse Records of the Day**

The verse finishes its account with three details that carry the passage's lesson. It was God who brought them out, and the believers were not expecting it; the People of the Book had thought their fortresses would hold, and God came at them from a direction they had not counted on. Then the destruction: *they destroyed their own houses with their own hands, and the hands of the believers* — the tribe dismantled what it could not carry away rather than leave it standing. And the verse closes with a call addressed to everyone still listening: *so take the lesson, you who have insight*.
**What the Lesson Is**

The ruins were visible enough to be a text. A community that had lived among the Muslims, signed an agreement, and then worked against it, found itself pulling down its own walls before it left. The verse does not invite the reader to gloat; it asks him to look at the sequence and recognize the pattern, which the sūrah then states a few verses later in the words of the faithful who were not fooled by the appearance of strength. The houses of a people who trusted in their houses are the first thing that has to be taken apart, and the Book records that they did the work with their own hands.

**The Banishment and the Second One It Points To**

The verse calls the expulsion the *first* banishment, and the word has been read for the hint inside it. Ibn Kathīr and others take it as the first of two clearings of the tribes that had broken their agreements in Madinah: the Banū al-Naḍīr were sent out here, and the Banū Qurayẓah were dealt with afterwards, so the phrase marks the opening of a sequence the reader of the sūrah already knows. A second reading takes the word in the sense of a departure that came first in the history of the community's dealings with them, without any second implied. The two readings agree on the fact the verse is recording: an expulsion that the community had not expected, carried out against a people who believed their fortresses were enough.

**What Was Done with the Houses and the Trees**

The sūrah explains the disposal of what the tribe left behind. The palm trees that the believers cut down or left standing were dealt with by God's permission (59:5 — *“Whatever palm trees you ˹believers˺ cut down or left standing intact, it was ˹all˺ by Allah’s Will, so that He might disgrace the rebellious”*), and the property that came to the community without a battle was assigned to the Messenger and then to the near relatives, the orphans, the poor and the traveller, so that wealth should not circulate only among the rich (59:6-7 — *“As for gains granted by Allah to His Messenger from the people of ˹other˺ lands, they are for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich.”*). The passage matters because it is honest about the messy part of the story: a siege that involved cutting down a tribe's date palms, and property that changed hands because an agreement had been broken. The Book gives the ruling for each item, and the sūrah then records the promise of the hypocrites that they would leave with the tribe and fight beside them if they were attacked, together with the sentence that they were lying (59:11-12 — *“Indeed, if they are expelled, the hypocrites will never leave with them.”*). The verse in front of us ends with the call to take the lesson, and the lesson includes the answer the community got when it looked for help from the men who had none to give.

## Verse 59:3

> Had Allah not decreed exile for them, He would have certainly punished them in this world. And in the Hereafter they will suffer the punishment of the Fire

**A Specific Contrast Explains the Worldly Punishment**

The sentence can raise a question: if exile is itself painful, why does it contrast exile with punishment in this world? The contrast concerns the kind of punishment, not the claim that banishment involved no suffering. Ibn Kathīr explains the unrealized alternative as killing or captivity and attributes that explanation to al-Zuhrī (Ibn Kathīr on 59:3 — *“Had Allah not decreed exile for them, He would have certainly punished them in this world. And in the Hereafter they will suffer the punishment of the Fire”*). This is an interpretive explanation from a named early scholar, not a Prophetic saying for which an invented hadith number should be supplied.

**The Unchosen Possibility Does Not Authorize Human Cruelty**

The declaration of what Allah would have done is not a command telling believers to supply a harsher outcome themselves. Its grammar is explanatory and conditional. It tells why the historical result must not be read as an inability to punish. Turning that explanation into permission for additional violence would reverse the sentence's function: a verse about the outcome Allah decreed would become a pretext for refusing that outcome and choosing another. The verse before this one describes the expulsion: the men whom God drove out of their homes went out at the hands of the community, and the verse after this one warns that the fire is waiting for them in the last life and that what happened to them in this one is a warning for anyone who sees it (59:2 — *“And He cast horror into their hearts so they destroyed their houses with their own hands and the hands of the believers.”* describes the scene, and 59:4 — *“This is because they defied Allah and His Messenger. And whoever defies Allah, then Allah is truly severe in punishment”* says that they set themselves against God and His Messenger). The sūrah of the Cow had already told the community that such men are thrown into fear by the very houses they barricade themselves into when God comes upon them (59:2 again in its own words — *“And He cast horror into their hearts so they destroyed their houses with their own hands and the hands of the believers.”*), and the verse in front of the reader adds that the punishment in this world was a mercy compared with what comes next.

## Verse 59:4

> This is because they defied Allah and His Messenger. And whoever defies Allah, then Allah is truly severe in punishment

**The Reason Given Is Defiance, Not Mere Weakness**

The verse states why the judgment under discussion occurred. Its explanation is moral: the people concerned defied Allah and His Messenger. Their loss is not interpreted simply as the result of inferior strength, unfortunate timing, or an unsuccessful calculation. Those factors may belong to a description of events, but they do not supply the reason revelation gives here. The words because they place responsibility in their stance toward divine authority.

**Defiance Names an Adversarial Stance**

The verb shāqqū means that they opposed or defied. The Quranic Arabic Corpus identifies it here as a form III verb from the root shīn–qāf–qāf and records the same verbal family in passages about opposition to the Messenger, including 4:115 — *“And whoever defies the Messenger after guidance has become clear to them and follows a path other than that of the believers, We will let them pursue what they have chosen, then burn them in Hell—what an evil end”* and 47:32 — *“Indeed, those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and defy the Messenger after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them; they will not harm Allah in the least, but He will render their deeds void”* (Quranic Arabic Corpus, root sh-q-q). This checked linguistic comparison adds precision to the translation. The verse concerns taking an opposing stance, not the mere existence of a question or the act of asking for an explanation.

Related forms of the root can concern splitting, but the contextual meaning must not be replaced by a dramatic etymology. One should not claim that the people literally divided God from His Messenger or that every disagreement carries the same religious force. The relevant verb describes opposition in this setting. Its seriousness follows from its object: Allah and the Messenger who conveys His guidance. That is different from treating any human official's displeasure as proof of opposition to God. The wording gives no one a blank check to identify personal criticism with religious rebellion. Careful attention to the verb so works in both directions. It preserves the gravity of deliberate defiance while preventing the warning from swallowing every dispute, uncertainty, or request for evidence.

## Verse 59:5

> Whatever palm trees you ˹believers˺ cut down or left standing intact, it was ˹all˺ by Allah’s Will, so that He might disgrace the rebellious

**The Verse Judges Both Felling and Preservation**

The verse places two actions under one declaration: some palms were cut down, and others remained standing. Its concern is not limited to defending the cutting. It also acknowledges preservation. A reading that extracts permission to destroy while ignoring the trees left intact would omit half of the stated situation. The two outcomes are treated together within the particular event to which revelation speaks.

The reports identify that setting. Ibn ʿUmar relates that the Prophet had palms belonging to Banū al-Naḍīr cut and burned at al-Buwayrah and connects the act with this revelation. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4031. Its attribution is to Ibn ʿUmar reporting the Prophet's action, not to an invented speech in which the Prophet explains every strategic reason. The place-name is a checked addition that anchors the account without enlarging it beyond its evidence. The verse itself uses the contrast between cutting and leaving; the report supplies burning as part of the historical event. Keeping those kinds of evidence distinct avoids silently inserting details into the translation. The central judgment remains what the verse states: neither the cutting carried out in this case nor the preservation fell outside Allah's permission.

**Permission Answers a Moral Question, Not Only a Question of Power**

The phrase by Allah's Will cannot be reduced here to the bare observation that no event escapes divine sovereignty. The immediate contrast concerns conduct whose legitimacy was in question. Ibn Kathīr explains the declaration as approval of the actions in this setting and records the concern that arose over cutting the trees (Ibn Kathīr on 59:5 — *“Whatever palm trees you ˹believers˺ cut down or left standing intact, it was ˹all˺ by Allah’s Will, so that He might disgrace the rebellious”*). In that context, the statement reassures the participants that the identified conduct was permitted. It is not only telling them that the event occurred.

## Verse 59:6

> As for the gains Allah has turned over to His Messenger from them—you did not ˹even˺ spur on any horse or camel for such gains. But Allah gives authority to His messengers over whoever He wills. For Allah is Most Capable of everything

**The Manner of Acquisition Determines the Claim to the Gains**

The verse distinguishes these gains by how they came under the Messenger's authority. The believers had not obtained them through the mounted exertion named in the sentence. That fact matters for entitlement. Property acquired in this manner is not simply available for participants to divide according to the assumptions they might bring from a battlefield. Before asking who should receive it, the verse establishes the kind of acquisition involved.

The legal term is fayʾ: property coming under Muslim authority without the fighting that gives rise to ordinary battle spoils. Ibn Kathīr applies that category here to the property of Banū al-Naḍīr and distinguishes it from acquisition through the use of cavalry and camelry in battle (Ibn Kathīr on 59:6 — *“But Allah gives authority to His messengers over whoever He wills.”*). This does not require a claim that no pressure or confrontation occurred. The classification concerns the manner in which the property was secured. Confusing a siege or surrender with a battle fought for booty would obscure the verse's legal point. The gains are real, but the claim to distribute them must follow their proper category. Mere presence near an event does not establish the same entitlement as every other form of participation in conflict.

**A Year's Provision Shows What Administration Could Look Like**

A report from ʿUmar gives a precise example of the Prophet's use of this property. He sold the dates produced by the Banū al-Naḍīr garden and retained enough food to meet his household's needs for a year. The account appears in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5357. The one-year provision is a checked detail beyond the verse's wording and helps distinguish regular maintenance from a vague claim of unrestricted consumption.

## Verse 59:7

> As for gains granted by Allah to His Messenger from the people of ˹other˺ lands, they are for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich. Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it. And whatever he forbids you from, leave it. And fear Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment

**A Public Gain Must Answer to More Than Powerful Claimants**

The verse identifies recipients whose claims are not measured simply by their ability to seize or bargain. The Messenger's close relatives, orphans, poor people, and needy travelers stand within the stated disposition of these gains. Several of these groups lack the influence through which wealth ordinarily attracts more wealth. Their inclusion means that administration must recognize needs and responsibilities that a competition among strong claimants could easily ignore.

**Ibn Masʿūd Applied the Command Beyond Property**

A report provides an early example of the wider reading. When Umm Yaʿqūb challenged Ibn Masʿūd about prohibitions he attributed to the Prophet, he referred her to this verse's command to accept the Messenger's direction. The exchange is recorded through ʿAlqamah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4886. The relevant point here is Ibn Masʿūd's use of the verse in a matter outside the distribution of gains. That is a checked addition to the immediate financial discussion.

**Reverence Governs Both Distribution and Reception**

The closing warning prevents this arrangement from becoming a contest in clever evasion. Those administering and those receiving the gains answer to Allah. A formally correct procedure can still be corrupted by pressure, concealed preference, or refusal to honor the recognized claim of someone without influence. Reverence reaches those motives as well as the visible transfer. Severe punishment is named because the command is not a request that may be ignored whenever an advantage appears.

The Prophet's general instruction in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7288, narrated by Abū Hurayrah, joins avoidance of what he prohibits with carrying out commands to the extent of one's ability (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7288). This report clarifies obedience without turning it into a demand for the impossible. Ability matters in fulfilling a positive duty; dislike of a ruling is not itself inability. The verse brings that serious obedience into the administration of material resources. Wealth must reach beyond a closed rich circle, the stated recipients must be recognized, and the Messenger's direction must govern both receipt and restraint. Its economic purpose and religious command are not rival concerns. Reverence for Allah is expressed here through accepting a distribution whose benefits cannot be reserved for the already powerful.

**Wealth That Must Not Circle Among the Rich**

The verse gives its reason in one sentence: so that wealth does not become a circle that passes between the rich among you. That sentence is the policy, and the shares that precede it are the method. The poor emigrants of Makkah, the orphans, the needy and the traveller are placed inside the disposition of property that came without a battle, and the verses that follow show what the arrangement was for: the Anṣār who housed and fed the newcomers and preferred them over their own selves although they were in need, and the prayer of the believers who came after them for their brothers in faith (59:8-10 — *“They are the ones true in faith”*). The Book's concern in all of it is what the sentence names: a community in which wealth circulates only among those who already hold it is a community that has lost its edge. The Prophet's own share in the verse is listed with the others rather than placed above them, and the closing command binds the whole arrangement to the account: fear God, and know that God is severe in punishment.

**The Two Ways the Book Takes Property**

The scholars drew from this passage the difference between the two kinds of property taken from an enemy. What is won by fighting is subject to the rules of the sūrah of the Spoils, where a fifth is set aside for the categories named there and the rest is distributed among the fighters (8:41 — *“Know that whatever spoils you take, one-fifth is for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers, if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and what We revealed to Our servant on that decisive day when the two armies met ˹at Badr˺.”*). What comes into the community's hands without a battle — the property of a people who surrendered or left, as the Banū al-Naḍīr left their houses — is not divided among the fighters at all, and this verse is the rule for it: the whole of it is at the disposal of the Messenger and the categories he is told to serve. The community's later practice followed the two verses together, and the principle behind them is the sentence in the middle of the verse: the wealth must move.

## Verse 59:8

> ˹Some of the gains will be˺ for poor emigrants who were driven out of their homes and wealth, seeking Allah’s bounty and pleasure, and standing up for Allah and His Messenger. They are the ones true in faith

**The Poor Who Were Driven Out**

The verse names one of the groups who had a share in the property that was taken without a fight, and it describes them in a few exact words: the poor who were driven out of their homes and away from their property, who were looking for help from God and for His approval, and who were standing up for God and His Messenger. The verse says that these are the people who are true to their word.

**What the Description Says**

Their leaving was not a planned move to a better address. The verse says they were driven out, which means other people made them leave, and they left behind houses and possessions they had built and gathered over a lifetime. That is why they were poor in Madinah when the verse was given: men who had owned property in Makkah were living in the shelter of the Ansar with nothing of their own. The Book keeps this fact in front of the community in several places, and it promises the men who leave their homes for God's sake that they will find room in the earth and plenty (4:100 states the promise — *“Those who leave their homes and die while emigrating to Allah and His Messenger—their reward has already been secured with Allah.”*), and it says that those who left and were driven out and suffered for God's cause will be given a home and a great reward (16:41 states both parts — *“But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew”*). The same sūrah of the Bee says that the reward of the next life is greater than the home they were given in this one, which is exactly how the community's own story turned out.

**What They Were Looking For**

The verse names two things they wanted: the generosity of God and His approval. The generosity means the provision they needed after losing everything, and the approval means that God would be pleased with them. The Book puts the two together in the same way when it tells the believers to spend seeking God's approval rather than to be seen by people (4:38 — *“Likewise for those who spend their wealth to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. And whoever takes Satan as an associate—what an evil associate they have”* describes the man who gives to be seen, and 2:265 — *“And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah’s pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce.”* describes the man who gives seeking God's approval as a garden on high ground that rain does good to). The verse does not stop at their need and does not pretend they were only interested in money: they depended on God for their living and they wanted His good pleasure, and both belonged to the same life.

**Standing Up for God and His Messenger**

The last description in the verse is that they stood up for God and His Messenger, which means they backed the message with their persons when it cost them something. The sūrah has already described the men who were settled in Madinah before them, who welcomed the newcomers and gave them a share of what they had and did not resent it (59:9 — *“As for those who had settled in the city and ˹embraced˺ the faith before ˹the arrival of˺ the emigrants, they love whoever immigrates to them, never having a desire in their hearts for whatever ˹of the gains˺ is given to the emigrants.”* names the men who were settled in the city and the state of their hearts). The verse in front of the reader names the newcomers as the ones who had made the bigger sacrifice, and the chapter's arrangement of shares follows that order. The Book names the same men elsewhere when it tells the community that God was pleased with the believers when they gave their pledge under the tree (48:18 — *“Indeed, Allah was pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you ˹O Prophet˺ under the tree.”* states the pledge and the calm that came down on them), and it names the poor among the emigrants as the ones who fought and held their ground.

**What the Verse Says to the Community After Them**

The verse closes by saying that these are the ones who are true to their word, which is the Book's highest description of belief. The chapter's three verses then work as a list of duties: the shares that go to the emigrants (59:8 is this verse — *“They are the ones true in faith”*), the shares that go to the men of Madinah who housed them (59:9 gives the second group — *“As for those who had settled in the city and ˹embraced˺ the faith before ˹the arrival of˺ the emigrants, they love whoever immigrates to them, never having a desire in their hearts for whatever ˹of the gains˺ is given to the emigrants. They give ˹the emigrants˺ preference over themselves even though they may be in need. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful”*), and the rule that the community must not let the wealth of the rich circulate only among the rich (59:7 states the principle — *“As for gains granted by Allah to His Messenger from the people of ˹other˺ lands, they are for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich.”*). What the verse asks of every later reader is the same thing it praises here: to know who paid the price for the community to exist, and to keep their need in front of the community's own spending.

## Verse 59:9

> As for those who had settled in the city and ˹embraced˺ the faith before ˹the arrival of˺ the emigrants, they love whoever immigrates to them, never having a desire in their hearts for whatever ˹of the gains˺ is given to the emigrants. They give ˹the emigrants˺ preference over themselves even though they may be in need. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful

**Another Person's Share Does Not Become an Injury to the Heart**

The residents do not harbor resentment over the gains given to the emigrants. Their love is tested at the point where a visible resource goes to someone else. It is easy to welcome people while assuming that the distribution of advantages will remain unchanged. The verse praises a deeper freedom: another's receipt does not automatically register as one's own deprivation or insult.

**Preference Has Weight Because Their Own Need Is Real**

Abū Hurayrah reports an incident in which the Prophet sought someone to host a hungry guest after finding no food available in his own households. A man of the Anṣār and his wife offered their limited food and themselves went without that evening. The report connects their conduct with this verse: Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4889. The initial absence of food in the Prophet's households is a verified detail that widens the picture of shared scarcity. The help did not emerge from effortless abundance. This particular praised act must not be converted into a general license to neglect dependents or pressure poor people into gifts they cannot safely make. Its force lies in willing generosity, not in an administrator's right to demand that others surrender their necessities.

**Selfishness Is More Than the Desire to Keep a Possession**

A verified Prophetic warning from Jābir links avarice with the destruction of earlier people, including bloodshed and treating forbidden things as permissible: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2578. The connection is exact rather than decorative. Greed becomes socially dangerous when desire authorizes whatever will satisfy it. Protecting the heart from that impulse therefore protects relationships and rights, not merely a person's reputation for generosity. The verse speaks of being saved from one's own selfishness, locating the threat close to home. The enemy is not always the newcomer who receives help.

**The Story the Verse Is Read With**

The verse is read alongside the famous report in Bukhārī about a man of the Helpers who had a guest and nothing but his children's supper. He put the children to bed without food, and he and his wife said that they were busy with the meal, and the guest ate while they went without. When the Prophet heard of it he said that God had been pleased with what the man and his wife had done, and the verse describing the Helpers who prefer others above themselves was read as the account of that night. The report gives the verse its shape: the deed in it was done by people who had a reason to keep the food, and the Book praises the reason they did not.
**The Word for the Virtue**

The verse uses the word *īthār* — putting another before oneself — and it names the exact point of the test: the Helpers found no envy in their hearts over what the newcomers were given, and they preferred them even when they themselves were in need. The Book then states the principle that covers the rest of the passage, that whoever is protected from his own greed is the one who succeeds, and the Sūrah of the Cleft puts the same protection at the head of its command to keep duty to God as far as a person can (64:16 — *“So be mindful of Allah to the best of your ability, hear and obey, and spend in charity—that will be best for you. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful”*). The jurists used the verse as the model for the distribution of the spoils and for the duty of the community's wealthier members to the poorer, and they read that the virtue was voluntary while the law set the floor.
**Why the Verse Belongs in the Sūrah**

The chapter deals with the expulsion of the tribe of Naḍīr, the spoils that followed, and the poverty of the emigrants who came to Madinah with nothing. This verse stands at the centre of the distribution, and it honours the men who received the fewest. The Book's arrangement is deliberate: the sūrah describes the wealth changing hands and then names the quality that made the arrangement work, which is that the people who had a claim on the money gave it away.

## Verse 59:10

> And those who come after them will pray, “Our Lord! Forgive us and our fellow believers who preceded us in faith, and do not allow bitterness into our hearts towards those who believe. Our Lord! Indeed, You are Ever Gracious, Most Merciful.”

**Later Believers Enter the Community Through a Prayer of Kinship**

Those who come afterward are described by what they ask Allah to give. Their relation to earlier believers is not founded on rivalry for an earlier place in history. They acknowledge that others preceded them and call them fellow believers. Arrival at a later time does not mean arriving without a community, because faith joins them to people whose lives may already have ended.

Ibn Kathīr reads this as the class that follows the emigrants and helpers in good conduct and prayer for them, and connects it with the followers praised in 9:100 — *“As for the foremost—the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers—and those who follow them in goodness, Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him.”* (Ibn Kathīr on 59:10 — *“And those who come after them will pray, “Our Lord!”*). The description can so reach beyond one immediate group of later arrivals. Its force lies in the quality of following, not simply in being born later. A person joins the praised pattern by adopting its prayer and disposition. This broad reading fits the absence of a terminal generation in the wording. It also preserves the immediate historical reference: the people who first carried the community's burdens are not displaced when others inherit its life. Later believers receive a way to acknowledge their debt without pretending that chronology alone makes anyone immune from the need for forgiveness.

**Prayer for the Absent Breaks the Habit of Transaction**

Those who preceded the speakers may not be present to hear the prayer or return a favor. Asking forgiveness for them is therefore not a performance designed to secure their immediate approval. A verified Prophetic instruction reinforces this direction: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2732a teaches that an angel responds with a corresponding prayer for the believer who prays for his brother in his absence (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2732a). The page's Arabic chain names Abū al-Dardāʾ, while its English narrator label differs; the collection and teaching are clear, but the conflicting label should not be silently repeated.

## Verse 59:11

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen the hypocrites who say to their fellow disbelievers from the People of the Book, “If you are expelled, we will certainly leave with you, and We will never obey anyone against you. And if you are fought against, we will surely help you.”? But Allah bears witness that they are truly liars

**A Promise of Fellowship Can Conceal a Different Allegiance**

The verse exposes speech made by people whose outward profession and inward stance do not agree. They are identified as hypocrites, and the recipients of their assurance are described as their fellows among the disbelieving People of the Book. The relation is one of shared opposition in this particular setting. A claim of fellowship can be real at the level of present interest while remaining false as a promise of costly support.

Ibn Kathīr identifies the senders with ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy and those like him, and the recipients with Banū al-Naḍīr. He describes the assurance as conveyed through a messenger (Ibn Kathīr on 59:11 — *“Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen the hypocrites who say to their fellow disbelievers from the People of the Book, “If you are expelled, we will certainly leave with you”*). That identification adds specificity beyond the verse's wording without turning the case into a judgment against every interreligious friendship. The fault is not simply that people from different communities speak to one another. It is deception, hypocrisy, and a declared alignment whose promises Allah exposes as lies. The wording directs attention to what is offered and whether the offer is true. Religious labels alone cannot substitute for that examination, and an outward show of solidarity cannot make a knowingly false undertaking honest.

---

**What the Promise Was Worth**

The verses that follow test the offer, and the test is the point of the passage. If the Banū al-Naḍīr are expelled, the hypocrites will not go out with them; if they are fought, they will not help them; and if they do come to help, they will turn and run (59:12 — *“And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them.”*). The assurance was given through a messenger in the days before the siege, when the houses of the tribe still stood and their allies were counting the strength of the two sides; when the day came, the promise cost nothing because it was never meant to be paid. The men who made it stayed in Madinah and kept their position in the community, which was precisely what they were protecting.
**The Line the Verse Draws**

The sūrah was not forbidding friendship between neighbours of different faiths. It was exposing a particular kind of deceit: men who lived inside the Muslim community, took its protection, and used a promise to outsiders as a way of keeping a foot in both camps. The verse's sentence is about the offer, not about the label. Where the promise is honest, the Book leaves the bond alone; where the promise was a lie, no profession of fellowship can make it a treaty.

## Verse 59:12

> Indeed, if they are expelled, the hypocrites will never leave with them. And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them. And even if the hypocrites did so, they would certainly flee, then the disbelievers would be left with no help

**The Promise Is Tested at Each Point Where It Would Cost Something**

The verse examines the alliance through concrete conditions. Expulsion would require the supposed supporters to leave with their allies. Fighting would require them to provide help. The sentence denies that they will do either. A general profession of loyalty is so brought to the exact circumstances in which loyalty would become an act rather than a declaration.

**A Name Added to an Alliance Does Not Necessarily Add Help**

Ibn Kathīr explains this clause as a denial that the hypocrites will fight alongside the threatened group, and calls the verse good news in its address to the believers (Ibn Kathīr on 59:12 — *“And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them.”*). That checked interpretive addition identifies another side of the statement. It is not only exposure for those relying on the promise; it is reassurance for those who might fear an enlarged hostile force. The same declaration corrects two opposing calculations. One side must not count help that will not arrive, and the other must not assign real power to every threatening assurance. This does not make prudent assessment unnecessary. It means that the revealed judgment gives information beyond appearances in this particular case. The promised coalition cannot be understood solely by listening to how confidently its members describe themselves.

**What the Verse Tested**

The verse names the three things the alliance promised and would not deliver: if the confederates were expelled, these men would not go out with them; if they were fought, they would not fight alongside them; and if they did fight, they would turn their backs, and then no one would help them. The Book states the outcome in advance and the history confirms it, since the tribe of Naḍīr was expelled from Madinah and the men who claimed the bond stayed where they were. The Sūrah of the Parties gives the same account of the breaking of alliances during the siege of Madinah (33:20 — *“They ˹still˺ think that the enemy alliance has not ˹yet˺ withdrawn. And if the allies were to come ˹again˺, the hypocrites would wish to be away in the desert among nomadic Arabs, ˹only˺ asking for news about you ˹believers˺. And if the hypocrites were in your midst, they would hardly take part in the fight”*), and the report of the campaign against the Banū Qurayẓah is the same lesson again.
**The Reason Given**

The verse that follows explains the omission: they are more afraid of the believers than of God, because they are a people who do not understand (59:13 — *“Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you ˹believers˺ than for Allah. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend”*). The Book's diagnosis is that the fear was misplaced, and the rest of the passage states the further reason: their hearts are divided, and their agreement does not hold (59:14 — *“That is because they are a people with no ˹real˺ understanding”*). The Sūrah of the Spoils gives the same analysis of the men who broke the treaties in the earlier years, and the Book returns to it whenever the community is tempted to measure an ally by his words.
**What the Community Learnt**

The passage belongs to the year the tribe of Naḍīr was removed, and the sūrah tells the story of the negotiations that preceded it. The Book's lesson is stated in the verse that calls the men a people without understanding, and the Prophet's own practice after the campaign shows what the lesson produced: the community kept its treaties and stopped counting on the promises of the groups that had already broken them. The passage is also read by the jurists in the law of treaties and alliances, and its rule is that a claim of support has to be tested by what it delivers.

## Verse 59:13

> Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you ˹believers˺ than for Allah. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend

**The Comparison Reveals a Disordered Scale of Fear**

The verse compares two fears within the same hearts. The believers appear more frightening to the people described than Allah does. The problem is not that they recognize a real human danger. It is that their response to that danger outweighs their recognition of divine authority. An immediate threat has become more decisive than the Lord who governs every threat and judges their conduct.

This distinction matters because ordinary caution is not itself unbelief. People can take precautions, feel alarm, and still revere Allah. The verse identifies a reversal of proportion, not the mere existence of a bodily reaction. Its moral concern is what commands the heart's practical allegiance. If fear of people determines what one will betray, conceal, or abandon, while fear of Allah supplies no effective restraint, the comparison has entered conduct. The words in their hearts locate that disorder where appearances alone might fail to disclose it. People can use confident language publicly while inwardly being ruled by dread. Revelation exposes that hidden ordering in this case. It does not ask the believers to guess everyone's inward fears from outward nervousness. It tells them a fact about the particular opponents whose apparent confidence might otherwise seem reliable.

**The Believers Receive Reassurance Without Being Made Sovereign**

The statement is addressed to the believers: they are more feared than they may realize. This corrects an assessment based only on their opponents' outward confidence. Ibn Kathīr reads the passage as reassurance to the believers and connects the comparison with 4:77 — *“Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who had been told, “Do not fight!”*, where some people fear human beings as much as Allah or more (Ibn Kathīr on 59:13 — *“Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you ˹believers˺ than for Allah. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend”*). This checked parallel makes the moral issue portable beyond one group: the same disorder can appear wherever human dread displaces divine reverence.

## Verse 59:14

> Even united, they would not ˹dare˺ fight against you except ˹from˺ within fortified strongholds or from behind walls. Their malice for each other is intense: you think they are united, yet their hearts are divided. That is because they are a people with no ˹real˺ understanding

**Violence Among Themselves Does Not Become Shared Courage**

Their malice toward one another is intense. The translation selects the sense of internal hostility for the expression about their strength or fierceness among themselves. Ibn Kathīr likewise explains it as severe enmity within the group (Ibn Kathīr on 59:14 — *“Their malice for each other is intense: you think they are united, yet their hearts are divided.”*). That reading fits the explicit statement about divided hearts which immediately follows. The context supplies a reason for preferring it over a simple claim that they have impressive strength when gathered.

**What an Observer Sees Is Not the Whole of the Alliance**

The address distinguishes appearance from reality: an observer thinks them united, while their hearts are divided. A common public position can hide incompatible aims. People may assemble, issue joint messages, and identify an opponent without sharing the deeper commitments that allow them to endure cost together. Outward organization is real, but it is not the whole truth of their relation.

**The Warning About Division Also Speaks to Believers**

A checked parallel in 8:46 — *“Obey Allah and His Messenger and do not dispute with one another, or you would be discouraged and weakened. Persevere! Surely Allah is with those who persevere”* warns believers that disputing with one another can bring discouragement and loss of strength, while directing them toward obedience and perseverance (Qurʾān 8:46 — *“Obey Allah and His Messenger and do not dispute with one another, or you would be discouraged and weakened. Persevere! Surely Allah is with those who persevere”*). This additional reference prevents the present description from becoming an occasion for effortless self-praise. The practical consequences of destructive conflict do not disappear when those involved carry the name of believers. They too must cultivate the qualities that make common action durable.

**What the Verse Says About Them**

The verse says that these men will not fight the believers together except in fortified towns or behind walls, and that their fighting among themselves is severe, so that a stranger would take them for one body while their hearts are divided. The sūrah then gives the reason: they are a people who do not understand. The battle at the trench had shown the same thing, where the confederates could not hold an alliance together for a season, and the Sūrah of the Parties describes the wind sent against them and the army that turned back (33:9 — *“O believers! Remember Allah’s favour upon you when ˹enemy˺ forces came to ˹besiege˺ you ˹in Medina˺, so We sent against them a ˹bitter˺ wind and forces you could not see.”*).
**The Rule the Sūrah Draws**

The Book's judgement on the alliance is stated a few verses later, where the sūrah says that if they were driven out they would not go out with them, and if they were fought they would not fight with them, and if they did fight they would turn their backs (59:12 — *“Indeed, if they are expelled, the hypocrites will never leave with them. And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them. And even if the hypocrites did so, they would certainly flee, then the disbelievers would be left with no help”*). The verse in front of us gives the reason for the whole assessment, and the commentators read it as a general statement about a faction whose public unity is not held together by anything. The Sūrah of the Spoils states the same thing about the men who broke their treaties in the earlier years (8:56-58 — *“˹namely˺ those with whom you ˹O Prophet˺ have entered into treaties, but they violate them every time, not fearing the consequences If you ever encounter them in battle, make a fearsome example of them, so perhaps those who would follow them may be deterred And if you ˹O Prophet˺ see signs of betrayal by a people, respond by openly terminating your treaty with them. Surely Allah does not like those who betray”*).
**What the Community Was to Learn**

The passage belongs to the removal of the Banū Naḍīr from Madinah, and the sūrah's purpose is to explain why an army was not needed to move them and why their allies did not come. The Book's lesson is placed in the phrase about the hearts, and the community's own history repeated it in the campaigns that followed: a coalition of complaints is not the same thing as a community with a purpose.

## Verse 59:15

> They are ˹both just˺ like those who recently went down before them: they tasted the evil consequences of their doings. And they will suffer a painful punishment

**The Same Formula Elsewhere Broadens the Lesson Without Erasing the Case**

Qur'an 64:5 — *“Have the stories of those who disbelieved before not reached you ˹pagans˺? They tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and they will suffer a painful punishment”* also recalls earlier disbelievers who experienced the evil results of their conduct and still face painful punishment (Qurʾān 64:5 — *“Have the stories of those who disbelieved before not reached you ˹pagans˺? They tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and they will suffer a painful punishment”*). This checked parallel adds a wider Qur'anic setting to the local historical identification. The combination of an experienced consequence and a further warning is not incidental. It directs attention to history as a present admonition under a final judgment that history alone cannot display completely.

**A Recent Precedent Makes the Failure to Learn More Exposed**

The comparison points to people whose downfall was recent. The warning does not require the audience to recover an obscure event from a distant age. Something close enough to remain within living awareness had already showed the consequence of a similar course. Recency strengthens the argument because it weakens the excuse that no relevant example was available.

**The Named Historical Reading Is Banū Qaynuqāʿ**

Ibn Kathīr identifies the immediate predecessors as Banū Qaynuqāʿ and attributes that interpretation to Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, and Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq (Ibn Kathīr on 59:15 — *“They are ˹both just˺ like those who recently went down before them: they tasted the evil consequences of their doings. And they will suffer a painful punishment”*). This checked identification supplies a historical referent for the comparison. It remains an interpretive identification, since the verse itself does not name the group. The distinction preserves both the value of the commentary and the exact limits of the wording.

## Verse 59:16

> ˹They are˺ like Satan when he lures someone to disbelieve. Then after they have done so, he will say ˹on Judgment Day˺, “I have absolutely nothing to do with you. I truly fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds.”

**The Comparison Exposes Encouragement Without Faithful Support**

The verse presents a compact comparison: Satan urges someone toward disbelief, then disowns the person once the urged act has been committed. The deceiver is active when another's decision is still being formed and absent as a supporter when responsibility must be faced. His interest lies in obtaining the act, not in the person's good. The apparent relation of guidance is therefore false from the beginning.

Ibn Kathīr applies the comparison to deceptive promises of support in the surrounding historical setting (Ibn Kathīr on 59:16 — *“˹They are˺ like Satan when he lures someone to disbelieve.”*). The moral pattern is what matters here: encouragement can induce a costly choice without creating the reliable bond it seems to offer. The verse does not need a named individual or an elaborate tale about how that individual fell. Its unnamed person allows the mechanism itself to stand clearly. Someone receives an invitation that promises advantage or removes hesitation, acts upon it, and then finds that the inviter will not share the burden. The comparison should therefore be read as an exposure of false dependence. The one who solicits a wrong is not necessarily a companion through its consequences, however persuasive his involvement appears before the decision is made.

**The Lure Does Not Remove the Person's Responsibility to Choose**

A checked parallel in 14:22 — *“And Satan will say ˹to his followers˺ after the judgment has been passed, “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, nor can you save me. Indeed, I denounce your previous association of me with Allah ˹in loyalty˺. Surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment.”* makes this distinction explicit. Satan acknowledges that he called and his followers responded, while denying the capacity to rescue them (Qurʾān 14:22 — *“I only called you, and you responded to me.”*). This additional reference clarifies the present comparison without supplying an invented conversation. The person's mistake is not simply meeting a deceiver. It is treating the deceiver's invitation as enough reason to abandon the truth. That distinction keeps the warning practical. A believer cannot guarantee never to encounter misleading speech, but can refuse to make its attractiveness the measure of its authority. The verse also resists a convenient transfer of all guilt to temptation. Influence is real and can be powerful, yet a promise from an untrustworthy guide does not become a release from one's own obligation to judge and act rightly. Both the solicitation and the response remain morally significant.

## Verse 59:17

> So they will both end up in the Fire, staying there forever. That is the reward of the wrongdoers

**Disavowal Does Not Separate the Deceiver From Judgment**

Both figures in the comparison reach the Fire: the one who enticed and the one who accepted the enticement. The deceiver's tried withdrawal has not removed his responsibility. He can announce distance from the person he misled, but he cannot make the history of his own action disappear. The verdict is given by Allah, not by whichever participant speaks most forcefully about innocence.

**A Wider Qur'anic Scene Preserves Responsibility on Both Sides**

Qur'an 7:38 — *“Allah will say, “Enter the Fire along with the ˹evil˺ groups of jinn and humans that preceded you.” Whenever a group enters Hell, it will curse the preceding one until they are all gathered inside, the followers will say about their leaders, “Our Lord! They have misled us, so multiply their torment in the Fire.” He will answer, “It has already been multiplied for all, but you do not know.”* describes followers entering the Fire and accusing the leaders who misled them. Their accusation does not produce release for the followers; the answer maintains punishment across the groups involved (Qurʾān 7:38 — *“They have misled us, so multiply their torment in the Fire.”*). This checked parallel adds a fuller example of the principle stated compactly here. Shared wrongdoing can generate mutual accusation without giving either party control of the divine account.

The parallel is useful because it resists two simplifications. One would place all responsibility on the leader, as though those who followed had made no choices. The other would place all responsibility on the followers, as though deliberate misguidance added no wrong of its own. Neither captures the Qur'anic presentation. Enticing others is an act, and responding is also an act. The present verse's dual outcome holds those responsibilities together. It does not make analysis of influence unnecessary; it makes that analysis more exact. The person who encouraged the wrong cannot hide behind the claim that someone else performed it. The person who performed it cannot regard another's encouragement as a complete defense. The final judge can recognize the contribution of each without accepting either one's try to rewrite the relation after it has produced its evil result.

## Verse 59:18

> O believers! Be mindful of Allah and let every soul look to what ˹deeds˺ it has sent forth for tomorrow. And fear Allah, ˹for˺ certainly Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**The Prophet Applied This Verse to an Immediate Need for Relief**

A report from Jarīr ibn ʿAbdillāh describes people arriving in severe poverty. The Prophet's visible concern was followed by prayer and an address in which he recited 4:1 — *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you”* and this verse, urging material assistance. Food, clothing, and money were brought until the need drew a substantial communal response. The account is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017a. It establishes a direct Prophetic use of the present instruction, not merely a later association with charitable work.

That application prevents self-examination from becoming only an inward inventory of feelings. Looking toward tomorrow can require seeing a hungry person today. The future account is prepared partly through one's response to an immediate, visible need. The report also includes encouragement to give even a very small contribution. This checked detail matters because a person can postpone generosity while waiting to have enough for an impressive gift. The verse's demand is not that everyone display equal resources. It is that each soul consider its actual opportunity to do good. An action modest in amount may still be a honest answer to the command, while a large capacity left unused remains part of what the person must examine.

**An Example Can Send More Than One Act Ahead**

The same report describes an Anṣārī man bringing a substantial contribution, after which others followed. The Prophet then explained the reward of setting a good example that others continue, and the corresponding burden of setting an evil example. These details are verified in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017a and add a dimension beyond the simple distribution of aid. A deed can shape subsequent deeds by making a course of action visible and practicable to others.

## Verse 59:19

> And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. It is they who are ˹truly˺ rebellious

**The Consequence Fits the Direction of the Neglect**

Allah causes the second forgetting. The verse does not leave the consequence as a purely accidental psychological effect. It describes a divine response corresponding to the chosen neglect. Those who set aside their Lord are left without a sound orientation toward their own good. The punishment is not foreign to the offense; it appears within the very life they sought to govern apart from remembrance.

Ibn Kathīr explains this as forgetting the good deeds that would benefit them upon their return to Allah, emphasizing a correspondence between action and recompense (Ibn Kathīr on 59:19 — *“And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. It is they who are ˹truly˺ rebellious”*). That checked explanation makes the consequence concrete. The problem is not only that a person feels less reflective. Opportunities for obedience are missed, duties are displaced, and beneficial action is neglected. A life can become full of activity while its preparation for the Hereafter remains empty. The divine attribution should not be used to excuse the initial neglect, since the sentence explicitly begins with what the people did. Nor should the warning be transformed into a claim that a passing distraction proves abandonment by Allah. It concerns a moral course and its fitting consequence, not a diagnosis of every unsettled mood or ordinary lapse.

**Useful Possessions Can Become Instruments of Forgetfulness**

Ibn Kathīr connects the warning with 63:9 — *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers”*, which cautions believers against allowing wealth and children to distract them from remembrance of Allah. This checked cross-reference adds a practical example beyond the per-verse account. The distractions need not be things evil in themselves. Real goods and real responsibilities can become disordered when they claim the whole field of attention.

## Verse 59:20
> The residents of the Fire cannot be equal to the residents of Paradise. ˹Only˺ the residents of Paradise will be successful

**Different Final Destinations Cannot Be Treated as Equivalent**

The verse states an inequality between the residents of the Fire and the residents of Paradise. Its subject is final standing before Allah, not the denial of ordinary human rights in this life. The two groups have reached destinations whose meanings cannot be merged into a single undifferentiated outcome. Judgment distinguishes; it does not declare every course of life equally successful.

**Success Is Defined by Salvation Rather Than Visibility**

A checked parallel in 3:185 — *“Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment. Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment”* defines triumph through being kept from the Fire and admitted to Paradise, while locating full recompense on the Day of Judgment (Qurʾān 3:185 — *“And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment.”*). This additional reference clarifies the criterion rather than merely repeating a favorable description of Paradise. Success includes rescue from what destroys and admission to what fulfills. The world can offer partial goods without supplying that final verdict. Possessions, recognition, and influence may be used well, but none is an adequate substitute for the end named here. Conversely, a person whose faithful life involves hardship has not failed on that account. The comparison frees evaluation from the demand that every true good must appear as immediate public advantage. The outcome before Allah is decisive, and the passage teaches the reader to order present ambition by that outcome rather than the other way around.

## Verse 59:21

> Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain, you would have certainly seen it humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah. We set forth such comparisons for people, ˹so˺ perhaps they may reflect

**Humility and Fracture Describe More Than Emotional Excitement**

The mountain is both humbled and torn apart. The first description concerns abasement before divine greatness; the second makes the effect upon its apparent strength unmistakable. Together they portray a response deeper than a passing impression. The object whose very shape suggests resistance cannot remain untouched by the encounter the verse imagines.

The cause is awe of Allah. The Qur'an's weight is not detached from the One who speaks through revelation. A listener who admires only the sound or force of the language while refusing its claim upon life has not exhausted what the verse asks him to consider. Awe recognizes authority, truth, promise, and warning. Ibn Kathīr explains the mountain as hypothetically endowed with comprehension of the Qur'an and so responding to what it understands (Ibn Kathīr on 59:21 — *“Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain, you would have certainly seen it humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah.”*). That checked explanation clarifies the nature of the comparison. The sentence is not a claim about the normal geological effects of sound waves. It imagines reception adequate to the revelation's meaning. The resulting humility is therefore not mindless collapse. It is a portrayal of what recognition of Allah's address would do to an otherwise formidable object.

**Related Images Confirm That Hardness Is Not the Highest Strength**

Ibn Kathīr links the verse with 2:74 — *“Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do”*, where stones release water, split, or fall in fear of Allah. He also cites 13:31 — *“If there were a recitation that could cause mountains to move, or the earth to split, or the dead to speak, ˹it would have been this Quran˺. But all matters are by Allah’s Will. Have the believers not yet realized that had Allah willed, He could have guided all of humanity? And disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes for their misdeeds, until Allah’s promise comes to pass. Surely Allah never fails in His promise”* in connection with the Qur'an and the movement of mountains. These checked cross-references add a wider setting for the present image. They challenge the assumption that a thing's worth lies in its capacity to remain unaffected. Receptivity to Allah is not a defect in creation's strength.

**Reflection Is the Response the Comparison Explicitly Seeks**

The verse names its purpose: people may reflect. The dramatic image is therefore not an end in itself. A listener who remembers only the torn mountain has not completed the movement requested. Reflection asks what the comparison reveals about Allah, the Qur'an, and the hearer's own response. It carries the image into judgment and conduct.

## Verse 59:22

> He is Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him: Knower of the seen and unseen. He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**Seen and Unseen Describe Human Limits, Not Limits Within Allah's Knowledge**

Allah knows the seen and the unseen. Things differ in their accessibility to human beings: some can be observed, others remain hidden, distant, inward, or beyond ordinary perception. The verse includes both within divine knowledge. What is unseen to a creature is not uncertain to Allah, and what becomes visible to people does not become newly discovered by Him.

This makes the distinction more than a reference to unusual hidden events. An intention concealed beneath an ordinary action belongs to the same comprehensive knowledge as an event publicly witnessed by thousands. Smallness does not remove a matter from His awareness, and publicity does not make it more knowable to Him. Ibn Kathīr explains the phrase as encompassing what creatures see and what they cannot see, without exception for the great or small (Ibn Kathīr on 59:22 — *“He is Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him: Knower of the seen and unseen. He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*). That checked explanation preserves the breadth of the wording. The believer need not provide Allah with information that He lacks when praying. Prayer expresses dependence upon the One who already knows the need. Likewise, concealment cannot make wrongdoing absent from His account. The same attribute grounds both assurance and responsibility because complete knowledge recognizes the hidden plea and the hidden act alike.

**A Prophetic Comparison Makes Mercy Understandable Without Measuring Its Limit**

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reports the Prophet drawing attention to a mother caring for a child among captives and teaching that Allah is more merciful to His servants than she is to her child. The account is verified in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5999. This additional evidence gives the names of mercy a concrete point of comparison while explicitly placing divine mercy beyond it.

## Verse 59:23

> He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect, the Source of Serenity, the Watcher ˹of all˺, the Almighty, the Supreme in Might, the Majestic. Glorified is Allah far above what they associate with Him ˹in worship˺

**The Names Describe One Lord Rather Than a Division of Divinity**

The declaration of Allah's sole divinity governs the entire list. Each name describes the same Lord. Kingship, holiness, protection, and might are not assigned to separate beings, nor do the names identify parts from which Allah is assembled. Their plurality gives a fuller description of the One who alone is worthy of worship.

Al-Malik, the King, establishes sovereignty at the beginning of the sequence. His authority is not borrowed from another ruler or granted by those He governs. Ibn Kathīr explains the name through ownership and rule over all things, without a power able to prevent His command (Ibn Kathīr on 59:23 — *“He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect, the Source of Serenity, the Watcher ˹of all˺, the Almighty, the Supreme in Might, the Majestic.”*). Created kings rule within limits: they inherit conditions they did not make and depend on resources they cannot fully control. Allah's kingship is not simply a larger instance of that dependence. The other names prevent the reader from imagining sovereignty through the defects familiar in human rulers. The King is also holy, perfect, and watchful. His greatness does not come at the expense of justice or knowledge. The sequence teaches whom the worshipper acknowledges as sovereign and why that acknowledgment cannot be transferred to an impressive creature.

**Holiness and Perfection Exclude the Defects of Created Rule**

The name al-Salām also matters in the language of prayer. Ibn Masʿūd reports that the Prophet corrected the practice of wishing peace upon Allah, explaining that Allah Himself is al-Salām. The report is verified in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 835. The correction distinguishes the One from whom safety and perfection come from creatures who need a prayer for safety. It is not a rejection of peacefulness as a good. It is a correction of direction: the servant depends upon Allah, not Allah upon the servant's blessing. Read in this verse, the report keeps the name from being reduced to a pleasant feeling. Divine perfection is the ground on which a creature can seek peace, not a state the creature must first supply to its Lord.

**Majesty Belongs to Allah Without Becoming Permission for Human Pride**

A report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī and Abū Hurayrah warns against contesting Allah's glory and majesty. It is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2620. This addition makes the practical distinction explicit: a divine perfection can expose a corresponding human vice when a creature tries to appropriate what belongs uniquely to its Lord. The report's garment imagery expresses that exclusive majesty and should not be turned into an invented physical description. Its ethical force is humility. Recognizing Allah as Majestic should loosen the demand to occupy the highest place among people. The name does not authorize the worshipper to imitate absolute sovereignty. It teaches him why his own dignity must remain compatible with obedience, dependence, and fair treatment of other creatures.

## Verse 59:24

> He is Allah: the Creator, the Inventor, the Shaper. He ˹alone˺ has the Most Beautiful Names. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Him. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise

**Creation Has Its Origin, Existence, and Form From One Lord**

The three opening names concern Allah's relation to what He brings into being. Al-Khāliq, the Creator, al-Bāriʾ, the Inventor, and al-Muṣawwir, the Shaper, distinguish aspects of creation without dividing the work among independent powers. The being who determines is the being who brings forth and gives form. Nothing in the sequence suggests that Allah needs another agent to complete a task beyond His own ability.

Ibn Kathīr explains the first name through determination and proportion, the second through bringing into existence, and the third through the particular form Allah wills (Ibn Kathīr on 59:24 — *“He is Allah: the Creator, the Inventor, the Shaper.”*). These distinctions help the reader see why the names are not empty repetition. A human plan can remain unrealized, and an existing material can await a form its maker struggles to impose. Allah's creative authority is not subject to those divisions of capacity. The names disclose a complete relation to the creature. They should not be treated as a compulsory timetable of successive divine experiments. The verse praises the One who knows and wills what He creates, not a craftsman learning by trial how to make an uncertain design succeed.

**Individual Form Belongs Within the Universal Act of Creation**

A checked parallel in 3:6 — *“He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise”* describes Allah shaping people in their mothers' wombs as He wills and closes with the same names Almighty and All-Wise (Qurʾān 3:6 — *“He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise”*). This additional reference gives the universal name a direct human application. It does not turn the three names here into a scientific classification of developmental stages. Such a claim would require evidence the wording does not supply. The parallel instead teaches dependence at the beginning of one's bodily life. No person first designs himself and then enters a relation with Allah. That relation precedes his ability to understand or choose. Recognition of the Shaper can therefore support gratitude and humility without becoming a warrant to judge another person's worth by bodily appearance. The Creator's relation to each person is more fundamental than the comparisons through which people rank one another's forms.

**The Report of Ninety-Nine Names Connects Learning With Salvation**

Abū Hurayrah transmits the report about ninety-nine names and the promise of Paradise for the person who preserves them. The checked version is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6410; its English rendering explains the response through belief in their meanings and corresponding action (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6410). The Arabic wording stresses preserving or memorizing them. The explanatory English should not be mistaken for an additional separate Arabic sentence, but it rightly directs attention beyond empty repetition.

**All Creation Glorifies the One Who Gives It Being**

Ibn Kathīr connects this statement with 17:44 — *“The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification. He is indeed Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving”*, which affirms the glorification of all things while limiting human understanding of their praise. The comparison restrains two impulses: reducing every creature's glorification to familiar human speech, and denying its reality because that speech is not heard. The verse supplies the fact without making the reader master of every mode in which it occurs. Its opening names give the declaration a clear foundation.
