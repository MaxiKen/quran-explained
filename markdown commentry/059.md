# Sūrah al-Ḥashr (Chapter 59) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:1

> Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah. For He is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise Encompasses More Than the Human Congregation**

The opening places all created existence in relation to Allah. Heaven and earth are not separate territories, one belonging to worship and the other to ordinary affairs. Everything within them falls under the declaration of glorification. Human devotion therefore does not begin praise in an otherwise silent universe. It joins a reality more extensive than any community's knowledge, language, or gathering. The verse makes that breadth its starting point rather than reaching it after a discussion of human history.

The repeated reference to whatever is in each realm prevents attention from stopping at the largest visible objects. The statement concerns the contents of creation, not merely the sky above and the ground below as a pair of grand symbols. Smallness does not remove a creature from dependence on Allah, and remoteness does not place it beyond His rule. Yet this universality must not be mistaken for the claim that every human choice is obedient. People can refuse the worship required of them. The verse speaks of creation's comprehensive relation to its Lord, a relation that no rebellious choice can abolish. Human refusal does not turn a creature into an independent being or make divine glory depend on human permission.

**Glorification Does Not Make the Creator Part of Creation**

To glorify Allah is to acknowledge His freedom from defect and from the limitations of created things. The direction of the sentence matters: creation glorifies Allah. It is not itself Allah, nor does its magnificence supply something missing from Him. The world is the worshipping side of the relation, not an extension of the One worshipped. The verse therefore permits wonder at created things while denying them the status of final objects of devotion.

This distinction also protects praise from being reduced to admiration for size or complexity. An impressive object can awaken reflection, but its scale does not make it sovereign. A living creature can possess remarkable powers while remaining dependent. Even the combined contents of heaven and earth do not cross the boundary between creatures and Creator by becoming numerous. Their glorification points beyond themselves. In practical terms, the verse sets a limit on every human tendency to treat a powerful feature of the world as ultimate. The world can be studied, cultivated, and appreciated without being worshipped. Its place in this sentence is already fixed: it bears witness to a perfection it does not create and an authority it does not confer. Praise is recognition of that order, not a bargain by which creatures bestow divinity.

**The Mode of Praise Need Not Be Familiar to Us**

The declaration is wider than our ability to describe how each creature glorifies Allah. It gives the fact of praise without presenting a catalogue of its forms. One should not fill that silence with imagined sounds or claims about the hidden language of particular objects. Nor should the absence of ordinary human speech be treated as proof that the declaration has no reality. Our familiarity with one mode of expression is not a measure of every possible relation between creatures and their Lord.

Two ways of understanding created praise should be distinguished. A thing's ordered existence can indicate its Maker's perfection, and a creature can glorify in a manner known to Allah even when people cannot understand it. The first explains how creation serves as a sign; the second takes seriously the declaration of praise beyond human comprehension. These need not be enemies, but reducing every instance to what people can infer would narrow the verse unnecessarily. Ibn Kathīr's commentary on this verse explicitly treats the whole creation as praising and revering Allah and connects that reading to 17:44. The latter passage places a limit on human understanding of such praise. The stronger approach therefore affirms the declaration without pretending to know all its mechanisms. Recognizing a limit of knowledge is different from denying what the verse affirms.

**Might Means No Creature Can Secure Independence**

The name Almighty follows the comprehensive declaration of praise. The divine authority to which creation responds is not one power competing with others inside the same world. No creature can establish a protected area beyond Allah's reach. Human strength can exceed the strength of another human being; it cannot become a rival to the authority that sustains both. This is why the first half of the verse and the closing name belong together. Universal praise corresponds to a sovereignty that is not local or conditional.

Ibn Kathīr explains this name here in terms of majesty that cannot be overcome. That checked linguistic and exegetical addition sharpens the point: might is not simply the ability to win a contest after meeting resistance on equal terms ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:1](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/1/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). Created powers do not become independent by resisting the obligations laid upon them. Their resistance remains the action of dependent beings. This does not make their choices unreal, but it prevents their choices from being mistaken for a successful escape from lordship. For worship, the consequence is clear. Praise is not flattery offered to a vulnerable ruler who needs reassurance. It recognizes an authority whose reality neither increases through applause nor diminishes through refusal.

**Wisdom Prevents Power From Being Read as Mere Force**

All-Wise stands beside Almighty. Power alone, when imagined through human examples, can suggest unrestrained appetite or the ability to impose anything at all. The second name excludes that misunderstanding of Allah. His action is not arbitrary, His judgment is not confused, and His command is not an experiment undertaken to discover what He does not know. The two names must be read together rather than assigning the world's order to wisdom and its difficult events to a separate, mindless force.

The same checked commentary explains wisdom here as belonging to what Allah ordains and to what He commands. That distinction adds precision: both the ordering of events and religious guidance fall under wisdom. It does not follow that a person can identify the complete reason for every event. Belief in divine wisdom and possession of that wisdom are different claims. A reader may affirm the former while lacking the latter. This guards against making confident explanations for suffering that revelation has not supplied. The verse gives grounds for trust, not a license to speak for Allah about every particular outcome. Its compact movement is therefore exact: all creation glorifies Him, no competing power can overcome Him, and His sovereignty is inseparable from wisdom. Worship answers that joined reality rather than fear of strength alone.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:2

> He is the One Who expelled the disbelievers of the People of the Book from their homes for ˹their˺ first banishment ˹ever˺. You never thought they would go. And they thought their strongholds would put them out of Allah’s reach. But ˹the decree of˺ Allah came upon them from where they never expected. And He cast horror into their hearts so they destroyed their houses with their own hands and the hands of the believers. So take a lesson ˹from this˺, O people of insight!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Expulsion Concerns a Particular Community**

The verse describes the removal of a particular group from its homes. Ibn Kathīr identifies that group as Banū al-Naḍīr, a Jewish tribe in the region of Madinah, and explains the event within the history of their broken agreement with the Prophet ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:2](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/1/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). This identification is necessary because the phrase People of the Book is broader than the group involved in this event. The passage is not a report that all scriptural communities were expelled, nor does it make every member of such a community responsible for the conduct of these participants.

The opening also assigns the decisive outcome to Allah. That does not remove the human actions described later in the same verse. People hold houses, assess defenses, feel fear, and dismantle buildings. Divine agency and these visible actions are not offered as competing accounts from which one must choose. The verse gives an interpretation of the outcome while naming the means through which it became visible. Its reference to a first banishment locates the event as a removal from settled life. No complete itinerary or chronology is stated here. The commentary should therefore distinguish the clear identification of the group from historical details that require separate evidence.

**Opponents and Believers Shared a Mistaken Forecast**

Two expectations are set beside each other. The believers did not expect the occupants to leave, and the occupants trusted their fortifications. These were not identical beliefs, but both treated the visible defenses as a strong reason to expect continued possession. The verse corrects both sides. It does not flatter the believers by pretending that they had always understood how the event would end. Their surprise is part of the lesson.

The occupants' error went further than recognizing the value of walls. Their confidence treated those walls as protection from Allah's reach. A material defense had been assigned an absolute meaning it could not bear. Fortifications can obstruct human attack; they cannot guarantee an outcome against divine decree. That distinction does not make practical preparation foolish. It makes ultimate reliance on preparation false. The same distinction explains why the believers' surprise matters. They too had allowed an assessment of visible strength to place a limit on what they thought possible. The passage is therefore not simply a contrast between wise victors and foolish losers. It exposes a shared narrowness of forecast while distinguishing the defiant confidence of those inside the strongholds. A sound account of events must leave room for factors that neither side has mastered.

**The Unexpected Opening Was Inside Their Hearts**

The decree arrived through a direction they had not anticipated, and the next clause names horror within their hearts. The external strength of a place is not identical with the internal ability of its occupants to endure. A fortified building can remain substantial while confidence collapses. The verse brings these two levels together: visible defenses had shaped expectations, but an unseen change in resolve helped overturn them. What seemed secure from outside was not secure in every respect.

Ibn Kathīr connects the unexpected reversal with 16:26, where a scheme is undone from a direction its makers did not foresee. This checked cross-reference adds a useful distinction. The problem is not merely that one calculation happened to be inaccurate. Human planning can fail at the very point its makers consider settled. Neither passage warrants claiming that every fearful person is being punished or that every failed defense reveals a person's moral standing. Here the explanation is given by revelation for this event. Outside such an identified case, fear may arise in many circumstances, and human observers do not possess Allah's complete judgment. The lesson is humility about the reach of one's plans, not an unrestricted right to label other people's suffering. The text describes a particular terror and gives its meaning within a particular judgment.

**Their Hands Undid What Their Walls Had Promised**

The destruction of the houses turns the reversal into something concrete. The very people who expected secure possession took part in dismantling what had housed and protected them. Their own hands appear alongside those of the believers. The verse does not leave the loss at the level of emotion or military expectation. It shows security being physically taken apart by people who had depended on it.

In the historical account transmitted by Ibn Kathīr, the departing occupants removed parts of their houses, including fittings they could carry away. This is an explanation preserved in his commentary, not a newly authenticated Prophetic saying. It makes the literal reference to their hands intelligible: salvaging possessions could involve destroying the buildings from which those possessions were taken. A moral reading can also recognize that their conduct had contributed to the loss of their homes. These readings need not cancel each other, but the literal action deserves priority because the verse expressly names hands and houses. Reducing the statement entirely to a figure of speech would weaken its sharp physical contrast. What had promised permanence became material for departure. The scene also prevents a simple account in which everything was done only by the victorious side. The text deliberately records participation on both sides in the destruction.

**Insight Means Learning Rather Than Merely Watching**

The closing command asks for a lesson, not just acknowledgment that an unusual event occurred. People can observe a reversal, recount its details, and still preserve the same false confidence in their own circumstances. Insight requires moving from the event to a sound judgment about dependence and the limits of apparent security. The command is addressed to those capable of seeing precisely because sight alone does not ensure understanding.

The lesson must remain proportionate to the evidence. The verse does not authorize a general policy of expelling religious minorities, treating inherited identity as guilt, or destroying homes whenever a powerful group claims divine approval. It interprets an identified event under revealed judgment. Its transferable warning concerns the error of treating worldly strength as immunity from Allah. The believers addressed must learn this too, since their own mistaken expectation is openly recorded. A lesson that only celebrates another group's loss would miss that self-correction. The event demands a change in the observer's judgment, not delight in destruction. Genuine insight acknowledges that resources are real without making them absolute, and that victory does not make the victor the source of the decree.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:3

> Had Allah not decreed exile for them, He would have certainly punished them in this world. And in the Hereafter they will suffer the punishment of the Fire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Exile Was an Appointed Outcome, Not an Escape From Judgment**

The verse interprets banishment by setting it beside a different possible worldly outcome. Exile did not occur because Allah could do nothing more. It occurred under His decree. The people concerned left their homes, but their departure must not be mistaken for having defeated divine judgment. The opening condition explains the meaning of the result: the actual form of their loss had been appointed rather than forced upon Allah by circumstances.

This distinction is necessary because a survivor can misread survival. Avoiding one feared outcome does not prove that no judgment has taken place. Here the form was removal from settled life rather than the alternative punishment mentioned in the sentence. The verse does not minimize exile. Losing one's home is not treated as a trivial administrative change. Rather, it denies that this loss exhausts the range of what Allah could have decreed. The counterfactual is supplied by revelation, not by an observer guessing what almost happened. That matters for interpretation. The reader is entitled to affirm this particular alternative because the verse states it, but not to construct imagined punishments for every other historical event. The knowledge claimed here belongs to Allah, whose decree gives the actual outcome its meaning.

**A Specific Contrast Explains the Worldly Punishment**

The sentence can raise a question: if exile is itself painful, why does it contrast exile with punishment in this world? The contrast concerns the kind of punishment, not the claim that banishment involved no suffering. Ibn Kathīr explains the unrealized alternative as killing or captivity and attributes that explanation to al-Zuhrī ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:3](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/1/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). This is an exegetical explanation from a named early scholar, not a Prophetic saying for which an invented hadith number should be supplied.

That checked addition clarifies the sentence while preserving the distinction between wording and interpretation. The verse itself says worldly punishment without naming its precise form. The commentary explains what the contrast can mean in the historical setting. It is sound to use that explanation to show why the conditional sentence is coherent; it would be less sound to pretend that the translation explicitly lists every alternative. Exile and a more destructive outcome need not be placed on opposite sides of a simple mercy-versus-judgment division. One can be a limited form of judgment compared with another. The passage's point is not that the actual loss was harmless, but that it was bounded by the decree. The limit was real, and so was the loss.

**The Unchosen Possibility Does Not Authorize Human Cruelty**

The declaration of what Allah would have done is not a command telling believers to supply a harsher outcome themselves. Its grammar is explanatory and conditional. It tells why the historical result must not be read as an inability to punish. Turning that explanation into permission for additional violence would reverse the sentence's function: a verse about the outcome Allah decreed would become a pretext for refusing that outcome and choosing another.

This boundary is especially important where commentary concerns the loss of homes and the prospect of death or captivity. The text does not invite celebration of suffering, nor does it give later readers authority to act as though they know Allah's hidden judgment against their neighbors. The referent is the particular group under discussion. An inherited religious identity is not a license to apply this threatened alternative indiscriminately to other people. Within the verse, divine judgment is certain because Allah states it. Outside that revealed identification, human beings must not manufacture the same certainty for groups they dislike. A responsible reading holds together the severity of the warning and the limit on the reader's authority. The verse is neither softened into a claim that nothing serious occurred nor expanded into an unrestricted human mandate to punish.

**The Hereafter Is Not Settled by the Manner of Departure**

The second sentence moves beyond worldly outcomes. Whether the people concerned leave by exile or suffer another form of worldly punishment does not determine the whole of their accountability. The punishment of the Fire is stated separately. This prevents the earthly event from being treated as a complete settlement after which no further judgment remains. The distinction between the two realms is therefore part of the verse's argument, not simply an added warning at its end.

Several false conclusions are excluded at once. Departure does not prove innocence. A less destructive worldly outcome does not prove divine approval. Suffering in this life does not, by itself, establish that every moral account has been closed. In this particular case the verse explicitly denies that worldly exile exhausts the consequence. It does not follow that every sufferer faces the same fate; the statement has an identified subject, and its force should not be detached from that subject. Nor does this short verse discuss the duration of punishment or provide a detailed description of the Fire. It asserts a future judgment with sufficient clarity to overturn the mistaken inference from present survival. More elaborate claims about the Hereafter require evidence beyond what this sentence supplies. Precision preserves the warning rather than weakening it.

**A Limited Outcome Can Still Carry a Complete Warning**

The two sentences create an asymmetry between what happens now and what remains ahead. The worldly consequence is one specified form among possible alternatives. The future consequence is not erased by that choice of form. A person who asks only how severe the immediate loss was therefore misses the main issue. The proper question is what the outcome means under Allah's judgment, and the verse answers it by joining decree with continuing accountability.

This also changes the observer's task. A historian may describe movement, losses, and survival; the revelation identifies the moral horizon within which this particular movement occurred. These are different kinds of account, and the second cannot be obtained merely by counting casualties or comparing property values. The verse teaches neither that material details are irrelevant nor that a smaller immediate loss is meaningless. It teaches that such details cannot, on their own, establish divine acquittal. Its warning is complete without an embellished account of terror or an imagined speech from those leaving. Exile was decreed, another worldly punishment was possible, and accountability did not end at departure. Holding those claims together avoids both extremes: describing the outcome as a fortunate escape from Allah, or treating its severity as permission for human beings to add punishments of their own.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:4

> This is because they defied Allah and His Messenger. And whoever defies Allah, then Allah is truly severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason Given Is Defiance, Not Mere Weakness**

The verse states why the judgment under discussion occurred. Its explanation is moral: the people concerned defied Allah and His Messenger. Their loss is not interpreted simply as the result of inferior strength, unfortunate timing, or an unsuccessful calculation. Those factors may belong to a description of events, but they do not supply the reason revelation gives here. The words because they place responsibility in their stance toward divine authority.

This also limits how the event can be used. The verse does not say that anyone who loses a contest must therefore have defied Allah. Its direction of reasoning runs from a revealed identification of defiance to a stated consequence. Reversing that direction would make every worldly defeat a verdict of guilt and every victory a certificate of righteousness. Nothing in this sentence authorizes that universal test. The people addressed are given the cause of a particular judgment, not a method for determining the spiritual worth of all groups by their political fortunes. The distinction is vital because a morally serious account of history can otherwise become admiration for whoever happens to prevail. Here Allah's judgment defines the meaning of the result; the result does not allow a spectator to invent Allah's judgment in every other case.

**Defiance Names an Adversarial Stance**

The verb shāqqū means that they opposed or defied. The Quranic Arabic Corpus identifies it here as a form III verb from the root shīn–qāf–qāf and records the same verbal family in passages concerning opposition to the Messenger, including 4:115 and 47:32 ([Quranic Arabic Corpus, root sh-q-q](https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=%24qq)). This checked linguistic comparison adds precision to the translation. The verse concerns taking an opposing stance, not the mere existence of a question or the act of asking for an explanation.

Related forms of the root can concern splitting, but the contextual meaning must not be replaced by a dramatic etymology. One should not claim that the people literally divided God from His Messenger or that every disagreement carries the same theological force. The relevant verb describes opposition in this setting. Its seriousness follows from its object: Allah and the Messenger who conveys His guidance. That is different from treating any human official's displeasure as proof of opposition to God. The wording gives no one a blank check to identify personal criticism with religious rebellion. Careful attention to the verb thus works in both directions. It preserves the gravity of deliberate defiance while preventing the warning from swallowing every dispute, uncertainty, or request for evidence.

**The Messenger's Authority Is Joined to the One Who Sent Him**

Allah and His Messenger appear together in the statement of the offense. The pairing does not make them two independent divine powers. The Messenger's authority belongs to his role as the one sent by Allah. Opposing him in that capacity is not simply a disagreement with one private person among many. It is resistance to the guidance and command he has been charged to convey.

The second half mentions defiance of Allah without repeating the Messenger. That shorter form does not cancel the first half. The opening has already identified the joined direction of the opposition, while the closing names the ultimate authority whose punishment makes that opposition perilous. Reading the two clauses together avoids two distortions. The Prophet must not be reduced to a messenger whose authoritative guidance can be rejected while claiming obedience to the sender. Nor should his authority be described as a second sovereignty detached from Allah. The sentence makes the relation clear through its movement from the paired objects of defiance to the single divine judge. This is a claim about the Messenger named in revelation. It is not a direct grant of the same unqualified standing to later rulers, institutions, teachers, or interpreters who may themselves err and require correction.

**A General Warning Emerges From a Particular Judgment**

The first clause concerns what they did; the second concerns whoever does it. The movement is from an identified case to a general warning. That generality prevents observers from treating the event as an exception with nothing to say about themselves. A person cannot dismiss the moral issue merely because he belongs to a different tribe, generation, or place. The condition concerns defiance of Allah, not possession of a particular ancestry.

At the same time, generality of warning is not generality of accusation. The verse does not announce that everyone shares the same conduct simply because the warning can address anyone. An accusation still concerns what a person has actually done, and human judgment must not pretend to possess complete knowledge of hearts. This distinction allows the warning to retain its breadth without becoming collective blame. It also makes self-examination a more faithful response than self-congratulation. The observer should ask whether he is resisting guidance, not merely identify with the side that escaped the particular loss. Universal moral language places all people under a standard; it does not allow one group to stand outside the standard as permanent accusers of the rest. The word whoever removes imagined immunity as surely as it removes any need to confine the lesson to the original participants.

**Severe Punishment Means the Warning Cannot Be Treated as Empty**

The ending asserts the seriousness of Allah's punishment. It does not provide a schedule, specify a new earthly sentence, or tell a reader how to impose one. Its purpose is to deny that defiance can be treated as a safe position merely because consequences are not immediately visible. The authority resisted is capable of judgment, and that judgment is not an empty threat.

Severity should not be confused with arbitrary anger. The verse begins by giving a reason and ends by affirming consequence. Its structure therefore links the warning to accountable conduct rather than to unpredictable force. Nor does the sentence invite a person to relish another's pain. The general conditional addresses anyone who might take the same stance, including the person hearing the warning with satisfaction at another group's defeat. Proper fear turns the sentence toward one's own responsibility. The severity belongs to Allah's judgment, while the listener's task is to respond to guidance rather than claim ownership of that judgment. This keeps the verse forceful without making it indiscriminate. A particular act of defiance explains the case; the wider warning removes immunity from anyone who would repeat that stance; and the final authority rests with Allah, not with human anger dressed in religious language.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:5

> Whatever palm trees you ˹believers˺ cut down or left standing intact, it was ˹all˺ by Allah’s Will, so that He might disgrace the rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Judges Both Felling and Preservation**

The verse places two actions under one declaration: some palms were cut down, and others remained standing. Its concern is not limited to defending the cutting. It also acknowledges preservation. A reading that extracts permission to destroy while ignoring the trees left intact would omit half of the stated situation. The two outcomes are treated together within the particular event to which revelation speaks.

A sound report identifies that setting. Ibn ʿUmar relates that the Prophet had palms belonging to Banū al-Naḍīr cut and burned at al-Buwayrah and connects the act with this revelation. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4031 ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4031](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4031)). Its attribution is to Ibn ʿUmar reporting the Prophet's action, not to an invented speech in which the Prophet explains every strategic reason. The place-name is a checked addition that anchors the account without enlarging it beyond its evidence. The verse itself uses the contrast between cutting and leaving; the report supplies burning as part of the historical event. Keeping those kinds of evidence distinct avoids silently inserting details into the translation. The central judgment remains what the verse states: neither the cutting carried out in this case nor the preservation fell outside Allah's permission.

**Permission Answers a Moral Question, Not Only a Question of Power**

The phrase by Allah's Will cannot be reduced here to the bare observation that no event escapes divine sovereignty. The immediate contrast concerns conduct whose legitimacy was in question. Ibn Kathīr explains the declaration as approval of the actions in this setting and records the concern that arose over cutting the trees ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:5](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/1/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). In that context, the statement reassures the participants that the identified conduct was permitted. It is not merely telling them that the event occurred.

The distinction matters because what happens and what is morally authorized cannot always be treated as identical. People can commit wrongs within a world governed by Allah. If the verse meant only that the cutting physically took place under His sovereignty, it would not resolve the moral concern described in the commentary. Here the revelation does resolve it. Yet that particular authorization is not a blank approval of every act of destruction later performed by believers. A person cannot point to this sentence and declare that his own decision must be right simply because he was able to carry it out. The authorization belongs to the case judged by revelation. Its existence demands care about authority rather than making authority unnecessary.

**Standing Trees Are Not a Rebuke to Those Who Cut Others**

Because preservation and cutting appear together, one group cannot claim that its action alone exhausted the permitted response in this event. Ibn Kathīr preserves an explanation from Mujāhid concerning disagreement among the emigrants, some of whom favored keeping the palms as property that might come into Muslim possession. The commentary understands the verse as approving both the felling that occurred and the decision to leave trees standing. This is a reported exegetical account, not a separately authenticated Prophetic instruction to be quoted as hadith.

The two actions are opposites at the level of any single tree, but they need not represent opposing moral allegiances in the circumstances as a whole. Cutting one palm and preserving another can both occur under an authorized course of action. That does not mean all disagreement is harmless or that every pair of opposite choices is equally correct. The judgment comes from this verse concerning this case. What it excludes is retrospective blame where revelation has granted permission. The preserved palms therefore should not be interpreted as proof that all cutting was sinful, while the cut palms should not be interpreted as proof that preservation was cowardly or disobedient. The text refuses that forced choice by explicitly bringing both kinds of conduct within its declaration.

**A Disputed Tree Name Does Not Change the Main Judgment**

The Arabic līnah is rendered here as palm trees. Ibn Kathīr records several understandings of the term: a particular kind of date palm, palms apart from certain named varieties, and a broader reading covering date palms generally. He attributes the broad interpretation to Ibn Jarīr. This lexical disagreement concerns the range of the noun, not the basic opposition between felling and leaving trees rooted in place.

The grounds of the alternatives lie in whether līnah names a recognized variety or functions more broadly in this context. The narrower reading may preserve a specific usage familiar to the original audience. The broader reading fits the general character of the action being discussed and is the safest basis for explaining the supplied translation, which does not identify a cultivar. Nothing in the practical point of this verse requires a modern reader to settle the botanical range before understanding the divine permission. It would therefore be misleading to make a confident cultivar identification carry the moral argument. Lexical precision is useful when it clarifies what is at stake; it becomes a distraction when uncertain classification is presented as the key to a judgment that the sentence states plainly. The trees were real and valuable, but the permission concerns the authorized action rather than an imagined property of one species.

**The Purpose Is Judgment, Not Destruction for Its Own Sake**

The closing clause identifies the disgrace of the rebellious as a purpose. The act is not presented as entertainment, uncontrolled anger, or an unlimited ideal of ruining an opponent's livelihood. Its place is within a specified judgment. The presence of trees left standing reinforces the limit: the verse does not celebrate complete devastation as the only faithful response.

That boundary should shape every attempt to derive a wider lesson. The passage can establish the permissibility of the identified actions without establishing that all productive property is always a lawful target. A general law of warfare would need the full body of relevant evidence, not one clause detached from the event and from the preservation named alongside cutting. Nor may later actors claim revealed knowledge of Allah's purpose for their own destructive acts merely because they use the same religious language. Here the purpose is known because Allah states it. The observer's task is to understand the judgment faithfully, including its authorization and its limits. The verse answers a moral uncertainty within a particular conflict: the cutting and preservation that occurred were permitted, and the outcome served a divine judgment. It does not make devastation an independent religious good or remove the need to justify conduct under an applicable rule.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:6

> As for the gains Allah has turned over to His Messenger from them—you did not ˹even˺ spur on any horse or camel for such gains. But Allah gives authority to His messengers over whoever He wills. For Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Manner of Acquisition Determines the Claim to the Gains**

The verse distinguishes these gains by how they came under the Messenger's authority. The believers had not obtained them through the mounted exertion named in the sentence. That fact matters for entitlement. Property acquired in this manner is not simply available for participants to divide according to the assumptions they might bring from a battlefield. Before asking who should receive it, the verse establishes the kind of acquisition involved.

The legal term is fayʾ: property coming under Muslim authority without the fighting that gives rise to ordinary battle spoils. Ibn Kathīr applies that category here to the property of Banū al-Naḍīr and distinguishes it from acquisition through the use of cavalry and camelry in battle ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:6](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/6/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). This does not require a claim that no pressure or confrontation occurred. The classification concerns the manner in which the property was secured. Confusing a siege or surrender with a battle fought for booty would obscure the verse's legal point. The gains are real, but the claim to distribute them must follow their proper category. Mere presence near an event does not establish the same entitlement as every other form of participation in conflict.

**Horses and Camels Name the Exertion Not Made for These Gains**

The reference to spurring horses and camels is concrete. It denies the kind of mounted effort through which the audience might otherwise claim to have won the property. The verse does not merely say that Allah helped them while they did everything normally required. It singles out something they did not do. Their relation to the gains must therefore begin with acknowledgment of an absence, not an enlarged account of their own contribution.

The wording is not an insult to the believers. It corrects a possible basis of demand. Receiving a benefit without the expected exertion calls for gratitude, but it does not create a right to invent exertion after the event. The distinction also prevents participation from being treated as a single undifferentiated fact. People can be involved in a confrontation without every legal consequence of battlefield acquisition following from that involvement. Here the named means of acquisition did not produce the gains. The verse makes that limitation explicit before speaking of the authority Allah grants. A reader should therefore resist reducing the animals to decorative imagery. They serve the argument: the claimed material basis for ordinary combatant entitlement is absent in this case. The property must be understood through the authority by which it was actually obtained rather than through the effort its recipients might imagine they deserved to be credited with.

**The Messenger Receives Authority, Not a Reward for Superior Greed**

Allah has turned these gains over to His Messenger. The sentence identifies the authorized recipient of control before it gives any full account of disposal. That order matters. The Messenger's position is not established by being the strongest claimant in a contest of private appetites. It is established by the divine grant named in the verse. Control follows authorization rather than mere ability to seize.

Ibn Kathīr explains that the Messenger had discretion over these gains and used them for righteous purposes benefiting the Muslims. That statement concerns entrusted administration, not a suggestion that the property existed only to enlarge personal luxury. A distinction must be kept between authority to manage an asset and a claim that every possible use of it is equally sound. The verse grants authority within the Messenger's relation to Allah; it does not present appetite as a source of law. Nor does it give later leaders an unrestricted personal title to every asset they administer. The named recipient is the Messenger, and the ground is the specific grant. The immediate lesson is about the source and character of rightful control. A material gain does not become morally unregulated simply because those who hoped to divide it lack an ordinary combatant claim.

**A Year's Provision Shows What Administration Could Look Like**

A verified report from ʿUmar gives a precise example of the Prophet's use of this property. He sold the dates produced by the Banū al-Naḍīr garden and retained enough food to meet his household's needs for a year. The account appears in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5357 ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5357](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5357)). The one-year provision is a checked detail beyond the verse's wording and helps distinguish regular maintenance from a vague claim of unrestricted consumption.

That example is relevant because gains need not consist only of objects divided at the moment of surrender. A productive holding can yield continuing produce that requires management. Selling dates and setting aside household food are acts of administration over time. The report does not prove that every holding should be managed in exactly the same way, nor that every household must store a year's supply. It shows what the Messenger actually did with part of the benefit. Provision for dependents is not automatically opposed to service of the wider community, and responsible planning is not identical with hoarding for display. The account should remain as specific as its evidence: dates were sold, and yearly household needs were provided for. Larger claims about all expenditures require their own verified reports rather than being added to this concise one.

**The General Power Belongs to Allah, Not to the Means**

The verse moves from this Messenger and these gains to Allah's granting authority to His messengers. The event is placed under a broader divine power. Horses, camels, and ordinary expectations of military exertion are means within the world; they do not set the outer boundary of what Allah can bring about. An outcome can be granted without following the particular route people anticipated.

The final name, Most Capable of everything, explains that freedom from dependence on means. It does not make human action meaningless or tell believers never to prepare. Rather, it denies that an expected instrument is sovereign over its result. The absence of mounted exertion did not make the transfer impossible, because the decisive authority did not belong to the mounts or their riders. At the same time, the statement is about Allah's granting, not a promise that any group claiming religious motives will receive whatever property it desires. His will is not an instrument at the disposal of human ambition. The verse therefore corrects both entitlement and explanation: the audience cannot claim these gains on the basis of an effort it did not make, and it cannot limit Allah's power to the means through which it usually expects gains to come. Proper administration begins with that acknowledgment of the giver and the grant.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:7

> As for gains granted by Allah to His Messenger from the people of ˹other˺ lands, they are for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich. Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it. And whatever he forbids you from, leave it. And fear Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Public Gain Must Answer to More Than Powerful Claimants**

The verse identifies recipients whose claims are not measured simply by their ability to seize or bargain. The Messenger's close relatives, orphans, poor people, and needy travelers stand within the stated disposition of these gains. Several of these groups lack the influence through which wealth ordinarily attracts more wealth. Their inclusion means that administration must recognize needs and responsibilities that a competition among strong claimants could easily ignore.

The opening attribution to Allah establishes the controlling authority; it does not describe a material need on Allah's part. The gains are governed by His ruling and administered through His Messenger. The named human recipients then make that authority concrete. An orphan's dependence and a traveler's immediate need cannot be dismissed because they have not generated a powerful demand. Nor does the verse reduce every recipient to the same condition. Kinship, loss of parental support, poverty, and interrupted access to resources are distinct grounds mentioned together. The distribution is ordered, not simply an expression of personal generosity by the administrator. Those who control the asset must first recognize that its proper use has already been placed under a rule. Possession of the means of distribution does not create unlimited ownership of the decision.

**Restricted Circulation Is a Defect in Distribution**

The verse states a purpose: these gains must not remain moving only among the rich. Circulation by itself is therefore insufficient. Money or property can change hands frequently while remaining enclosed within a wealthy circle. The question is not only whether an asset moves, but who is able to receive its benefit. A lively exchange among already powerful owners can leave the people named in this verse untouched.

The purpose helps explain why the list of recipients is integral to the rule. The poor and the traveler are not decorative reminders appended after the real claimants have been satisfied. Their inclusion works against exclusion from the flow of resources. At the same time, the sentence does not condemn every instance of wealth or state that all holdings must be equal in amount. Its immediate subject is the disposal of the gains described here. A sound broader lesson follows from that specific rule: public distribution should not be designed so that existing wealth is the only effective qualification for receiving more. The verse supplies a principle of access and responsibility, not a complete economic program in a few words. Its declared purpose is nevertheless strong enough to judge an arrangement that appears orderly while systematically benefiting only those who already possess abundance.

**Taking and Leaving Both Acknowledge the Messenger's Authority**

The audience is told to accept what the Messenger gives and leave what he forbids. These two directions cover more than eagerness to receive a favorable share. Acceptance includes restraint where a desire is denied. An authority honored only when it distributes benefits has not actually been accepted as an authority. The paired commands therefore test both appetite and obedience.

The immediate setting is the disposition of gains, so the practical application concerns accepting the Messenger's allocation rather than attempting to claim what has not been assigned. Yet the wording of giving and forbidding supports a wider rule of obedience. The specific occasion supplies its first application without exhausting its meaning. That wider reading does not depend on ignoring the context. The same reason for obeying the Messenger in distribution applies when he conveys other binding guidance from Allah. The verse's final appeal to reverence also places the response within religion rather than ordinary political preference. A recipient may have wanted a different outcome, but desire does not become a second source of entitlement. The command disciplines both the hand reaching for a gain and the will resisting a prohibition.

**Ibn Masʿūd Applied the Command Beyond Property**

A verified report provides an early example of the wider reading. When Umm Yaʿqūb challenged Ibn Masʿūd about prohibitions he attributed to the Prophet, he referred her to this verse's command to accept the Messenger's direction. The exchange is recorded through ʿAlqamah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4886 ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4886](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4886)). The relevant point here is Ibn Masʿūd's use of the verse in a matter outside the distribution of gains. That is a checked addition to the immediate financial discussion.

His reasoning did not claim that every Prophetic ruling appears as a separate sentence in the Qur'an. It treated the Qur'an's command to obey the Messenger as establishing the authority of guidance genuinely transmitted from him. This makes verification essential rather than optional. A false attribution cannot acquire authority merely by invoking the command to obey. The report therefore supports the broader reading while leaving the duty to establish what the Prophet actually taught intact. The financial context remains the verse's starting point, and the authoritative relationship it expresses reaches beyond that context. Reading the command only as a rule about receiving property would fail to account for this documented Companion's application; reading it as approval of every later religious claim would go beyond what that application establishes.

**Reverence Governs Both Distribution and Reception**

The closing warning prevents this arrangement from becoming a contest in clever evasion. Those administering and those receiving the gains answer to Allah. A formally correct procedure can still be corrupted by pressure, concealed preference, or refusal to honor the recognized claim of someone without influence. Reverence reaches those motives as well as the visible transfer. Severe punishment is named because the command is not a request that may be ignored whenever an advantage appears.

The Prophet's general instruction in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7288, narrated by Abū Hurayrah, joins avoidance of what he prohibits with carrying out commands to the extent of one's ability ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7288](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:7288)). This verified report clarifies obedience without turning it into a demand for the impossible. Ability matters in fulfilling a positive duty; dislike of a ruling is not itself inability. The verse brings that serious obedience into the administration of material resources. Wealth must reach beyond a closed rich circle, the stated recipients must be recognized, and the Messenger's direction must govern both receipt and restraint. Its economic purpose and religious command are not rival concerns. Reverence for Allah is expressed here through accepting a distribution whose benefits cannot be reserved for the already powerful.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:8

> ˹Some of the gains will be˺ for poor emigrants who were driven out of their homes and wealth, seeking Allah’s bounty and pleasure, and standing up for Allah and His Messenger. They are the ones true in faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Their Need Is Named Without Reducing Them to Their Loss**

The verse identifies poor emigrants as recipients of support. Their poverty is concrete, but it is not the whole account of who they are. The sentence also names what they endured, what they sought, and what they stood for. This restores moral agency to people whose material condition might otherwise become their only public description. They are not simply persons who possess little; they are believers whose commitment has survived dispossession.

The bracketed opening in the translation makes the connection to material gains explicit. Support is not merely praise spoken over hardship. A share of resources is involved. That combination matters because admiration can become a way of avoiding practical responsibility: one celebrates another person's sacrifice while leaving its costs entirely upon him. The verse does not take that course. It joins recognition to provision. Nor does it suggest that receiving support cancels the worth of the emigrants' sacrifice. Their need is not an embarrassment that must be hidden in order to preserve their honor. They can be sincere, active supporters of the Messenger and legitimate recipients of assistance at the same time. The opening description holds those facts together instead of forcing a choice between dependence and dignity.

**Being Driven Out Is Different From Choosing a More Profitable Address**

Their departure involved the loss of homes and wealth. The wording does not describe an ordinary move undertaken because another place promised higher earnings. They were driven out. That coercive element is essential to understanding both their need and the moral weight of their migration. The people who appear poor in their new setting had not necessarily lacked possessions before. Their current condition cannot be read backward as a complete history of their lives.

Home and wealth name related but distinct losses. A home provides settled belonging and shelter; wealth supplies means of support and action. Losing both threatens not only present comfort but the ability to rebuild an independent life. The verse's recognition of both therefore explains why resource allocation to these emigrants is not an incidental kindness. It answers an identifiable deprivation. At the same time, the passage does not supply a detailed inventory of every person's property or a single account of how each was expelled. Its common description is enough to establish the relevant condition. They had been separated from the material base of their lives and remained faithful through that separation. Giving them a share acknowledges a cost already borne rather than treating their hardship as proof that they had contributed nothing.

**Seeking Bounty Does Not Compete With Seeking Allah's Pleasure**

The emigrants seek Allah's bounty and His pleasure. The pairing prevents material need and spiritual purpose from being made enemies. They can ask Allah for provision while also seeking His approval. Faith does not require them to regard food, shelter, and a restored livelihood as shameful objects of hope. What gives the pursuit its direction is the Lord from whom they seek these goods and the commitment within which they seek them.

Bounty has a breadth that should not be narrowed to one immediate payment. The verse identifies a posture of dependence on Allah for good, while pleasure names the desired divine approval. Material recovery without that approval would not exhaust their aim. Conversely, spiritual aspiration does not make their bodily needs unreal. A checked parallel in 16:41 joins migration after persecution with a good worldly settlement and a greater reward in the Hereafter ([Qurʾān 16:41](https://quran.com/an-nahl/41)). That additional reference supports the joined horizon without promising that every emigrant will become wealthy. The present verse honors people who seek both provision and acceptance. It does not divide them into one group concerned with livelihood and another concerned with faith. Their worldly need is held within their larger pursuit of Allah's favor.

**Supporting Allah Means Serving His Cause, Not Supplying a Divine Lack**

They stand up for Allah and His Messenger. Allah does not need material rescue from His creatures. The expression concerns their support for His cause and the Messenger who conveys it. Their commitment takes a visible social form: they do not merely preserve a private opinion after leaving home but align their lives with the guidance for which they have suffered loss.

This active description balances the account of expulsion. They were acted upon by others, yet they are also actors in the believing community. Dispossession has not reduced them to passivity. Their support may demand courage, endurance, and practical cooperation, but the verse does not limit its meaning to a catalogue of particular actions. The essential relation is allegiance expressed in conduct. It also prevents the gains from being interpreted as compensation for a purely private financial disappointment. Their need belongs to a history of fidelity and service. Such support does not purchase divine approval as though Allah were a dependent patron exchanging favors. It manifests the sincerity of people who have already made a costly commitment. The sentence places their material situation, their purpose, and their public stance in one account, so that none can be understood adequately by ignoring the others.

**Truth in Faith Appears as Agreement Between Claim and Conduct**

The closing affirmation identifies them as true in faith. Truthfulness here is more than accurate speech in an isolated exchange. Their conduct agrees with the allegiance they profess. A declaration of belief can be inexpensive when nothing threatens it; their declaration has endured pressure upon home, property, and settled life. The verdict is not produced by hardship alone, but by their faithful response within it.

This distinction matters because suffering does not automatically establish the truth of every claim made by a sufferer. The verse supplies its own full description: these emigrants sought Allah's favor and supported His Messenger through their loss. The final affirmation gathers those elements rather than replacing them with a sentimental rule that deprivation itself sanctifies. It also provides the proper basis for honoring them. Their worth is not measured by the wealth they retained, since much had been taken from them. It is measured by the correspondence between faith and action that Allah affirms. Assistance therefore reaches people whose material weakness coexists with moral strength. The verse teaches the community to see both accurately: the poverty requiring support and the sincerity deserving recognition. Neither should be used to erase the other, and neither is adequately honored by words that refuse the practical provision with which the verse begins.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:9

> As for those who had settled in the city and ˹embraced˺ the faith before ˹the arrival of˺ the emigrants, they love whoever immigrates to them, never having a desire in their hearts for whatever ˹of the gains˺ is given to the emigrants. They give ˹the emigrants˺ preference over themselves even though they may be in need. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Established Residents Welcome Newcomers as Fellow Believers**

The verse praises those who had already made their home in the city and received faith before the emigrants arrived. Their earlier settlement could have become a basis for exclusion: they were present first, possessed local connections, and could have treated newcomers as permanent outsiders. Instead, they love those who come to them. Welcome is described as a disposition of the heart, not merely compliance with a public arrangement.

The distinction between settlement and faith matters. The city supplies a place to live; faith supplies the relation in which the arriving believers are received. Earlier residence does not become a spiritual rank that entitles its holders to withhold fellowship. Nor does the verse say that the residents believed before every emigrant first believed. The translation carefully relates their established position to the emigrants' arrival. The praise concerns what they did with the advantages of having a home and a community already in place. Those advantages became means of hospitality rather than tools of humiliation. Love makes the newcomer more than an object of relief. It gives him a place among people whose own identity is strengthened, not threatened, by recognizing his belonging.

**Another Person's Share Does Not Become an Injury to the Heart**

The residents do not harbor resentment over the gains given to the emigrants. Their love is tested at the point where a visible resource goes to someone else. It is easy to welcome people while assuming that the distribution of advantages will remain unchanged. The verse praises a deeper freedom: another's receipt does not automatically register as one's own deprivation or insult.

This does not mean that the residents lack needs or cease to understand material value. Their own need is explicitly acknowledged later in the sentence. The absence of resentful desire therefore cannot be explained by saying that they already possess everything they could want. Their hearts refuse to turn the emigrants' provision into a grievance. That distinction identifies an important form of generosity before any additional gift is made. One can accept a just allocation without spoiling it through envy, contempt, or a demand that the recipient remain visibly indebted. The verse locates this freedom inside the breast because outward silence alone might conceal ongoing hostility. Its praise concerns an inward consent that lets another person's good remain good. The community's resources can then serve need without becoming a constant contest over whose honor each transfer appears to confirm.

**Preference Has Weight Because Their Own Need Is Real**

They put others before themselves despite being in need. This is īthār, giving another preference where one's own claim or desire is also present. The act differs from passing on what has no value to the giver. Its cost is part of why it reveals character. Yet the verse does not erase the residents' needs in order to praise them. Their generosity is intelligible because their deprivation remains visible.

Abū Hurayrah reports an incident in which the Prophet sought someone to host a hungry guest after finding no food available in his own households. A man of the Anṣār and his wife offered their limited food and themselves went without that evening. The report connects their conduct with this verse: Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4889 ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4889](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4889)). The initial absence of food in the Prophet's households is a verified detail that widens the picture of shared scarcity. The help did not emerge from effortless abundance. This particular praised act must not be converted into a general license to neglect dependents or pressure poor people into gifts they cannot safely make. Its force lies in willing generosity, not in an administrator's right to demand that others surrender their necessities.

**Selfishness Is More Than the Desire to Keep a Possession**

The closing phrase identifies an inner danger from which a person needs protection. Shuḥḥ, grasping selfishness or avarice, can make one's own appetite the measure of everyone else's rights. It reaches beyond an occasional reluctance to give. A person governed by it begins to see other people's benefits as thefts from an imagined universal claim of his own. The residents' love, lack of resentment, and preference for others are concrete opposites of that rule.

A verified Prophetic warning from Jābir links avarice with the destruction of earlier people, including bloodshed and treating forbidden things as permissible: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2578 ([Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2578](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2578)). The connection is exact rather than decorative. Greed becomes socially dangerous when desire authorizes whatever will satisfy it. Protecting the heart from that impulse therefore protects relationships and rights, not merely a person's reputation for generosity. The verse speaks of being saved from one's own selfishness, locating the threat close to home. The enemy is not always the newcomer who receives help. It may be the possessive judgment within the established resident who feels that every gain must first pass through his hands. Divine protection is needed because that judgment can disguise itself as fairness.

**Success Is Freedom From Possession's Claim Over the Person**

The final verdict calls those protected from selfishness successful. Success is not defined by how much they retained after others arrived. It concerns what no longer governs them. Their resources may diminish through generosity, yet they are not described as losers. Their freedom to love and give reveals a good that cannot be measured solely by the balance of what remains in their possession.

That verdict gathers the verse's stages into one account. Established belonging becomes welcome; another's allocation becomes a good accepted without envy; personal need does not make preference impossible. These are not disconnected virtues but signs that the self's demands have ceased to exercise absolute rule. The verse does not ask readers to deny ordinary needs or regard all ownership as evil. It praises people who can recognize their needs without allowing those needs to cancel everyone else's. Such people can inhabit a community rather than merely compete within it. Their inward freedom makes room for another household's security and another believer's dignity. The success named at the end is therefore already visible in the relationships described before it, while remaining a success that Allah ultimately grants and recognizes.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:10

> And those who come after them will pray, “Our Lord! Forgive us and our fellow believers who preceded us in faith, and do not allow bitterness into our hearts towards those who believe. Our Lord! Indeed, You are Ever Gracious, Most Merciful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Later Believers Enter the Community Through a Prayer of Kinship**

Those who come afterward are described by what they ask Allah to give. Their relation to earlier believers is not founded on rivalry for an earlier place in history. They acknowledge that others preceded them and call them fellow believers. Arrival at a later time does not mean arriving without a community, because faith joins them to people whose lives may already have ended.

Ibn Kathīr reads this as the class that follows the emigrants and helpers in good conduct and prayer for them, and connects it with the followers praised in 9:100 ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:10](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/10/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). The description can thus reach beyond one immediate group of later arrivals. Its force lies in the quality of following, not simply in being born later. A person joins the praised pattern by adopting its prayer and disposition. This broad reading fits the absence of a terminal generation in the wording. It also preserves the immediate historical reference: the people who first carried the community's burdens are not displaced when others inherit its life. Later believers receive a way to acknowledge their debt without pretending that chronology alone makes anyone immune from the need for forgiveness.

**The Petition Begins With Need Rather Than Superiority**

The speakers ask forgiveness for themselves before including those who came before. They do not position themselves as spotless judges benevolently requesting mercy for flawed predecessors. Their own need is the first admission. The same divine forgiveness is sought for both groups, placing them together under a mercy neither can supply for itself.

This shared petition avoids two mistakes in remembering earlier believers. Admiration need not become a claim that they never needed forgiveness. Recognition of their faults need not become contempt that forgets the speaker's own faults. Asking Allah to forgive is compatible with honoring sincere faith precisely because forgiveness addresses real human need. The prayer therefore makes neither romantic perfection nor habitual accusation the basis of belonging. It supplies a more truthful bond: people at different points in history depend upon the same Lord. The order of the request trains humility without making later believers incapable of moral judgment. They can distinguish right from wrong while remembering that they too will need mercy when others recount their lives. The prayer places that recognition before any temptation to treat distance from the past as proof of superiority to those who lived it.

**Bitterness Requires Removal From the Heart, Not Only From Speech**

The second request concerns an inward condition. The speakers ask that bitterness toward believers not be allowed to settle in their hearts. They seek more than the ability to avoid rude words in public. Ghill, rancor or settled resentment, can persist beneath polite conduct and shape the way another person's actions are interpreted. The petition asks Allah to remove that concealed hostility at its root.

Its scope extends to those who believe, not only to a favored circle of friends. A heart can preserve warmth toward familiar people while feeding contempt toward believers elsewhere or in another generation. The prayer does not allow that narrowness to define fellowship. Yet freedom from rancor is not the same as refusing to name injustice. A wrong can be opposed without making hatred the permanent organizing principle of one's relation to people. The verse asks for a clean heart, not an empty moral judgment. This makes the petition demanding: the speaker cannot satisfy it merely by moving resentment into religious language or calling every hostile impulse zeal. What matters is whether the heart is being governed by a desire for another believer's good under Allah's judgment. The request recognizes that this work requires divine help rather than confidence in one's own emotional fairness.

**Prayer for the Absent Breaks the Habit of Transaction**

Those who preceded the speakers may not be present to hear the prayer or return a favor. Asking forgiveness for them is therefore not a performance designed to secure their immediate approval. A verified Prophetic instruction reinforces this direction: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2732a teaches that an angel responds with a corresponding prayer for the believer who prays for his brother in his absence ([Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2732a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2732a)). The page's Arabic chain names Abū al-Dardāʾ, while its English narrator label differs; the collection and teaching are clear, but the conflicting label should not be silently repeated.

The report adds a concrete reason to value unseen goodwill. Praying for another's forgiveness does not diminish the supplicant's own share of mercy. The good sought for someone else can return as good sought for the speaker. This breaks the rivalry that treats divine favor as though it were a small store divided among competing claimants. In the present verse, the prayer binds generations precisely because it does not require an exchange of visible benefits. A later believer can do something meaningful for a predecessor by turning to Allah on his behalf. The bond is maintained through worship rather than possession of the same place or time.

**The Divine Names Give the Petition Its Ground of Hope**

The prayer ends by naming Allah's graciousness and mercy. The speakers do not ask for a purified heart on the assumption that their emotional habits are already pure. They appeal to the One whose compassion makes both forgiveness and inward correction possible. The repeated address to their Lord keeps the requests personal and dependent. It is not merely a statement of communal ideals recited without an addressee.

The two main requests belong together. Forgiveness deals with guilt; freedom from bitterness deals with a disposition that can generate further wrong. The prayer therefore seeks mercy concerning what has happened and protection concerning what the heart may otherwise continue to produce. Its ending is hopeful without making the work trivial. A believer who asks sincerely must resist feeding the resentment he asks Allah to remove. The verse offers a form of belonging that can survive the passing of generations: acknowledgment of earlier faith, shared confession of need, and a heart turned away from rancor. Later arrival is not a defect to overcome through rivalry. It is an opportunity to continue the community's life by asking its Lord for the mercy all its members require.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:11

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen the hypocrites who say to their fellow disbelievers from the People of the Book, “If you are expelled, we will certainly leave with you, and We will never obey anyone against you. And if you are fought against, we will surely help you.”? But Allah bears witness that they are truly liars.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise of Fellowship Can Conceal a Different Allegiance**

The verse exposes speech made by people whose outward profession and inward stance do not agree. They are identified as hypocrites, and the recipients of their assurance are described as their fellows among the disbelieving People of the Book. The relation is one of shared opposition in this particular setting. A claim of fellowship can be real at the level of present interest while remaining false as a promise of costly support.

Ibn Kathīr identifies the senders with ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy and those like him, and the recipients with Banū al-Naḍīr. He describes the assurance as conveyed through a messenger ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:11](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/11/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). This checked historical identification adds specificity beyond the verse's wording without turning the case into a judgment against every interreligious friendship. The fault is not simply that people from different communities speak to one another. It is deception, hypocrisy, and a declared alignment whose promises Allah exposes as lies. The wording directs attention to what is offered and whether the offer is true. Religious labels alone cannot substitute for that examination, and an outward show of solidarity cannot make a knowingly false undertaking honest.

**They Offer to Share the Loss of Home**

The first assurance concerns expulsion. The speakers say they will leave alongside the people addressed if those people are driven out. This is a promise to share a major cost, not merely an expression of sympathy. Remaining comfortably at home while others depart would be one thing; abandoning one's own settled position to accompany them would be another. The promise claims the second kind of loyalty.

Its persuasive force lies in reducing the recipients' sense of isolation. People facing removal might make different choices if they believe companions will share the consequences. A false undertaking can therefore influence conduct before the promised assistance is ever required. The liar does not need to wait until the final crisis to cause harm. He may already be encouraging a decision through support he has no intention of giving. The verse records the conditional structure because it shows the reach of the assurance: if this severe event occurs, fellowship will still hold. Its truth cannot be measured by warmth of language alone. The relevant question is whether the speaker genuinely binds his own future to the cost he names. Allah's concluding witness strips away the confidence created by the promise and reveals the falsehood at its source.

**Absolute Language Tries to Make the Assurance Unquestionable**

The next declaration excludes obedience to anyone against the recipients. Its terms are sweeping. No unnamed authority, later pressure, or competing instruction is supposed to weaken the pledged allegiance. The speakers try to remove every reason their allies might have for doubting them. They do not offer limited assistance with clearly stated conditions; they speak as though no condition could ever change their position.

Such absolute language can sound stronger than a careful promise precisely because it refuses qualification. Yet confidence of expression is not proof of strength of intention. A speaker may use certainty to prevent the listener from asking what resources, commitments, or risks stand behind the words. The verse's judgment exposes that difference. These assurances are not reliable because they are emphatic. The practical issue is not whether every broad promise is necessarily false; it is that this particular one has been declared false by Allah. The detail also reveals a moral inversion. The speakers promise that no rightful command will restrain their chosen alliance, while their own falsehood makes the alliance untrustworthy. A declaration of loyalty severed from truth cannot become dependable merely by claiming independence from every other obligation. The refusal of limits may be part of the deception rather than evidence of devotion.

**Military Help Is Promised as Though Words Were Already Strength**

The third assurance concerns being fought against. The speakers promise help under direct attack. They move from sharing expulsion to resisting force, extending their claim of solidarity across the possible forms of danger. The recipients are invited to regard them as a source of effective support, not only sympathetic speech.

The verse does not at this point list troops, weapons, or arrangements. Its concern is the promise and the divine verdict upon it. That restraint keeps the moral issue clear. A declaration can create an appearance of added strength without adding the reliable commitment on which strength depends. The recipients may count support that exists only in speech. This is why lying about assistance is more than a private defect of character. It can alter another group's assessment of danger and possibility. The person who offers the assurance may gain influence at little immediate cost, while those persuaded bear the risks of acting upon it. The sentence names that asymmetry without requiring a detailed reconstruction of every subsequent decision. The assurance is already culpable because it presents as dependable what its speakers do not truly intend. The promised help is being used to shape another's conduct before any real help is supplied.

**Allah's Witness Judges the Promise Before Appearances Can Vindicate It**

The closing statement is direct: Allah bears witness that they are liars. The authority of that judgment does not rest on the Prophet possessing access to every hidden conversation or on observers waiting until every possible condition has occurred. Allah knows the reality of the undertaking. Ibn Kathīr explains that the words were issued without the intention to fulfill them. This grounds the condemnation in present dishonesty, not merely in a later inability that might overtake an otherwise sincere promise.

That distinction matters for moral judgment. Human beings can genuinely intend to help and then encounter circumstances they could not overcome. Such a case is not automatically identical with knowingly issuing an empty assurance. Here revelation identifies the latter. The promise is false at the point of utterance, however convincing its terms appear. The verse thereby sets divine testimony against the speakers' repeated certainty. Their words invite reliance; Allah's witness removes its foundation. The observer is taught to distinguish allegiance claimed from allegiance honestly undertaken. Fellowship, shared danger, and emphatic language can all be imitated by a liar. None can replace truth as the ground on which another person's trust should rest.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:12

> Indeed, if they are expelled, the hypocrites will never leave with them. And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them. And even if the hypocrites did so, they would certainly flee, then the disbelievers would be left with no help.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise Is Tested at Each Point Where It Would Cost Something**

The verse examines the alliance through concrete conditions. Expulsion would require the supposed supporters to leave with their allies. Fighting would require them to provide help. The sentence denies that they will do either. A general profession of loyalty is thus brought to the exact circumstances in which loyalty would become an act rather than a declaration.

The conditions matter because empty support often remains impressive while the cost is unspecified. People can agree that they stand together without confronting what departure, danger, and loss would require from each participant. Here the terms are explicit. Sharing expulsion means surrendering the safety of remaining behind; military support means entering danger rather than praising courage from a distance. The divine statement does not evaluate the alliance by its slogans or by the number of people nominally attached to it. It evaluates whether the promised action will occur when needed. This is the point at which a vulnerable group can least afford uncertainty. A promise believed in advance may already have influenced its decisions, yet the value of that promise depends on conduct during the crisis. The verse removes the appearance of reliability before it can be mistaken for actual protection.

**Refusal to Leave Reveals a Limit Hidden by Shared Language**

The first denial concerns accompanying the expelled. The hypocrites' attachment to their own place will not be overcome by their professed fellowship. They will remain while those who trusted them bear the disruption. The loss is therefore not shared in the way the alliance had suggested. The verse exposes the gap between claiming another's cause and accepting another's cost.

This does not make every person who cannot accompany a displaced community a hypocrite. The judgment concerns these identified speakers and the undertaking they falsely made. A person may honestly state limits from the beginning, lack a means of departure, or have duties that prevent accompanying others. Such cases differ from offering an absolute assurance that one does not intend to honor. Truthful limitation can be morally better than impressive support that never existed. The verse's force lies in that specificity. It denies the promised departure rather than condemning all who remain at home in every circumstance. Those facing expulsion are being taught that the appearance of companionship must not be counted as a second home or a shared means of survival. Words cannot carry them when the speakers refuse to move. The practical reality of the alliance is narrower than its language had made it seem.

**A Name Added to an Alliance Does Not Necessarily Add Help**

The second denial concerns assistance in battle. The presence of supposed allies on one's side of a political divide does not mean their strength is available when pressure arrives. An alliance can appear larger on paper than it is in action. The verse names that discrepancy without requiring a count of weapons or fighters. What is absent is the dependable help itself.

Ibn Kathīr explains this clause as a denial that the hypocrites will fight alongside the threatened group, and calls the verse good news in its address to the believers ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:12](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/11/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). That checked exegetical addition identifies another side of the statement. It is not only exposure for those relying on the promise; it is reassurance for those who might fear an enlarged hostile force. The same declaration corrects two opposing calculations. One side must not count help that will not arrive, and the other must not assign real power to every threatening assurance. This does not make prudent assessment unnecessary. It means that the revealed judgment gives information beyond appearances in this particular case. The promised coalition cannot be understood solely by listening to how confidently its members describe themselves.

**Even Supposed Assistance Would Not Become Steadfast Support**

The verse then considers an additional possibility: even if help were attempted, the helpers would flee. This does not contradict the prior denial. A conditional statement can test a further assumption without asserting that the assumption will occur. The argument is comprehensive: the expected assistance will not be given, and imagining it given still does not produce the security its recipients seek.

The distinction is between the beginning of assistance and its endurance. Someone might appear, make a gesture, or briefly enter the field without possessing the resolve needed to remain. The mere sight of supporters is not equivalent to durable support. The conditional exposes the weakness beneath that appearance. It prevents the listener from escaping the warning by proposing a minimal intervention as proof that the alliance could still save them. The help envisioned would collapse at the point where persistence matters. The text is not celebrating a universal rule that all human allies must fail. It judges the reliability of these particular supporters. Its conditional form makes the analysis sharper rather than uncertain: even the favorable scenario someone might imagine does not change the stated result. The recipients' hope fails both under the actual prediction and under the alternative they might offer against it.

**The Final Lack of Help Is the Real Measure of the Assurance**

The closing result is abandonment without effective help. Every preceding clause is directed toward that practical outcome. A promise of shared departure, a promise of defense, and a hypothetical appearance of assistance all fail to supply what the endangered group needs. The verse measures an undertaking by whether it can bear the weight placed upon it, not by the emotional confidence it first creates.

This gives the passage a serious moral lesson about offering support. A false promise can leave another person worse prepared than an honest refusal, because it encourages reliance and may delay a realistic response to danger. The wrong is therefore not limited to the speaker's failure to maintain a good reputation. It reaches the choices made by those who believed him. Yet the verse also guards the believers against fear generated by theatrical solidarity. An alliance without truth and endurance is not as strong as its announcements. Its members may speak together while refusing to suffer together. The final absence of help reveals the difference. A person reading the warning should therefore ask not merely whether support is loudly claimed, but whether it is truthful, capable, and willing to remain when the named cost arrives. In this case, revelation answers that question decisively.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:13

> Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you ˹believers˺ than for Allah. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison Reveals a Disordered Scale of Fear**

The verse compares two fears within the same hearts. The believers appear more frightening to the people described than Allah does. The problem is not that they recognize a real human danger. It is that their response to that danger outweighs their recognition of divine authority. An immediate threat has become more decisive than the Lord who governs every threat and judges their conduct.

This distinction matters because ordinary caution is not itself unbelief. People can take precautions, feel alarm, and still revere Allah. The verse identifies a reversal of proportion, not the mere existence of a bodily reaction. Its moral concern is what commands the heart's practical allegiance. If fear of people determines what one will betray, conceal, or abandon, while fear of Allah supplies no effective restraint, the comparison has entered conduct. The words in their hearts locate that disorder where appearances alone might fail to disclose it. People can use confident language publicly while inwardly being ruled by dread. Revelation exposes that hidden ordering in this case. It does not ask the believers to guess everyone's inward fears from outward nervousness. It tells them a fact about the particular opponents whose apparent confidence might otherwise seem reliable.

**A Visible Threat Has Displaced an Unseen Accountability**

Human force is visible in ways that the final judgment is not. Its movement can be observed, its proximity can be measured, and its consequences can feel immediate. The people described allow that immediacy to determine the whole scale of importance. Their failure is not simply a shortage of information about their opponents. It is an inability to place available information within a true account of authority and consequence.

The verse therefore concerns practical understanding. Someone can know religious language and still act as though only what can happen today matters. Knowledge that does not affect judgment may remain on the tongue while another principle governs decisions. Here that governing principle is greater dread of the believers than of Allah. The sentence does not deny the believers' real presence or power; it denies that created power deserves the supreme place assigned to it. The mistake is especially serious where fear leads people to violate duties they would acknowledge in calmer circumstances. The immediate danger then becomes an excuse to ignore the authority that should govern the response to it. Understanding requires more than seeing the approaching risk. It requires knowing what that risk can and cannot justify, and before whom one remains answerable while confronting it.

**The Believers Receive Reassurance Without Being Made Sovereign**

The statement is addressed to the believers: they are more feared than they may realize. This corrects an assessment based only on their opponents' outward confidence. Ibn Kathīr reads the passage as reassurance to the believers and connects the comparison with 4:77, where some people fear human beings as much as Allah or more ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:13](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/11/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). This checked parallel makes the moral issue portable beyond one group: the same disorder can appear wherever human dread displaces divine reverence.

Reassurance is not a grant of divinity to those who are feared. Their opponents' distorted perception does not make the believers independent masters of events. Nor does being feared automatically establish moral excellence. The message concerns the weakness of the opponents' understanding and the instability beneath their posture. A believer who responds by seeking to become an object of unlimited human terror would reproduce the error from the other side. The proper lesson is that apparent confidence need not reveal inward steadiness, and that the power of human beings must still be placed beneath Allah. The verse comforts without inviting worship of strength. Its final explanation prevents the audience from mistaking an opponent's excessive fear for the correct measure of its own ultimate authority.

**Lack of Comprehension Is a Failure to Connect Knowledge With Judgment**

The closing reason is that these people do not comprehend. The statement does not concern intelligence measured as cleverness, memory, or skill in negotiation. The people can perceive a danger sharply enough to fear it. What they fail to understand is its proper place within the larger truth they must face. Their fear is intense partly because their judgment is narrow.

This kind of failure can coexist with elaborate planning. A person may calculate what a human opponent can do while refusing to reckon with the moral consequence of his own response. The calculation then remains technically busy and spiritually incomplete. The verse identifies that missing comprehension as a cause, not merely an insult after the comparison. A truer understanding of Allah would reorder fear and place restraints upon what the person does to escape human pressure. It would not necessarily remove every feeling of danger. It would prevent danger from becoming a rival law. The distinction gives the verse a demanding ethical meaning: understanding is tested by what one permits fear to authorize. A heart that knows the relative standing of creature and Creator cannot consistently treat the creature's displeasure as the final reason for action while treating divine judgment as remote and negotiable.

**Right Fear Restores Responsibility Instead of Erasing Caution**

The verse's corrective is implicit in its comparison. Allah must not occupy the lesser place in the heart's scale of accountability. Human beings can injure, threaten, or impose costs, but none can make disobedience righteous simply by making obedience difficult. Proper reverence places those pressures under a duty rather than allowing them to define duty for themselves.

This does not mean seeking danger unnecessarily or shaming a frightened person for needing protection. Such reactions would confuse courage with carelessness and divine judgment with human cruelty. The practical contrast is between prudent action governed by faith and action governed by the conviction that human power is ultimate. The former can seek safety honestly; the latter may sacrifice truth whenever pressure is strong enough. In the case described, revelation identifies that second ordering within the opponents' hearts. The believers are not told to become complacent but to understand what the opponents' show of strength conceals. The passage's shortness gives the comparison its force: greater fear of people, lesser reverence for Allah, and deficient comprehension belong to one moral disorder. Correcting that disorder begins by returning created danger to its limited place beneath the authority that judges both the threatened and those who threaten.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:14

> Even united, they would not ˹dare˺ fight against you except ˹from˺ within fortified strongholds or from behind walls. Their malice for each other is intense: you think they are united, yet their hearts are divided. That is because they are a people with no ˹real˺ understanding.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Outward Coalition Still Depends on Protective Barriers**

The verse describes opponents whose combined appearance exceeds their willingness to face open danger. Even together, they depend upon fortified places or walls. The point concerns these people's courage and cohesion in the stated confrontation. It is not a general condemnation of defensive architecture or a rule that anyone using cover must be morally deficient. The walls matter here because they reveal a limit concealed by the coalition's threatening appearance.

Their union does not produce the steadiness observers might expect from adding forces together. Physical protection supplies something that mutual trust has not supplied. That distinction challenges a simple arithmetic of power. More people under one outward designation do not necessarily create a more dependable body. The question is whether those people can act together when danger requires confidence in one another. The verse's description answers that question by naming the conditions under which they will fight. Their dependence on barriers is part of an account that also names intense internal malice. The two details belong together: external fortification cannot by itself repair an inwardly divided association. A wall may protect bodies from an attack while leaving the hearts behind it separated by distrust.

**Violence Among Themselves Does Not Become Shared Courage**

Their malice toward one another is intense. The translation selects the sense of internal hostility for the expression concerning their strength or fierceness among themselves. Ibn Kathīr likewise explains it as severe enmity within the group ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:14](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/11/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). That reading fits the explicit statement about divided hearts which immediately follows. The context supplies a reason for preferring it over a simple claim that they possess impressive strength when gathered.

Force can be present without being usable as common strength. If members direct it against one another, what looks like collective power may consume itself in rivalry. The verse does not deny the intensity of their hostility; it identifies its direction. They can be fierce internally while lacking the solidarity needed to meet an external challenge. This prevents a superficial equation between harshness and courage. A group that speaks violently or treats its own members severely is not thereby united or steadfast. Its internal coercion may be evidence of the opposite. The moral diagnosis is sharper than calling the opponents weak in every respect. They possess a destructive energy, but the relation among them prevents that energy from becoming trustworthy cooperation.

**What an Observer Sees Is Not the Whole of the Alliance**

The address distinguishes appearance from reality: an observer thinks them united, while their hearts are divided. A common public position can hide incompatible aims. People may assemble, issue joint messages, and identify an opponent without sharing the deeper commitments that allow them to endure cost together. Outward organization is real, but it is not the whole truth of their relation.

Ibn Kathīr records Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī's application of the description to the hypocrites and the People of the Book in this setting. The wording can also expose divisions within the forces themselves; the core meaning does not require that every fracture fall along one named communal boundary. The clear point is inward disunity beneath outward combination. That is why the verse is addressed to the observer's judgment. It corrects the mistake of treating coordinated appearance as sufficient evidence of shared purpose. The correction should not become a license to invent secret divisions within every group one dislikes. Here the inward fact is disclosed by revelation. Its general lesson concerns caution about appearances, not confidence that one's own speculation about other hearts must be true. Visible unity can be assessed, but its deepest sincerity is not automatically visible with it.

**Understanding Would Connect Shared Action to a Shared Moral Ground**

The explanation is that they lack real understanding. Their failure is not an inability to construct defenses or organize an outward alliance. Those capacities are already evident in the scene. What is missing is comprehension of the conditions that make people genuinely able to stand together. A coalition based only on common resentment can retain intense internal rivalry because its members have not learned to govern their own desires by a binding truth.

The verse does not propose that every difference of opinion destroys unity. Hearts can be joined while people disagree honestly about a particular judgment. The division described here is deeper: malice works within the association itself. Members do not merely hold different views; they lack the trust through which differences can be handled without turning one another into enemies. Understanding would recognize that common danger is not enough to heal such a condition. A shared target can postpone an argument without resolving its cause. The resulting coalition may look settled until the cost of cooperation must be distributed. Then the same selfishness that shaped internal enmity reappears. The verse's explanation therefore reaches beneath strategy to moral perception. Their problem is not only where they stand in relation to the believers, but how they stand in relation to one another.

**The Warning About Division Also Speaks to Believers**

A checked parallel in 8:46 warns believers that disputing with one another can bring discouragement and loss of strength, while directing them toward obedience and perseverance ([Qurʾān 8:46](https://quran.com/al-anfal/46)). This additional reference prevents the present description from becoming an occasion for effortless self-praise. The practical consequences of destructive conflict do not disappear when those involved carry the name of believers. They too must cultivate the qualities that make common action durable.

The contrast is not between having walls and having none, nor between speaking forcefully and speaking softly. It is between outward arrangements unsupported by inward fidelity and cooperation grounded in a real moral bond. The present verse exposes the former in a particular hostile coalition. The parallel calls believers to avoid the disputes that could weaken their own common life. Neither passage requires dishonest agreement or suppression of all correction. Unity that depends on concealment of injustice is itself unstable. What must be refused is the malice that makes another member's defeat feel more desirable than the community's good. The verse's account of divided hearts gives a realistic measure of power: numbers, fortifications, and common announcements cannot compensate indefinitely for the absence of trust among the people expected to act together.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:15

> They are ˹both just˺ like those who recently went down before them: they tasted the evil consequences of their doings. And they will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Recent Precedent Makes the Failure to Learn More Exposed**

The comparison points to people whose downfall was recent. The warning does not require the audience to recover an obscure event from a distant age. Something close enough to remain within living awareness had already demonstrated the consequence of a similar course. Recency strengthens the argument because it weakens the excuse that no relevant example was available.

Yet having an example nearby is not the same as understanding it. People can know that another group suffered and still suppose that their own resources, allies, or methods make them fundamentally different. The verse denies that assumed exemption. The present subjects resemble their predecessors in the morally relevant respect: their conduct brings an evil consequence. The comparison does not require every detail of the two histories to match. An earlier group can differ in size or circumstances while supplying a true warning about the same kind of self-defeating course. The verse therefore uses history as evidence to be understood, not as a collection of events to be admired from a distance. The recent loss matters because those who know of it still have to decide whether it will correct their own judgment or remain merely another group's misfortune.

**The Named Historical Reading Is Banū Qaynuqāʿ**

Ibn Kathīr identifies the immediate predecessors as Banū Qaynuqāʿ and attributes that interpretation to Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, and Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:15](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/11/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). This checked identification supplies a historical referent for the comparison. It remains an exegetical identification, since the verse itself does not name the group. The distinction preserves both the value of the commentary and the exact limits of the wording.

The local reading fits the emphasis on a recent precedent known to the audience. It does not require a sweeping account of every earlier defeated community. Nor does the force of the verse depend on fixing an exact number of months between events. Recency is stated; a precise interval would need independent historical evidence. The main relation is between an available warning and conduct that fails to heed it. Giving the group a name helps locate that relation in the history of Madinah, but the moral comparison should not be reduced to the name itself. The reader still has to understand what the earlier outcome demonstrates. A date or tribal identification without that understanding would turn the verse's admonition into a bare chronicle and leave its challenge to present judgment untouched.

**Tasting Makes Consequence an Experience Rather Than a Rumor**

The predecessors tasted the evil consequences of their own doings. Taste is the language of direct experience. What may previously have seemed an abstract warning became something they had to undergo. The expression does not suggest that they merely heard that their conduct was wrong. Its consequences reached them in a form that could no longer be kept at the distance of discussion.

Their doings are named as the source of the outcome's moral character. The loss is not interpreted here as random misfortune. Revelation connects it to their course of action. That connection must be kept within the case Allah identifies. It does not authorize observers to infer the guilt of every suffering population from the fact that suffering has occurred. The verse's direction is from known conduct under revealed judgment to a stated consequence, not from every painful event to a guessed accusation. Its imagery remains strong without that generalization. To taste a consequence is to encounter what one's earlier decisions helped bring about. Those watching afterward can learn without repeating the experience, but only if they allow the connection between conduct and outcome to challenge the flattering account they may hold of their own plans.

**A Painful Future Is Not Cancelled by a Worldly Loss**

The final statement names a painful punishment in addition to what the predecessors have already tasted. The earthly consequence does not settle the entire account. Their history therefore cannot be reduced to a rise and fall whose moral meaning ends when the visible event is over. Revelation places the case within a longer horizon of judgment.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors. One might treat the worldly loss as proof that no further accountability remains, as though suffering automatically discharged every wrong. Or one might focus only on a future penalty and dismiss the warning carried by the actual event. The verse holds both together. The experienced consequence is real, and the threatened punishment remains serious. It does not give a detailed description of the latter or specify every feature of its duration in this sentence. Its purpose is to prevent the observer from mistaking the visible chapter of history for the whole judgment. A recent disaster can be a lesson without being the final accounting. The people now compared with their predecessors must therefore reckon with more than whether their immediate plans will succeed. The moral issue reaches beyond the part of the outcome they can see or calculate.

**The Same Formula Elsewhere Broadens the Lesson Without Erasing the Case**

Qur'an 64:5 also recalls earlier disbelievers who experienced the evil results of their conduct and still face painful punishment ([Qurʾān 64:5](https://quran.com/at-taghabun/5)). This checked parallel adds a wider Qur'anic setting to the local historical identification. The combination of an experienced consequence and a further warning is not incidental. It directs attention to history as a present admonition under a final judgment that history alone cannot display completely.

The parallel does not make every historical case identical. The present verse emphasizes a recent example, giving its immediate audience an especially close warning. The broader passage shows how the same moral reasoning can address people in other settings. Both require more than recollection. Knowledge of earlier outcomes becomes useful when it corrects the assumption that one's own group is exempt from accountability. The comparison therefore should not produce delight in another community's suffering. It should produce a more honest assessment of conduct before its consequences have become unavoidable. The recent predecessors have already tasted what later observers can still learn from. The verse's force lies in that opportunity: an available lesson need not become a repeated experience if the hearer allows it to change the course that made the comparison applicable.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:16

> ˹They are˺ like Satan when he lures someone to disbelieve. Then after they have done so, he will say ˹on Judgment Day˺, “I have absolutely nothing to do with you. I truly fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison Exposes Encouragement Without Faithful Support**

The verse presents a compact comparison: Satan urges someone toward disbelief, then disowns the person once the urged act has been committed. The deceiver is active when another's decision is still being formed and absent as a supporter when responsibility must be faced. His interest lies in obtaining the act, not in the person's good. The apparent relation of guidance is therefore false from the beginning.

Ibn Kathīr applies the comparison to deceptive promises of support in the surrounding historical setting ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:16](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/11/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). The moral pattern is what matters here: encouragement can induce a costly choice without creating the reliable bond it seems to offer. The verse does not need a named individual or an elaborate tale about how that individual fell. Its unnamed person allows the mechanism itself to stand clearly. Someone receives an invitation that promises advantage or removes hesitation, acts upon it, and then finds that the inviter will not share the burden. The comparison should therefore be read as an exposure of false dependence. The one who solicits a wrong is not necessarily a companion through its consequences, however persuasive his involvement appears before the decision is made.

**The Lure Does Not Remove the Person's Responsibility to Choose**

Satan lures the person, and the person disbelieves. The sentence distinguishes the invitation from the response. It does not portray disbelief as a physical compulsion that makes the human being's agency disappear. The deceiver bears responsibility for urging the wrong; the person remains responsible for accepting it. Recognizing the first does not erase the second.

A checked parallel in 14:22 makes this distinction explicit. Satan acknowledges that he called and his followers responded, while denying the capacity to rescue them ([Qurʾān 14:22](https://quran.com/ibrahim/22)). This additional reference clarifies the present comparison without supplying an invented conversation. The person's mistake is not simply meeting a deceiver. It is treating the deceiver's invitation as sufficient reason to abandon the truth. That distinction keeps the warning practical. A believer cannot guarantee never to encounter misleading speech, but can refuse to make its attractiveness the measure of its authority. The verse also resists a convenient transfer of all guilt to temptation. Influence is real and can be powerful, yet a promise from an untrustworthy guide does not become a release from one's own obligation to judge and act rightly. Both the solicitation and the response remain morally significant.

**The Moment of Disavowal Reveals What the Relationship Was Worth**

The turn comes after the person has done what Satan wanted. The timing is essential. Before the act, continued encouragement serves the deceiver's purpose. Afterward, the person's need for help supplies no reason for the deceiver to remain faithful. The relationship has been organized around exploitation rather than mutual obligation.

The declaration of having nothing to do with the person is emphatic. It does not mean Satan had no part in the preceding temptation; the verse has just stated that part. His disavowal is therefore not a reliable rewriting of the history. It is the withdrawal of claimed association when association becomes costly. The person cannot undo the choice merely by discovering that the apparent guide has abandoned him. This makes the warning valuable before the choice is made. One must ask whether an invitation is true and right, not merely whether its speaker seems willing to stand nearby for the moment. The sequence reveals a common form of deception without reducing all human encouragement to it. A trustworthy adviser wants the advised person's good; the satanic pattern wants the wrong act and then treats the actor as disposable. The difference becomes unmistakable at the point where the actor needs support most.

**A Declaration of Fear Does Not Make the Deceiver Righteous**

Satan says that he fears Allah. In the comparison, this statement comes after he has urged another toward disbelief. It must therefore not be read as proof that his earlier conduct was obedient or that his withdrawal is a model of piety. Religious language can state a reality while being used within a self-protective act. The timing and surrounding conduct determine what the declaration is doing.

The verse does not ask the reader to decide every question about the inner quality of Satan's fear. Its sufficient point is that the fear he now names did not make his invitation safe. A guide who invokes Allah only when abandoning those he misled has not shown the reverence that should have restrained the misleading act at its beginning. This distinction is useful for recognizing manipulative speech more generally. A solemn appeal to divine authority cannot erase one's own earlier part in another's wrongdoing. Nor does disowning the person amount to repairing the harm done through encouragement. The declaration attempts to place distance between tempter and tempted, but the verse preserves their actual sequence. The reader hears the claim of fear while already knowing who initiated the lure. That order prevents pious wording from controlling the moral interpretation of the whole encounter.

**The Lord of All Worlds Cannot Be Reduced to One Party's Escape Clause**

The divine title at the end affirms a sovereignty encompassing both deceiver and deceived. Satan invokes the Lord of all worlds as a reason to disavow the person, but that lordship was equally real when the invitation was issued. It cannot be called into existence only at the moment when accountability becomes inconvenient. The title exposes the contradiction within selective reverence.

For the person being warned, the proper response is therefore to reckon with Allah's authority before accepting the lure. The appeal that arrives after the wrong cannot supply the security that should have been sought through obedience from the beginning. The verse does not need to complete the entire story of judgment in order to make this point. Its own scene is the movement from temptation to abandonment. The promise of companionship fails, the human choice remains, and the deceiver's religious language cannot make his guidance trustworthy after the fact. The comparison teaches a form of moral foresight: judge advice by its truth and by the authority it honors now, not by the reassurance that someone else will stand with you later. A person who abandons guidance for such reassurance may discover that the promised companion has already prepared a declaration of distance.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:17

> So they will both end up in the Fire, staying there forever. That is the reward of the wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Disavowal Does Not Separate the Deceiver From Judgment**

Both figures in the comparison reach the Fire: the one who enticed and the one who accepted the enticement. The deceiver's attempted withdrawal has not removed his responsibility. He can announce distance from the person he misled, but he cannot make the history of his own action disappear. The verdict is given by Allah, not by whichever participant speaks most forcefully about innocence.

The word both is decisive. It joins in the outcome those whose relation appeared to break apart when responsibility approached. Their shared destination is not a restoration of friendly solidarity. It is the exposure of a shared accountability that neither party controls. The deceiver is not rescued by blaming the follower, and the follower is not rescued merely by identifying the deceiver. The sentence brings the comparison to its moral end without adding a long dispute. Whatever each may say, Allah knows the part each played. A human declaration of disassociation can change a public position; it cannot alter an act already committed. The verse therefore answers the illusion that a manipulator can enjoy the effect of his encouragement and then step outside its consequences by refusing further association with the person who acted upon it.

**Being Misled Does Not Make the Chosen Wrong Disappear**

The second participant has suffered betrayal, but betrayal does not by itself cancel the responsibility attached to his own choice. The verse is not treating the enticement as imaginary. It is recognizing that influence and response are distinct actions. A person can be genuinely deceived by an invitation and still have acted wrongly in accepting it. The moral account must include both facts.

This is a precise claim about the comparison, not a blanket denial of coercion, ignorance, or other circumstances that can affect human responsibility. The verse has already identified a person who responds to the invitation to disbelief. It is that narrated relation which the verdict completes. One should not detach the outcome and impose it upon every victim of manipulation regardless of circumstances. Here the deceiver's wrong does not transform the follower's wrong into obedience. The shared end also does not prove that their degrees of responsibility are identical. A common destination can be stated without a complete description of every distinction within judgment. The clear lesson is that blame cannot simply be transferred away from oneself by pointing to another guilty person. Naming the tempter may establish his culpability; it does not automatically establish the innocence of the one who followed.

**A Wider Qur'anic Scene Preserves Responsibility on Both Sides**

Qur'an 7:38 describes followers entering the Fire and accusing the leaders who misled them. Their accusation does not produce release for the followers; the answer maintains punishment across the groups involved ([Qurʾān 7:38](https://quran.com/al-araf/38)). This checked parallel adds a fuller example of the principle stated compactly here. Shared wrongdoing can generate mutual accusation without giving either party control of the divine account.

The parallel is useful because it resists two simplifications. One would place all responsibility on the leader, as though those who followed had made no choices. The other would place all responsibility on the followers, as though deliberate misguidance added no wrong of its own. Neither captures the Qur'anic presentation. Enticing others is an act, and responding is also an act. The present verse's dual outcome holds those responsibilities together. It does not make analysis of influence unnecessary; it makes that analysis more exact. The person who encouraged the wrong cannot hide behind the claim that someone else performed it. The person who performed it cannot regard another's encouragement as a complete defense. The final judge can recognize the contribution of each without accepting either one's attempt to rewrite the relation after it has produced its evil result.

**Permanent Residence Makes the Failed Promise Fully Visible**

The verse states that both remain in the Fire forever. The invitation that seemed to offer advantage has led to an enduring loss. This contrast exposes the real value of the deceptive promise. Its attraction belonged to the moment before the choice; its consequence cannot be measured by that brief attraction. A person who judged the invitation only by immediate reassurance would have left out the largest part of what was at stake.

The permanence also makes the deceiver's abandonment especially empty. He did not possess the ability to provide the security his influence suggested. Neither withdrawal nor mutual dependence produces an escape from the verdict. The wording gives no basis for imagining that their shared residence will become a source of comfort or protection. They are together in consequence, not in a successful alliance. The sentence should be allowed to retain its severity without the addition of speculative details about the Fire. Its own terms establish the essential reversal: what appeared to be guidance toward benefit ends in lasting punishment. The comparison therefore calls for judgment before an invitation is accepted. The truth of the proposed act matters more than the temporary confidence supplied by the person urging it, because confidence can vanish while responsibility remains.

**Wrongdoing, Not Cleverness or Victimhood, Defines the Verdict**

The final sentence calls this the recompense of wrongdoers. Reward in the translation means the return corresponding to action, not approval or a desirable prize. The evaluative term is wrongdoing. It strips away the competing descriptions by which the participants might seek a better account of themselves: the clever adviser who merely offered an option, or the follower who assigns his entire conduct to someone else's influence.

The general wording carries the principle beyond the two figures while preserving the need for an actual moral judgment. It does not authorize people to call every opponent a wrongdoer and assign him this destiny. Allah states the verdict in a case whose reality He knows. The human lesson is to refuse the structure of wrongdoing before it becomes a shared consequence. Do not lead another into an act you cannot truthfully defend before Allah; do not accept such an act merely because someone offers companionship in it. The comparison ends not with the deceiver's excuse but with Allah's judgment. That final authority is what makes the verdict more than a contest over who can blame whom. The promise, the response, and the abandonment all fall under a knowledge that neither participant can manipulate through his later description of events.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:18

> O believers! Be mindful of Allah and let every soul look to what ˹deeds˺ it has sent forth for tomorrow. And fear Allah, ˹for˺ certainly Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Requires an Examination of What Is Actually Being Done**

The address is to believers, and the command is to examine what each soul has sent ahead. Belonging to the community does not remove the need for self-accounting. A person must look at conduct rather than rely on the name he bears, the intentions he prefers to remember, or the approval he receives from others. The question concerns what has become an act.

Every soul makes the responsibility individual without making it isolated. People live through relationships, but no one can substitute another person's vigilance for his own. A household, teacher, or community may encourage good, yet each person must consider his contribution to the account. The command to look implies deliberate attention. Ordinary busyness can conceal a moral pattern from the person producing it. One completes tasks, answers demands, and moves into another day without asking what those actions are preparing. The verse interrupts that movement. It does not demand an impossible recovery of every forgotten detail before anything else can be done. It asks for honest scrutiny of the direction and substance of one's deeds. Faith becomes alert conduct rather than a confidence that serious examination is necessary only for people outside the believing community.

**Tomorrow Brings the Final Account Near to Present Decisions**

The coming judgment is called tomorrow. The expression places the future close enough to govern today's actions, not as a date that human beings can calculate. A person may regard the Hereafter as remote because its arrival is unseen. The verse counters that emotional distance by speaking of the next horizon toward which present conduct is already moving.

Sending ahead is equally precise. Deeds do not wait for the actor to begin thinking seriously about the future before acquiring significance. They are already being prepared for that meeting. The question is therefore not whether one will send something, but what one is sending. Neglect is not a neutral interval outside the account. Nor does a good intention concerning a future reform automatically replace the conduct currently taking place. The image joins present agency and future consequence without encouraging despair about the past. Its practical pressure falls on what can be corrected and performed now. A believer does not need knowledge of the final hour to know that an act of justice is better preparation than a concealed wrong. The near language of tomorrow turns an immense theological truth into a question that can be asked before the next decision is made.

**The Prophet Applied This Verse to an Immediate Need for Relief**

A verified report from Jarīr ibn ʿAbdillāh describes people arriving in severe poverty. The Prophet's visible concern was followed by prayer and an address in which he recited 4:1 and this verse, urging material assistance. Food, clothing, and money were brought until the need drew a substantial communal response. The account is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017a ([Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1017a)). It establishes a direct Prophetic use of the present instruction, not merely a later association with charitable work.

That application prevents self-examination from becoming only an inward inventory of feelings. Looking toward tomorrow can require seeing a hungry person today. The future account is prepared partly through one's response to an immediate, visible need. The report also includes encouragement to give even a very small contribution. This checked detail matters because a person can postpone generosity while waiting to possess enough for an impressive gift. The verse's demand is not that everyone display equal resources. It is that each soul consider its actual opportunity to do good. An action modest in amount may still be an honest answer to the command, while a large capacity left unused remains part of what the person must examine.

**An Example Can Send More Than One Act Ahead**

The same report describes an Anṣārī man bringing a substantial contribution, after which others followed. The Prophet then explained the reward of setting a good example that others continue, and the corresponding burden of setting an evil example. These details are verified in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017a and add a dimension beyond the simple distribution of aid. A deed can shape subsequent deeds by making a course of action visible and practicable to others.

This does not mean that influence replaces personal action or that a person should give merely to become admired. In the report, the example serves an existing command and a genuine need. Its benefit lies in encouraging obedience, not in manufacturing a reputation. The connection to this verse is exact: what a soul sends ahead can include the consequences of the conduct it initiates. A first act of generosity can make hesitation give way to shared help. An evil precedent can likewise normalize a wrong. The command to examine therefore reaches beyond the immediate size of an act to the direction in which it leads others. One should ask not only what is being done, but what is being made easier for others to do afterward. Responsibility includes the example embodied in conduct.

**Repeated Reverence Keeps Self-Accounting Truthful**

Mindfulness of Allah frames the instruction on both sides. The believer is first told to revere Him, then to examine deeds, then to fear Him again. The repetition prevents self-assessment from becoming a private exercise in congratulation. The measure is not merely whether a person can tell a favorable story about his behavior. It is whether the conduct can stand before the Lord who knows its reality.

Allah's complete awareness supplies the foundation. He knows the act, its circumstances, and what the person attempts to conceal from his own attention. That knowledge makes honest examination necessary, but it also makes unnoticed good meaningful. The purpose is not to produce endless anxious calculation detached from action. The Prophetic application to charity shows a practical movement: recognize what is required and do it. A useful examination identifies a neglected duty, a wrong requiring repair, or an opportunity for generosity, then changes conduct. The verse's closing assurance leaves no need to perform that reform for an audience. What is sent ahead does not become real only when others praise it. It is already known to Allah. Reverence, scrutiny, and action belong together because the coming account concerns the life one is actually preparing, not merely the life one intends someday to begin.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:19

> And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. It is they who are ˹truly˺ rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forgetting Allah Becomes a Failure to Care for the Self Truly**

The verse joins two forms of forgetting. People neglect Allah, and He causes them to neglect themselves. The result reverses what self-centered conduct often promises. A person may turn from divine obligation in order to preserve more freedom for personal wants, yet the outcome is a diminished understanding of what genuinely benefits the person who wants. Attention to appetite can increase while care for the self becomes less truthful.

Forgetting oneself here does not mean losing one's name or becoming unable to recognize a face. The closing judgment of rebellion places the statement in a moral setting. The issue is neglect of the self's responsibility and final good. A person may remain skilled in earning, planning, and defending his preferences while failing to prepare for the meeting that gives those activities their ultimate measure. The verse therefore distinguishes self-preoccupation from self-knowledge. The former can be constant without producing the latter. Its warning is not that every difficulty with memory has a spiritual cause. It concerns an established refusal to keep Allah in view and the corresponding loss of direction in one's own life. The relation to the Creator is not an optional addition to a fully understood self; neglecting that relation distorts the account the self gives of its own needs.

**The Consequence Fits the Direction of the Neglect**

Allah causes the second forgetting. The verse does not leave the consequence as a purely accidental psychological effect. It describes a divine response corresponding to the chosen neglect. Those who set aside their Lord are left without a sound orientation toward their own good. The punishment is not foreign to the offense; it appears within the very life they sought to govern apart from remembrance.

Ibn Kathīr explains this as forgetting the good deeds that would benefit them upon their return to Allah, emphasizing a correspondence between action and recompense ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:19](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/19/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). That checked explanation makes the consequence concrete. The problem is not merely that a person feels less reflective. Opportunities for obedience are missed, duties are displaced, and beneficial action is neglected. A life can become full of activity while its preparation for the Hereafter remains empty. The divine attribution should not be used to excuse the initial neglect, since the sentence explicitly begins with what the people did. Nor should the warning be transformed into a claim that a passing distraction proves abandonment by Allah. It concerns a moral course and its fitting consequence, not a diagnosis of every unsettled mood or ordinary lapse.

**Useful Possessions Can Become Instruments of Forgetfulness**

Ibn Kathīr connects the warning with 63:9, which cautions believers against allowing wealth and children to distract them from remembrance of Allah. This checked cross-reference adds a practical example beyond the per-verse account. The distractions need not be things evil in themselves. Real goods and real responsibilities can become disordered when they claim the whole field of attention.

A person can care for a household faithfully while remembering that its needs exist under divine guidance. The same outward tasks can also be pursued in a way that makes every obligation to Allah indefinitely postponable. The distinction concerns ordering, not abandonment of family or work. The verse does not teach that recovering oneself requires ceasing to provide for dependents. It teaches that provision must not become an excuse for forgetting what makes the provider accountable. This is why self-forgetfulness can be difficult to recognize. Its activities may look respectable and necessary. The failure appears in what they continually displace. A schedule filled with valued tasks can still reveal that worship, repentance, and justice never receive their due. The cross-reference makes the warning recognizable without requiring a sensational example. Ordinary abundance can conceal a profound loss when it becomes the whole definition of what the self exists to maintain.

**Remembering the Self Requires More Than Following Its Desires**

The verse's paradox challenges the assumption that the self knows its good simply by wanting something. A desire identifies an object of attraction; it does not determine whether pursuing that object is wise, just, or beneficial in the final account. Remembering oneself truthfully includes remembering one's dependence, limits, duties, and destination. Those dimensions cannot be supplied by appetite alone.

This does not mean that every desire is suspect or that personal well-being has no place in faith. It means that well-being must be understood within a true relation to Allah. Rest, provision, companionship, and lawful pleasure can belong to that relation. They become misleading when treated as complete answers to the question of what a human life is for. The person who has forgotten Allah may still defend the self vigorously while no longer protecting its deepest interests. He may resent an obligation precisely because it would rescue him from a harmful course. The verse therefore makes remembrance a form of recovery rather than a theft of personal identity. The self is not erased by acknowledging its Lord. It is understood more accurately. Neglecting that acknowledgment does not produce an independent self; it produces a self whose account of its own good has become dangerously incomplete.

**Rebellion Names the Moral Condition, Not an Unchosen Illness**

The final description is rebellion. It confirms that the verse concerns departure from obedience rather than a medical condition affecting memory or identity. The people have forgotten Allah in the sense of neglecting His claim upon them. Their resulting self-neglect is judged within that chosen moral course. This prevents the verse from being misused against people experiencing illness, confusion, grief, or ordinary mental fatigue.

The command not to resemble them is addressed as a live warning. Its purpose is to change conduct before neglect hardens into a settled way of life. A useful response is not endless speculation about whether every distraction is a punishment. It is a return to the acts through which remembrance becomes real: honoring a known duty, correcting a wrong, and restoring the place of worship in daily life. The verse gives a reason for those acts that reaches beyond bare compliance. They preserve the person's relation to his own true good. The warning's sequence is exact: disregard of Allah, a corresponding loss of self-directed wisdom, and the verdict of rebellion. It exposes a false promise of independence by showing that a life organized without its Lord can become a life unable to recognize what it owes even to itself.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:20

> The residents of the Fire cannot be equal to the residents of Paradise. ˹Only˺ the residents of Paradise will be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Different Final Destinations Cannot Be Treated as Equivalent**

The verse states an inequality between the residents of the Fire and the residents of Paradise. Its subject is final standing before Allah, not the denial of ordinary human rights in this life. The two groups have reached destinations whose meanings cannot be merged into a single undifferentiated outcome. Judgment distinguishes; it does not declare every course of life equally successful.

The statement is necessary because practical life can be organized as though the distinction has no force. People may acknowledge Paradise and the Fire in speech while evaluating every choice only by immediate convenience. If both paths are treated as interchangeable in action, the confessed difference remains unused. The verse makes the contrast a rule for judgment now. It does not require the reader to identify the final destination of every person he meets. The groups are named by the outcomes Allah assigns, and human beings do not possess complete access to that assignment. The moral demand is to recognize that the outcomes themselves are not equal. A person cannot truthfully seek salvation while treating what leads toward it and what leads away from it as equivalent merely because both may offer a temporary worldly advantage.

**Residence Concerns a Settled Outcome Rather Than a Passing Mood**

Both groups are called residents. The language concerns a condition in which they are established, not merely an emotion experienced for a moment. The verse therefore places the comparison beyond the changing fortunes through which people usually judge success. A joyful day or a painful season does not settle the account of an entire life. The final abode supplies a measure that temporary circumstances cannot provide.

This also explains why the verse does not need to describe every feature of either destination in order to establish the contrast. The Fire and Paradise already carry opposed meanings of judgment and reward. The focus here is their non-equivalence. Expanding the sentence into imagined landscapes would distract from that logical force. A reader is asked to make a distinction that can govern choices, not to collect unverified details of the unseen. Nor should residence language be used to erase all distinctions among those judged. The verse does not present a complete account of every category of sin, mercy, or punishment. It makes the necessary comparison between the two named groups. Its simplicity is not lack of depth: it forces an answer to the question of whether ultimate safety and ultimate loss can be treated as the same kind of result.

**Success Is Defined by Salvation Rather Than Visibility**

The second sentence identifies the residents of Paradise as successful. The first clause denies equality; the second names which outcome constitutes attainment. Success is not left as a label that each person can attach to whatever he happens to obtain. In this final sense, the person who reaches Paradise has gained what makes the whole life successful.

A checked parallel in 3:185 defines triumph through being kept from the Fire and admitted to Paradise, while locating full recompense on the Day of Judgment ([Qurʾān 3:185](https://quran.com/ali-imran/185)). This additional reference clarifies the criterion rather than merely repeating a favorable description of Paradise. Success includes rescue from what destroys and admission to what fulfills. The world can offer partial goods without supplying that final verdict. Possessions, recognition, and influence may be used well, but none is an adequate substitute for the end named here. Conversely, a person whose faithful life involves hardship has not thereby failed. The comparison frees evaluation from the demand that every true good must appear as immediate public advantage. The outcome before Allah is decisive, and the passage teaches the reader to order present ambition by that outcome rather than the other way around.

**The Contrast Makes Moral Indifference Intellectually Incoherent**

If the two destinations are not equal, choices cannot be evaluated only by whether they satisfy a present desire. The verse does not provide a catalogue of duties, but it establishes a reason why duties and prohibitions matter. They belong to a life with a final account. Treating obedience and deliberate wrongdoing as equally acceptable would ignore the very distinction the sentence affirms.

This is not an argument that a person can earn Paradise through a mechanical total calculated without dependence on Allah. The verse names the successful outcome; it does not reduce divine judgment to arithmetic. Its immediate correction is against indifference, not against hope. A believer can seek mercy while taking conduct seriously. Indeed, hope directed toward Paradise should make the promised end more influential in action, not less. The contrast also restrains envy of apparent worldly success. A person may acquire something through wrongdoing that another refuses to acquire that way. The visible difference is real, but it is not the final comparison. The verse refuses the assumption that the person with the immediate advantage must be the one who has truly won. Judgment can disclose that an apparent gain was part of a losing course, while a costly act of fidelity belonged to a successful life.

**The Statement Is a Call to Choose Well, Not to Boast of One's Destination**

The residents of Paradise are successful, but the verse does not invite a reader to announce his own admission in advance and use it as a rank over others. The promised outcome should direct desire and conduct toward Allah. Presumption would turn a warning about final distinctions into another instrument of present self-importance.

A sound response combines longing, humility, and action. One seeks the good end, recognizes the need for divine mercy, and refuses to make temporary advantage the complete measure of right. These responses are not additional conditions invented for the sentence. They follow from taking its comparison seriously without claiming knowledge it does not grant to the reader. The verse makes clear what success is while leaving judgment with Allah. That balance protects both moral clarity and human restraint. People should not pretend that the Fire and Paradise are equivalent; neither should they pretend to control who belongs to each. The final account will reveal the distinction fully. In the meantime, the statement gives life a direction: ultimate success is not being admired, comfortable, or victorious for a season, but reaching the abode of divine reward. Every lesser aim must be judged in relation to that greater end.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:21

> Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain, you would have certainly seen it humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah. We set forth such comparisons for people, ˹so˺ perhaps they may reflect.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Mountain Makes the Weight of Revelation Visible**

The comparison places the Qur'an before a mountain, an image of size, hardness, and apparent stability. The mountain would be humbled and split by awe of Allah. The point is not that a human being should perform a physical experiment by reciting toward rock. It is a revealed comparison through which the seriousness of the Qur'an's address becomes visible in a form the imagination can grasp.

The demonstrative this Qur'an keeps the object specific. The verse is not praising language in general or every moving speech. It concerns the revelation actually given to people, with its claims upon understanding and conduct. The mountain's response measures the gravity of that gift. Something apparently immovable is pictured as yielding before what human hearers can too easily treat as familiar sound. The contrast therefore turns the comparison back toward its recipients. A person may hear often and respond little, not because the message lacks weight but because repetition has been allowed to dull attention. The mountain gives that failure a visible scale. Its solidity does not make it indifferent in the imagined encounter; the human listener's greater capacity to understand should not become a reason for a less serious response.

**Humility and Fracture Describe More Than Emotional Excitement**

The mountain is both humbled and torn apart. The first description concerns abasement before divine greatness; the second makes the effect upon its apparent strength unmistakable. Together they portray a response deeper than a passing impression. The object whose very shape suggests resistance cannot remain untouched by the encounter the verse imagines.

The cause is awe of Allah. The Qur'an's weight is not detached from the One who speaks through revelation. A listener who admires only the sound or force of the language while refusing its claim upon life has not exhausted what the verse asks him to consider. Awe recognizes authority, truth, promise, and warning. Ibn Kathīr explains the mountain as hypothetically endowed with comprehension of the Qur'an and thus responding to what it understands ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:21](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/21/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). That checked explanation clarifies the nature of the comparison. The sentence is not a claim about the normal geological effects of sound waves. It imagines reception adequate to the revelation's meaning. The resulting humility is therefore not mindless collapse. It is a portrayal of what recognition of Allah's address would do to an otherwise formidable object.

**The Human Heart Is Reproved for Remaining Unmoved**

The comparison asks what it means when a human heart remains hard before the same revelation. People possess capacities for understanding, choice, and response that make the Qur'an's address directly applicable to them. If the imagined mountain would yield, the listener cannot make hardness an achievement. Indifference is not proof of strength in relation to a truth one ought to receive.

This is the force emphasized by Ibn Kathīr: human hearers should become humble through understanding Allah's command. The argument does not require that every sincere person display the same outward emotion. Tears, silence, fear, and quiet resolve cannot be measured by a single visible standard. The relevant question is whether understanding produces a truthful response. A person can be outwardly moved while refusing a known obligation, just as another can listen quietly and then correct a wrong. The verse seeks reflection that reaches the will, not an emotional performance for an audience. Its reproof falls upon the refusal to yield to guidance, not upon a naturally reserved manner. The mountain's imagined fracture makes the claim of revelation serious enough to challenge habitual resistance. The heart is asked to become receptive, not to imitate a spectacle of breaking.

**Related Images Confirm That Hardness Is Not the Highest Strength**

Ibn Kathīr links the verse with 2:74, where stones release water, split, or fall in fear of Allah. He also cites 13:31 in connection with the Qur'an and the movement of mountains. These checked cross-references add a wider setting for the present image. They challenge the assumption that a thing's worth lies in its capacity to remain unaffected. Receptivity to Allah is not a defect in creation's strength.

The parallel with stones is particularly useful because it turns a familiar material into a moral comparison. What seems harder than a human heart can still become an image of yielding, while a person capable of understanding may refuse to respond. The present verse sharpens that contrast through the mountain's scale. It does not invite contempt for human embodiment or a claim that people are valuable only when overwhelmed. Their ability to receive the Qur'an is a responsibility and a gift. The image rebukes failure to use that ability well. A strength that consists only in resisting every claim can become a form of incapacity. The truer strength of the listener lies in answering guidance, even when doing so requires surrendering a defended habit or an admired account of oneself.

**Reflection Is the Response the Comparison Explicitly Seeks**

The verse names its purpose: people may reflect. The dramatic image is therefore not an end in itself. A listener who remembers only the torn mountain has not completed the movement requested. Reflection asks what the comparison reveals about Allah, the Qur'an, and the hearer's own response. It carries the image into judgment and conduct.

The wording concerning perhaps does not express ignorance on Allah's part. It presents the intended human response as something the hearers are called to undertake, not something the comparison mechanically forces. Revelation addresses people who can still resist. Its power does not abolish their responsibility to listen and think. A practical reflection might begin with a command one has repeatedly heard but avoided, or a promise one has confessed without allowing it to govern fear. The image then ceases to be distant: the question is whether the heart will yield where the mountain is pictured as yielding. The verse gives no need for an unverified mystical report to complete its meaning. Its own argument is sufficient. The revelation is weighty, the imagined mountain responds with awe, and human beings are called to consider why their own understanding should not produce a more faithful response.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:22

> He is Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him: Knower of the seen and unseen. He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Worship Is Directed to One Lord Rather Than Divided Among Powers**

The verse identifies Allah and declares that no other is worthy of worship. The statement is not merely that He is stronger than other gods within a shared order. It denies their rightful claim to divinity. Worship belongs to Him alone, and no created power acquires a share of that right through usefulness, fear, admiration, or inherited custom.

The declaration also orders the names that follow. Perfect knowledge and mercy are not separate divine beings or competing sources of help. They belong to the one Lord named at the beginning. This unity matters for the worshipper's response. A person does not need to seek knowledge from one ultimate power and compassion from another, as though no single Lord knew his condition and cared for him. The same Allah knows and is merciful. The verse therefore joins exclusive worship to a description that makes trust intelligible. Its opening is not an isolated formula preceding unrelated attributes. It establishes who is being described and why no rival can claim the devotion directed toward Him. The reader is called to recognize one sovereign object of worship whose knowledge is complete and whose mercy is neither borrowed nor confined by the weakness of a creature.

**Seen and Unseen Describe Human Limits, Not Limits Within Allah's Knowledge**

Allah knows the seen and the unseen. Things differ in their accessibility to human beings: some can be observed, others remain hidden, distant, inward, or beyond ordinary perception. The verse includes both within divine knowledge. What is unseen to a creature is not uncertain to Allah, and what becomes visible to people does not become newly discovered by Him.

This makes the distinction more than a reference to unusual hidden events. An intention concealed beneath an ordinary action belongs to the same comprehensive knowledge as an event publicly witnessed by thousands. Smallness does not remove a matter from His awareness, and publicity does not make it more knowable to Him. Ibn Kathīr explains the phrase as encompassing what creatures see and what they cannot see, without exception for the great or small ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:22](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/21/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). That checked explanation preserves the breadth of the wording. The believer need not provide Allah with information that He lacks when praying. Prayer expresses dependence upon the One who already knows the need. Likewise, concealment cannot make wrongdoing absent from His account. The same attribute grounds both assurance and responsibility because complete knowledge recognizes the hidden plea and the hidden act alike.

**Complete Knowledge Makes Mercy Exact Rather Than Indiscriminate Ignorance**

The names of mercy follow the declaration of knowledge. Allah's compassion is not the kindness of someone who fails to understand what has happened. He knows the creature's condition fully. His mercy therefore does not depend on an incomplete account that must be carefully managed to preserve sympathy. Nothing needs to be hidden from Him in order for supplication to be meaningful.

This relation can change the way a person approaches repentance. Before human beings, shame may create pressure to soften an account or conceal the worst detail. Before Allah, concealment is impossible, but hope remains because the One who knows is also merciful. The pairing refuses both despair and presumption. Complete knowledge prevents a person from treating mercy as approval obtained through deception. Complete mercy prevents him from assuming that being fully known must make return impossible. The verse does not specify every condition of forgiveness in this sentence. It reveals the Lord to whom the creature turns. The point is not that all outcomes are identical, but that divine knowledge and compassion are inseparable attributes of the same God. One should not imagine a merciful Lord who is unaware of reality or an all-knowing Lord from whose description mercy has been removed.

**The Two Names Give Mercy an Emphatic Place in the Description**

Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm are rendered Most Compassionate and Most Merciful. Both name mercy, while their pairing gives that mercy emphasis and breadth. The English words should not be treated as if they establish a complete technical division by themselves. What the verse makes certain is that mercy is central to the divine description, not an occasional concession reluctantly added to power.

Ibn Kathīr emphasizes Allah's encompassing mercy and connects the names with mercy in this life and the Hereafter. The immediate passage does not require the reader to resolve every scholarly distinction between the two forms in order to worship faithfully. It does require that neither name be emptied of its force. Allah is not described as merely capable of mercy if some external influence persuades Him. Mercy belongs to His perfection. At the same time, its breadth does not erase the reality of justice stated elsewhere in revelation. A selective reading that uses these names to declare every deed harmless would fail to receive the same Lord's commands. The names give a foundation for hope, dependence, gratitude, and return. They do not make indifference to Him a more accurate response than obedience.

**A Prophetic Comparison Makes Mercy Understandable Without Measuring Its Limit**

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reports the Prophet drawing attention to a mother caring for a child among captives and teaching that Allah is more merciful to His servants than she is to her child. The account is verified in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5999 ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5999](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5999)). This additional evidence gives the names of mercy a concrete point of comparison while explicitly placing divine mercy beyond it.

The comparison does not make Allah a creature with bodily or emotional limitations. It begins with a mercy people recognize and directs them toward a greater reality. The mother's care is not the measure that confines Allah's compassion; it is an accessible sign through which hearers can understand that compassion is not a cold abstraction. Read with the present verse, the report also joins mercy to complete knowledge. Allah knows the unseen dimensions of need that even the most devoted human caregiver cannot fully know. Worship is therefore directed to the One whose awareness and compassion exceed every created example. The verse ends where hope can stand securely: the only Lord worthy of worship knows all that is manifest and hidden, and He is named by mercy. Neither ignorance nor indifference limits the One to whom the believer turns.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:23

> He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the AllPerfect, the Source of Serenity, the Watcher ˹of all˺, the Almighty, the Supreme in Might, the Majestic. Glorified is Allah far above what they associate with Him ˹in worship˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Names Describe One Lord Rather Than a Division of Divinity**

The declaration of Allah's sole divinity governs the entire list. Each name describes the same Lord. Kingship, holiness, protection, and might are not assigned to separate beings, nor do the names identify parts from which Allah is assembled. Their plurality gives a fuller description of the One who alone is worthy of worship.

Al-Malik, the King, establishes sovereignty at the beginning of the sequence. His authority is not borrowed from another ruler or granted by those He governs. Ibn Kathīr explains the name through ownership and rule over all things, without a power able to prevent His command ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:23](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/21/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). Created kings rule within limits: they inherit conditions they did not make and depend on resources they cannot fully control. Allah's kingship is not simply a larger instance of that dependence. The other names prevent the reader from imagining sovereignty through the defects familiar in human rulers. The King is also holy, perfect, and watchful. His greatness does not come at the expense of justice or knowledge. The sequence teaches whom the worshipper acknowledges as sovereign and why that acknowledgment cannot be transferred to an impressive creature.

**Holiness and Perfection Exclude the Defects of Created Rule**

Al-Quddūs, the Most Holy, and al-Salām, rendered AllPerfect here, deny impurity, deficiency, and disorder in the divine nature and action. The names do not merely state that Allah has fewer faults than human rulers. They place Him beyond the category of a flawed power needing correction by a superior standard. Worship does not complete Him or repair an inadequacy in His being.

The name al-Salām also matters in the language of prayer. Ibn Masʿūd reports that the Prophet corrected the practice of wishing peace upon Allah, explaining that Allah Himself is al-Salām. The report is verified in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 835 ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 835](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:835)). The correction distinguishes the One from whom safety and perfection come from creatures who need a prayer for safety. It is not a rejection of peacefulness as a good. It is a correction of direction: the servant depends upon Allah, not Allah upon the servant's blessing. Read in this verse, the report keeps the name from being reduced to a pleasant feeling. Divine perfection is the ground on which a creature can seek peace, not a state the creature must first supply to its Lord.

**Security and Watchfulness Join Trust to Complete Knowledge**

Al-Muʾmin is rendered the Source of Serenity. In the checked commentary, explanations include granting security, confirming truth, and attesting the faith of faithful servants. These senses differ in emphasis but share a relation to assurance. The name is not used of Allah in the dependent sense in which a human being believes what he has been taught. Allah does not acquire certainty from someone above Him.

The security reading fits the supplied translation most directly. It presents Allah as the giver of assurance rather than the recipient of it. Al-Muhaymin, the Watcher, then connects that assurance with oversight. Ibn Kathīr explains it through witnessing the deeds of servants. The protector does not overlook what those under His care do or suffer. This joins comfort and accountability: the hidden injury is known, and the hidden betrayal is known. Trust cannot be built by separating one from the other. A watchfulness imagined without goodness could seem threatening in a merely arbitrary way; security imagined without knowledge could be unreliable. Together these names describe a Lord whose care is not blind and whose knowledge is not absent from His rule. The believer seeks refuge with One who knows the full reality of the condition from which refuge is sought.

**Might Is Not Vulnerable to a Stronger Rival**

Al-ʿAzīz, the Almighty, declares invincible authority. Allah's rule is not one force among others whose balance might later shift against Him. Al-Jabbār, rendered Supreme in Might, further affirms the irresistible reach of His sovereignty. A creature can resist an obligation in the sense of choosing disobedience; it cannot make itself independent of the Lord who sustains and judges it.

This distinction prevents two errors. Disobedience is not evidence that divine power has failed, since the creature's morally responsible action still occurs within Allah's governance. Nor does divine sovereignty make disobedience morally innocent, as though everything that happens must therefore be something Allah commands. The verse names the One whose authority no rival can overthrow, not a rule that every powerful human act deserves approval. A tyrant cannot borrow these divine names to make his own coercion holy. His power remains limited, dependent, and accountable. Allah's might, by contrast, is inseparable from the holiness and perfection already named. The sequence therefore protects the concept of power from being defined solely by its most abusive human examples. True divine supremacy contains no ignorance, insecurity, or need to magnify itself by unjustly reducing a creature.

**Majesty Belongs to Allah Without Becoming Permission for Human Pride**

Al-Mutakabbir, the Majestic, names greatness that belongs to Allah in truth. Human arrogance claims a superiority a dependent creature does not possess, often by refusing truth or degrading others. Divine majesty is not that false claim enlarged. Allah does not pretend to be greater than He is. His greatness is real, while the creature's attempt at absolute greatness is a contradiction of its condition.

A verified report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī and Abū Hurayrah warns against contesting Allah's glory and majesty. It is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2620 ([Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2620](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2620)). This addition makes the practical distinction explicit: a divine perfection can expose a corresponding human vice when a creature tries to appropriate what belongs uniquely to its Lord. The report's garment imagery expresses that exclusive majesty and should not be turned into an invented physical description. Its ethical force is humility. Recognizing Allah as Majestic should loosen the demand to occupy the highest place among people. The name does not authorize the worshipper to imitate absolute sovereignty. It teaches him why his own dignity must remain compatible with obedience, dependence, and fair treatment of other creatures.

**Glorification Rejects Every Partner Proposed for Such a Lord**

The closing declaration places Allah above the partners people associate with Him. After the names have described His sovereignty and perfection, the denial of partnership has a developed basis. A supposed rival would need the independence, knowledge, holiness, and authority that the verse attributes to Allah alone. No dependent object can acquire those qualities merely because people worship it.

Glorification therefore is not an ornamental ending to a list. It is the worshipper's truthful response to what the names disclose. The more clearly Allah's perfection is understood, the less plausible it becomes to divide devotion among creatures treated as independent sources of ultimate safety or power. The sentence also guards against distorting the names themselves. Kingship must not be imagined as human tyranny, watchfulness as ignorance needing informants, or majesty as insecure pride. Allah is above those defective analogies as well as above proposed partners. The verse begins with exclusive divinity and ends with transcendence, enclosing the names within a single act of recognition. The proper response is worship marked by trust, reverence, and humility before the One whose authority is perfect and whose perfection needs no partner to complete it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:24

> He is Allah: the Creator, the Inventor, the Shaper. He ˹alone˺ has the Most Beautiful Names. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Him. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation Has Its Origin, Existence, and Form From One Lord**

The three opening names concern Allah's relation to what He brings into being. Al-Khāliq, the Creator, al-Bāriʾ, the Inventor, and al-Muṣawwir, the Shaper, distinguish aspects of creation without dividing the work among independent powers. The being who determines is the being who brings forth and gives form. Nothing in the sequence suggests that Allah needs another agent to complete a task beyond His own ability.

Ibn Kathīr explains the first name through determination and proportion, the second through bringing into existence, and the third through the particular form Allah wills ([Ibn Kathīr on 59:24](https://quran.com/en/al-hashr/21/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir)). These distinctions help the reader see why the names are not empty repetition. A human plan can remain unrealized, and an existing material can await a form its maker struggles to impose. Allah's creative authority is not subject to those divisions of capacity. The names disclose a complete relation to the creature. They should not be treated as a compulsory timetable of successive divine experiments. The verse praises the One who knows and wills what He creates, not a craftsman learning by trial how to make an uncertain design succeed.

**Individual Form Belongs Within the Universal Act of Creation**

The name Shaper prevents thought from stopping at the bare fact that things exist. Creatures have distinct forms, proportions, and characteristics. Their existence is not an undifferentiated mass unrelated to divine knowledge. Allah's creative authority reaches the particular as well as the general. A person does not become more fully understood by treating individuality as a detail outside the Creator's concern.

A checked parallel in 3:6 describes Allah shaping people in their mothers' wombs as He wills and closes with the same names Almighty and All-Wise ([Qurʾān 3:6](https://quran.com/ali-imran/6)). This additional reference gives the universal name a direct human application. It does not turn the three names here into a scientific classification of developmental stages. Such a claim would require evidence the wording does not supply. The parallel instead teaches dependence at the beginning of one's bodily life. No person first designs himself and then enters a relation with Allah. That relation precedes his ability to understand or choose. Recognition of the Shaper can therefore support gratitude and humility without becoming a warrant to judge another person's worth by bodily appearance. The Creator's relation to each person is more fundamental than the comparisons through which people rank one another's forms.

**The Beauty of the Names Is the Perfection They Truly Describe**

The Most Beautiful Names belong to Allah. Their beauty is not merely the pleasant sound of the words or the elegance of writing them. They disclose true perfection: knowledge without ignorance, power without dependence, wisdom without error, and the other qualities by which Allah has made Himself known. The names are beautiful because their meanings are worthy of the Lord they describe.

This has consequences for how they are studied and used in worship. Memorization can help preserve them, but the names should not become detached tokens treated as useful apart from their meaning. Calling upon the Creator expresses dependence; remembering the All-Wise disciplines the claim to understand every event; praising the Almighty denies ultimate power to created rivals. The verse does not authorize invention of any title that a person finds emotionally appealing. Its declaration concerns the names that truly belong to Allah. The proper response is receptive rather than proprietary: the worshipper learns how Allah names Himself rather than constructing a deity out of preferred qualities. Beauty therefore joins truth and devotion. A name misunderstood through a defective human analogy can obscure the very perfection it should reveal, so careful understanding belongs to reverence rather than standing against it.

**The Report of Ninety-Nine Names Connects Learning With Salvation**

Abū Hurayrah transmits the report concerning ninety-nine names and the promise of Paradise for the person who preserves them. The checked version is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6410; its English rendering explains the response through belief in their meanings and corresponding action ([Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6410](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6410)). The Arabic wording emphasizes preserving or memorizing them. The explanatory English should not be mistaken for an additional separate Arabic sentence, but it rightly directs attention beyond empty repetition.

The report gives a definite promise without providing, in this version, a complete enumerated list. A commentary should not invent such a list and attribute it to this number. Nor does the statement that Allah has ninety-nine names carrying this promise logically require that no other name belongs to Him. The verse itself makes no numerical restriction. Its concern is the beauty and rightful ownership of the names. The hadith adds encouragement to learn and preserve them seriously. That encouragement fits the verse's movement toward glorification: knowledge of Allah should become praise and a life ordered by what is known. Reciting a name while deliberately denying its claim upon conduct cannot be the ideal toward which the teaching directs the believer.

**All Creation Glorifies the One Who Gives It Being**

Whatever is in the heavens and earth glorifies Allah. The declaration brings the vast plurality of created forms under a single relation of praise. Their diversity does not imply divided sources of divinity. The One who creates and shapes them is the One to whom their glorification is directed. Human praise joins that comprehensive relation rather than establishing Allah's worth for the first time.

Ibn Kathīr connects this statement with 17:44, which affirms the glorification of all things while limiting human understanding of their praise. The comparison restrains two impulses: reducing every creature's glorification to familiar human speech, and denying its reality because that speech is not heard. The verse supplies the fact without making the reader master of every mode in which it occurs. Its opening names give the declaration a clear foundation. Creatures owe their being and form to Allah; their praise acknowledges a perfection they do not possess independently. Human rebellion cannot turn any part of creation into a rival source of ultimate power. Even the person who refuses commanded worship remains a creature. The universal glorification therefore magnifies the responsibility of the human listener without making his refusal capable of diminishing Allah's actual greatness.

**Might and Wisdom Close the Account of Creation Together**

The final names are Almighty and All-Wise. Power and wisdom remain joined at the end of the chapter. Allah does not merely have the ability to produce effects; His creative and governing action is not ignorant or arbitrary. Ibn Kathīr explains wisdom here in relation to His decrees and legislation. The same Lord orders what exists and gives guidance concerning how responsible creatures should live.

This ending keeps contemplation of creation from becoming admiration for force alone. The world is not finally explained by a blind power, and religious command is not a detached addition from another source. Both belong under the sovereignty of the One whose names are beautiful. Yet recognizing divine wisdom does not grant a person complete knowledge of the reason for every particular event. Reverence includes admitting that limit. The verse supplies grounds for trust without licensing confident inventions about suffering or hidden purposes. Its closing movement is complete: Allah creates and shapes, His names express perfection, all creation glorifies Him, and His authority is inseparable from wisdom. The appropriate human response is to know Him through the names He gives, worship Him without partners, and live as a dependent creature whose Lord is neither powerless nor unwise.
