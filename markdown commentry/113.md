# Sūrah al-Falaq (Chapter 113) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Splitting Dawn and the Refuge from Hidden Darkness**

Al-Falaq, "The Daybreak" or "The Rising Dawn," is the one-hundred-and-thirteenth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 5 verses of early Makkan revelation (with some exegetes linking its specific invocation to a Medinan occasion). Together with Sūrah an-Nās, it forms the celebrated pair known in prophetic tradition as *Al-Muʿawwidhatayn* (The Two Protectors / The Two Surahs of Refuge). In Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (814), the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ said to ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir: *“Have you not seen verses revealed tonight the likes of which have never been seen? 'Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-falaq' and 'Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-nās'!”* It opens with the command of spiritual sanctuary: *“Say, 'I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak, from the evil of that which He created. From the evil of darkness when it settles, and from the evil of the blowers in knots, and from the evil of an envier when he envies!'”* (113:1–5). Sūrah al-Falaq is the great spiritual shield against external, cosmic, occult, and psychological evils that threaten human life from the dark.

**The Structural Architecture and the Four External Evils**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Falaq moves across two distinct movements:

1. The Divine Refuge (1): *“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak (*Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-falaq*)!”* *Al-Falaq* is the sudden, violent splitting of the morning light through the pitch-black womb of night. The imagery is profound: just as God has the power to shatter the darkness of night with the blazing light of dawn, He has the sovereign power to shatter any evil, illness, or dark curse that ensnares the human soul.

2. The Four Evils of Creation (2–5): - *General Evil* (2): *“From the evil of that which He created (*Min sharri mā khalaq*)!”* (encompassing predatory beasts, poisonous insects, diseases. Hostile forces). - *The Settling Darkness* (3): *“And from the evil of darkness when it settles (*wa-min sharri ghāsiqin idhā waqab*)!”* (nightfall, when criminals, beasts, harmful spirits, and anxieties roam free). - *Occult Malice* (4): *“And from the evil of the blowers in knots (*wa-min sharri al-naffāthāti fī al-ʿuqad*)!”* (practitioners of sorcery and dark rituals who blow malicious incantations into knots). - *Psychological Poison* (5): *“And from the evil of an envier when he envies (*wa-min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasad*)!”* (the venom of the evil eye and malicious jealousy).

The historical occasion of al-Falaq and an-Nās is documented in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (5763) and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2189) by ʿĀ'ishah.

## Verse 113:1
> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak

**ʿĀdha Is the Verb of Someone Who Cannot Win**

To seek refuge — *ʿādha*, *istaʿādha* — is to place yourself under a stronger party because you cannot hold your own position. The word carries inability in its meaning; nobody takes refuge from something he can defeat.

The Qur'an commands this verb rather than permitting it, and commands it to the Prophet ﷺ himself: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (7:200); *“And if you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Indeed, He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (41:36); *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.”* (16:98). A man protected from error is told to seek protection from temptation, before prayer and during it.

**Falaq Is What Gets Split Open**

*Falaq* comes from a root meaning to cleave, to split something lengthwise. The dawn is a *falaq* because the night appears to be split by it — the dark is opened and light comes through the opening. The Qur'an uses the same root for God's own action: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision.”* (6:96). God is *fāliq al-iṣbāḥ*, the Cleaver of the morning.

The word also carries a metaphorical sense in the Arabic lexicographical tradition: the emergence of clarity after uncertainty, of truth after confusion. On that reading the verse asks for shelter in the Lord who splits open confusion, and the request becomes a request for understanding as much as for safety. The Qur'an supplies the same idea elsewhere: *“And declare, ‘The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish.’”* (17:81).

**A Chapter Revealed to Undo a Spell**

The occasion most often given for these two sūrahs is the report that magic was worked on the Prophet ﷺ. ʿĀʾisha narrated that he began to imagine he was doing things he was not doing. That two men came to him in a dream, one at his head and one at his feet. That one said he had been bewitched; that the magician was Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam; that the material used was a comb, the hair gathered on it, and the outer covering of the pollen of a male date-palm; and that it lay in the well of Dharwān. He went to the well, returned, and refused to have the objects brought out, saying he had been cured by Allah and feared the matter would spread evil among the people; the well was then filled in. That is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3268.

Bukhārī's report does not name these two sūrahs. The connection comes through the interpretive tradition, which records that Gabriel identified a knotted string on which the spell had been whispered, that ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib was sent to fetch it, and that the fourteen verses of the two chapters were recited over its fourteen knots..

**The Two Protectors**

Together with the next chapter this one is called *al-Muʿawwidhatān*, the Two Protectors, from the verb of seeking refuge that opens both. Their standing is stated in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections. ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir reported the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying: "What wonderful verses have been sent down this night, the like of which has never been seen — *Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the dawn*, and *Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of men*" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 814a).

The preeminence of these two chapters is underscored by multiple authentic transmissions from ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir. Imām Aḥmad and an-Nasāʾī recorded that while traveling along an arduous path, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ insisted on dismounting so that ʿUqbah could ride, demonstrating immense humility. The Prophet ﷺ then said: “O ʿUqbah! Shall I not teach you two sūrahs that are the best two chapters people ever recite?” He taught him *Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-falaq* and *Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-nās*, subsequently leading the morning prayer with them. Afterward he urged: “Recite them whenever you go to sleep and whenever you rise; no supplicant ever beseeches with the like of them, and no seeker of refuge ever finds sanctuary in anything comparable.” An-Nasāʾī also recorded from Ibn ʿĀbis al-Juhanī that the Prophet ﷺ explicitly designated these two sūrahs as the absolute pinnacle of protection available to humanity. Imām Aḥmad recorded that Ubayy ibn Kaʿb testified that Jibrīl commanded the Prophet ﷺ to speak these very words, and the Companions established them firmly in the codex.

## Verse 113:2
> from the evil of whatever He has created

**The Evil Belongs to the Created Thing, Not to the Creating**

The construction is exact: *sharr mā khalaqa* — the evil of what He created. The genitive attaches evil to the creature, and the act of creation stays with God.

That distinction is not decorative. It reflects a consistent convention in the Qur'an, which attributes good to God without qualification and handles evil through other grammars. The jinn in their own chapter say of themselves: *“We do not know whether evil is intended for those on earth, or whether their Lord intends for them what is right.”* (72:10) — even the possibility of evil is spoken of passively, without naming its author.

Ibn Kathir clarifies that *min sharri mā khalaq* encompasses every harmful dimension within created existence. Thābit al-Bunānī and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī explained that the scope explicitly includes Hellfire, Iblīs, his demonic progeny, and all creatures endowed with potential malice, whether predatory beasts, venomous vermin, or rebellious beings. The refuge is sought not from creation in its entirety — for God created existence in truth and wisdom — but from the capacity for corruption and injury that certain created entities possess when misdirected.

**Creation as a Veil That Also Reveals**

The reading belongs to the contemplative strand of commentary rather than to the plain sense, and it is preserved in the tradition alongside the others. Its support in the Qur'an is the language of light and sign: *“Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.”* (24:35), and the constant claim that created things are *āyāt*, signs, whose function is to be seen through.

**Asking Protection From What God Made**

The clearest statement is in the verse about magic at Babylon: *“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will.”* (2:102). The harm is genuine — it broke marriages — and it operates only within a permission it does not control.

Refuge in the Lord is therefore refuge in the owner of the thing that threatens you. The prayer does not set God against His creation; it appeals from a creature to its Creator, on the ground that the Creator's authority over it is total. That is why the title used in verse one is *Lord* — *rabb*, the one who governs and disposes — rather than a name of power or of mercy. Lordship over the daybreak includes lordship over everything the daybreak reveals.

## Verse 113:3
> and from the evil of the night when it grows dark

**Ghāsiq and Waqaba Describe a Darkness That Arrives**

Two words carry the verse, and both were argued over. *Ghāsiq* is the active participle of a root meaning to be dark or black; as a noun it names the dark one, the darkening thing. *Waqaba* means to enter, to sink in, to spread over — of the sun, to set; of night, to come down and cover.

The dominant reading is stronger, because the participle *ghāsiq* is a word about darkness and not about temperature, and because the pairing of a dark thing with a darkening verb is the natural construction.

Ibn Kathir records that Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan, and Qatādah all explained *al-ghāsiq idhā waqab* as the night when its darkness advances, thickens, and completely envelops the horizon. Az-Zuhrī glossed the phrase as the moment the sun sets, initiating the nocturnal realm where hidden perils emerge. Early scholars also recorded that the desert Arabs applied the term to the descent or setting of the Pleiades (*al-Thurayyā*), an astronomical transition during which illnesses and contagions were observed to proliferate.

**The Moon Reading Rests on a Report From ʿĀʾisha**

A substantial body of commentary identifies *al-ghāsiq* with the moon. The evidence is a Prophetic statement, not a lexical inference. ʿĀʾisha reported: the Prophet ﷺ looked at the moon and said, "O ʿĀʾisha, seek refuge in Allah from the evil of this, for this is *al-ghāsiq idhā waqab*" — the darkening one when it darkens or sets. The report is in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3366, which al-Tirmidhī called *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ* and Dārussalām grades *ḥasan*.

The interpretive method here is worth noting, because it is the method the tradition uses throughout. Where a word in the Qur'an admits two referents and a report from the Prophet ﷺ attaches one of them to the word itself, both are kept. The report does not narrow the verse; it fills it out. A reciter who thinks of the setting moon and a reciter who thinks of the closing night are both inside the phrase.

A minority identified *al-ghāsiq* with the Pleiades, *al-Thurayyā*, at their setting, on the ground that disease and pestilence were held to become prevalent in the season when that cluster declines.

**The Sūrah Is Recited at the Hour It Names**

The practice attached to these verses places them in the dark. ʿĀʾisha reported that every night, when the Prophet ﷺ went to his bed, he joined his hands, breathed into them, recited into them this chapter, the next one. *Say: He is Allah—One*, and then wiped over as much of his body as he could, beginning with the head, the face and the front of the body, doing that three times. That is Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī.

The other report puts the same chapters outdoors. Travelling between al-Juḥfa and al-Abwāʾ, when a wind and intense darkness came over the party, the Prophet ﷺ sought refuge reciting the two chapters and told ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir to do the same, adding that no one has ever sought refuge with anything comparable to them (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1463).

**Night Is Also His, and That Is Why the Prayer Works**

Verse one names God as Lord of the daybreak and verse three asks for protection from the night. The two are the same governance seen from two ends, and the Qur'an says so directly: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (6:96).

## Verse 113:4
> and from the evil of those ˹witches casting spells by˺ blowing onto knots

**The Same Gesture, Used in the Opposite Direction**

The gesture is the same one the verse condemns: blowing, in a repeated act, directed at the person who needs help. What differs is the content. Over the knots went an incantation addressed to powers below God; into the palms went the revelation of God, and the breath carried it across the body rather than binding it into a cord.

**What the Reports Say About Who Did It**

The authenticated account of the Prophet's bewitchment names a man: Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam, of the Jews of Medina, who used a comb, the hair gathered on it, and the covering of a male date-palm's pollen, and hid the material in the well of Dharwān (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3268). Other accounts, preserved in the interpretive literature, describe women of his household performing the knot-work, and the early commentators connected the feminine plural of this verse to them.

Ibn Kathir documents that Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, and Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk agreed that *an-naffāthāt* denotes the practitioners of sorcery who tie knots in strings or cords, blowing their malevolent incantations directly into each knot. In the Book of Medicine in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, ʿĀʾishah recounted that the spell cast against the Prophet ﷺ by Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam — an ally of the Jews of Banū Zurayq — was embedded in a comb and hair beneath a rock in the well of Dharwān. When the Prophet ﷺ was healed and the object retrieved, ʿĀʾishah asked why he did not execute the culprit or expose the matter publicly. The Prophet ﷺ replied with characteristic forbearance: “Allah has cured me, and I dislike stirring up evil among the people.” Furthermore, early traditions record that when the Prophet ﷺ suffered physical affliction, Jibrīl descended and recited the divine ruqyah over him: “In the Name of Allah I perform ruqyah for you, from every illness that harms you, and from the evil of every envious soul and evil eye; may Allah grant you complete healing.”

**Magic Is Real in the Qur'an, and It Is Never Independent**

The Qur'an's treatment of sorcery has two fixed points, and this verse sits between them. The first is that the practice exists and does damage. Al-Baqarah describes what people learned at Babylon: *“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter.”* (2:102). Marriages broke; the buyer of the craft lost his share in what comes next.

The noun in the verse, *ʿuqda*, appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in a personal rather than a magical sense. Moses, sent to Pharaoh, asks: *“and remove the impediment from my tongue”* (20:27) — in Arabic, *wa-ḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī*, untie a knot from my tongue. The word is the same, and so is the logic: a knot is something tied that the one tied by it cannot untie himself.

The Qur'an uses the same idea for the social bond at 2:102 — *“They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon.”* — the rift between husband and wife is a loosening of a knot that was tied in the marriage contract. Tying and untying are the book's ordinary language for what joins people and what separates them. The blowers on knots are working at that level, in the dark, with a cord.

## Verse 113:5
> and from the evil of an envier when they envy.”

**The First Two Sins in the Qur'an Were Envy**

The tradition reads the chapter's last verse as an appeal against the oldest evil on record. The first sin in the heavens was Iblīs's refusal, and the Qur'an gives his reason in his own words: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* (7:12), after he alone refused to prostrate (7:11 — *“Surely We created you, then shaped you, then said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused to prostrate with the others”*). The comparison that produced the refusal is the structure of envy — another's advantage experienced as one's own diminishment.

**Envy Is Not Admiration, and It Is Not Ambition**

Arabic separates three states that English often blurs. *Ḥasad* is the wish that another's blessing be removed — the pain is at his having it, and the desire is that he stop. *Ghibṭa* is the wish for the like of what he has, without his losing it; the tradition treats this as legitimate and even praiseworthy. Between them lies ordinary ambition, which is directed at a thing rather than at a person.

The Qur'an legislates the difference directly: *“And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties.”* (4:32). The instruction is structural: the craving is redirected from the other person's possession to a request addressed to God. Wanting what another has becomes wanting from the One who gives.

The same word appears in the description of those who oppose the revelation: *“Or do they envy the people for Allah's bounties? Indeed, We have given the descendants of Abraham the Book and wisdom, along with great authority.”* (4:54). Envy there is not a private feeling but a public position — a refusal to accept that a blessing has been given where it was given.

The first sin on earth was the killing of Abel. *“So Cain threatened, ‘I will kill you!’ His brother replied, ‘Allah only accepts ˹the offering˺ of the sincerely devout.’”* (5:27), and the accepted offering was Abel's. The verse records the outcome: *“Yet Cain convinced himself to kill his brother, so he killed him—becoming a loser.”* (5:30). The motive the commentators give is the same one: his brother's acceptance.

**The Evil Eye Is Treated as Real**

The tradition has read this verse against the evil eye from the earliest period, and the reading rests on a Prophetic statement rather than on inference. Abū Hurayrah reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "The effect of an evil eye is a fact" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5740, in the Book of Medicine, in a chapter headed by that claim.

The comprehensive sufficiency of this chapter against both human and unseen hostility is established in a report recorded by at-Tirmidhī (who graded it *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*), an-Nasāʾī, and Ibn Mājah from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī. He stated that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was accustomed to seeking refuge through diverse invocations against the evil eye of jinn and humankind. However, once the *Muʿawwidhatayn* were revealed, he adopted them as his complete, solitary shield, laying aside all other formulas in favor of their divine protection.

**Envy Inside the Community, Not Only Outside It**

The verse is often read as protection against outsiders, and the Qur'an itself supplies the correction. The envy described in al-Baqarah belongs to people who had received a scripture: *“Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them.”* (2:109).

The same sūrah locates the origin of religious division inside a religious community: *“Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them.”* (3:19). Division followed knowledge, and its cause was not ignorance but resentment of where the blessing had landed.
