# Sūrah al-Bayyinah (Chapter 98) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:1

> The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists were not going to desist ˹from disbelief˺ until the clear proof came to them

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence the Exegetes Call Difficult**

The Arabic of this verse is unusually compressed, and the commentators say so openly. *Lam yakun alladhīna kafarū min ahl al-kitāb wa-l-mushrikīna munfakkīna ḥattā ta'tiyahumu al-bayyinah* — literally, those who disbelieved among the People of the Book and the polytheists were not going to be separated, or to desist, until the clear proof came to them. The difficulty is in the verb *munfakk*, which means to be loosened, separated, or to cease from a state. Some read it as ceasing from disbelief, others as ceasing to be left alone. Both readings are grammatically available, and both have been defended. The compression is deliberate: the verse states a condition that held until a specific moment, and the moment is named at the end rather than at the beginning. What the verse is not is obscure in its main claim — something changed when the clear proof arrived.

**Al-Bayyinah: The Word and the Range It Covers**

*Bayyinah* comes from *bayyana*, to make clear or manifest, and it denotes a proof that is evident rather than merely asserted. The Qur'an uses the word widely. God sent His messengers with clear proofs (57:25); Mūsā came with clear proofs (2:87; 7:105); Jesus is described as making things clear (43:63). The word is not confined to this sūrah's context or to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ. That range matters for interpretation, because it allows the verse to be read at two levels at once: the clear proof here is the Qur'an and the Messenger, and the same category covers what was given to earlier communities. The sūrah takes its name from this word, and the naming is accurate — the whole chapter is about what happens when evidence that cannot be mistaken arrives.

**Two Groups Named in One Breath**

The verse addresses the People of the Book and the polytheists together, and the pairing is significant. These were the two religious constituencies of Arabia: communities with scripture and communities without. The Qur'an usually treats them separately, with different arguments for each — to the People of the Book it appeals to their own books, and to the polytheists it argues from creation. Here they are joined because they share a feature: both persisted in a position, and both positions were tested by the same event. The joining is not a claim that the two are identical in belief. It is a claim that they behaved the same way when the evidence arrived, and that the behaviour, not the doctrine, is what the sūrah is examining.

**The Principle Underneath: No Punishment Before a Messenger**

One of the strongest readings of the verse is not about persistence in disbelief but about divine procedure. On this reading, the disbelievers would not be destroyed or punished until the clear proof had reached them, which places the verse inside the Qur'an's consistent rule: We never punish until We have sent a messenger (17:15). The reading has real support. The Qur'an states the principle repeatedly and treats it as a matter of justice rather than of convenience, and it explains why messengers are sent before nations are held to account. On this reading, *were not going to desist* means they would not be abandoned to their fate without warning. The alternative reading — that they would not leave their disbelief until the proof came — is the more common and fits the following verses better, since the sūrah goes on to describe what they did when it arrived. Both readings agree that the arrival of the proof was the decisive moment.

**Reading the Verse as a Statement About Persistence**

The more common reading gives the verse a psychological edge. It says that these groups were settled in their positions and would not have moved from them on their own argument, their own reflection, or their own interests. Movement required something external — a proof so clear that continued refusal became a decision rather than a conclusion. The verse therefore describes a deadlock that only revelation could break, and it explains why the Qur'an so often insists on the clarity of what it brings. The claim is not that people are incapable of thought. It is that a settled community will not unpick its own settlement without being confronted by something it cannot reframe. The clear proof is that confrontation.

**Why the Proof Is Named and Not Described**

The verse says the clear proof came and stops. No content is given, no argument is summarised, no evidence is listed in this verse. The two verses that follow supply the description: a messenger from God, reciting scrolls of utmost purity, containing upright commandments. The separation is a rhetorical choice. Naming the proof first establishes its arrival as a fact; describing it afterwards allows the description to be received as information rather than as defence. The Qur'an frequently asserts before it explains, and the assertion is what gives the explanation its weight. A reader who has accepted that a clear proof arrived reads the description of scrolls and commandments as an account of something real rather than as a claim competing with other claims.

**The Verse as the Thesis of the Sūrah**

Everything in al-Bayyinah follows from this sentence. Verses two and three describe the proof; verse four describes what happened when it arrived; verse five states what had actually been required of those communities all along; verses six to eight give the two outcomes. The sūrah is therefore an argument in six moves, and the first verse is the premise. Its subject is not doctrine but response — what people do when the evidence is no longer disputable. That subject is why the sūrah can move from a statement about persistence to a statement about eternal outcomes in seven verses. The transition is not abrupt once the premise is granted: if a proof is genuinely clear, then continued refusal is genuinely a choice, and a choice of that kind has consequences.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:2

> a messenger from Allah, reciting scrolls of ˹utmost˺ purity

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Proof Identified: A Person and a Text**

The previous verse named the clear proof without describing it. This verse supplies the description, and the description has two parts: a messenger and a written revelation. Neither is sufficient alone. A messenger without a text is a claim; a text without a messenger is a document of unknown origin. The Qur'an's self-presentation requires both, and the verse names them in that order — the person first, then what he recites. The pairing answers the two objections the Quraysh and the People of the Book actually raised: that Muḥammad was an ordinary man, and that his message had no verifiable source. The verse does not argue against either objection here; it states the two facts that together constitute the proof and leaves the argument to the rest of the Qur'an.

**Ṣuḥuf Muṭahharah: Scrolls, and What Purity Means Here**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet or scroll — a written surface rather than a bound book. The Qur'an uses the word for the written records of revelation given to earlier prophets: the scrolls of Ibrāhīm and Mūsā (87:19), and the sheets on which the Torah was written for Mūsā (7:145). *Muṭahharah*, purified, describes them as free from what defiles: from falsehood, from alteration, from the admixture of human invention. The adjective is doing specific work. The Qur'an's charge against earlier scriptures is not that they were false at their origin but that they were changed by human hands (2:79). To call these scrolls purified is to claim that this text has not undergone that process, and the claim is verifiable in a way that claims about lost originals are not.

**The Purity of the Pages Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The description has close parallels. The Qur'an describes itself as being on honoured pages, exalted and purified, in the hands of scribes who are noble and righteous (80:13–16), and it swears by a hidden Book that none touch except the purified (56:77–79). The language is consistent: the revelation exists in a written form, that form is protected, and the protection is described as purification. Al-Bayyinah's phrase sits inside this pattern and adds one thing — that the pages are recited. The combination of writing and recitation is characteristic of this revelation from its first verses, where the command to read was given to a man who could not read and the pen was named as an instrument of divine teaching (96:1–4). The scrolls here are the same instrument in the same service.

**A Messenger From Allah, or the Angel Who Brought the Message**

Most commentators take the messenger to be Muḥammad ﷺ, and the context supports the reading: the following verses describe a figure whose prophethood people divided over, and that is the Prophet. Some read the messenger as Gabriel, on the grounds that the Qur'an describes him as the one who brought the revelation down (26:193). The two readings are not far apart, since Gabriel is the messenger between God and the Prophet and the Prophet is the messenger between God and the people. The majority reading is the more natural in this sentence, because the sūrah is describing the proof that came to the People of the Book and the polytheists, and they encountered a man, not an angel. What both readings share is the insistence that the message has a chain of transmission ending in God, which is the point the phrase *min Allāh* makes.

**Recitation as the Mode of Delivery**

The participle *yatlū* — reciting — describes how the scrolls reach people. They are not deposited somewhere for the interested to consult; they are read aloud, in public, to audiences who may not be literate. The Qur'an's own transmission began this way and continued this way, with memorisation running alongside writing from the start. The choice of verb matters for what the proof consists of. A written document proves something only to those who read it; a recited document proves something to everyone within earshot. The messenger described here is therefore a reader before he is an author, and the proof he carries is available to the illiterate and the scholar on identical terms. That accessibility is part of what makes it a clear proof rather than a specialist one.

**The Report About This Sūrah and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb**

The sūrah has a documented place in the Prophet's own teaching. Anas ibn Mālik reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: Allah has commanded me to recite to you *lam yakun alladhīna kafarū* — this sūrah. Ubayy asked, did He mention me to you by name? He said yes, and Ubayy wept. The report is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 810 and in Musnad Aḥmad. Its significance for this verse is that the sūrah was recited to a named individual on divine instruction, which is the practice described in the verse enacted in the Prophet's own lifetime. A messenger reciting to a listener is not an abstraction here; it is a recorded event, and the listener's weeping is the response the sūrah expects from those who take the proof seriously.

**The Scrolls, and the Written Record This Revelation Produced**

The description of scrolls has a historical counterpart that can be checked. The revelation was written down during the Prophet's lifetime by appointed scribes, on the materials available — palm stalks, flat stones, pieces of leather and parchment — while being memorised in parallel by a large number of Companions. After his death the written material was gathered into a single collection under Abū Bakr, and a standardised copy was produced and distributed under ʿUthmān to prevent the divergence in recitation that had begun to appear in the provinces. The surviving textual tradition descends from that standardisation. The verse's claim about purified scrolls is therefore not merely a theological assertion; it describes a process that left physical results, and the process is documented in the earliest Islamic historical sources. A text described as written and pure was in fact written, collected and preserved, and the collection happened within two decades of the revelation.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:3

> containing upright commandments

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kutub Qayyimah: The Word and Its Sense**

*Qayyimah* is the feminine of *qayyim*, an intensive form from the root *q-w-m*, to stand, to be upright, to maintain. A *qayyim* thing is one that stands straight and keeps other things straight; the word is used of a person who manages and sustains a household. Applied to commandments, it carries both senses: they are themselves straight, and they straighten what they govern. The Qur'an uses the same root for the upright religion in the next verse and for the description of the Qur'an as having no crookedness (18:1). The adjective is therefore not a general term of praise but a technical claim about internal consistency. A book containing upright commandments is one whose commands do not contradict each other and whose demands can actually be met.

**Upright as Free From Contradiction**

The exegetes gloss the phrase as free from error and deviation, distinguishing truth from falsehood without crookedness. The gloss is supported by the Qur'an's own description of itself — that it cannot be proven false from any angle, being a revelation from the All-Wise and Praiseworthy (41:42) — and by the statement that He revealed the Book to His servant and placed no crookedness in it (18:1). The claim is testable in a specific way. A body of law delivered over twenty-three years, in response to changing circumstances, would be expected to contain inconsistencies; the claim is that it does not. The Qur'an challenges its audience to look for them: do they not reflect upon the Qur'an? Had it been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much inconsistency (4:82). The verse's adjective is therefore a claim that has an available method of checking.

**Commandments Rather Than Narratives**

The word *kutub* here is often rendered as books or writings, and the verse specifies their content as commandments. That specification is worth attending to, because the Qur'an contains a great deal that is not commandment — narrative, parable, description of the unseen, argument. To describe the scrolls as containing upright commandments is to identify their operative core. The revelation is not primarily a story or a cosmology; it is a law with a narrative around it. This is why the sūrah's fifth verse can summarise the whole requirement in a single sentence about worship, prayer and alms. The verse is telling the reader what the proof demands rather than what it entertains, and the demand is the part that divides people.

**Uprightness as the Criterion of a Revelation**

The Qur'an measures revelations by their moral content rather than by their antiquity or their miracles. A book whose commands are upright produces upright people; a book whose commands have been altered produces the confusion the Qur'an describes in earlier communities. The criterion is practical, and it is available to anyone who reads the commands. Al-Bayyinah uses it here without elaboration, which suggests the audience was expected to be able to apply it. A listener in seventh-century Arabia knew what his own society required and what the Qur'an required, and the comparison was open to him. The verse is not asking for assent to a mystery; it is pointing at a set of instructions and describing their character.

**What the Verse Claims About Earlier Scriptures**

Read beside the charge of alteration elsewhere in the Qur'an, this verse makes a comparative claim. Earlier revelations were given in a pure state and were subsequently changed by human hands (2:79; 3:78). These scrolls are described as purified and upright in the present tense, which is a claim about their current condition and not only about their origin. The claim is what distinguishes this revelation in the Qur'an's own account: the preservation is part of the proof. God states it plainly elsewhere: indeed, We have sent down the Reminder, and We will surely preserve it (15:9). The verse's two adjectives — upright, in scrolls that are purified — restate that claim in the compressed form the sūrah uses throughout.

**Why Purity and Uprightness Are Named Together**

The two verses describe the scrolls as purified and their contents as upright, and the pairing is not redundant. Purity concerns the text's transmission — that it has not been contaminated by human addition. Uprightness concerns its content — that what it commands is straight. A text could be perfectly preserved and morally worthless, or morally excellent and hopelessly corrupted in transmission. The Qur'an claims both properties, and it needs both for the argument the sūrah is making. A proof that is clear must be legible and it must be sound; a scroll that has been altered is not legible in the relevant sense, and a command that is crooked is not sound. The two adjectives close the two available lines of objection, which is why they appear in adjacent verses.

**How the Claim Can Be Checked**

The verse makes a claim about the internal consistency of a body of commandments, and the Qur'an supplies the method for testing it. Do they not then reflect on the Qur'an? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies (4:82). The challenge is addressed to people who can read the text, and it invites them to look for the specific defect that accumulated legislation usually shows — commands that contradict each other, rules that cannot be followed together, statements that cancel earlier ones. The sūrah's adjective is a claim of exactly this kind, and it is falsifiable in principle. Whatever one concludes about the revelation as a whole, the verse is not asking for credulity; it is pointing at a property of the text that a reader can examine.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:4

> It was not until this clear proof came to the People of the Book that they became divided ˹about his prophethood˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Division After Knowledge, Not Before**

The verse states a sequence, and the sequence is the indictment. The People of the Book were not divided until the clear proof arrived; the division came after. Before the Prophet ﷺ, these communities had a shared expectation — the Qur'an records that they used to ask for victory through the coming messenger against those who disbelieved (2:89). After his arrival, they disagreed about him. The order matters because it removes the most common excuse for division, which is that people lacked information. They had the information; they had been waiting for it; and when it came, they split. The verse is therefore not a lament about disagreement in general. It is a statement about a specific failure that occurred at a specific moment, with the evidence present.

**A Pattern the Qur'an States Repeatedly**

The pattern is not confined to this sūrah. Humanity was one community, then God sent the prophets as bearers of good news and warning, and He sent down with them the Book in truth to judge between people concerning what they differed about — and none differed about it except those who were given it, after the clear proofs had come to them, out of envy among themselves (2:213). We gave them clear proofs from the Command, and they did not divide until after knowledge had come to them, out of rivalry among themselves (45:17). The same statement in three places establishes it as a principle rather than an incident. Revelation does not create division from nothing; it reveals a division that already exists in people's intentions and gives them something to divide over.

**The Reason Given: Envy, Not Evidence**

The Qur'an supplies the cause of the division in the parallel verses, and the cause is not intellectual. In Sūrat al-Baqarah and Sūrat al-Jāthiyah the reason named is envy and rivalry among themselves. The people who divided had the proofs; what they lacked was the willingness to accept a messenger who was not from their own group. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same motive in its account of the rejection: when there came to them that which they recognised, they disbelieved in it (2:89). The verse in al-Bayyinah does not repeat the reason, but it does not need to; the sūrah's audience knew the parallel passages, and the pattern was already established in the revelation they had received. The division is presented as a moral event with a discoverable motive.

**The Command Not to Be Like Them**

The Qur'an draws an explicit lesson for its own community from this pattern. Do not be like those who divided and differed after the clear proofs came to them — for them is a terrible punishment (3:105). The warning is addressed to Muslims, not to the People of the Book, and its placement matters. A community that has received a clear proof is in the same structural position as the communities that divided before it, and the possibility of repeating their behaviour is real rather than theoretical. Al-Bayyinah states the fact of division without drawing the lesson explicitly, but the next verse performs the same function by restating the requirement that had been given all along. The sūrah assumes its reader will make the connection.

**The Report About the Number of Sects, and Its Standing**

The exegetes cite a report here in which the Prophet ﷺ said that the Jews divided into seventy-one sects, the Christians into seventy-two, and this community would divide into seventy-three, all of them in the Fire except one — those who follow what he and his Companions followed. The transmission is documented and so is the dispute over it. Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2641, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr, is graded ḍaʿīf by Dār al-Salām; Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4597, from ʿAwf ibn Mālik, is graded ḥasan by al-Albānī; Sunan Ibn Mājah 3991 carries a ḥasan grading from Dār al-Salām. The grading is not the only difficulty. The clause placing all but one sect in the Fire is the part scholars have contested: Ibn al-Wazīr al-Yamānī held that it meets none of the conditions of validity and called it an addition without an authentic basis, and Ibn Ḥazm rejected the report outright. An honest citation gives the numbers, the grades and the dispute together rather than the number alone. What the Qur'an establishes without any of it is that division follows knowledge, and that the pattern is a warning rather than a comfort. The number of sects is not the point of the verse; the fact of division is.

**Division as a Test Rather Than a Failure of the Proof**

One further consideration keeps the verse from being read as a criticism of revelation. If a clear proof produced uniform agreement in every case, it would function as coercion rather than as evidence, and belief compelled by proof would have no moral content. The Qur'an is explicit that God could have made humanity one community and chose not to: had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community of believers, but they will always choose to differ (11:118). The arrival of a clear proof therefore separates people according to what they already are, which is the function the sūrah's final verses describe. The division recorded in this verse is the mechanism by which the distinction in verses six and seven becomes visible. It is a test with a result, not an accident.

**The Division Inside the Receiving Community**

The verse describes a historical event, and the Qur'an elsewhere turns the same observation into a warning for Muslims. Do not be like those who divided and differed after the clear proofs came to them (3:105). The warning only makes sense if the possibility is real for the community receiving it. Al-Bayyinah states the fact of division without addressing the reader directly, but the structure of the sūrah does the addressing: a community that has received scrolls of purity and upright commandments stands in the same position the People of the Book stood in, and the same outcome is available to it. The verse is therefore not a piece of polemic against other communities so much as a description of a mechanism, and mechanisms do not respect the boundaries between groups.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:5

> even though they were only commanded to worship Allah ˹alone˺ with sincere devotion to Him in all uprightness, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. That is the upright Way

**Expanded Commentary**

**Innamā: The Particle That Restricts**

The verse opens with *innamā*, a particle of restriction: not, or only. Everything else is excluded. The communities addressed here were not given a complex system whose essentials had been lost; they were given four things, and the four are named. The restriction is the verse's argument. Whatever else these communities had accumulated — disputations, sectarian divisions, elaborations of law, claims about status — none of it was part of the command. The particle does the work that a paragraph of criticism would otherwise have to do, and it does it more effectively, because it does not attack the additions so much as decline to recognise them. What was commanded is stated, and everything beyond it is left outside the statement.

**Ikhliṣ: Sincerity as Purification**

*Mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn* — devoting religion sincerely to Him — uses a root meaning to purify or to make something free of admixture. The word *ikhlāṣ* in Islamic usage denotes doing an act for God alone, without the mixture of a second motive. The Qur'an makes it the condition of acceptance: they were commanded only to worship God with sincere devotion to Him, inclining away from falsehood (98:5), and the parallel in Sūrat al-Bayyinah's own vocabulary is the description of the scrolls as purified. The same idea appears in the statement that whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord should do righteous work and associate none in the worship of his Lord (18:110). Sincerity here is not an emotional quality but a structural one: the act has one addressee, not two.

**Ḥunafāʾ: The Word and Its Abrahamic Reference**

*Ḥanīf* derives from a root meaning to incline, and in Qur'anic usage it describes one who inclines away from idolatry toward the oneness of God. The word is attached above all to Ibrāhīm: Ibrāhīm was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but a ḥanīf, submitting, and he was not of the polytheists (3:67); and follow the creed of Ibrāhīm as a ḥanīf (3:95). The Prophet ﷺ is instructed in the same terms: set your face toward the faith as a ḥanīf (10:105; 30:30). The word therefore carries a specific genealogy. To call the required worship ḥanīf is to place it in the line of Ibrāhīm, which is the line all three Abrahamic communities claim. The verse is appealing to a shared ancestry in order to establish a shared requirement.

**Three Acts Named, in a Fixed Order**

Worship, prayer, alms. The sequence is not accidental and it recurs throughout the Qur'an: those who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them (2:3). The order moves from the general to the specific — the orientation of the whole person, then the fixed act of worship, then the fixed act of giving. Prayer concerns the body and time; alms concern property and other people. Together they cover the two resources a human being actually controls. The verse is therefore not listing virtues but naming the two practices by which devotion is measured, and the ordering suggests that the interior orientation comes first and the two practices are its expression.

**Dīn al-Qayyimah: The Upright Religion**

The verse closes by naming what it has described: that is the upright religion. The same root appeared two verses earlier in the description of the commandments as upright, and the repetition ties the two statements together. The scrolls contain upright commandments; the upright commandments are these four. The identification is unusually direct, and it has a consequence for the argument of the sūrah. If the upright religion is worship with sincerity, prayer and alms, then a community that has divided over other matters has divided over something outside the command. The verse does not say this; it simply defines the religion, and the definition does the work.

**What the Verse Says About the Substance of All Revelation**

The verse is phrased in the past tense and refers to earlier communities, but its content is what Islam itself requires. Worship with sincerity, established prayer, paid alms — these are not superseded requirements but the continuing ones. The verse therefore makes a claim about the unity of revelation: the substance given to the People of the Book is the substance given here, and the differences between the communities are not in the requirement. Commentators read the upright religion both as the religion of Ibrāhīm and as the religion with which Muḥammad ﷺ was sent, and the two readings converge rather than compete. What the verse asserts is that the demand has been the same all along, and that the division described in the previous verse was never about the demand.

**Four Requirements as a Test of Any Religious Claim**

The verse gives a compact standard, and the standard can be applied. Whatever a religious tradition claims for itself — antiquity, miraculous origin, institutional authority, numbers of adherents — the verse asks four questions instead: is the worship directed to God alone, is it sincere, is the prayer established, is the alms paid? The questions concern practice and orientation rather than pedigree. The method is characteristic of the Qur'an's arguments, which consistently measure a claim by what it produces in conduct rather than by what it asserts about itself. A tradition that answers these four questions is, in the verse's terms, the upright religion; a tradition that has replaced them with something else has, in the same terms, added to the command rather than kept it.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Answer to the Division**

Verse four described communities that split after the clear proof arrived. This verse explains why the split was never necessary. The requirement had been stated in full and it was short: worship God alone with sincerity, in uprightness, with prayer established and alms paid. Nothing in that list required a community to divide over a messenger's lineage, a change of sanctuary, or a point of law. The division described earlier therefore occurred outside the command, which is what makes it a failure rather than a difficulty. The sūrah's argument is complete at this point: the proof was clear, the requirement was simple, and the disagreement was about neither.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:6

> Indeed, those who disbelieve from the People of the Book and the polytheists will be in the Fire of Hell, to stay there forever. They are the worst of ˹all˺ beings

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict Stated Without Mitigation**

The sūrah has spent five verses establishing what was required and what was given. This verse states the outcome for those who refused both, and it does so in the plainest available form. No condition is attached, no exception is offered, and no mitigating circumstance is entertained. The Qur'an frequently pairs its threats with statements about the breadth of mercy, and elsewhere it does so in the same breath; here it does not, because the verse is the conclusion of an argument that has already established the clarity of the evidence. The severity is a function of the premise. A person who refuses a demand he never understood is in a different position from one who refuses a demand that was made unmistakable, and the sūrah has spent its first five verses making the unmistakability the case.

**Sharr al-Bariyyah: The Superlative of the Created**

*Barīyah* denotes created beings collectively, and *sharr al-bariyyah* is therefore the worst of everything created. The superlative is absolute rather than comparative within a group: not the worst of the People of the Book, and not the worst of the Arabs, but the worst of created things. The exegetes note that the word covers human beings and, on some readings, the jinn as well. The claim is severe and it is specific. The Qur'an does not often rank creatures against each other in this way, and when it does the ranking is tied to a stated reason. The reason here has been given in the preceding verses: a clear proof arrived, a simple requirement was named, and both were refused. The superlative measures the distance between what was offered and what was rejected.

**The Qur'an's Own Parallel Superlative**

The same formulation appears in Sūrat al-Anfāl, and the parallel shows what the ranking is based on. Indeed, the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah are the wilfully deaf and dumb, who do not understand (8:22); and again: the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah are those who persist in disbelief, never to have faith (8:55). In both cases the description is attached to a refusal to use the faculties one has — hearing, speech, understanding. The Qur'an's ranking is therefore not about origin, tribe or capacity. It is about the deliberate non-use of what was given. Al-Bayyinah's verse belongs to the same series, and the series explains the logic: a creature equipped to recognise the truth, presented with it clearly, who refuses it, has failed in the specific function for which he was equipped.

**Why the Two Groups Are Named Again**

The verse repeats the pairing from the first verse — the disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists — and the repetition has a purpose. Between the two mentions, the sūrah has described the proof and the requirement, so the second naming is a naming of people who have now been fully addressed. They are not the same as the groups mentioned in verse one; they are those groups after the evidence arrived and after the requirement was stated. The repetition therefore marks the completion of the procedure: the proof came, the command was restated, and the refusal is now a considered position. This is why the verse can be so absolute. Nothing is left for these groups to plead in the sūrah's own account.

**Khālidīn Fīhā: Permanence**

The verse adds that they will remain in the Fire forever, and the addition is what distinguishes this outcome from punishment in the ordinary sense. Punishment that ends is a correction; punishment that does not end is a state. The Qur'an uses the same phrase for the inhabitants of the Garden, which sets up the symmetry the next verse completes. The permanence is not presented as a fact about God's severity but as a consequence of the finality of the refusal. A person who has refused a clear proof for the whole of a life has not made a mistake that can be corrected by time; the position is settled, and the outcome corresponds to the settledness. The verse states the correspondence without arguing for it.

**The Verse and the Question of Who Is Included**

The verse specifies the disbelievers from the People of the Book, and the specification is important. Not everyone from those communities is included; the qualifier restricts the statement to those who disbelieved. The Qur'an says the opposite about the others in the same register: they are not all alike; among the People of the Book is an upright community who recite the verses of Allah during the night and prostrate (3:113). The verse is therefore a statement about a position rather than about a people, and the distinction has been maintained consistently in the Qur'an's treatment of these communities. What is condemned is the refusal of a clear proof, and that refusal can occur in any community, including the one receiving this sūrah.

**The Verse Against Despair and Against Presumption**

A verse this severe does two things at once, and both are useful. Against despair, it states that the outcome is tied to a refusal, not to a community or a birth. Nobody is condemned in this verse for belonging to a group; the qualifier — those who disbelieve — restricts the statement to a position a person holds, and positions can change while life lasts. Against presumption, it states that the availability of mercy does not remove the consequence of refusal. The sūrah has spent five verses establishing that the proof was clear and the requirement simple, and this verse is what follows from that establishment. The two effects belong together. A reader who takes only the severity from the verse misreads it, and a reader who takes only the opening for change misreads it as well.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:7

> Indeed, those who believe and do good—they are the best of ˹all˺ beings

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counterpart in a Single Verse**

The verse is short, and its brevity is deliberate. What precedes it described the worst of created beings at length, with reasons and with a specified permanence. This verse gives the opposite ranking in ten words, with no elaboration and no description of reward — the reward comes in the verse after. The asymmetry in length is not an accident of style. The negative case required argument, because it had to establish that the refusal was considered rather than ignorant. The positive case requires none, because the criteria are already stated: belief and righteous deeds. The sūrah has spent five verses defining what was required, and this verse simply announces who met it.

**Khayr al-Bariyyah and the Question of Comparison**

The phrase mirrors the one in the previous verse exactly, with the superlative reversed: the best of created beings. The parallelism makes the comparison explicit — the same category of creatures, ranked in two opposite directions, with the difference located entirely in belief and action. The Qur'an rarely uses this superlative of a group, and its use here is a claim about human potential rather than about human achievement. The same creature who can become the worst of created things can become the best, and the distance between the two is covered by conduct rather than by nature. That is the sūrah's central claim, and it is why the two verses are placed side by side without commentary between them.

**The Verified Report: O Best of Creation**

The phrase has a documented use in the Prophet's own speech, and the report clarifies how the superlative functions. Anas ibn Mālik reported that a man came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and said, O best of creation. The Prophet replied: that is Ibrāhīm, peace be upon him. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2369. The answer is instructive in two ways. It deflects the title from himself, which is consistent with his habitual practice; and it locates the superlative in a person distinguished by submission rather than by achievement. Read with this sūrah, the report shows that the ranking is not a competition among human beings but a description of a rank that God assigns on the basis of faith and conduct. The verse's plural — those who believe and do good — makes the same point structurally: the rank is open, not reserved.

**Believers and Angels: The Question the Exegetes Raised**

Some commentators argued from this verse that righteous human beings are superior to the angels, on the grounds that the superlative is unrestricted and includes all created beings. Others restricted the comparison to the creatures of this world. The dispute is real but its stakes are lower than they appear. The verse is not making a claim about angelology; it is ranking human outcomes. What the unrestricted superlative does establish is that a believing, righteous human being occupies a rank the verse does not qualify, and the Qur'an elsewhere supports the dignity of that position: the angels were commanded to prostrate to Adam, and they did (2:34). The argument from the verse should be kept where the verse keeps it — as a statement about what a human being can become.

**Deeds Named Alongside Faith, Again**

The formula *alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* appears here as it does throughout the Qur'an, and its repetition in this sūrah is pointed. Verse five defined the requirement as worship with sincerity, prayer and alms. This verse describes those who met it as believers who did good, which is the same requirement in summary form. The pairing excludes two errors: a faith that produces no action, and an action that is not rooted in faith. The sūrah's argument depends on both halves. The groups condemned in the previous verse had the outward forms of religion and refused the demand; the group praised here has the belief and the conduct together. The verse is therefore not a comfort but a restatement of the criteria.

**The Symmetry Between Verses Six and Seven**

The two verses form a pair, and the pairing is the sūrah's structure in miniature. Same subject — created beings. Same grammatical form — a superlative with a definite article. Same opening particle — *inna*. The only differences are the direction of the ranking and the description of the people ranked. Nothing else distinguishes them, which means the sūrah has isolated a single variable: what a person believes and does. Lineage, community, era and prior religious affiliation are all excluded from the comparison, and their exclusion is the point. The two verses together state that the ranking of created beings runs on one axis, and that the axis is available to anyone.

**A Rank Open to Everyone in the Sūrah's Audience**

The verse describes a group, not an individual, and the group is defined by two acts available to anyone. No lineage is mentioned, no era, no community of origin, no prior religious affiliation. A person from the People of the Book who believes and does good is inside the description; a person from the polytheists who does the same is inside it; a Muslim whose belief produces no good deeds is outside it. The openness of the rank is the sūrah's answer to the divisions described in verse four. The communities divided over who belonged to God; this verse states the criterion by which belonging is actually determined, and the criterion contains none of the terms the communities were arguing about.

**Why the Verse Names Deeds and Not Only Belief**

The verse could have praised belief alone, and the Qur'an elsewhere speaks of faith without adding the second clause. Here it does not, and the addition is pointed. The communities criticised in the previous verses were not atheists; they held beliefs, they had scriptures, and they claimed a covenant. What the verse requires beyond belief is action, and the sūrah has already defined the action in its fifth verse. The pairing closes the avenue of a faith that consists of affiliation. A person who belongs to the right group and does nothing is not inside this description, and a person outside every group who believes and acts is.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:8

> Their reward with their Lord will be Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. This is ˹only˺ for those in awe of their Lord

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jannāt ʿAdn: Gardens of Residence**

*ʿAdn* denotes settling and permanent residence, and the phrase describes gardens as a dwelling rather than a visit. The Qur'an uses the term in several places, including the promise of splendid homes in the Gardens of Eternity (9:72). The word choice answers a question the verse does not raise: whether the reward is temporary. It is not. The same permanence denied to the Fire's inhabitants in verse six is granted here, and the symmetry is exact — *khālidīna fīhā abadan*, remaining in it forever, in both cases. The verse is describing a state of settled residence, with the image of rivers flowing beneath, which in an Arabian context is the picture of a place where nothing needs to be carried in and nothing runs out.

**The Reward Is With Their Lord**

The phrase *jazāʾuhum ʿinda rabbihim* — their reward is with their Lord — locates the reward in relation to God rather than in a place. The preposition *ʿinda* indicates presence, and its use here is significant: the reward is held by God, not stored somewhere independent of Him. The Qur'an makes a related point about the durability of what is with God: what is with you runs out, but what is with God remains (16:96). A reward held by God cannot be lost, diminished, or taken by another party. The verse's phrasing therefore adds a security to the promise that a description of gardens alone would not carry, and it prepares for the statement about pleasure that follows, since the reward held with God turns out to include God's own attitude toward its recipients.

**Riḍwān: Pleasure in Both Directions**

The verse states a mutual condition: God is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. The Qur'an gives the same formula elsewhere: Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him (5:119), and the return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing (89:28). The mutuality is the summit of the promise. A reward consisting only of gardens would leave the recipient in possession of things; a reward that includes God's pleasure leaves him in a relationship. The Qur'an ranks the two explicitly: and above all, the pleasure of Allah — that is the ultimate triumph (9:72). Al-Bayyinah's verse places the pleasure before the closing condition rather than after the description of gardens, which gives it the emphasis the ranking requires.

**Why Mutual Pleasure Is the Summit**

The mutuality answers something that a one-sided pleasure would not. God's pleasure with a person is a verdict; the person's pleasure with God is a state of the heart that has no remaining complaint. The combination describes a relationship in which nothing is unresolved — no grievance on either side, no unmet expectation, no residue of the struggle that characterised the life before. The Qur'an describes the believers' condition in the Garden in similar terms: We will remove whatever resentment is in their breasts (7:43). The verse's two clauses are therefore not a repetition but a completion. Each side is at rest with the other, and the rest is what the verse identifies as the reward's highest component.

**The Closing Condition: For Those in Awe of Their Lord**

The verse ends by restricting the promise: that is for whoever feared his Lord. The restriction is the sūrah's final word on who the reward belongs to, and it returns to the vocabulary of verse five — devotion, uprightness, the establishment of prayer. *Khāfa rabbahu* describes a person who stood in awe of God, and the awe is what produced the conduct described earlier. The placement of the condition at the end is significant. After the description of gardens, rivers, permanence and mutual pleasure, the verse closes not with an encouragement but with a qualification. The promise is real and it is bounded, and the boundary is the same one the sūrah drew at the start: a person who took the clear proof seriously and lived accordingly.

**The Sūrah's Ending and the Path Back to Its Beginning**

Al-Bayyinah began with a statement about people who would not desist until the clear proof arrived, and it ends with a statement about those who feared their Lord. The distance between the two is the sūrah's whole subject. Nothing in the chapter suggests that the proof was insufficient or that the requirement was unclear; verses two, three and five establish the opposite. What the chapter examines is the response, and it divides humanity by that response alone. The final verse names the quality that produced the good outcome, and the naming is the sūrah's last instruction: the difference between the best and the worst of created beings was not knowledge, since both had it, but what awe did with the knowledge in the ones who had it.

**Rivers Beneath the Gardens: The Image and Its Context**

The phrase *tajrī min tahtihā al-anhār* — rivers flowing beneath it — recurs throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden, and its repetition is not filler. For an audience in an arid region, a garden with running water beneath it is the picture of a place that does not depend on rain, on caravans or on labour. Everything that made life precarious in Arabia has been removed from the image. The verse pairs that image with permanence — to stay there forever — and then with something neither image nor permanence can supply: mutual pleasure with God. The structure moves from provision, to duration, to relationship, and the movement indicates a ranking. The rivers are the least of what is promised here, and they are mentioned first because they are the part a seventh-century listener could picture.
