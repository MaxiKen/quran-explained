# Sūrah al-Bayyinah (Chapter 98) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Clear Proof and the Best of Creation**

Al-Bayyinah, "The Clear Proof"—also titled classically as *Lam Yakun*, *Al-Bariyyah*, or *Al-Qayyimah*—is the ninety-eighth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 8 verses of early Medinan revelation. It takes its name from verses 1 and 4, which declare that the factions of disbelief among the People of the Scripture and the polytheists were not going to depart from their ways until there arrived to them *Al-Bayyinah* (The Clear Proof): *“A Messenger from Allah, reciting purified scriptures (*ṣuḥufan muṭahharah*), in which are correct and valuable writings (*kutubun qayyimah*)”* (98:2–3). Sūrah al-Bayyinah is celebrated for containing the foundational constitutional definition of true, primordial religion (*Dīn al-Qayyimah*) in verse 5, and delivers the ultimate, stark of being judgment on human character: designating those who reject truth as *Sharru al-Bariyyah* (The Worst of All Creation), and those who believe and work righteousness as *Khayru al-Bariyyah* (The Best of All Creation).

**The Structural Architecture and the Core of Religion**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Bayyinah moves across three distinct movements:

1. Verse 5 expresses the eternal, universal charter of all prophetic faith across history: *“And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, [being] sincere to Him in religion, inclining to truth (*ḥunafā'*), and to establish prayer and to give Zakat. And that is the correct, upright religion (*Dīn al-Qayyimah*)!”*

3. The Two Destinies of Creation (6–8): Contrasts the two ultimate moral tiers of the universe: - *The Worst of Creation* (6): *“Indeed, those who disbelieve among the People of the Scripture and the polytheists will be in the fire of Hell, abiding eternally in it. Those are the worst of creatures (*sharru al-bariyyah*)!”* - *The Best of Creation* (7–8): *“Indeed, those who have believed and done righteous deeds—those are the best of creatures (*khayru al-bariyyah*)!”* Their reward is the eternal Gardens of Eden beneath which rivers flow, crowned with the mutual divine pleasure: *“Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. That is for whoever has feared / held awe of his Lord!”*

In prophetic biography, Sūrah al-Bayyinah is immortalized by an extraordinary, tearfully intimate historical event recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (4960) and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (799) by Anas ibn Mālik.

## Verse 98:1

> The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists were not going to desist ˹from disbelief˺ until the clear proof came to them

**A Sentence the Exegetes Call Difficult**

The Arabic of this verse is unusually compressed, and the commentators say so openly. *Lam yakun alladhīna kafarū min ahl al-kitāb wa-l-mushrikīna munfakkīna ḥattā ta'tiyahumu al-bayyinah* — literally, those who disbelieved among the People of the Book and the polytheists were not going to be separated, or to desist, until the clear proof came to them. The difficulty is in the verb *munfakk*, which means to be loosened, separated, or to cease from a state. Some read it as ceasing from disbelief, others as ceasing to be left alone. Both readings are grammatically available, and both have been defended. The compression is deliberate: the verse states a condition that held until a specific moment, and the moment is named at the end rather than at the beginning. What the verse is not is obscure in its main claim — something changed when the clear proof arrived.

**Al-Bayyinah: The Word and the Range It Covers**

*Bayyinah* comes from *bayyana*, to make clear or manifest, and it denotes a proof that is evident rather than merely asserted. The Qur'an uses the word widely. God sent His messengers with clear proofs (57:25); Mūsā came with clear proofs (2:87; 7:105); Jesus is described as making things clear (43:63). The word is not confined to this sūrah's context or to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ. That range matters for interpretation, because it allows the verse to be read at two levels at once: the clear proof here is the Qur'an and the Messenger, and the same category covers what was given to earlier communities. The sūrah takes its name from this word, and the naming is accurate — the whole chapter is about what happens when evidence that cannot be mistaken arrives.

**The Principle Underneath: No Punishment Before a Messenger**

One of the strongest readings of the verse is not about persistence in disbelief but about divine procedure. On this reading, the disbelievers would not be destroyed or punished until the clear proof had reached them, which places the verse inside the Qur'an's consistent rule: We never punish until We have sent a messenger (17:15). The reading has real support. The Qur'an states the principle repeatedly and treats it as a matter of justice rather than of convenience, and it explains why messengers are sent before nations are held to account. On this reading, *were not going to desist* means they would not be abandoned to their fate without warning. The alternative reading — that they would not leave their disbelief until the proof came — is the more common and fits the following verses better, since the sūrah goes on to describe what they did when it arrived. Both readings agree that the arrival of the proof was the decisive moment.

## Verse 98:2

> a messenger from Allah, reciting scrolls of ˹utmost˺ purity

**Ṣuḥuf Muṭahharah: Scrolls, and What Purity Means Here**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet or scroll — a written surface rather than a bound book. The Qur'an uses the word for the written records of revelation given to earlier prophets: the scrolls of Ibrāhīm and Mūsā (87:19), and the sheets on which the Torah was written for Mūsā (7:145). *Muṭahharah*, purified, describes them as free from what defiles: from falsehood, from alteration, from the admixture of human invention. The adjective is doing specific work. The Qur'an's charge against earlier scriptures is not that they were false at their origin but that they were changed by human hands (2:79). To call these scrolls purified is to claim that this text has not undergone that process, and the claim is verifiable in a way that claims about lost originals are not.

**The Purity of the Pages Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The description has close parallels. The Qur'an describes itself as being on honoured pages, exalted and purified, in the hands of scribes who are noble and righteous (80:13–16), and it swears by a hidden Book that none touch except the purified (56:77–79). The language is consistent: the revelation exists in a written form, that form is protected, and the protection is described as purification. Al-Bayyinah's phrase sits inside this pattern and adds one thing — that the pages are recited. The combination of writing and recitation is characteristic of this revelation from its first verses, where the command to read was given to a man who could not read and the pen was named as an instrument of divine teaching (96:1–4). The scrolls here are the same instrument in the same service.

**A Messenger From Allah, or the Angel Who Brought the Message**

Most commentators take the messenger to be Muḥammad ﷺ, and the context supports the reading: the following verses describe a figure whose prophethood people divided over, and that is the Prophet. Some read the messenger as Gabriel, on the grounds that the Qur'an describes him as the one who brought the revelation down (26:193). The two readings are not far apart, since Gabriel is the messenger between God and the Prophet and the Prophet is the messenger between God and the people. The majority reading is the more natural in this sentence, because the sūrah is describing the proof that came to the People of the Book and the polytheists, and they encountered a man, not an angel. What both readings share is the insistence that the message has a chain of transmission ending in God, which is the point the phrase *min Allāh* makes.

**The Report About This Sūrah and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb**

The sūrah has a documented place in the Prophet's own teaching. Anas ibn Mālik reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: Allah has commanded me to recite to you *lam yakun alladhīna kafarū* — this sūrah. Ubayy asked, did He mention me to you by name? He said yes, and Ubayy wept. The report is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 810 and in Musnad Aḥmad. Its significance for this verse is that the sūrah was recited to a named individual on divine instruction, which is the practice described in the verse enacted in the Prophet's own lifetime. A messenger reciting to a listener is not an abstraction here; it is a recorded event, and the listener's weeping is the response the sūrah expects from those who take the proof seriously.

## Verse 98:3

> containing upright commandments

**Kutub Qayyimah: The Word and Its Sense**

*Qayyimah* is the feminine of *qayyim*, an intensive form from the root *q-w-m*, to stand, to be upright, to maintain. A *qayyim* thing is one that stands straight and keeps other things straight; the word is used of a person who manages and sustains a household. Applied to commandments, it carries both senses: they are themselves straight, and they straighten what they govern. The Qur'an uses the same root for the upright religion in the next verse and for the description of the Qur'an as having no crookedness (18:1). The adjective is therefore not a general term of praise but a technical claim about internal consistency. A book containing upright commandments is one whose commands do not contradict each other and whose demands can actually be met.

**Upright as Free From Contradiction**

The exegetes gloss the phrase as free from error and deviation, distinguishing truth from falsehood without crookedness. The gloss is supported by the Qur'an's own description of itself — that it cannot be proven false from any angle, being a revelation from the All-Wise and Praiseworthy (41:42) — and by the statement that He revealed the Book to His servant and placed no crookedness in it (18:1). The claim is testable in a specific way. A body of law delivered over twenty-three years, in response to changing circumstances, would be expected to contain inconsistencies; the claim is that it does not. The Qur'an challenges its audience to look for them: do they not reflect upon the Qur'an? Had it been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much inconsistency (4:82). The verse's adjective is therefore a claim that has an available method of checking.

**What the Verse Claims About Earlier Scriptures**

Read beside the charge of alteration elsewhere in the Qur'an, this verse makes a comparative claim. Earlier revelations were given in a pure state and were later changed by human hands (2:79; 3:78). These scrolls are described as purified and upright in the present tense, which is a claim about their current condition and not only about their origin. The claim is what distinguishes this revelation in the Qur'an's own account: the preservation is part of the proof. God states it plainly elsewhere: indeed, We have sent down the Reminder, and We will surely preserve it (15:9). The verse's two adjectives — upright, in scrolls that are purified — restate that claim in the compressed form the sūrah uses throughout.

**How the Claim Can Be Checked**

The verse makes a claim about the internal consistency of a body of commandments, and the Qur'an supplies the method for testing it. Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies (4:82). The challenge is addressed to people who can read the text, and it invites them to look for the specific defect that accumulated legislation usually shows — commands that contradict each other, rules that cannot be followed together, statements that cancel earlier ones. The sūrah's adjective is a claim of exactly this kind, and it is falsifiable in principle. Whatever one concludes about the revelation as a whole, the verse is not asking for credulity; it is pointing at a property of the text that a reader can examine.

## Verse 98:4

> It was not until this clear proof came to the People of the Book that they became divided ˹about his prophethood˺—

**Division After Knowledge, Not Before**

The verse states a sequence, and the sequence is the indictment. The People of the Book were not divided until the clear proof arrived; the division came after. Before the Prophet ﷺ, these communities had a shared expectation — the Qur'an records that they used to ask for victory through the coming messenger against those who disbelieved (2:89). After his arrival, they disagreed about him. The order matters because it removes the most common excuse for division, which is that people lacked information.

**A Pattern the Qur'an States Repeatedly**

The pattern is not confined to this sūrah. Humanity was one community, then God sent the prophets as bearers of good news and warning. He sent down with them the Book in truth to judge between people about what they differed about — and none differed about it except those who were given it, after the clear proofs had come to them, out of envy among themselves (2:213). We gave them clear proofs from the Command, and they did not divide until after knowledge had come to them, out of rivalry among themselves (45:17). The same statement in three places establishes it as a principle rather than an incident. Revelation does not create division from nothing; it reveals a division that already exists in people's intentions and gives them something to divide over.

**The Report About the Number of Sects, and Its Standing**

The exegetes cite a report here in which the Prophet ﷺ said that the Jews divided into seventy-one sects, the Christians into seventy-two, and this community would divide into seventy-three, all of them in the Fire except one — those who follow what he and his Companions followed. The transmission is documented and so is the dispute over it. Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2641, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr, is graded ḍaʿīf by Dār al-Salām; Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4597, from ʿAwf ibn Mālik, is graded ḥasan by al-Albānī; Sunan Ibn Mājah 3991 carries a ḥasan grading from Dār al-Salām. The grading is not the only difficulty. The clause placing all but one sect in the Fire is the part scholars have contested: Ibn al-Wazīr al-Yamānī held that it meets none of the conditions of validity and called it an addition without an authentic basis, and Ibn Ḥazm rejected the report outright. An honest citation gives the numbers, the grades and the dispute together rather than the number alone. What the Qur'an establishes without any of it is that division follows knowledge, and that the pattern is a warning rather than a comfort. The number of sects is not the point of the verse; the fact of division is.

Do not be like those who divided and differed after the clear proofs came to them — for them is a terrible punishment (3:105).

The Qur'an is explicit that God could have made humanity one community and chose not to: had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community of believers, but they will always choose to differ (11:118).

## Verse 98:5

> even though they were only commanded to worship Allah ˹alone˺ with sincere devotion to Him in all uprightness, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. That is the upright Way

**Ikhliṣ: Sincerity as Purification**

*Mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn* — devoting religion sincerely to Him — uses a root meaning to purify or to make something free of admixture. The word *ikhlāṣ* in Islamic usage denotes doing an act for God alone, without the mixture of a second motive. The Qur'an makes it the condition of acceptance: they were commanded only to worship God with sincere devotion to Him, inclining away from falsehood (98:5), and the parallel in Sūrat al-Bayyinah's own vocabulary is the description of the scrolls as purified. The same idea appears in the statement that whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord should do righteous work and associate none in the worship of his Lord (18:110). Sincerity here is not an emotional quality but a structural one: the act has one addressee, not two.

**Innamā: The Particle That Restricts**

The verse opens with *innamā*, a particle of restriction: not, or only. Everything else is excluded. The communities addressed here were not given a complex system whose essentials had been lost; they were given four things, and the four are named. The restriction is the verse's argument. Whatever else these communities had accumulated — disputations, sectarian divisions, elaborations of law, claims about status — none of it was part of the command. The particle does the work that a paragraph of criticism would otherwise have to do, and it does it more effectively, because it does not attack the additions so much as decline to recognise them.

**Ḥunafāʾ: The Word and Its Abrahamic Reference**

*Ḥanīf* derives from a root meaning to incline, and in Qur'anic usage it describes one who inclines away from idolatry toward the oneness of God. The word is attached above all to Ibrāhīm: Ibrāhīm was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but a ḥanīf, submitting, and he was not of the polytheists (3:67); and follow the creed of Ibrāhīm as a ḥanīf (3:95). The Prophet ﷺ is instructed in the same terms: set your face toward the faith as a ḥanīf (10:105; 30:30). The word therefore carries a specific genealogy. To call the required worship ḥanīf is to place it in the line of Ibrāhīm, which is the line all three Abrahamic communities claim. The verse is appealing to a shared ancestry to establish a shared requirement.

**Three Acts Named, in a Fixed Order**

Worship, prayer, alms. The sequence is not accidental and it recurs throughout the Qur'an: those who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them (2:3). The order moves from the general to the specific — the orientation of the whole person, then the fixed act of worship, then the fixed act of giving. Prayer concerns the body and time; alms concern property and other people. Together they cover the two resources a human being actually controls. The verse is therefore not listing virtues but naming the two practices by which devotion is measured, and the ordering suggests that the interior orientation comes first and the two practices are its expression.

## Verse 98:6

> Indeed, those who disbelieve from the People of the Book and the polytheists will be in the Fire of Hell, to stay there forever. They are the worst of ˹all˺ beings

**Sharr al-Bariyyah: The Superlative of the Created**

*Barīyah* denotes created beings collectively, and *sharr al-bariyyah* is therefore the worst of everything created. The superlative is absolute rather than comparative within a group: not the worst of the People of the Book, and not the worst of the Arabs, but the worst of created things. The exegetes note that the word covers human beings and, on some readings, the jinn as well. The claim is severe and it is specific. The Qur'an does not often rank creatures against each other in this way, and when it does the ranking is tied to a stated reason. The reason here has been given in the preceding verses: a clear proof arrived, a simple requirement was named, and both were refused. The superlative measures the distance between what was offered and what was rejected.

**The Qur'an's Own Parallel Superlative**

The same formulation appears in Sūrat al-Anfāl, and the parallel shows what the ranking is based on. Indeed, the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah are the wilfully deaf and dumb, who do not understand (8:22); and again: the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah are those who persist in disbelief, never to have faith (8:55). In both cases the description is attached to a refusal to use the faculties one has — hearing, speech, understanding. The Qur'an's ranking is therefore not about origin, tribe or capacity. It is about the deliberate non-use of what was given. Al-Bayyinah's verse belongs to the same series, and the series explains the logic: a creature equipped to recognise the truth, presented with it clearly, who refuses it, has failed in the specific function for which he was equipped.

**The Verse and the Question of Who Is Included**

The verse specifies the disbelievers from the People of the Book, and the specification is important. Not everyone from those communities is included; the qualifier restricts the statement to those who disbelieved. The Qur'an says the opposite about the others in the same register: they are not all alike; among the People of the Book is an upright community who recite the verses of Allah during the night and prostrate (3:113). The verse is therefore a statement about a position rather than about a people, and the distinction has been maintained consistently in the Qur'an's treatment of these communities. What is condemned is the refusal of a clear proof, and that refusal can occur in any community, including the one receiving this sūrah.

## Verse 98:7

> Indeed, those who believe and do good—they are the best of ˹all˺ beings

**The Verified Report: O Best of Creation**

The phrase has a documented use in the Prophet's own speech, and the report clarifies how the superlative functions. Anas ibn Mālik reported that a man came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and said, O best of creation. The Prophet replied: that is Ibrāhīm, peace be upon him. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2369. The answer is instructive in two ways. It deflects the title from himself, which is consistent with his habitual practice; and it locates the superlative in a person distinguished by submission rather than by achievement. Read with this sūrah, the report shows that the ranking is not a competition among human beings but a description of a rank that God assigns on the basis of faith and conduct. The verse's plural — those who believe and do good — makes the same point structurally: the rank is open, not reserved.

**Believers and Angels: The Question the Exegetes Raised**

Some commentators argued from this verse that righteous human beings are superior to the angels, on the grounds that the superlative is unrestricted and includes all created beings. Others restricted the comparison to the creatures of this world. The dispute is real but its stakes are lower than they appear. The verse is not making a claim about angelology; it is ranking human outcomes. What the unrestricted superlative does establish is that a believing, righteous human being occupies a rank the verse does not qualify, and the Qur'an elsewhere supports the dignity of that position: the angels were commanded to prostrate to Adam, and they did (2:34). The argument from the verse should be kept where the verse keeps it — as a statement about what a human being can become.

## Verse 98:8

> Their reward with their Lord will be Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. This is ˹only˺ for those in awe of their Lord

**Jannāt ʿAdn: Gardens of Residence**

*ʿAdn* denotes settling and permanent residence, and the phrase describes gardens as a dwelling rather than a visit. The Qur'an uses the term in several places, including the promise of splendid homes in the Gardens of Eternity (9:72). The word choice answers a question the verse does not raise: whether the reward is temporary. It is not. The same permanence denied to the Fire's inhabitants in verse six is granted here, and the symmetry is exact — *khālidīna fīhā abadan*, remaining in it forever, in both cases. The verse is describing a state of settled residence, with the image of rivers flowing beneath, which in an Arabian context is the picture of a place where nothing needs to be carried in and nothing runs out.

**The Reward Is With Their Lord**

The phrase *jazāʾuhum ʿinda rabbihim* — their reward is with their Lord — locates the reward in relation to God rather than in a place. The preposition *ʿinda* indicates presence, and its use here is significant: the reward is held by God, not stored somewhere independent of Him. The Qur'an makes a related point about the durability of what is with God: what is with you runs out, but what is with God remains (16:96). A reward held by God cannot be lost, diminished, or taken by another party. The verse's phrasing therefore adds a security to the promise that a description of gardens alone would not carry, and it prepares for the statement about pleasure that follows, since the reward held with God turns out to include God's own attitude toward its recipients.

**Riḍwān: Pleasure in Both Directions**

The verse states a mutual condition: God is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. The Qur'an gives the same formula elsewhere: Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him (5:119), and the return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing (89:28). The mutuality is the summit of the promise. A reward consisting only of gardens would leave the recipient in possession of things; a reward that includes God's pleasure leaves him in a relationship. The Qur'an ranks the two explicitly: and above all, the pleasure of Allah — that is the ultimate triumph (9:72). Al-Bayyinah's verse places the pleasure before the closing condition rather than after the description of gardens, which gives it the emphasis the ranking requires.

**Why Mutual Pleasure Is the Summit**

The mutuality answers something that an one-sided pleasure would not. God's pleasure with a person is a verdict; the person's pleasure with God is a state of the heart that has no remaining complaint. The combination describes a relationship in which nothing is unresolved — no grievance on either side, no unmet expectation, no residue of the struggle that characterised the life before. The Qur'an describes the believers' condition in the Garden in similar terms: We will remove whatever resentment is in their breasts (7:43). The verse's two clauses are therefore not a repetition but a completion. Each side is at rest with the other, and the rest is what the verse identifies as the reward's highest component.
