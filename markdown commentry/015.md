# Sūrah al-Ḥijr (Chapter 15) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Taunts of Madness and the Consolation of the Prophet**

The historical context of al-Ḥijr reflects the brazen insolence of the Qurayshi aristocracy, who dismissed the Prophet's warnings as hallucinations and ridiculed his prophetic office, crying: *“O you upon whom the message has been sent down, indeed you are mad! Why do you not bring us the angels if you are of the truthful?”* (15:6–7). The sūrah addresses this hostility with majestic reassurance. It reminds the Prophet ﷺ that no messenger ever walked the earth without being subjected to mockery (*“There never came to them a messenger except that they used to mock him,”* 15:11), and that the day will inevitably arrive when those who denied will desperately wish they had surrendered to Islam (15:2 — *“˹The day will come when˺ the disbelievers will certainly wish they had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). The delay in divine intervention is not impotence, but the unfolding of a pre-measured decree; every community has a known, determined term (*kitābum maʿlūm*) which can neither be anticipated nor delayed.

**The Structural Unfolding of the Chapter**

The movement of Sūrah al-Ḥijr proceeds from cosmic defense to primordial drama, and from historical ruins to devotional serenity. The opening movement (1–25) establishes the incorruptibility of revelation, shows the heavens adorned with constellations (*burūj*) and protected by shooting stars from eavesdropping devils, and displays the earth balanced with towering mountains and life-giving winds that fertilize the clouds. The second movement (26–48) stages the primeval creation of humanity: God fashioning Adam from sounding clay altered from black mud (*ṣalṣāl min ḥama'in masnūn*), breathing into him of His Spirit, and commanding the angels to prostrate. It dramatizes the haughty rebellion of Iblīs, who claims superiority based on fire, his expulsion from the divine presence, and his sworn oath to adorn vanity on earth and mislead humanity—except the sincere, purified servants (*ʿibādukumu al-mukhlaṣīn*), over whom he has no authority. The third movement (49–84) transitions into historical validation: Abraham receiving the angels who bring news of a righteous son and announce the imminent annihilation of the people of Sodom; the catastrophic morning blast and raining stones that buried the towns of Lot; the destruction of the forest dwellers (*Aṣḥāb al-Aykah*); and the tragic fate of the stonemasons of al-Ḥijr (Thamūd), whose engineered mountain fortresses could not avail them when the screaming blast (*al-ṣayḥah*) seized them at sunrise. The closing movement (85–99) returns to the Prophet's heart with exquisite tenderness: reminding him of the cosmic purpose of creation, bestowing upon him the *Seven Oft-Repeated Verses* and the Great Qur'an (15:87 — *“We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran”*), commanding him to proclaim truth openly without fear (*fa-ṣdaʿ bi-mā tu'mar*, 15:94 — *“So proclaim what you have been commanded, and turn away from the polytheists”*), comforting him for the constriction he feels in his breast from their insults (15:97 — *“We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say”*). Providing the immortal divine prescription: *“So exalt [Allah] with praise of your Lord and be of those who prostrate, and worship your Lord until there comes to you the certainty [of death]”* (15:98–99).

## Verse 15:1

> Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran

**What "Clear" Commits the Book To**

*Mubīn* comes from a root that carries both being clear and making clear, so the word can describe a text that is plain in itself and a text that exposes what is true. The Qur'an uses the same adjective for the Book in several places with the two nouns traded back and forth: alif lām rāʾ opens 12:1 with *“These are the verses of the clear Book.”* (12:1), Ṭā Sīn opens 27:1 with *“These are the verses of the Quran. The clear Book.”* (27:1), and 36:69 closes the exchange by saying *“This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran”* (36:69). The attribute travels between the two titles because it belongs to the revelation rather than to one of its names. Clarity here is not a boast about style. It is a claim about content: the Book separates what is true from what is not, and it does so in ordinary Arabic, which is the language its first audience spoke and the reason the demand it makes is a fair one. Six sūrahs in a row — 10 through 15 — open with these letters and with a statement about revelation, and al-Ḥijr follows the sequence with its own two titles. The Qur'an takes the same line elsewhere, as in *“This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”* (2:2), where the guidance is stated as a property of the text rather than as a conclusion the reader must first reach. The opening of al-Ḥijr is a credential, and a credential is worth nothing unless the thing it certifies is then produced.

**The Same Words, With the Emphasis Moved**

Reading the neighbouring openings side by side shows the flexibility of the formula and, with it, what each sūrah chose to stress. Sūrah Yūnus opens with *“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom.”* (10:1). Sūrah Hūd opens with *“˹This is˺ a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained”* (11:1); Sūrah Ibrāhīm opens by announcing *“˹This is˺ a Book which We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ so that you may lead people out of darkness and into light”* (14:1). Wisdom, perfection and explanation, light — each sūrah puts a different weight on the same small sentence, and al-Ḥijr chooses clarity. The emphasis fits what comes later, where the men who rejected the message are answered not with a new sign on demand but with an argument about what they already had in front of them. Clarity is the one quality that makes that answer available. It also carries a warning: what is plain cannot be pleaded as obscure.

**One Revelation in Two Media**

The two titles are not synonyms with the same shading. *Kitāb* names the revelation as something written, and *qurʾān* names it as something recited; the root *qaraʾa* means to read aloud, and the Book's first command is built on it, *“Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—”* (96:1). From the beginning, then, the message has moved through two channels, a written page and a recited voice, and the Muslim community has never treated either as optional. The written form preserves the exact wording across generations, so that no one can quietly adjust it; the recited form carries it into memory, so that the loss of every manuscript would not end it. 2:185 ties the Quran to the month of its revelation as a guide for humanity, and 72:1 records a group of jinn listening to what the translation calls *“a wondrous recitation”* (72:1). Clarity belongs to both forms. A text that is memorised and recited by thousands at once, in a language still alive, cannot easily be argued to have been garbled, and the verse's plain claim about clarity is therefore a claim about public evidence as much as about style.

## Verse 15:2

> ˹The day will come when˺ the disbelievers will certainly wish they had submitted ˹to Allah˺

**A Wish That Has Nothing Left to Change**

The Arabic opens with the particle *rubba* joined to *mā*, a construction the grammarians classify as a device for frequency: many a time, and then again. What follows is the verb of wishing, *yawadda*, and its object is a state in the past — that they had been submitters. Wishing of this kind has no future in it. A person can wish for what lies ahead, but the wish in this verse reaches backward toward something already decided, and Arabic has a specific form for exactly that regret: the *law* of longing, *law kānū muslimīn*, if only they had surrendered. The Qur'an uses the same form on the tongues of the condemned elsewhere: *“Oh! If only we had obeyed Allah and obeyed the Messenger!”* (33:66), and the followers who beg for one more try say *“If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us.”* (2:167). Wishes of this shape are admissions rather than requests, and they arrive only when the power to act has already been taken away.

**When the Wish Is Made**

Al-Kalabī places the moment at death, when a person sees what awaits him; others place it on the Day of Judgment, when the books are opened. The wording of the verse settles neither, and the restraint looks intentional. Regret is being described as a certainty rather than dated, and the Qur'an returns to the scene more than once: *“On that Day, those who denied ˹Allah˺ and disobeyed the Messenger will wish they were reduced to dust”* (4:42) and *“the disbelievers will cry, ‘I wish I were dust.’”* (78:40). Both texts put the wish at the reckoning, where a person's verdict is public and the escape routes have closed.

**A Grammar Lesson in a Ruined Hope**

Sūrah al-Anʿām gives the same regret in fuller form: *“Oh! If only we could be sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord and we would ˹surely˺ be of the believers.”* (6:27). Then it says what such people would do if returned. The verse above it exposes the actual value of posthumous promises: the person making one is bargaining with a currency that no longer exists. Read beside that scene, the brevity of 15:2 — *“˹The day will come when˺ the disbelievers will certainly wish they had submitted ˹to Allah˺”* becomes sharper. It does not report a speech or a bargain. It reports a single verb of longing and the words *if only they had submitted*, nothing more. The rest of the sentence has already been carried off by time. Also worth noticing is the verb's subject: those who disbelieved, identified by an act rather than a lineage. The verse is not making a claim about tribes or nations. It is describing a class that will be filled from every nation, and the wish will be as universal as the verdict that produced it.

**What Submission Means in the Wish**

The word they wish they had chosen is *muslimūn*, submitters, not the name of a community formed centuries later. In the Qur'an the term belongs to obedience itself. Ibrāhīm, told to surrender, answers, *“I submit to the Lord of all worlds.”* (2:131), and the same prophet is later described as one who *“submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist”* (3:67). The Book's own summary of acceptable religion is *“Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them”* (3:85), where Islam means the surrender that every messenger taught. So the regret is specific. It is not the complaint of people who wish they had been born elsewhere or in another century. It is the admission of people who spent their lives defending their own authority against God's, and who one day learn that the whole contention was pointless. They do not wish they had known more.

Sūrah al-Naml states this without softening: *“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly.”* (27:14).

## Verse 15:3

> ˹So˺ let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by ˹false˺ hope, for they will soon know

**An Imperative That Lets Go Rather Than Orders**

The sentence begins with *dharhum*, leave them — a command, but one issued to the Prophet about people he cannot force. Arabic uses the imperative for release in the same way a man says, leave him to his business. The Qur'an repeats the construction through the Makkan sūrahs whenever the argument has been made and refused: *“So leave them ˹O Prophet˺ in their heedlessness for a while.”* (23:54), *“So leave them until they face their Day in which they will be struck dead—”* (52:45). Twice in nearly identical words, *“So let them indulge ˹in falsehood˺ and amuse ˹themselves˺ until they face their Day”* (43:83; 70:42 — *“So let them indulge ˹in falsehood˺ and amuse ˹themselves˺ until they face their Day, which they have been threatened with”*). Read together, these commands sound the same note: the messenger's task is to deliver the warning, not to enforce the outcome. Release is not approval. It is the shape that mercy takes when a creature is determined to choose badly, and the same sūrah that commands it also reports the reservation of the final word, *“for they will soon know”*.

**The Threat That Is Left Unnamed**

*Sawfa yaʿlamūn* — soon they will know. The sentence stops at knowing. No fire is described, no blast, no morning of ruin, and the omission is not modesty but method. The Book has already told them what they are; the punishment is left as the part they will discover for themselves. Where the Qur'an does spell out the outcome, the wording is notably restrained, as in *“We allow them enjoyment for a little while, then ˹in time˺ We will force them into a harsh torment.”* (31:24) or the parallel *“for they will soon know”* attached to the command *“So let them be ungrateful for all We have given them, and ˹let them˺ enjoy themselves ˹for now˺!”* (29:66). The phrase also carries a quiet rebuke. Knowledge is what they are short of, not pleasure, and the one thing that will finally arrive is the thing they had every chance to get earlier at no cost.

**Enjoyment as Blessing or as Delay**

There is a doctrine underneath this verse, and other passages state it openly. The same prolonging of comfort can be mercy or it can be a leash: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178), and the warning that *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired”* (6:44). Prosperity therefore cannot be read as a verdict on the person enjoying it. The Qur'an never allows the arithmetic that says wealth and ease equal divine approval. What the present verse does is place the reader in front of that confusion while it is still unresolved. Two men sit at the same table. One will rise and give thanks; the other will ask for the table to be eternal. The command, *leave them*, is issued because at the moment it is spoken, only the future can tell them apart.

**Why the Command Had to Be Repeated**

Muslims reciting these sūrahs over the centuries of the Makkan period heard the same release worded again and again, and the repetition had a purpose for the community that would inherit the message. The later Muslim community would have power, and the memory of this verse would keep the mission from turning into something else. Sūrah al-Muddaththir answers the same problem with *“And leave to me ˹O Prophet˺ the one I created all by Myself”* (74:11), and the point of *to me* is that the case has a judge, which is why the prophet can walk away from it with a clear conscience and an empty hand.

It commands believers to *“Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.”* (2:172), and it asks the same again in 16:114 — *“So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah’s favours, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺”*.

Sūrah al-Kahf attaches it to labour: *“So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.”* (18:110).

## Verse 15:4

> We have never destroyed a society without a destined term

**"A Destined Term" and the Register of Decrees**

The Arabic phrase is *kitābun maʿlūm*, a known writing, and the same word *kitāb* serves elsewhere for the register in which God's decrees are recorded. Sūrah al-Burūj ends by describing the Qur'an itself as *“˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet.”* (85:22), and Sūrah al-Ḥadīd states that *“No calamity ˹or blessing˺ occurs on earth or in yourselves without being ˹written˺ in a Record before We bring it into being.”* (57:22). The register is not a metaphor for fate in general; it is described as a written text with contents, which is why the commentators call it *al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*, the Preserved Tablet. The point of the image is precision. A term that is written is a term that is known, and a term that is known has an author who is not surprised by what happens when it arrives. The verse's comfort and its warning come from the same place: nothing about a people's end is improvised, and nothing about their conduct is unobserved.

**The Term Is Also Time Granted**

Read alone, a fixed date for destruction could sound like a sentence passed in advance. The Qur'an treats it as the opposite for those willing to use it, because the interval between the decree and its execution is the space in which warning is delivered and change remains possible. *“Had the people of those societies been faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, We would have overwhelmed them with blessings from heaven and earth.”* (7:96). The principle is stated in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ: *“We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺.”* (17:15). One town in the Book actually turned: *“If only there had been a society which believed ˹before seeing the torment˺ and, therefore, benefited from its belief, like the people of Jonah.”* (10:98). The fixed term did not stop the people of Jonah from being spared. What it stopped was the illusion that they could decide when the reckoning came. A deadline in the hands of a just authority is a warning for as long as it has not passed, and this verse keeps that door in view.

**What the Verse Refuses to Say**

Two things are withheld, and both are important. The verse does not name the term for any living community, and it does not describe the destruction. The men who mocked the Prophet are left knowing only that the rule exists, which keeps them from either fatalism or false security. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states the principle in a way that shows the withholding is not accidental: *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the punishment. Had it not been for a time already set, the punishment would have certainly come to them ˹at once˺.”* (29:53). The challengers wanted a date, and they were not given one.

**A Term for Towns, a Term for Souls**

The same order applies to persons, and the Qur'an states it in the same vocabulary. *“He is the One Who created you from clay, then appointed a term ˹for your death˺ and another known only to Him ˹for your resurrection˺”* (6:2), and *“Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes. And Allah is All‑Aware of what you do.”* (63:11). Sūrah Fāṭir gathers both registers into one sentence: *“No female ever conceives or delivers without His knowledge. And no one's life is made long or cut short but is ˹written˺ in a Record.”* (35:11). Reading 15:4 — *“We have never destroyed a society without a destined term”* beside these texts removes any suggestion that God has one policy for nations and another for individuals. There is one practice throughout: a term is set, the term is recorded, and the term is honoured — for a person and for a town alike. The difference between them is only the scale of the story and the number of people whose choices are folded into it. The verse about the town is therefore a reminder written small enough for one reader to carry.

*“And your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ would never destroy a society unjustly while its people were acting rightly.”* (11:117).

## Verse 15:5

> No people can advance their doom, nor can they delay it

**A Word That Means Both a People and a Span**

*Ummah* is one of the most elastic words in the language. It names a community gathered around a shared way of life, and it also names a measured period of time. Sūrah Hūd uses it in the second sense when it says *“And if We delay their punishment until an appointed time, they will definitely say, ‘What is holding it back?’”* (11:8) — the Arabic there is *ilā ummatin maʿdūdah*, to a numbered span. The classical commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī and Ibn Kathīr, read the word in that verse as a period of years. The same usage appears in Sūrah Yūsuf, where a released prisoner remembers his companion *after some time* (12:45 — *“˹Finally,˺ the surviving ex-prisoner remembered ˹Joseph˺ after a long time and said, “I will tell you its interpretation, so send me forth ˹to Joseph˺.”*). So the two halves of this verse sit in a single word's shadow: the people whose term is fixed are named by the very noun that can mean the term itself. A community is, in this vocabulary, a measured stretch of history with a name attached.

**Terms for Persons, Terms for Peoples**

The Qur'an applies the same rule at both scales, and it expects the two to be understood together. *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time.”* (3:145), and Noah, warning his people, tells them that God *“will forgive your sins, and delay your end until the appointed time. Indeed, when the time set by Allah comes, it cannot be delayed”* (71:4). The Book nowhere suggests that a community has a term which its members' deaths do not share, or that an individual's death is unrelated to the collective end. What a person cannot do for himself, no assembly can do for him either. This is why the teaching about the fixed term never became, in the Qur'an's own usage, an excuse for passivity. The verse concerns the one thing that is out of reach, and by locating the boundary precisely it leaves everything inside that boundary unexcused.

**The End of Bargaining**

Sūrah al-Anʿām records a scene that shows what the fixed term removes from the world: the possibility of negotiating. *“Oh! If only we could be sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord and we would ˹surely˺ be of the believers.”* (6:27). Sūrah al-Sajdah reports the same plea in the same terms, with the condemned asking to be returned so that they may do good (32:12 — *“We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good.”*). Both scenes are staged after the hour has struck, and both are answered by silence, because the plea cannot be granted: the interval in which such a decision would have counted has closed. That is the practical content of 15:5 — *“No people can advance their doom, nor can they delay it”*. A community cannot buy an extension of its day of reckoning by good behaviour in the last hour, nor can it shorten the day of its relief by impatience. This is a cold statement of fact, and it is placed in the sūrah just before the mockers are answered, so that no one mistakes the delay of punishment for a plea accepted.

**Living With a Date Nobody Knows**

A term that is certain and unknown produces a particular discipline. The believer cannot plan as though the world will end tomorrow, nor live as though it never will; both errors are excluded by the same verse. Work, migration, trade, medicine, preparation for the next generation — all of these remain sensible, because they belong to the interval, not to its length. What the verse removes is the superstition that a nation's survival can be negotiated by ritual or flattery: the Qur'an reports the earlier communities asking their prophets what was holding the punishment back, and receiving no answer that gave them a number. The correct posture is stated in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf: *“Have they ever reflected on the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and everything Allah has created, and that perhaps their end is near?”* (7:185).

## Verse 15:6
> They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane

**What the Title "Reminder" Claims**

*Al-dhikr* means both remembrance and mention, and as a name for the revelation it carries a claim that is easy to miss. A reminder does not bring news from nowhere; it recalls what a person has reason to know already. The Qur'an uses the title for the Book sent to Muḥammad — *“And We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Reminder. That you may explain to people what has been revealed for them”* (16:44) — and also, in other passages, for the scriptures that came before him, when it tells the doubters to *“ask those who have knowledge ˹of the Scriptures˺”* (16:43). Sūrah al-Ṭā Hā states the purpose directly, describing the revelation as *“a reminder to those in awe ˹of Allah˺”* (20:3), and 36:11 calls those who accept it people who *“follow the Reminder”*. Nowhere does the Book claim to be saying something wholly unheard of, and that is why the mockers' question was answerable in principle from the first day. What they were handed was a recalling, not an invasion.

**The Same Charge Against Every Messenger**

The accusation in this verse is not an isolated insult; it is the standard defence of an audience that has been confronted and has nothing to say. The Qur'an records the pattern with a formula: *“Similarly, no messenger came to those before them without being told: ‘A magician or a madman!’”* (51:52). Noah's people *“rejected Our servant, calling ˹him˺ insane. And he was intimidated.”* (54:9). Pharaoh said of Mūsā, *“Your messenger, who has been sent to you, must be insane.”* (26:27).

The men of Qāf said of the Prophet *“A madman, taught by others!”* (44:14), and Sūrah al-Qalam records them saying almost exactly the same thing, *“He is certainly a madman.”* (68:51). The uniformity is the argument. A charge that appears in the same form against prophets separated by centuries and continents is describing the accuser, not the accused.

**Why Insanity Was the Convenient Charge**

A man who stands up and says that God has spoken to him presents his hearers with a hard choice. Either he is telling the truth, in which case they must change the way they live, or he is not, in which case he is either a liar or deceived. The charge of madness chooses a third possibility that requires no moral response from the hearers at all. Sūrah al-Mu'minūn answers the charge by pointing at the content: *“In fact, he has come to them with the truth, but most of them are resentful of the truth.”* (23:70).

**God's Own Answer to the Accusation**

The reply to the taunt is not left to the Prophet, and it is not an apology. Sūrah al-Ṭūr joins the command to continue the work with a verdict on the charge: *“So ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺. For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman.”* (52:29). Sūrah al-Takwīr says it in four words, *“And your fellow man is not insane.”* (81:22), pointing at the fact that they had known him for forty years before he spoke a verse. Sūrah al-Qalam closes the accusation by naming what the revelation actually is: *“But it is simply a reminder to the whole world.”* (68:52). Across all three answers, the defence is the same: the man is known, the message is a reminder, and the charge explains nothing that the accusers could not have checked. The sūrah's own reply comes a few verses later, and it will not be an argument about the Prophet's mind.

## Verse 15:7
> Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?”

**The Angels the Questioners Already Had in Mind**

When these men spoke of angels, they spoke of beings they had already placed in a scheme. The Book reports the scheme in pointed terms: *“And they attribute ˹angels as˺ daughters to Allah—glory be to Him!— the opposite of what they desire for themselves.”* (16:57). The accusation is repeated in 17:40 — *“Has your Lord favoured you ˹pagans˺ with sons and taken angels as ˹His˺ daughters? You are truly making an outrageous claim”*, where they are asked whether God has favoured them with sons and taken daughters for Himself, and in 43:19 — *“Still they have labelled the angels, who are servants of the Most Compassionate, as female. Did they witness their creation? Their statement will be recorded, and they will be questioned”*, where the angels are said to have been labelled female; the Qur'an asks whether they witnessed their creation. Sūrah al-Najm and Sūrah al-Ṭūr press the same contradiction between the daughters they assigned to God and the sons they wanted for themselves. Their so-called goddesses, named in the Book, turn out under examination to be a name and a rumour: *“Instead of Allah, they only invoke female gods and they ˹actually˺ invoke none but a rebellious Satan—”* (4:117). Angels were familiar figures in this imagination, which is exactly why demanding to see one required no revolution in their beliefs — only a check on the man who spoke for God.

**The Same Request in Many Shapes**

The demand in this verse is one entry in a long ledger, and the ledger's variety is the clearest proof that no single sign would have satisfied it. They ask, in one place, *“Why has no ˹visible˺ angel come with him?”* (6:8); in another, why an angel was not sent along to share the warning (25:7 — *“If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner”*); in another, why angels did not arrive as escorts (43:53 — *“Why then have no golden bracelets ˹of kingship˺ been granted to him or angels come with him as escorts!”*). Sūrah al-Isrāʾ collects the whole shopping list: a spring gushing from the earth, a garden of palms and grapes, the sky falling in pieces, gold, a staircase to climb — and among them the demand to bring God and the angels face to face (17:90, 92 — *“They challenge ˹the Prophet˺, “We will never believe in you until you cause a spring to gush forth from the earth for us”*). Sūrah Hūd notes the same pattern, *“If only a treasure had been sent down to him, or an angel had come with him!”* (11:12). A questioner who changes the required proof every time it is discussed is not measuring evidence. He is refusing to be bound by anything he is shown, and the Qur'an says so plainly. Sūrah al-Anʿām anticipates the objection before it can be made.

Its verdict removes the ground from under this verse's questioners: *“Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign ˹they demanded˺, they still would not have believed— unless Allah so willed.”* (6:111).

The same sūrah records the solemn oaths they swore in advance, that a sign would make believers of them, and the answer they received: *“Signs are only with Allah.”* (6:109).

**Why No Demonstration Was Offered**

The Book's policy is consistent: revelation sets its own terms. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Had there been angels walking the earth, well settled, We would have surely sent down for them an angel from heaven as a messenger.’”* (17:95). Messengers come from among the people they address, in their language, with their history; an angel messenger would have been suited to an angel community. Sūrah al-Raʿd states the duty that remains: *“You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide.”* (13:7). Once the messenger has delivered the message, nothing in his commission obliges him to stage a verification for people who have decided at the outset what would persuade them. And the restriction protects them as much as it rebukes them: to demand a sign from God is to invite the answer that arrives with force, from which no one escapes.

---
## Verse 15:8

> We do not send the angels down except for a just cause, and then ˹the end of˺ the disbelievers will not be delayed

**Angels Are Sent on Assignment**

The verse answers a taunt with a fact about how the world is managed. Angels do not descend as courtiers answering a summons from the public; they come with an order attached, *bi-l-ḥaqq*, in truth, meaning here with the right that God has decreed — which, in the context of a demand for proof, means with judgment. The Book gives the same principle in the mouth of the angels themselves: *“We only descend by the command of your Lord. To Him belongs whatever is before us, and whatever is behind us, and everything in between.”* (19:64). Their motion is commanded, and the command has legal content.

**The Word "Delayed" and Where It Points**

The Arabic behind *will not be delayed* is *munẓarīn*, the passive participle of a verb meaning to grant a postponement — the exact condition granted to those who are allowed time to reconsider. The same root appears in the exchange at 7:15, where the reply to a request for delay is *“You are delayed ˹until the appointed Day˺.”* (7:15). In the mouth of the disbelievers in this verse, the word carries its full weight: they had asked for signs; what they would forfeit by receiving them is the postponement that made their lives possible. Sūrah Hūd shows them demanding to know what holds their punishment back (11:8 — *“And if We delay their punishment until an appointed time, they will definitely say, “What is holding it back?”*), and Sūrah Yūnus shows the answer: *“For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.”* (10:49). The vocabulary of respite runs through the sūrah's early verses, and here it is placed directly in front of the men playing with signs.

**What Kind of Evidence the Qur'an Actually Offers**

Readers who ask why God does not simply send the angel are missing what the Book presents as its own proof. The evidence on offer is the Qur'an itself, in a language its audience commanded, together with the creation they woke up inside every day and the record of earlier nations they walked past on their travels. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ points at the human channel: *“And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: ‘Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?’”* (17:94). The objection is never really about the strength of the proof; it is about the rank of the messenger. Once the messenger must be human, the scandal of a message that can be examined, memorised and argued with follows immediately. The sign they demanded would have spared them that argument by ending the debate altogether, and the answer in this verse is that the end of the debate is not on the table as an option for the undecided.

**A Rebuke Hidden Inside an Answer**

Notice the order of the reply. It does not begin with the pronoun of majesty claiming the angels, nor with a description of their glory. It begins with *we do not send down*, a statement about practice, and practice implies reasons and rules. The effect is to move the question out of the register of spectacle and into the register of law. Angels come with truth — a just cause — and a just cause in this context is what the mockers were trembling to escape without knowing it. Sūrah al-Furqān describes the day of that arrival: *“˹But˺ on the Day they will see the angels, there will be no good news for the wicked, who will cry, ‘Keep away! Away ˹from us˺!’”* (25:22). The disbelievers who asked to see angels wanted them as witnesses for the defence. The verse's quiet answer is that the day the angels come, the court will not be convened on their terms.

## Verse 15:9

> It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it

**The Reminder, Named for What It Does**

The Book is called *al-dhikr* here, the reminder, the same title the mockers used when they addressed the Prophet in verse 6. The name carries a claim about the audience as much as about the text: a reminder lands on knowledge a person already has, however buried. The Qur'an describes itself this way repeatedly — *“but as a reminder to those in awe ˹of Allah˺”* (20:3), *“But it is simply a reminder to the whole world.”* (68:52) — and the word also covers the earlier scriptures when the Book speaks of what was sent before. So the verse's guarantee is not offered for a novelty that needed to be defended as an invention. It is offered for a recalling that touches what a human being already suspects about his own condition, and such a recalling is not easily smothered, because the ground it works on is present in every generation.

**A Claim About the Text That Can Be Checked**

The preservation announced here is specific, and other passages spell out its content. *“It cannot be proven false from any angle. ˹It is˺ a revelation from the ˹One Who is˺ All‑Wise, Praiseworthy.”* (41:42) rules out corruption entering the Book from any direction — before it, in the form of a prior scripture that would contradict it, or behind it, in the form of a later revision. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ offers a test that only a stable text can be subjected to: *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies.”* (4:82). And Sūrah al-Kahf opens by praising the One who revealed the Book to His servant *“allowing no crookedness in it”* (18:1). Each of these statements makes sense only if the wording being discussed is fixed. A moving target cannot be examined for inconsistency, and a text that might be edited cannot be defended as free of contradiction.

**Preservation Through Reciters and Memory**

How the guarantee is realised in practice is described in the Book's own terms. Sūrah al-Qiyāmah, addressing the Prophet, says of the revelation: *“It is certainly upon Us to ˹make you˺ memorize and recite it.”* (75:17). The mechanism named is memory carried by people, not a sealed vault, and that is what the historical record shows: the text was transmitted by mass recitation in every generation, with chains of teachers, before and alongside the written copies. The classical commentators noticed the double channel. Al-Rāzī explains the protection as extending to the miraculous ordering of the Book, and al-Qushayrī reports the view that God preserves it through the reciters, whose hearts he calls the treasuries of His Book. The two readings are complementary rather than rival: the words are guarded in memory and in writing at the same time, so that the failure of either channel alone would not lose a syllable. Physical evidence points the same way. Two leaves of an early Qur'an in Hijazi script, held in the Cadbury Research Library at the University of Birmingham, were radiocarbon dated in 2015 to a span of 568–645 CE, placing the parchment within the Prophet's lifetime or shortly after it. The surviving text of Sūrahs 18 to 20 agrees with the wording recited today. The dating applies to the parchment rather than to the ink, so the folios witness an early written text rather than a precise date of copying.

## Verse 15:10

> Indeed, We sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ among the groups of early peoples

**A Line of Messengers, and One Man Standing in It**

The verse is addressed to a man whose town had just declared him mad, and its first word is *before you*. The Qur'an regularly answers the isolation of the Prophet by widening the frame: what is happening in Makkah has happened on a scale too large for any single city to be the whole story. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the same continuity in the form of a genealogy of revelation: *“Indeed, We have sent revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ as We sent revelation to Noah and the prophets after him.”* (4:163).

**Every Community Had Its Warner**

The Qur'an makes the universality of the mission explicit, and this verse belongs to that set. *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods.’”* (16:36), and *“There is no community that has not had a warner.”* (35:24). Sūrah Yūnus describes the mechanism of judgment that follows once the messenger has arrived: *“And for every community there is a messenger. After their messenger has come, judgment is passed on them in all fairness, and they are not wronged.”* (10:47). These statements together rule out the objection that a people could be condemned for what they never heard, and they also rule out the idea that revelation belongs to one ethnic group. The Makkans' claim to be the custodians of the sacred house sat uneasily beside the fact that God had been sending messengers to nations they regarded as nothing, and this verse quietly disturbs that comfort by mentioning the earlier groups without distinguishing them.

**One Religion, Sent Repeatedly**

What makes the history coherent is the identity of the message. *“He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ ‘Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it.’”* (42:13). The Qur'an's account of prophetic history is therefore not a story of evolving religions but of one religion renewed. Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus are not competitors; the divisions condemned in that verse are the ones later communities built around the same message. For the Prophet in Makkah, this has a practical consequence.

## Verse 15:11

> but no messenger ever came to them without being mocked

**From Laughter to the Thing Laughed At**

The Book records what comes after the mockery with a phrase that repeats almost verbatim across the sūrahs: the punishment of the mockers is *what they used to ridicule*. *“˹Other˺ messengers had already been ridiculed before you ˹O Prophet˺, but those who mocked them were overtaken by what they used to ridicule.”* (6:10), and Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ repeats the sentence word for word (21:41 — *“˹Other˺ messengers had already been ridiculed before you ˹O Prophet˺, but those who mocked them were overtaken by what they used to ridicule”*). Elsewhere the point is made with the same vocabulary: the deniers are *“overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”* at the reckoning (39:48 — *“And the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds will unfold before them, and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”*; 45:33 — *“And the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds will unfold before them, and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”*), and Sūrah Ghāfir explains that this overtaking came after they had been proud of what knowledge they had (40:83 — *“When their messengers came to them with clear proofs, they were prideful in whatever ˹worldly˺ knowledge they had, and were ˹ultimately˺ overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”*). The symmetry is exact. Laughter at a warning is answered by the arrival of the warning, and the last laugh in the record belongs to the thing that was laughed at.

**Which Nation Escaped Notice**

No nation is excepted. Sūrah al-Mu'minūn describes the sequence in its own terse way: *“Then We sent Our messengers in succession: whenever a messenger came to his people, they denied him. So We destroyed them, one after the other, reducing them to ˹cautionary˺ tales.”* (23:44). Sūrah al-Raʿd adds the delay that this verse implies — the mockers are not struck down at the moment of the joke: *“Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you, but I delayed the disbelievers ˹for a while˺ then seized them. And how ˹horrible˺ was My punishment!”* (13:32). The pattern, then, has two parts: mockery begins, and judgment waits. The waiting is what the men in Makkah mistook for a verdict in their favour. It is the same mistake the sūrah has already addressed in its opening verses, where prosperity and delay are treated as a licence rather than a test.

**The Consolation Inside a Sour Sentence**

Muslims reading this verse in later centuries have found in it something more than a history lesson. The first thing the Qur'an tells a man whose message has been turned into a joke is that he is on the road that every messenger walked, and that the road is not a sign that he has failed. Sūrah Yā Sīn states the same verdict in a tone that is closer to mourning than rebuke: *“Oh pity, such beings! No messenger ever came to them without being mocked.”* (36:30). The sorrow in that verse is directed at the mockers, not at the mocked. They were warned by people who wanted nothing from them, and the warning was returned as entertainment. A community that hears this verse is expected to remember it when the same treatment is offered to the people who carry the message in their own time, and to recognise how the joke is designed to work.

**The Difference Between Mockery and Refutation**

One detail matters for the way this verse is used in argument. The mockers in these stories never refute the message. They attack the messenger's sanity, his motives, his poverty, his origins, his followers. That is not a coincidence but a consequence: mockery is chosen when refutation is not available, and the Book treats the two as different orders of activity. A person who examines the Qur'an, finds a contradiction and argues from it is doing something the Book invites: *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies.”* (4:82). The men in these verses did not take up that invitation.

## Verse 15:12

> This is how We allow disbelief ˹to steep˺ into the hearts of the wicked

**"This Is How": Causation and Consequence**

The opening words *this is how* join this verse to what precedes it. Mockery, refusal, the demand for signs that no evidence would satisfy — this is the road, and the verse reports where the road leads. God allows disbelief to enter such hearts because they have already opened the door by their own choices. The Qur'an states this pattern repeatedly. *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first”* (6:110), and to the Israelites who kept provoking Mūsā: *“So when they ˹persistently˺ deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate.”* (61:5). The order in both cases is deviation first, then divine confirmation of it. Sūrah al-Baqarah describes the same thing happening to mockers: *“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.”* (2:15). The punishment for refusing light is being left in the dark, and that is precisely what is described here.

**The Verb Behind "Steep"**

The Arabic verb is *naslukuhu*, and al-Rāzī collects two pictures for it that come from ordinary life. The first is a thread passed through the eye of a needle; the second is a spear driven through the body of an animal. Both images describe the same motion — something threaded through a narrow passage until it is inside — and both are drawn from the language of physical handling rather than of instruction.

**The Gradual Hardening No One Notices**

Cloth soaks slowly. That is the point of the image, and it explains why the doctrine in this verse is so serious. Nobody decides to become a denier in a single gesture. The process is a series of small refusals, each with its own excuse: not today, not in front of these people, not while this business deal is unfinished. The Qur'an describes the endpoint in a warning to those who treat the message as a curiosity: *“Whoever Allah allows to stray, none can guide, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.”* (7:186). Blindness is described not as a punishment inflicted from outside but as the natural state of a person who kept choosing not to look. Sūrah Yūnus links the same fate to a specific attitude, those who do not expect to meet God being left to wander in their defiance (10:11 — *“But We leave those who do not expect to meet Us to wander blindly in their defiance”*). The heart that has been steeped in denial no longer registers the evidence that once made it uneasy.

## Verse 15:13

> They would not believe in this ˹Quran˺ despite the ˹many˺ examples of those ˹destroyed˺ before

**The Ruins on the Road**

Sūrah al-Rūm asks the question that this verse assumes has an answer: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might; they cultivated the land and developed it more than these ˹Meccans˺ ever have. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs.”* (30:9). The archaeology of judgment is being used as instruction. Sūrah al-Ḥajj describes what the travellers could see: *“Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!”* (22:45). Wells abandoned and palaces standing empty are the specific images, and they are the images of a civilisation whose infrastructure outlasted its people. The Qur'an's argument does not require the Makkans to accept a prophet's report about the distant past. It asks them to read what was already in the landscape of their own journeys.

**The Same Complaint in Earlier Communities**

The refusal described here is not new either, and the Book says so in a verse that supplies the diagnosis: *“Those who have no knowledge say, ‘If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!’ The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike.”* (2:118). The uniformity of the complaint is itself the evidence. Each generation believes its demand is original and its scepticism a personal achievement, and each generation repeats the sentence that was spoken against the messenger before it. Sūrah Yūnus closes the argument by answering it directly: *“Consider all that is in the heavens and the earth!”* (10:101), then adds that *“neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe.”* (10:101). The verse under commentary is the same verdict, stated flatly and without illustration.

**Denial Has Its Own Repertoire**

Other verses show what the rejection sounds like when it is pressed: the same words recur with the boredom of a cliché. Sūrah al-Anʿām records the accusation that the Qur'an is *“nothing but ancient fables!”* (6:25), Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn reports the same sneer, *“Ancient fables!”* (83:13), and Sūrah al-Furqān notes the standard of proof claimed by those who know nothing (2:118 again in substance — *“Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!” The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith”*). Denial here is not a position with arguments; it is a set of reflexes, and the Book catalogs them so that a reader can recognise the routine when he meets it. What is common to all of them is that none engages the content of what was recited. The charges concern the messenger's sanity, his sources, or the age of the stories. The Book's own test — find one inconsistency in it — is offered freely and taken up rarely.

**The Word Behind "Examples": the Sunnah of the Earlier Peoples**

The phrase rendered *examples of those destroyed before* is, in Arabic, *sunnat al-awwalīn*, the established way of the former peoples, and the noun is worth pausing over. A *sunnah* in the language is a path worn by repeated travel, then a practice followed because it has been followed before, and the Qur'an applies the word to God's own dealing with nations. *“Tell the disbelievers that if they desist, their past will be forgiven. But if they persist, then they have an example in those destroyed before them.”* (8:38). Sūrah Fāṭir asks the mockers whether they are waiting for anything but *“the fate of those ˹destroyed˺ before”*, adding that *“You will find no change in the way of Allah, nor will you find it diverted ˹to someone else˺.”* (35:43). The fixed formula recurs: *“˹This is˺ Allah's way, already long established ˹in the past˺. And you will find no change in Allah's way.”* (48:23; 33:62 — *“That was Allah’s way with those ˹hypocrites˺ who have gone before. And you will find no change in Allah’s way”*). Nothing here is an improvisation. The ruin of the deniers is a road that has been walked many times, and the verse that follows this one describes the response that keeps the traffic moving.

## Verse 15:14

> And even if We opened for them a gate to heaven, through which they continued to ascend

**A Gate, Not a Glimpse**

The image is deliberately excessive. What is opened is *bāb*, a gate, not a window; what they do is ascend, not look up. The picture is of sustained access to the place from which revelation comes — the very access the mockers claimed they wanted when they asked why angels were not sent down, and the very thing they said would settle the question of whether a message was true. The verb used for their movement, *yaʿrujūn*, is built from the same root as *miʿrāj*, the ladder or ascent, and the same root describes the motion of the angels in *“˹through which˺ the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit will ascend to Him”* (70:4). Human beings are being imagined making the journey that belongs to the angels. The barrier the verse removes is the last one available: distance, altitude, and the separation between the visible world and the source of the message.

**Seeing Is Not Believing**

The Qur'an distinguishes sharply between what the eye receives and what the heart accepts, and it makes the distinction part of its account of human responsibility. *“I will turn away from My signs those who act unjustly with arrogance in the land. And even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them.”* (7:146). The same sūrah that describes the sealing of hearts describes a sight that is covered, not a sky that is empty: *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered.”* (2:7). The verse under commentary assumes the eye is working perfectly. The travellers are imagined climbing through a gate and looking at what lies beyond, and the sight registers. What fails is the interpretation, and the failure happens where the person is, not where the evidence is.

**The Ceiling of Curiosity**

There is a habit of mind that treats the world as a collection of puzzles and itself as a spectator with no stake in them. The demand in these verses is that habit in religious dress: show me something extraordinary and I will reconsider. The Qur'an's answer is that extraordinary sights are available and always have been — a sky furnished with towers, a body that came from clay, a book recited in the desert — and that the appetite for wonders is not the same as the desire for truth. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ says of the mockers that they only listened for entertainment (21:2 — *“Whatever new reminder comes to them from their Lord, they only listen to it jokingly”*). A man who reads the creation as entertainment will read a gate to heaven the same way, and will return to his business with a story to tell. The verse does not deny the wonder. It denies that wonder by itself does anything to the person watching it.

**Doors of the Sky in the Qur'an**

The image of an opening in the sky is not invented for this verse; the Book uses it elsewhere, and always with judgment attached. When the flood came, *“We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain”* (54:11), and the mercy of that rain was simultaneously the instrument of the punishment. At the Hour, *“The sky will be ˹split˺ open, becoming ˹many˺ gates”* (78:19). And the doors are shut in the face of the arrogant: *“Surely those who receive our revelations with denial and arrogance, the gates of heaven will not be opened for them, nor will they enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle.”* (7:40). Set beside these passages, the hypothetical in this verse loses its air of generosity. A gate opened in the sky is not a spectacle arranged for the benefit of spectators; it is the sign of an order being executed. The mockers asked to be taken up through it. The Book's other uses of the same door suggest what such a journey would actually have been.

## Verse 15:15

> still they would say, “Our eyes have truly been dazzled! In fact, we must have been bewitched.”

**"In Fact, We Have Been Bewitched"**

The Arabic uses *bal*, a particle that corrects a statement and replaces it with a stronger one, so the two clauses escalate: not merely dazzled but actually under a spell. Sorcery in the Qur'an's vocabulary is *siḥr*, and the charge is familiar from the sūrah's own context: the Makkans had already used it against the Prophet when they heard the Qur'an. Sūrah Yūnus reports the reaction to the truth arriving, *“This is certainly pure magic!”* (10:76), and Sūrah al-Zukhruf reports the same dismissive move, *“This is magic, and we totally reject it.”* (43:30). The accusation in this verse takes the pattern one step further. The same men who called the messenger a madman and a sorcerer would, on seeing a gate opened in the sky, apply the sorcery label to their own eyes — anything to avoid the conclusion that the messenger was right.

**The Charge That Followed Every Messenger**

This was not an improvisation by the Makkans. When Mūsā confronted Pharaoh, the chiefs announced, *“He is indeed a skilled magician”* (7:109), and Pharaoh said the same to those around him (26:34 — *“Pharaoh said to the chiefs around him, “He is indeed a skilled magician”*). The accusation of magic served as a standard explanation for a prophet's authority in Egypt and in Arabia alike. It appears in the case of Jesus as well: 5:110 — *“How I prevented the Children of Israel from harming you when you came to them with clear proofs and the disbelievers among them said, “This is nothing but pure magic.”* records the favours given to him — the bird of clay, the healing of the blind and the leper, the raising of the dead — and the response of those who watched. The accusation survives because it works. It requires no examination of the message, no reckoning with its content, and no change of life. It also explains why the Book places the charge next to the signs, in verse after verse, as the fixed answer of the unpersuaded to anything they cannot account for.

**The Self-Sealing Circle**

Notice the shape of the logic. A stranger comes with a message, and if his words are persuasive they are magic; if he climbs to heaven the climbers are bewitched; if he works wonders, they are tricks; if he is poor, he cannot be a messenger; if he eats food, he cannot be an angel. Each response closes one question and opens none. The Qur'an records this habit in the demand for signs, and it says of those who took the position that they would not believe even if every sign were assembled in front of them unless God willed it (6:111 — *“Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign ˹they demanded˺, they still would not have believed—unless Allah so willed.”*). That is the situation the present verse illustrates in a single image. The gate has been opened, and the debate is already over — not by the power of the evidence, but by the determination of the one who saw it.

**What the Qur'an Says About Magic Itself**

The Book treats sorcery as a real practice rather than a superstition, and it denies it the two things its practitioners claimed: power over the divine will, and success. Mūsā answers Pharaoh's magicians with *“Is this what you say about the truth when it has come to you? Is this magic? Magicians will never succeed.”* (10:77), and on the day of the contest he is told what will happen to their work: *“And magicians can never succeed wherever they go.”* (20:69). Sūrah al-Baqarah gives the fullest account, describing magic taught in Babylon and adding the protection clause that changes the whole story: *“Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved.”* (2:102). Sorcery in this framework is a technique with a limited field, and its limits are the point. It can mislead and divide; it cannot open heaven, and it cannot make a prophet what his enemies claimed he was. Sūrah al-Māʾidah lists the practices that belong to *“Satan's handiwork”* and orders them abandoned (5:90 — *“O believers! Intoxicants, gambling, idols, and drawing lots for decisions are all evil of Satan’s handiwork. So shun them so you may be successful”*). The men in this verse, imagining that a genuine wonder would be magic, were accusing the truth of being the one thing it demonstrably was not.

## Verse 15:16

> Indeed, We have placed constellations in the sky, and adorned it for all to see

**Towers in the Night**

The word rendered *constellations* is *burūj*, and its basic sense in Arabic is towers or fortresses — the raised, fortified structures of a city wall. Applied to the sky, the word pictures the stars as fixed edifices rather than as scattered points. The Qur'an uses the same noun in Sūrah al-Burūj, where an oath is sworn *“By the sky full of constellations”* (85:1). Sūrah al-Furqān combines the stars with the two great luminaries: *“Blessed is the One Who has placed constellations in the sky, as well as a ˹radiant˺ lamp and a luminous moon.”* (25:61). Al-Kalabī, among the early exegetes, is reported to have identified the *burūj* with the twelve well-known constellations that mark the path of the sun and moon. Whether the word is read as the stations of those two bodies or as the great star groups generally, the sense of a structured order rather than a random scatter is the same.

**The Sky as Something Built**

The verse's second clause uses the language of craft: the sky was *adorned*. Elsewhere the Qur'an is even more explicit about what was done to it. *“Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration”* (37:6), and Sūrah Qāf puts the building and the beautifying in a single line: *“Have they not then looked at the sky above them: how We built it and adorned it ˹with stars˺, leaving it flawless?”* (50:6). The verbs are borrowed from architecture and jewellery, which is a considered choice.

**Adorned for the Onlooker**

The verse ends by naming the beneficiary: the sky is decorated *for those who look*. The Arabic participle is plural and indefinite, which widens the audience beyond believers, beyond Arabs, beyond any group with a claim on the message. Beauty here is not a private revelation granted to a mystic; it is a public offer repeated nightly. The Qur'an returns to the same thought when it asks its audience to consider the whole of creation: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Consider all that is in the heavens and the earth!’”* (10:101). Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the conclusion: *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason.”* (3:190). The onlooker is the addressee of revelation in its widest form, and his looking is treated as the beginning of an argument rather than a distraction from one.

**Signs in the Horizons and in the Self**

The Qur'an does not rely on the sky alone, and it says so in a verse that reads like a programme for everything this passage is doing: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53). The two arenas named there are the ones the sūrah is about to walk through — the sky with its towers, then the earth with its harvests, then the human being made from clay. The order is deliberate and it runs outward to inward, from what anyone can see with his head tilted back to what he would have to look at in a mirror. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ supplies the summary sentence for the physical half of the evidence: *“Do the disbelievers not realize that the heavens and earth were ˹once˺ one mass then We split them apart? And We created from water every living thing.”* (21:30). The verse under commentary is one panel of that larger display, and the sūrah's confidence is that the display has never been hidden from anybody.

The Qur'an states the challenge in a form that invites inspection rather than assent: *“You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate. So look again: do you see any flaws?”* (67:3), and then, *“Then look again and again— your sight will return frustrated and weary.”* (67:4).

## Verse 15:17

> And We protected it from every accursed devil

**The Sky as a Guarded Space**

The verse attaches a second act to the first. The sky was not only built and decorated; it was also secured. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ states the same fact in a single image: *“And We have made the sky a well-protected canopy, still they turn away from its signs.”* (21:32). Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt describes both halves of the work in consecutive lines, adorning the lowest heaven with stars *“and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil.”* (37:7). The Book consistently presents the visible universe as a bounded and administered place rather than an open field. What reads in one verse as beauty reads in the next as a wall. This is characteristic of the Qur'an's the order of the universe: whatever is described as gift is also described as governed, and the two accounts never contradict each other because the same hand is said to be behind both.

**What "Accursed" Means in This Vocabulary**

The Arabic word rendered *accursed* is *rajīm*, a passive form describing someone driven away or pelted off, and it is used of Satan in a formula every Muslim recites: *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.”* (16:98). The word is not a decoration on the name; it states the being's condition. He is the one banished from the presence he once enjoyed, and the expulsion is what he is working from. Read with that in mind, the verse's protection is not a skirmish between equals at the edge of the atmosphere. It is a boundary enforced against an intruder who has already been expelled from one domain and is refused access to another. The Book never presents the devil as a rival power; it presents him as a trespasser with a long record of failure.

**Satans, Jinn, and the Worlds They Belong To**

The Qur'an uses the word *shayṭān* for agents of corruption drawn from more than one order of being. Sūrah al-Anʿām states that God has appointed for every prophet enemies from *“devilish humans and jinn”* (6:112), and Sūrah Fāṭir warns people that Satan is an enemy who must be treated as one: *“Surely Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy.”* (35:6). The verses immediately following this one speak of the jinn who tried to listen in, and the Qur'an's own usage allows those beings to be called satans when they behave like the arch-satan. This is not a demotion of the jinn or a confusion of categories. It is a description of a role. The Book is interested in what an agent does and whose order he serves, and the protection announced in this verse is aimed at that behaviour rather than at a species.

**The Same Guard in Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt**

The parallel passage states the whole arrangement in three consecutive verses, and it deserves to be read beside this one. *“Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration”* (37:6), *“and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil.”* (37:7), *“They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side”* (37:8). The structure matches the sūrah here exactly: beauty first, then the guard, then the reason for it. Two details are added by the parallel. The protection is described as a function of the stars themselves, so that the decoration of the previous verse and the defence of this one are the same installation seen from two sides. And the exclusion is total: the devils cannot hear the assembly at all, which means the eavesdropping imagined in the next verse of this sūrah is a try at something already denied to them. Read together, the two passages leave no room for the idea that revelation competes with other traffic from the unseen world.

Sūrah al-Nās asks refuge from *“the lurking whisperer— who whispers into the hearts of humankind”* (114:4-5), and the phrase marks the boundary precisely.

## Verse 15:18

> except the one eavesdropping, who is then pursued by a visible flare

**The Theft of a Hearing**

The Arabic phrase describes a person who steals a hearing, and the metaphor is carefully chosen. What happens in the highest company is a conversation; the eavesdropper takes a fragment of it and carries it down as property that does not belong to him. The Qur'an does not dignify the practice by calling it knowledge. It calls it theft, and the theft is of the smallest possible unit: a word, a snatch, whatever can be caught before discovery. Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt explains the impossibility of the try: *“They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side”* (37:8), so that the only gain available is a fragment, *“But whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is ˹instantly˺ pursued by a piercing flare.”* (37:10). The verse under commentary belongs to that passage's language, and it makes the point by beginning with the exception that proves the rule.

**The Jinn, the Soothsayers, and the Old Trade**

These verses are traditionally read as dealing with the traffic that fed the fortune-tellers of Arabia. The jinn, who hear something and descend with it before being caught, would add to it a hundred inventions, and the soothsayers would sell the mixture as news of the unseen. Sūrah al-Jinn records the situation from the other side, in the words of the jinn themselves: *“˹Earlier˺ we tried to reach heaven ˹for news˺, only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars.”* (72:8). *“We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping, but whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for them.”* (72:9). The testimony is striking because it comes from the group whose business had just been ruined. They describe an established practice that has been shut down, and they describe it as a change they themselves noticed.

**The People Who Consulted the Jinn**

Sūrah al-Jinn records the other end of the trade, the human clients who were paying for it: *“And some men used to seek refuge with some jinn—so they increased each other in wickedness.”* (72:6). The relation described there is not a transaction between equals but a mutual corruption, in which the human party hands over his fear and the jinn party hands over a rumour, and both are made worse by the exchange. Sūrah al-Māʾidah places the whole apparatus of divination in the same category as intoxicants and gambling, describing them as *“evil of Satan's handiwork”* (5:90). The sūrah's closing picture therefore does more than describe a punishment meted out in the upper air. It dismantles the business model. If the sources of hidden news cannot reach the assembly where the news originates, then the oracle's shelf is empty. The men paying for a glimpse of the future were buying the jinn's own words — which the jinn themselves admit were lies: *“We certainly thought that humans and jinn would never speak lies about Allah.”* (72:5).

## Verse 15:19

> As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and caused everything to grow there in perfect balance

**The Ground Underneath the Argument**

After the sky, the sūrah looks down. The verse is part of a sequence that answers a demand for extraordinary proof by pointing at ordinary creation, and the earth is the most ordinary thing there is — the surface a man walks on without ever thinking about it. The Arabic verb rendered *spread out* describes a surface laid flat and extended, the way a mat is unrolled, and Sūrah al-Nabaʾ uses the same picture in its description of the earth *“smoothed out ˹like a bed˺”* (78:6). Sūrah Qāf repeats the wording of this verse almost exactly, joining the spreading, the mountains and the growth in one line: *“As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and produced in it every type of pleasant plant—”* (50:7). The Book's habit is consistent here: it draws attention to a habitable surface rather than to an astonishing spectacle, because the habitable surface is the wonder that nobody bothers to wonder at.

**Mountains as Pegs and Anchors**

The mountains are described as *firm*, and the Qur'an elsewhere explains what their firmness does. *“He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers. Pathways so you may find your way.”* (16:15), and again in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ: *“And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way.”* (21:31). Sūrah al-Nabaʾ uses the image of pegs driven into a tent (78:7 — *“and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs”*), and the commentators, al-Qurṭubī among them, read the verses as a statement about the earth's stability for the people living on it. The claim is practical rather than geological: the ground is held in a condition where human life can continue, with routes across it and rivers through it. Notice that the same sentence gives pathways in the same breath as protection. A world that only held still would be safe and useless; the verse's earth is both anchored and navigable.

**Everything in Due Measure**

The final words state that what grows does so *in perfect balance*, and the Arabic participle suggests something weighed on a scale. Al-Zamakhsharī reads it as a reference to God's wisdom apportioning each thing its quantity, which places the phrase in the same family as *“Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained.”* (54:49) and *“He has created everything, ordaining it precisely.”* (25:2). Sūrah al-Shūrā draws the conclusion for provision: God *“sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure”* (42:27), and adds that if He had given provisions in abundance instead, people would have transgressed across the land. The verse's balance is therefore not merely an aesthetic remark about the natural world. It is a statement about supply: seed, soil, rain and season are calibrated so that life is possible and yet not guaranteed, and the calibration is the work of the same agent who has just been asked to produce a sign.

**The Mountain as a Picture of Firmness**

Commentators have long read the mountains in this verse as something more than geology, and the reading is worth reporting because it has shaped how Muslims have spoken about the religion itself. A mountain is fixed, rooted and immovable while everything around it shifts; the Sacred Law, the prophets and the signs of God are described in the same terms, as realities that anchor human life and keep it from sliding. Sūrah al-Raʿd supports the comparison by listing the mountains beside rivers and fruits of every kind and then concluding that in this there are signs for people who reflect (13:3 — *“Surely in this are signs for those who reflect”*).

## Verse 15:20

> And We made in it means of sustenance for you and others, who you do not provide for

**The Table Was Set Before the Guests Arrived**

The verse speaks of provision placed in the earth, and its word order carries a claim about priority. The means of life were arranged in the world before human beings came to the world to use them: the soil capable of holding seed, the rivers, the grazing, the minerals, the seasons. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states the arrangement plainly: *“We have indeed established you on earth and provided you with a means of livelihood. ˹Yet˺ you seldom give any thanks.”* (7:10). Read this way, the verse is not describing a system that responds to people but one that was fitted to them in advance, which is why the demand for a sign in the preceding verses is treated as an odd request.

**Al-Zamakhsharī's Correction of the Provider**

Al-Zamakhsharī gives the point a sharp form: people suppose that they are the ones providing for their dependants, and the supposition is an error, because God is the Provider and He sustains both the supposed provider and the one provided for. He cites the verse in which God describes Himself as *“the Supreme Provider—Lord of all Power, Ever Mighty”* (51:58), and Sūrah Fāṭir presses the same question: *“Is there any creator other than Allah who provides for you from the heavens and the earth?”* (35:3). What the commentators are guarding against is a habit of speech rather than a doctrine: the parent who says he feeds his children is describing a channel, not a source. The verse under commentary keeps the channel and removes the ownership. A person may cultivate, harvest, store and sell; what he may not do is believe that the supply began with him.

**The Creatures Nobody Counts**

The first reading of this verse — that it includes animals humans do not feed themselves — has a resonance that its first audience could not have measured. Biology now estimates roughly 8.7 million species of eukaryotic life on the planet, with about 6.5 million on land and 2.2 million in the oceans, and by the same analysis some 86 per cent of land species and 91 per cent of marine species have not yet been described. Every one of them is fed, and almost none of them is fed by human beings. The verse does not state a species count, but it does make a claim about the reach of provision, and the modern figure makes the old sentence harder to read as a metaphor. Sūrah Hūd states the guarantee in a single line: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah.”* (11:6).

**Gratitude as the Only Sensible Response**

Everything in the verse points toward one human posture. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf ends its statement of provision with the observation that people seldom give thanks (7:10 — *“We have indeed established you on earth and provided you with a means of livelihood. ˹Yet˺ you seldom give any thanks”*), and the Qur'an treats ingratitude as the failure that turns abundance into a case against the one enjoying it. Sūrah Ibrāhīm reports the promise attached to the opposite response: *“If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe.”* (14:7). The logic of the verse is simple enough for a child: food arrives, so someone provides it; the provider is not me, so the thanks belong elsewhere. What the Book adds is the warning that the failure to draw that conclusion is not a small oversight. It is the beginning of the disposition described in the sūrah's opening verses, in which a person eats and enjoys and is diverted by hope until the day he is told what he was eating for.

Sūrah Yūnus makes the inference explicit: *“It is Allah Who created you, then gives you provisions, then will cause you to die, and then will bring you back to life.”* (30:40).

## Verse 15:21

> There is not any means ˹of sustenance˺ whose reserves We do not hold, only bringing it forth in precise measure

**The Treasuries and Their Owner**

The Arabic word rendered *reserves* is *khazāʾin*, the storehouses of a king, and the Qur'an uses it as a fixed part of its vocabulary for God's relation to the world. Elsewhere the point is put as a rebuke to those who imagine another owner: *“Or do they possess the treasuries of your Lord, or are they in control ˹of everything˺?”* (52:37). In the story of the hypocrites, who thought withholding their spending would starve the Prophet's community: *“But to Allah ˹alone˺ belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, yet the hypocrites do not comprehend.”* (63:7). The image carries two ideas at once. A treasury implies abundance held in reserve rather than a fixed quantity that might run out, and it implies an owner with the authority to release or withhold. The verse begins there and then applies the idea to everything that exists, so that the whole creation is described as drawing on a supply that is not only enough but held by someone.

**A World Built to a Measure**

The last phrase, *in precise measure*, is the complement of the treasuries. If the supply were unlimited in its release, the world would be a formless flood; if it were left to chance, there would be no basis for gratitude or complaint. The Qur'an states the principle of measure repeatedly as a feature of creation itself: *“Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained.”* (54:49), and *“He has created everything, ordaining it precisely.”* (25:2). Sūrah al-Shūrā applies it to wealth and provision directly, explaining that God *“sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure”* (42:27), and that boundless abundance would have made people transgress. The measure in this verse is therefore not a limitation on God's generosity. It is the reason the world is livable and the reason human beings can be held responsible for what they do with what arrives. The rain is the case everyone can verify, and the Book uses it as a lesson in dependence, because water is the one provision that no human being can manufacture at will and no society can store indefinitely. Later in this sūrah the subject returns with the winds that carry and fertilise, and Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah makes the argument in a direct question: *“Have you considered the water that you drink? Is it you who sent it down from the clouds, or is it We Who send down?”* (56:68-69), followed by the warning that God could have made it bitter.

**What a Believer Owes the Reserved Supply**

The Sufi commentator Abū Saʿīd al-Kharrāz, who died in 286/899, drew from this verse a conclusion that al-Sulamī preserves: only a person who does not fully believe its truth would place his reliance on anything other than God for his worldly or his otherworldly affairs. The inference runs directly from the text, and it is a statement about ultimate reliance rather than about effort. The Qur'an nowhere commands Muslims to abandon work; Sūrah al-Jumuʿah tells them, once the prayer has ended, to *“disperse throughout the land and seek the bounty of Allah”* (62:10), and the same Book that names God the Provider names Him the one who apportions provision to whom He wills. The distinction is between means and source. A farmer sows because the treasuries are opened through causes; he trusts because holding them closed would defeat every effort he makes.

## Verse 15:22

> We send fertilizing winds, and bring down rain from the sky for you to drink. It is not you who hold its reserves

**Winds That Do a Job**

The Arabic participle rendered *fertilizing* is *lawāqiḥ*, and it is used in two senses that the commentators kept distinct and the Qur'an leaves standing together. In one sense the winds impregnate the clouds, gathering the moisture and driving it to where rain falls; in the other they pollinate the plants, carrying the pollen on which fruiting depends. Al-Ṭabrisī notes that the rain-bearing clouds are what the word describes, and the pairing is not accidental in a sūrah that has just spoken of harvests. Sūrah al-Furqān presents the same winds as *“ushering in His mercy”* (25:48), and Sūrah al-Rūm calls them bearers of good news given so that people may taste mercy and seek provision (30:46 — *“And one of His signs is that He sends the winds, ushering in good news ˹of rain˺ so that He may give you a taste of His mercy, and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and perhaps you will be grateful”*). Wind is invisible, uncountable and unowned by anybody; the verse gives it a function, a direction and a purpose, and in doing so it removes the last excuse for treating the world as a place of random events.

**"You Are Not the Keepers of Its Stores"**

The final words use the vocabulary of storage: *mā antum lahu bi-khāzinīn*, you are not its treasurers. The same root appeared in the preceding verse, which spoke of the reserves that God holds, and the connection between the two statements is deliberate. One says the supply is held by God; the other says the human being is not the one holding it. Sūrah al-Mu'minūn adds the consequence that ought to keep a person awake: *“We send down water from the sky in a precise measure and cause it to settle into the earth; and surely We are well able to take it away.”* (23:18). What humans call a reservoir is a temporary loan with no guarantee of renewal, and the Qur'an names the situation honestly instead of flattering the tenant.

**From Water to Gratitude**

The sūrah has been building an argument about provision for several verses, and this one turns it toward the human response. Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah makes the transition explicit: after asking who sends the rain, it adds, *“If We willed, We could make it salty. Will you not then give thanks?”* (56:70). The question is not sentimental. It is a claim that the ordinary glass of water on an ordinary day is evidence, and that the evidence has been arranged to be noticed rather than argued. The men who mocked the Prophet wanted angels; they were answered with wind and rain, the two things they could neither produce nor explain, and the verse leaves them holding a cup and a responsibility that no amount of cleverness can transfer to anyone else.

**Why the Qur'an Asks Instead of Asserting**

Other passages take the same material and turn it into a question, and the shift in grammar is instructive. *“Or ˹ask them,˺ ‘Who guides you in the darkness of the land and sea, and sends the winds ushering in His mercy? Is it another god besides Allah?’”* (27:63). Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah puts the same challenge to the water itself: *“Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:69). Sūrah al-Nūr describes the machinery in detail, the clouds driven and joined and piled, the hail poured where God wills and turned aside from where He wills (24:43 — *“And He sends down from the sky mountains ˹of clouds˺ loaded with hail, pouring it on whoever He wills and averting it from whoever He wills.”*), and Sūrah al-Raʿd adds the image of valleys flowing each to its own capacity (13:17 — *“He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity.”*). The verse under commentary is the plain statement of what those passages ask. The Book prefers the question because the answer is not in dispute until a person is asked directly, and because the audience being addressed already knew who they thanked for rain.

Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt describes what the same element did to the people of ʿĀd: *“And in ˹the story of˺ ’Âd ˹was another lesson,˺ when We sent against them the devastating wind.”* (51:41).

## Verse 15:23

> Surely it is We Who give life and cause death. And We are the ˹Eternal˺ Successor

**Two Acts Held by One Agent**

The Arabic packs its emphasis twice into a short sentence, and the effect is to place the beginning and the end of every living creature in a single pair of hands. Life is given, death is caused, and both verbs have the same subject. This is not a comment on biology; it is a claim about ownership. Sūrah Ghāfir states the point in its sharpest form: *“He is the One Who gives life and causes death. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!”* (40:68).

**The Name at the End of the Verse**

The verse closes with a title, *al-Wārith*, the Inheritor, and the image is legal rather than botanical. An heir is the one who remains when the other holders of a property have gone; he does not acquire anything the owner did not have, he simply outlasts every other claim. Applied to God, the name means that everything which exists will pass out of the hands of its temporary holders and return to the One whose it always was. Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ states it plainly: *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself. All authority belongs to Him. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (28:88), and Sūrah al-Raḥmān puts the two halves of the thought in consecutive verses: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺.”* (55:26-27). The name is a statement about the end of the story, and the sūrah has placed it directly after a verse about water.

**Life and Death in the Sūrah's Argument**

The placement of this verse inside a passage about rain, harvest and sustenance is deliberate. The sūrah has been arguing from the visible to the invisible: the sky is furnished, the earth produces in measure, the water is sent down and its reserves are not held by the people who drink them. Now it names the agent behind all of that as the giver of life and death, and the reasoning becomes visible. Anyone who has watched a dead field turn green after rain has seen the pattern that the resurrection repeats on a larger scale. Sūrah al-Rūm draws the inference explicitly: *“See then the impact of Allah's mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead.”* (30:50). The verse under commentary supplies the principle, and the sūrah's later verses will supply the scene.

**Every Creature Standing Between Two Decrees**

Read on its own, the sentence is a summary of the condition of every living thing. Each of them is between an act that has already happened and an act that has not yet happened, and neither is under its own control. Sūrah al-Mu'minūn ties the same power to the ordinary rhythm of the day: *“And He is the One Who gives life and causes death, and to Him belongs the alternation of the day and night.”* (23:80). The alternation is the point: night falls on everyone at once, without regard for plans. A person who has understood this verse does not become gloomy, because the Qur'an never treats mortality as a reason for despair. What the verse removes is the illusion of self-possession, and what it leaves in its place is a creature who knows whom he is dealing with.

Sūrah al-Ḥadīd states the pairing of power and mortality in a single breath — to Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, He gives life and causes death, He is capable of everything (57:2 — *“To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And He is Most Capable of everything”*).

## Verse 15:24

> We certainly know those who have gone before you and those who will come after ˹you˺

**A Knowledge That Makes Anonymity Impossible**

Most wrongdoing is possible because of an audience. A person behaves differently when watched, and he assumes that what is hidden will stay hidden. The Qur'an repeatedly dismantles the assumption, and this verse is one of its cleanest statements: the ones who came before, the ones who are here, and the ones who will come after are all in view. Sūrah al-Mu'minūn adds that God also knows the mechanics of every life: *“No female ever conceives or delivers without His knowledge.”* (35:11). The result is not a surveillance state but an universe in which nothing is actually private, including the intentions behind the acts. The sūrah's mockers were laughing in a public street; the verse quietly informs them that the laughing was not the part that mattered most, and that the audience they had not considered was already in attendance.

**The Generations on Both Sides of the Present**

Human beings experience history as a narrow window. A person knows his own decade, remembers something of his parents' generation, and imagines little about the ones behind him or the ones ahead. The verse removes that window as a limit on God's attention, and it does so at the exact point in the sūrah where the audience is being told that the dead will be raised. Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah makes the same distinction in its account of the foremost, of whom *“˹They will be˺ a multitude from earlier generations and a few from later generations.”* (56:13-14). The point is not the arithmetic. It is that the earliest and the latest generations are counted together in one book, and that a person's position on the timeline gives him no advantage in the reckoning.

**Those Who Press Forward and Those Who Hold Back**

Read as a description of conduct, the verse touches a nerve that any religious community recognises. Every assembly contains people who move at very different speeds: some take on the obligations with an appetite, some do the minimum, and some are present in body only. The Book says here that the difference is seen, named and measured. Sūrah al-Tawbah uses the same vocabulary of precedence to describe the earliest believers: *“As for the foremost—the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers—and those who follow them in goodness, Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him.”* (9:100). Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah places *“the foremost ˹in faith˺”* first among the ranks of Paradise (56:10 — *“and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺”*). The verse therefore functions as encouragement rather than surveillance alone. To be known as one of those who went ahead is, in the Book's economy, a honour that nobody can confer or withdraw.

**Nothing Small Enough to Escape the Record**

The Qur'an's fullest description of this knowledge appears in Sūrah al-Anʿām, and it reads like an answer to anyone who imagines that God's attention operates at the level of nations rather than persons: *“With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him. And He knows what is in the land and sea. Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.”* (6:59). The leaf and the grain are the point. If the fall of a single leaf is registered, then the categories of people named in the verse under commentary — the earlier generations and the later ones — are not being tracked as groups. They are known one by one, with what each of them did. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ attaches the same certainty to the daily conduct of a family and ends with the words *“Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.”* (4:1).

## Verse 15:25

> Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ will gather them together ˹for judgment˺. He is truly All-Wise, All-Knowing

**The Same Assurance in Other Sūrahs**

The Book repeats the promise with the same vocabulary of certainty whenever it addresses doubt about the resurrection. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān puts it in the form of a prayer that is also a confession: *“Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt.”* (3:9). Sūrah al-Anʿām goes further and grounds it in God's own attribute: *“He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment— about which there is no doubt.”* (6:12). The gathering, in other words, is not only something God can do; it is something He has committed Himself to, and the commitment is stated as an expression of mercy rather than of menace. The verse under commentary belongs to that family of statements, and it carries the same reassurance: the assembly is being convened because justice requires it.

**Why the Objection Was So Stubborn**

The Makkan objection to the resurrection was not a philosophical puzzle; it was a refusal that carried social consequences, since a reckoning after death would change how a person could live in Makkah. The Book records the argument in its opponents' own words elsewhere: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’”* (36:78). The sūrah's answer here is subtler. It does not begin with power over dust; it begins with knowledge of persons. The men being addressed were being told that their names, families, dealings and arguments were all held by someone who had no trouble tracking generations that had never met. If the objection had been that a just God would not bother, the names at the end of the verse answer it. If the objection was that he would not know, the same two names answer that too.

**The Visible Version of the Same Claim**

The Qur'an habitually argues for the raising of the dead from the greening of the earth, and the sūrah has just spent several verses on rain falling on dry ground. Sūrah al-Rūm draws the conclusion aloud: *“See then the impact of Allah's mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead.”* (30:50), and Sūrah Fuṣṣilat says the same with an earlier stage of the process in view: *“you see the earth devoid of life, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell.”* (41:39). A farmer watching a field come back does not regard the event as a suspension of nature; he regards it as the normal work of the One who made the field. The verse under commentary is the doctrinal statement of what the farmer has already seen.

**The Answer Given to the Decayed Bones**

Sūrah Yā Sīn reports the same objection and answers it with two arguments that belong together, and both of them are compressed into the names at the end of this verse. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being.’”* (36:79). The first argument is the precedent of creation; the second is knowledge of what has been created, down to the last particle. The sūrah then adds an observation from ordinary life, that the same God who gives fire from green wood can surely reconstitute what He assembled once already (36:80 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who gives you fire from green trees, and—behold!—you kindle ˹fire˺ from them”*). Sūrah al-Naḥl states the timing without hesitation: bringing about the Hour would take the blink of an eye or less, and God is capable of everything (16:77 — *“Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less.”*). The men asking the question were measuring a resurrection against their own difficulty, not His.

## Verse 15:26

> Indeed, We created man from sounding clay moulded from black mud

**The Several Names the Qur'an Gives the Clay**

The Book describes this beginning in several ways, and the variation repays attention. Al-Anʿām speaks simply of clay (6:2 — *“He is the One Who created you from clay, then appointed a term ˹for your death˺ and another known only to Him ˹for your resurrection˺—yet you continue to doubt”*), al-Mu'minūn of *“an extract of clay”* (23:12), al-Ṣāffāt of *“a sticky clay”* (37:11), and al-Sajdah of God originating human creation from clay before perfecting it (32:7 — *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay”*). Some exegetes read the different terms as stages in a single process — dust, then water, then a sticky mixture, then a dried form — while others read them as aspects of the same material described from different angles. The variation also matches the Book's general habit of describing central events repeatedly, in different settings, so that each telling brings out a different implication. Here the emphasis falls on the forming and the dryness; in the sūrah's later narrative the same material will return as an argument in a dispute.

**One Origin as the Foundation of Human Equality**

If every person traces back to a lump of shaped mud, no family, tribe or nation has a claim to superior substance. The Qur'an states the consequence elsewhere in the plainest terms: *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul. From it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women.”* (4:1), and Sūrah al-Ḥujurāt draws the social conclusion: *“Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another. Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you.”* (49:13). The verse under commentary gives the material fact on which those two statements rest. In a city where lineage was currency, the reminder that the founding ancestor was clay was not a poetic flourish; it was an argument about how people should treat one another.

**The Fuller Account of How a Person Is Made**

One passage sets out the process in a sequence rather than a single image, and it is addressed precisely to people who doubted that they could be brought back: *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—to show ˹Our power˺ to you.”* (22:5). The same passage goes on to describe the settling of the embryo in the womb for an appointed term and the stages of life that follow, ending with the feeblest age, when a person knows nothing after having known much. Set beside the verse under commentary, the point is cumulative. The Book takes a being who began as dark, moulded earth and traces every stage of his formation as an act of the same will, then asks him how he supposes that will has become incapable. Sūrah al-Rūm adds the diversity of languages and colours as further evidence of a creator who is not short of means (30:22 — *“And one of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages and colours.”*).

Sūrah al-Raḥmān makes the image explicit by comparing it to pottery: *“He created humankind from ˹sounding˺ clay like pottery”* (55:14).

## Verse 15:27

> As for the jinn, We created them earlier from smokeless fire

**What the Word "Jinn" Names**

The Arabic root behind *jinn* carries the sense of concealment, which is why the word is used both for these creatures and for anything hidden from sight. The Qur'an treats the jinn as a created order with the same moral accountability as human beings: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56). They hear, they choose, they are addressed by revelation — Sūrah al-Jinn opens with the report that a group of them listened to the Qur'an and went back to their people as warners (72:1 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened ˹to the Quran,˺ and said ˹to their fellow jinn˺: ‘Indeed, we have heard a wondrous recitation”*). Nothing in the Book supports the picture of these creatures as a class of magical servants summoned by human beings. What the Book insists on is that they are a community with obligations, and that the traffic between them and human beings was never authorised.

**Fire and Clay in the Wrong Hands**

The material described in this verse becomes, a few lines later in the sūrah's narrative, the basis of an argument, and the Book has already recorded how that argument was made elsewhere: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* (7:12; 38:76 — *“He replied, “I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”*). That inference is rejected in the strongest terms, and the rejection has consequences for the way a Muslim reads this verse. Fire is not nobler than clay; both are created things, and the difference between them says nothing about the rank of a person or a spirit before God. The verse under commentary gives the material fact without endorsing any hierarchy, and the reader is expected to hold both halves of the lesson: yes, the jinn were created from fire, and no, that fact entitles them to nothing. Rank in this Book is measured by obedience and by consequence, not by composition.

**An Unseen Order and the Limits of Human Knowing**

The Qur'an mentions the jinn often enough to establish that they exist and rarely enough to leave the details alone. It does not describe their societies, their lifespans, their appearance or their numbers. The information given is always attached to a moral purpose: to show that the world is larger than human perception, to warn against consulting them for hidden knowledge, and to correct the human habit of assuming that reality ends where human senses end. A believer who takes the Book seriously receives an account of the world with more inhabitants than he can see, and with no invitation to speculate about them. What he is told is enough for his conduct, and the Book's reticence about everything else is itself a lesson in how to handle knowledge of the unseen. The jinn themselves are shown to be ignorant of what people wrongly credited them with. Sūrah Sabaʾ reports that when Solomon died, *“nothing indicated to the ˹subjected˺ jinn that he was dead except the termites eating away his staff”*, and it draws the conclusion in their own case: *“if they had ˹really˺ known the unseen, they would not have remained in ˹such˺ humiliating servitude.”* (34:14). Sūrah al-Naml shows one of them offering Solomon a service he could perform but making no claim to hidden knowledge (27:39 — *“One mighty jinn responded, “I can bring it to you before you rise from this council of yours. And I am quite strong and trustworthy for this ˹task˺.”*). Whatever power these creatures have, the Book never grants them the one thing human beings were most eager to buy from them.

The material is described as a flame that gives no smoke, and the Qur'an uses the same phrase in Sūrah al-Raḥmān: *“and created jinn from a ˹smokeless˺ flame of fire.”* (55:15).

## Verse 15:28

> ˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to create a human being from sounding clay moulded from black mud

**The Announcement Made Before the Act**

God does not create the human being first and explain afterwards. He states the intention to the angels before the act, and the Qur'an preserves the wording: I am going to create. The same pattern appears in the fuller account in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where the announcement names the purpose — *“I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.”* (2:30) — and the angels respond with the question that shows they had grasped what such a creature might do: *“Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?”* (2:30). The answer given there, that God knows what they do not know, is withheld here. This telling is not concerned with the objection. It is concerned with what the human being is made of, and the announcement delivers that fact immediately.

**The Angels as Witnesses to Their Own Test**

The announcement puts the angels in a position they had not occupied before. The Book mentions their exact standing elsewhere: *“And to Allah ˹alone˺ bows down ˹in submission˺ whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures, as do the angels—who are not too proud ˹to do so˺.”* (16:49).

**What the Announcement Says About Human Worth**

A creature made of dark moulded clay is announced in the divine presence before he exists. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ states the outcome of that decision in a sentence that every human being inherits: *“Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures.”* (17:70). Nothing in that dignity comes from the material of the body or from anything the person has done. It was conferred in advance, before the first human being drew breath, in an announcement to an assembly that had already been serving its Lord without interruption. That is the shape of honour in the Qur'an: it arrives as a gift and a responsibility, and the only question the Book ever asks about it is what a person does with what he was given rather than what he was made of.

**One Material for the Whole Species**

The announcement speaks of a human being, and the material named belongs to no one in particular. Every member of the species is described in the same terms elsewhere: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20). Sūrah Ghāfir traces the whole of a life from that dust through its stages to old age and an appointed end (40:67 — *“He is the One Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old—though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah’s power˺”*). The effect of the shared origin is to make the pride of lineage and the pride of nation absurd on their face. A man who boasts about his family is making a claim about the quality of mud, and the Qur'an has already told him whose mud it was. The verse under commentary establishes the material before any human being exists to take credit for it, and the equality that Muslim scholars derived from this narrative — that no people has a claim to substance over another — rests on this announcement to the angels.

## Verse 15:29

> So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him, fall down in prostration to him.”

**Two Acts Before the Command**

The sentence places two divine acts before it asks anything of anyone. The first is fashioning: the Arabic verb describes giving something its due proportion, so that every part is fitted to its purpose. The second is the breathing of the spirit, which turns the shaped form into a living being. Nothing in the order is accidental — the body is prepared and then animated, and only after both acts does the command go out. Sūrah al-Sajdah states the same sequence with an added phrase about the work being done properly: *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay.”* (32:7). Perfection here is a statement about craft, not about material. The same hands that shaped a vessel from the earth's dark mud made it exactly as it needed to be for what was going to be placed inside it.

**A Command Addressed to Those Present**

What follows the two acts is an imperative: fall down before him. The command is plural and it is issued to the assembled angels, and everything that happens in the next verses is a response to those four words. The Book states the event in five other places — al-Baqarah, al-Aʿrāf, al-Isrāʾ, al-Kahf and Ṣād — with the same outcome each time. The Qur'an does not soften the command or explain why a being made of clay should be bowed to; it reports the order and lets the response of the creatures reveal what kind of creatures they are. Sūrah al-Kahf adds a detail that widens the scene: the command went to the angels, and one among them who was of the jinn rebelled against his Lord's command (18:50 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord.”*). The verse under commentary gives only the order, and the order is unconditional.

**Why the Prostration Is Not Worship of a Creature**

A question arises for any reader: how can a creature be asked to bow to another creature? The Qur'an itself shows prostration functioning as a mark of respect among human beings in the story of Joseph, whose family fell down before him in fulfilment of his dream: *“Then he raised his parents to the throne, and they all fell down in prostration to Joseph.”* (12:100).

**The Same Command Told Six Times**

The Book returns to this moment again and again: the command to bow, the general obedience, and the single refusal. Al-Baqarah records it as a covenant (2:34 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful”*), al-Aʿrāf as part of the shaping of the human being (7:11 — *“Surely We created you, then shaped you, then said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused to prostrate with the others”*), al-Isrāʾ and Ṭā Hā as a reminder to people who would rather follow the refuser, and Ṣād as a scene that begins with God's announcement of the creation (38:71-72 — *“˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to create a human being from clay So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him, fall down in prostration to him.”*). The repetition serves the Qur'an's habit of anchoring the human story at its beginning: every debate about obedience, every temptation, every argument about who deserves authority, leads back to the moment when a command was given to beings who could see what human beings cannot. The verse under commentary contributes the two acts that made the command reasonable — the shaping and the breathing — and then leaves the reader to watch what the angels do.

**The Breath, the Trust, and the Accountability**

What the breathing of the spirit produces is not only a living body but a responsible creature. The Qur'an states the weight of that arrangement in a verse about the trust that the rest of creation refused: *“Indeed, We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they ˹all˺ declined to bear it, being fearful of it. But humanity assumed it, ˹for˺ they are truly wrongful ˹to themselves˺ and ignorant ˹of the consequences˺.”* (33:72). The passage does not say precisely which trust is meant, and the commentators debated it, but the human capacity to obey or refuse — the very capacity being tested in this scene — is the thing that makes the offer meaningful.

## Verse 15:30

> So the angels prostrated all together—

**The Obedience That Needed No Discussion**

The command was given, and the verse reports the response in a single clause with no interval. Sheer obedience is all. The Book elsewhere describes the angels as creatures whose obedience is not an achievement but their condition. The descriptions are consistent: they do not speak until God has spoken and they act only on His command (21:27 — *“who do not speak until He has spoken, ˹only˺ acting at His command”*), they fear their Lord and do whatever they are commanded (16:50 — *“They fear their Lord above them, and do whatever they are commanded”*), they never disobey whatever God orders and always do as commanded (66:6 — *“O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”*). The verse under commentary is that description in action. No question is raised, no condition attached, no delay recorded between the imperative of the previous verse and the prostration of this one. In a sūrah whose audience was arguing about signs, the contrast between an assembly that moves when told and a city that demands explanations is unmistakable.

**The Angels' Standing in the Order of Creation**

Honour belongs to the angels in the Qur'an — they are described as honoured servants (21:26 — *“And they say, “The Most Compassionate has offspring!” Glory be to Him! In fact, those ˹angels˺ are only ˹His˺ honoured servants”*) and as the nearest to God (4:172 — *“The Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels nearest to Allah.”*) — and the verse's point is not that they are unimpressive. It is that their standing never becomes a reason for standing apart. They are witnesses to the arrangement of the world and they carry out its business, and yet when the command comes to bow before a creature of clay, they bow. The Book records the divine rebuke to any creature that would think rank excuses obedience: *“The Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels nearest to Allah.”* (4:172). Two of the highest created stations, a prophet and the closest angels, are named in one sentence precisely because pride at the top of the order would be the most destructive kind.

**An Identical Line in Another Sūrah**

Sūrah Ṣād contains the same scene and, at this point in the story, brings the same words over into its own account: after the announcement of the creation of a human being from clay and the breathing of the spirit, it records *“So the angels prostrated all together—”* (38:73), and then, in the following verse, the single exception. The two sūrahs run in parallel for several verses because both are retelling one event to two different audiences, and the reader who notices the overlap is meant to notice the difference in what each sūrah surrounds it with. Al-Ḥijr places the scene in the middle of a passage about signs in creation; Ṣād places it where a proud people are being reminded that the first refusal of God's command came from a creature who thought he was made of something better.

**Service as the Angels' Constant State**

The prostration in this verse is one visible instance of a service that runs continuously in the Book's picture of the world. The angels carry out the decrees of God in the affairs of creation, and Sūrah al-Qadr describes their movement on the night of the decree: *“That night the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every ˹decreed˺ matter.”* (97:4). Sūrah al-Nabaʾ pictures them standing in ranks on the day of reckoning, silent until given permission to speak (78:38 — *“on the Day the ˹holy˺ spirit and the angels will stand in ranks. None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true”*). Everything recorded about them is obedience, ranked and disciplined, and the permission clause that recurs in these descriptions matters: even their speech waits on a grant. The scene in this sūrah is therefore not an isolated act by creatures who usually do something else. It is the standing order of their existence applied to a single moment, which is why the obedience is described in one clause without a word of explanation.

## Verse 15:31

> but not Iblîs, who refused to prostrate with the others

**Refusal as a Chosen Act**

The verb used here is *abā*, which means to refuse with the will. The Book pairs it elsewhere with pride so that the reader never mistakes it for weakness of understanding: *“And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Adam,’ so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful.”* (2:34). Sūrah al-Kahf describes the same act from the standpoint of loyalty: he *“was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord”* (18:50). Nothing in any account suggests that he misunderstood the order, doubted who gave it, or failed to hear it. He knew the command, understood it, and declined. This is why the sūrah will spend the next verses on his reasoning: the Book is not describing a mistake but a position, argued and defended, and the argument is the most dangerous thing about it.

**Pride, the Root That Does Not Show**

The Book names the motive in the parallel accounts and leaves it implicit here. He refused, and he did so out of a sense of his own superiority, which is what the source of every similar refusal has always been. Sūrah al-Naḥl states God's attitude in a single sentence: *“Without a doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal. He certainly does not like those who are too proud.”* (16:23). Pride in this vocabulary is not high self-regard alone; it is the belief that a creature stands above an obligation. That belief is what made a being who had spent his existence in the presence of God capable of setting his own assessment of a command over the command itself. The verse reports the act in four words, and the rest of the sūrah will show what such a belief produces.

**The Refusal That Became a Pattern**

The Book treats this refusal as the beginning of a division that runs through human history. Iblīs expected to win followers, and Sūrah Sabaʾ reports the outcome in a sentence that reads like a ledger: *“Indeed, Iblîs' assumption about them has come true, so they ˹all˺ follow him, except a group of ˹true˺ believers.”* (34:20). The exceptions receive as much attention in the Qur'an as the majority, because the whole contest is about which of them a person joins. Iblīs is also described as the one who makes wrongdoing attractive, and the Book's vocabulary for his work — whispering, promising, adorning — all proceeds from the posture he adopted in this verse. He was the first creature to decide that a command from God could be assessed rather than obeyed, and the consequences were not limited to himself. Every later refusal draws on his example, which is why the Book names him in connection with those who follow him rather than treating the scene as a private quarrel in an invisible assembly.

## Verse 15:32

> Allah asked, “O Iblîs! What is the matter with you that you did not join others in prostration?”

**A Hearing Before Any Sentence**

No punishment is announced in this verse, and none of the three accounts passes sentence before the creature has spoken. The Book's judicial manner is worth noticing here because it recurs at the final judgment, where the condemned are asked questions they cannot answer — *“Did a warner not come to you?”* (67:8) — and reply that warners did come and were rejected (67:9 — *“They will reply, “Yes, a warner did come to us, but we denied and said, ‘Allah has revealed nothing. You are extremely astray.’”*). Those questions do not gather information either; they make the record public in the accused's own voice. Applied to Iblīs, the method shows that his expulsion was not a hasty reaction to a wrong step. He was asked, he was heard, and he used the hearing to argue his case. The severity of what follows is the severity of a verdict that could not have been different, because the creature had stated his position and refused to revise it.

**A Question From the One Who Needs No Answer**

The verse records God addressing the creature by name and asking him why he is not among those who bowed. Nothing about the question is an inquiry after information, since the Book everywhere states that God knows what is hidden and what is revealed. The interrogative is a summons. It gives the offender the floor and requires him to say aloud what he has decided, so that the position he holds is brought out of concealment and into his own words. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf uses the same method — *“What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you?”* (7:12) — and Sūrah Ṣād presses it further: *“Did you ˹just˺ become proud? Or have you always been arrogant?”* (38:75). Across the three accounts, God asks, and the creature answers, and the answer is his own judgement of himself.

**The Chance He Did Not Take**

Everything in the scene gives room for a return. He is addressed, named, asked, and given the opportunity to speak, and the Book records no threat that would have made a honest answer costly. A creature who had understood the situation could have said that he had failed an obligation, and the record of Adam's response in the same story shows where such an answer leads: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we shall surely be among the losers.”* (7:23). The question in this verse is therefore a test of what the creature will do with a chance that he will never have again.

**The Question Put to Adam and Eve**

The same manner of questioning appears later in the story, and the difference in outcome shows what the question was for. After the first couple had been misled and had covered themselves with leaves, *“their Lord called out to them, ‘Did I not forbid you from that tree and ˹did I not˺ tell you that Satan is your sworn enemy?’”* (7:22). They were asked a question and they answered with a confession, and the Book records the result immediately: *“Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.”* (2:37). Al-Ḥijr does not narrate that part of the story, but a reader who knows it can see the two answers standing side by side. The God who asks is the same in both scenes. What differs is the answer, and the Book lets the contrast speak without comment.

**A Sending That Leaves No Excuse Behind**

The pattern of asking before judging is not limited to the beginning of the story. The Book states it as a general principle of how God deals with communities: *“This ˹sending of the messengers˺ is because your Lord would never destroy a society for their wrongdoing while its people are unaware ˹of the truth˺.”* (6:131). The question put to Iblīs is the first instance of that principle in the sūrah's narrative: the creature knows, the creature is addressed, and the creature is given the floor. The same care that makes a warning precede a punishment shapes this interrogation.

## Verse 15:33

> He replied, “It is not for me to prostrate to a human You created from sounding clay moulded from black mud.”

**An Answer to a Question That Was Not Asked**

He was asked why he had not joined the prostrators. He replies by explaining what he is not prepared to bow to, and the reply never touches the actual question of his obligation to obey. This is the structure of the refusal in all three accounts, and the fuller versions show what lies behind it: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* (7:12; 38:76 — *“He replied, “I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”*). The shape of the argument is a comparison between two materials, followed by a conclusion about rank. What is missing from the argument is the only premise that mattered — that a command from God settles an obligation regardless of what the creature makes of the reasoning behind it. The verse reports the answer as it was given, and the reader is expected to see that it is not an answer at all.

**Fire and Clay: a Syllogism With a Missing Premise**

The reasoning has the form of a syllogism: fire is nobler than clay; I was created from fire; therefore I am nobler than the one created from clay; therefore it is not for me to bow. The Qur'an's own test of rank is stated in a verse addressed to all humanity: *“Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you.”* (49:13).

**What Knowledge Has to Do With Rank**

Where the Book does compare people, it compares what they carry rather than what they were made from. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Are those who know equal to those who do not know?’”* (39:9). The comparison in that verse is between knowledge and ignorance, both of which are things a person can acquire or neglect, and neither of which any creature is born holding as a birthright. Iblīs made the opposite move: he treated an origin he did not choose as a title he had earned. The Qur'an nowhere denies that the materials of creation differ in their properties — fire and clay behave differently, and the Book says so plainly. What it refuses is the leap from a difference in composition to a difference in worth, and the refusal is definitive: the creature who tried to make that leap was answered with expulsion rather than with an argument.

**The Command, Not the Reason, Was the Test**

The deepest trouble with the reply is that it substitutes analysis for obedience. He was not asked to understand why a being of clay should be bowed to; he was told to bow. The Book states the principle that his answer violated in a verse about the community of believers: *“It is not for a believing man or woman—when Allah and His Messenger decree a matter—to have any other choice in that matter.”* (33:36). Submission that waits for a satisfactory explanation is not submission but review, and review is not a posture a creature can hold toward its maker.

**The Reply He Was Not Able to Give**

The Qur'an preserves another answer to a similar accusation, spoken by a human being who was being pressed to deny his creator. A companion in the story of the two men in the garden asks, *“Do you disbelieve in the One Who created you from dust, then ˹developed you˺ from a sperm-drop, then formed you into a man?”* (18:37). The argument is the opposite of the one Iblīs made: it reasons from the same material — dust, then a drop, then a formed human — to the goodness and power of the maker, rather than from material to personal superiority.

---

## Verse 15:34

> Allah commanded, “Then get out of Paradise, for you are truly cursed

**"Paradise" and What Leaving It Meant**

The place he is ordered out of is the elevated garden, the dwelling of the angels and the setting of the scene, and the loss is total. In the parallel telling, God adds a detail that makes the reason explicit: *“Then get down from Paradise! It is not for you to be arrogant here. So get out! You are truly one of the disgraced.”* (7:13). The location was not compatible with the attitude. Arrogance in a place where every creature is a servant, among angels whose service is unbroken, was a contradiction waiting to be resolved, and the resolution was his removal. The verse does not describe the regret, the looking back, or any of the small humiliations that a reader might imagine. It records the command and moves on, which gives the sentence more weight rather than less.

**The Word "Cursed" and Where It Was Used Before**

The Arabic term is *rajīm*, the same word this sūrah used eleven verses earlier when it said the sky was protected *“from every accursed devil”* (15:17). The connection is exact and probably deliberate. What was described there as a fact about the sky — that the being and his kind are excluded from the highest company — appears here as part of his sentence in the same vocabulary. The title is not a new name invented for the occasion; it is a condition already in force, now stated to his face. The Book also attaches the term to any creature that misleads, which is why a Muslim says the same word when seeking refuge before reciting: *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.”* (16:98). The command to leave in this verse is where that condition was declared.

**The Pride That Was the Real Cause**

The Book states the motive repeatedly, and it does not reduce it to a bad decision made in a moment. Al-Baqarah says he *“refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful”* (2:34), and al-Aʿrāf records the words that show the arrogance in operation. Pride in the Qur'an is not simply a high opinion of oneself; it is the refusal to accept a command on the grounds that it does not suit one's station, and that is precisely what happened here. Sūrah al-Naḥl states God's attitude toward the condition in a sentence that any reader can carry away: *“God certainly does not like those who are too proud.”* (16:23, substance). The creature's expulsion from the garden is the outward form of an inward state that made the place impossible for him, and the sentence makes visible what had already happened inside.

**A Verdict With No Route Back in the Same Scene**

The Book records what Adam and Eve did after their mistake, and the contrast illuminates this verse. When they were confronted, *“they replied, ‘Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers.’”* (7:23), and they were forgiven. Iblīs makes no equivalent move. He does not ask for pardon, confess a fault, or acknowledge the command he refused, and the sentence he receives is spoken in a scene with no route back inside it. The Book leaves the reader to draw the comparison, and the comparison is severe. The difference between the two cases is not the size of the wrong but the direction of the response, and this verse is the point at which the second possibility closes for the creature who chose the first.

---

## Verse 15:35

> And surely upon you is condemnation until the Day of Judgment.”

**What Condemnation Means in the Qur'an**

The word rendered *condemnation* is *laʿnah*, and everywhere the Book uses it the sense is removal from mercy rather than the infliction of pain. Sūrah al-Baqarah describes those who die in rejection as *“condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.”* (2:161), and Sūrah al-Aḥzāb states the same of those who offend God and His Messenger, adding that the condemnation continues in this world and the next (33:57 — *“Surely those who offend Allah and His Messenger are condemned by Allah in this world and the Hereafter. And He has prepared for them a humiliating punishment”*). When the term is applied to Iblīs, then, the claim is that the creature has been severed from the thing he had forfeited by his own act, and the rest of what follows in his story is the working out of a condition already set. The Qur'an never describes him as weak, foolish or ignorant. What it describes is a being cut off from mercy and aware of it.

**The Hadith on Pride and the Garment It Vies For**

The Prophet is reported to have related the words of God about the root of this whole scene: *“Pride is My cloak and greatness My robe. He who competes with Me in respect of either of them I shall cast into Hell-fire.”* The report is in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4090, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī, with a parallel in Sunan Ibn Mājah 4174, and it is included as the nineteenth of the well-known Forty Hadith Qudsi. The image explains the verdict in this verse. A cloak belongs to the one wearing it, and a creature that drapes itself in greatness has taken up an attribute that has no second owner. What Iblīs asserted in the previous verses was an assessment of his own station; the report names that assertion as a rivalry, and the casting out described here is the outcome the report predicts.

**The Same Curse in the Parallel Accounts**

Sūrah Ṣād gives the sentence in nearly the same words, with the possessive added: *“And surely upon you is My condemnation until the Day of Judgment.”* (38:78). Al-Aʿrāf records the expulsion with a different emphasis, ending with the words *“You are truly one of the disgraced.”* (7:13), and adding, in the verse that reports his request for time, that the request came from a creature already under sentence. The consistency across three tellings is striking. Each one keeps the same elements: the order to leave, the condition of being outcast, and a duration that ends at the day of recompense. The variations are small, and none of them softens the sentence into a temporary lesson. The Book reports the outcome the same way every time it tells the story.

**The Contrast With the Response of Adam**

The Book records how human beings handled their own failure, and putting the two responses side by side shows what this curse was for. Adam and his wife, once they had slipped, said *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers.”* (7:23), and the Book reports that God turned toward them. The expelled creature never makes such a request; in the verses that follow, he asks only for time. The sentence in this verse is therefore not a sentence passed on a creature who begged and was refused. It is the outcome for one who did not beg. The Book records the sequence of both stories in the same chapter of revelation so that no reader can miss what was available and what was chosen instead.

## Verse 15:36

> Satan appealed, “My Lord! Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.”

**The Plea That Asks for Time and Not Pardon**

Of all the things a creature under sentence could request, he asks for an extension. There is no apology in the sentence, no admission of fault, no request for the removal of the curse; he asks to be kept alive. The parallel accounts keep exactly the same wording: *“He appealed, ‘Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.’”* (7:14), and *“Satan appealed, ‘My Lord! Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.’”* (38:79). What makes the absence of a plea for forgiveness so striking is that the Book has already shown what a plea looks like when it is made. His own refusal to make one, after being addressed and questioned, is the fullest demonstration of the arrogance that had just been condemned in him.

**"My Lord": the Submission He Could Not Abandon**

The appeal opens with the words *my Lord*, and the address matters. The creature who has just refused an order, argued against it, and been expelled for it still calls God his Lord. He does not claim sovereignty, deny his maker, or set himself up as an alternative deity. Sūrah al-Baqarah states plainly what his situation became when it describes him as *“becoming unfaithful”* (2:34, ending), and the Book elsewhere records his admission that God is the one who created him. His rebellion is therefore not unbelief in the ordinary sense. It is a monstrous inconsistency: a creature that fully acknowledges who owns him, refuses to obey, argues about rank instead, and then asks that owner for more life. The Qur'an records all of it, and the intelligence displayed in the asking is part of the warning.

**Despair Turned Into a Program**

The lexicographers connected the name Iblīs with an Arabic root that carries the sense of despair, and whether or not the etymology is accepted, the psychology fits the verses: a creature with no hope of return, still powerful, still informed, and now devoted to making sure that others join him. Sūrah Sabaʾ reports the outcome he anticipated, that most follow him and only a group of true believers hold out (34:20 — *“Indeed, Iblîs’ assumption about them has come true, so they ˹all˺ follow him, except a group of ˹true˺ believers”*). The plea in this verse is the moment his grievance acquires a plan. He cannot wound the One who expelled him, so the world that was made for human beings becomes the field of his campaign. Everything the Book will say about his methods — whispering, promising, dressing wrongdoing as attractive — grows out of this short sentence asking for time.

**The Longer Version of the Same Vow**

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ records the vow with the details supplied: *“cursed by Allah—who said, ‘I will surely take hold of a certain number of Your servants. I will certainly mislead them and delude them with empty hopes. Also, I will order them and they will slit the ears of cattle and alter Allah's creation.’”* (4:118-119), and it closes with a verdict on anyone who takes him as a guardian instead of God. The addition of empty hopes is important, because it names the currency he trades in. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ puts the same list of methods in the form of an order given to him — incitement, cavalry and infantry, partnership in wealth and children, promises — and then states what the promises are worth: nothing but delusion (17:64, ending — *“But Satan promises them nothing but delusion”*). Set beside the verse under commentary, the shorter telling loses none of its meaning. He asked for time; the fuller accounts show what he intended to do with it.

## Verse 15:37

> Allah said, “You will be delayed

**The Same Verb, Two Opposite Outcomes**

The word used in this verse for being delayed comes from the same root that appears earlier in the sūrah, where the men who demanded a sign from the sky were told that when the angels come *“the disbelievers will not be delayed”* (15:8). One verse announces the end of postponement for a mocking town; this one announces its grant to a rebellious spirit. The Book uses one vocabulary for a single reality: time is granted and time is withdrawn, and the difference lies completely with the decree of the One who sets the terms. There is no contradiction here to resolve, and the commentators treat the two verses as parts of one doctrine.

**A Request Granted in Four Words**

The answer to the plea is one clause, and it contains no rebuke, no condition, and no further discussion of the argument the creature had made about his own composition. He asked for time, and time was given. This is the same economy the sūrah shows in the parallel accounts: al-Aʿrāf records the exchange and its answer without commentary (7:14-15 — *“He appealed, “Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.” Allah said, “You are delayed ˹until the appointed Day˺.”*), and Ṣād repeats the grant in identical terms (38:80 — *“Allah said, “You will be delayed”*). The Book's manner here is worth attention. When the creatures of the Qur'an make large demands or large pronouncements, the response is often surprisingly short, because the response is not the argument. Here the brevity itself is a message: the request was never in dispute, and nothing about being granted it changes what the creature is or what he has done.

**The Form of the Grant**

The Arabic is an active statement of granting, and the phrase the translation gives is a single clause about his future condition. Later in the sūrah, the same exchange appears in al-Aʿrāf in the form of a sentence that enrols him among a class: you are among those granted delay (7:15 — *“Allah said, “You are delayed ˹until the appointed Day˺.”*). The versions differ in this small way, and the difference is instructive. One tells him that he will be postponed; the other tells him that he has become one of a group of postponed beings, a member of a category rather than the holder of a private exception. Both are accurate to what happened, and neither suggests that he has obtained a privilege. What has been given is what every living creature has: an interval that is not endless and is not the creature's to manage.

**A Grant Is Not an Approval**

Readers who wonder how a request from such a creature could be granted miss the distinction the Book keeps throughout: permission is not endorsement, and delay is not forgiveness. Sūrah al-Qalam states the principle in the context of the deniers who mistake their prosperity for a verdict: *“So leave to Me ˹O Prophet˺ those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot understand.”* (68:44). The Qur'an names this drawing-on *istidrāj*, the leading of a person step by step while he believes he is being favoured. What Iblīs received is the greatest instance of it in the Book: an extension of existence for the whole duration of human history, granted to a creature who had just been sentenced, and granted in answer to his own request. He obtained a longer life, not a different standing.

## Verse 15:38

> until the appointed Day.”

**Al-Aʿrāf and Ṣād on the Same Appointment**

The other two tellings of this exchange preserve the phrase and the surrounding material. Al-Aʿrāf records the plea for postponement and the answer that follows, then the creature's declaration of his intentions about human beings (7:14-17 — *“He appealed, “Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.” Allah said, “You are delayed ˹until the appointed Day˺.” He said, “For leaving me to stray I will lie in ambush for them on Your Straight Path I will approach them from their front, their back, their right, their left, and then You will find most of them ungrateful.”*). Ṣād gives the request, the grant, and the reference to the appointed day, then moves to the same declaration and to God's statement about those who will be beyond his reach (38:79-83 — *“until the appointed Day.”*). Set beside this sūrah, the three accounts agree on every element that matters: a request, a grant, a limit, and a campaign. The Qur'an's repetition of the story across different sūrahs functions as corroboration rather than redundancy, and a reader who follows the same event through all three is in a position to see which details each passage chose to highlight.

**The Hidden Hour and Human Responsibility**

The Book makes a doctrine of the fact that the last hour is not announced, and it gives the reason: *“The Hour is sure to come. My Will is to keep it hidden, so that every soul may be rewarded according to their efforts.”* (20:15). A date that could be counted down would turn conduct into scheduling; a world with no date at all would have no accountability. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf asks its readers whether they have reflected on the signs of the heavens and the earth and on the possibility that their end is near (7:185 — *“Have they ever reflected on the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and everything Allah has created, and that perhaps their end is near?”*), and the question only has force because the hour cannot be calculated. The creature in this scene is inside the same arrangement as every human being. He has been given a term that he knows is coming and cannot determine, which is precisely the condition the Book describes for all accountable creatures.

**What the Limit Does to the Campaign**

Everything the creature is about to announce must be accomplished within the term set here. He will work through history, mislead generations, dress wrongdoing as attractive, and try to draw the children of Adam away from their Lord, and all of it happens inside a period whose end is already recorded. The Book's confidence in describing the enemy is built on this. When the sūrah later tells a believer to seek refuge from temptation, the refuge is offered against an adversary whose reach is known, whose methods are recorded, and whose sentence is dated. Sūrah al-Naḥl summarises the limitation in one sentence: *“He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.”* (16:99). The appointed day in this verse is the outer boundary of everything he has been permitted to do.

**The Interval as a Gift to Human Beings**

The delay granted in the previous verse and bounded in this one is given to an enemy, and yet the Book consistently treats the same interval as a service to the people he works against. It is the time in which a person can hear a warning, change a course, repair an injury, and return to his Lord; the Qur'an's endless appeals to reconsider depend on its existence. Sūrah al-Nūr states the response that settles the matter for a believer who is called to judgment: *“We hear and obey.”* (24:51). Between the sentence passed on the first refuser and the day appointed for its conclusion, the whole of human history is laid out as an opportunity, and the sūrah's reader is standing inside it. What the creature received as a working permit turns out to be the space in which everyone else is invited to choose.

## Verse 15:39

> Satan responded, “My Lord! For allowing me to stray I will surely tempt them on earth and mislead them all together

**An Accusation Dressed as a Preposition**

The Arabic phrase rendered *for allowing me to stray* is built on the preposition *bāʾ* followed by the act attributed to God, and its effect is causal: because you led me astray, I will do this. The creature who argued about his own rank and refused an order now assigns the responsibility for his own ruin to God. The Book places the two responses to divine questioning side by side, and the contrast could not be sharper. When Adam and his wife were asked about their mistake, they said *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves.”* (7:23). Iblīs says, in practice, that he has been wronged. He does not deny the act; he transfers its authorship, and with it the blame, to the only party who cannot be blamed.

**The Doctrine the Sentence Touches**

The sentence touches a real question that the Qur'an treats in several places: how are statements about God's guidance and misguidance to be understood? The Book does say that God leaves some people to stray and guides others, as in the verse about the parables that *“He leaves many to stray. Guides many”* (2:26, substance), and in the account of the condemned who admit that they never really invoked anything at all, which the Qur'an concludes by saying, *“This is how Allah leaves the disbelievers to stray.”* (40:74). The Book also explains the order of events in the case of a person who goes wrong: *“So when they ˹persistently˺ deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate”* (61:5), and it shows the same pattern in Iblīs himself, whose pride came first and whose expulsion followed. The creature's preposition skips the part of the story in which he chose.

**The Arena Is the Earth**

His vow specifies the earth as the place where the campaign will be conducted, and the Book has already established why. It is the place human beings were sent after their mistake, *“You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.”* (2:36). Their time there is short, provisional, and full of the pressures that make choice real: livelihood to be earned, families to be raised, rivals to be placated, illness and death to be absorbed. The creature who was expelled from the garden is not permitted to reach into that arrangement directly; he works from within it, using the ordinary substance of a life as his material. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf describes him lying in wait on the straight path, approaching from front, back, right and left (7:17 — *“I will approach them from their front, their back, their right, their left, and then You will find most of them ungrateful.”*), which is the language of a roadside ambush rather than a siege.

**The Excuse That Became a Standard Argument**

The Book records the same move being made by human beings, and it treats it as a self-contradiction rather than a defence: *“Had it been Allah's Will, neither we nor our forefathers would have associated others with Him ˹in worship˺ or made anything unlawful.”* (6:148). Sūrah al-Naḥl reports the identical argument from earlier communities and answers it with a single line about the messengers' duty: to deliver the message clearly (16:35 — *“So did those before them. Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly”*). Sūrah al-Zukhruf gives the verdict on the reasoning itself: *“They have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They do nothing but lie.”* (43:20). What the creature says in this verse, then, is the original of a formula that has been reused by every generation that wanted to keep its conduct and blame its maker. The sūrah records it first, in the mouth of the enemy, so that a reader can recognise the sentence when it is spoken by someone he knows.

## Verse 15:40

> except Your chosen servants among them.”

**The Exception Voluntarily Acknowledged**

The creature's own vow contains its limit. He has just claimed the whole of humanity as his field, and in the same breath he carves out a group he cannot reach: your chosen servants. Nothing forced the admission; he was not being cross-examined and no correction had been made to his statement. The exception is his own assessment of where his influence stops, and it therefore carries an authority other statements about him do not. The Book records the same limitation in other places, sometimes in God's words: *“˹Allah added,˺ ‘You will truly have no authority over My ˹faithful˺ servants.’”* (17:65), and sometimes as an outcome: the assumption he made about people came true for most of them, with a group of true believers excluded (34:20 — *“Indeed, Iblîs’ assumption about them has come true, so they ˹all˺ follow him, except a group of ˹true˺ believers”*). What the enemy states here is later confirmed from above.

**What Makes a Person Out of Reach**

Since the enemy states a limit, a reader naturally asks what puts anyone on the safe side of it, and the Book answers not with a list of achievements but with a description of the person's inner orientation. Sūrah al-Naḥl makes the criterion trust: *“He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.”* (16:99). The Book never suggests that those who trust are the ones who are never approached; it suggests that when they are approached, the approach fails. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf gives a practical rule for the moment of contact: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (7:200), and al-Naḥl places the same instruction around recitation of the Qur'an. The protection in this verse is therefore a relationship, actively maintained, rather than a status a person holds without effort.

**God's Choice Is Stated Plainly Elsewhere**

The Qur'an has no difficulty in describing God as the one who selects among people, and it says so in connection with guidance, messengership and mercy. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states it in the same chapter where guidance and misguidance are discussed: *“Nor would Allah ˹directly˺ reveal to you the unseen, but He chooses whoever He wills as a messenger.”* (3:179). Sūrah al-Shūrā describes God choosing for Himself whoever He wills among those who turn toward Him (42:13 — *“Allah chooses for Himself whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns ˹to Him˺”*). The exception that the enemy is forced to admit is so not a title the righteous invented for themselves; it is an arrangement the Book names from beginning to end. The creature who thought he could measure creatures by their material has learned that the designation belongs to the one who created them, and he is left to work around people he cannot move.

**Servanthood as the Mark of Safety**

The group that is beyond his reach is named in this verse as servants, and the title is the whole criterion. It is the same word the Qur'an uses as a badge of honour for the highest creatures: the angels are described as honoured servants who do not speak until God has spoken (21:26-27 — *“who do not speak until He has spoken, ˹only˺ acting at His command”*), and elsewhere as *“servants of the Most Compassionate”* (43:19). The creature who refused to bow did so because he judged service beneath him; the ones he cannot touch are precisely those who accept it as their identity. In the Book's vocabulary, then, the word that the proud consider a humiliation is the word that marks the protected. Sūrah al-Zumar describes the same safety from the other side, telling those who avoided the worship of false gods and turned to God that they have good news in store (39:17 — *“And those who shun the worship of false gods, turning to Allah ˹alone˺, will have good news. So give good news to My servants ˹O Prophet˺”*). The verse under commentary gives the enemy's own testimony to the same fact.

## Verse 15:41

> Allah said, “This is the Way, binding on Me

**A Commitment God Takes Upon Himself**

The Qur'an uses the language of God binding Himself in a small number of places, and each one is a statement about mercy rather than obligation. Sūrah al-Anʿām says of Him: *“He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment— about which there is no doubt.”* (6:12, ending). The Book also describes His promise in the same way when it tells the believers that God has undertaken to grant them a reward. Nothing in this vocabulary suggests that a creature has imposed a duty on the Creator; the point is that God has announced His own commitment and that His announcement cannot fail. Understood this way, the phrase in this verse is one of the most reassuring sentences in the sūrah: the enemy's campaign is answered by a pledge made before the campaign began.

**The Straight Path in the Book's Language**

The noun is *ṣirāṭ*, and it appears in the Qur'an over and over as the road that leads to God. The opening sūrah of the Book asks for it: *“Guide us along the Straight Path, the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.”* (1:6-7). Sūrah al-Anʿām describes it as a single road with other ways branching off it: *“Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way.”* (6:153). Sūrahs Maryam, Yā Sīn, and al-Zukhruf all summarise the path in the same words, that God alone is Lord and should be worshipped (19:36 — *“˹Jesus also declared,˺ “Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.”*; 36:61 — *“but to worship Me ˹alone˺? This is the Straight Path”*; 43:64 — *“Surely Allah ˹alone˺ is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.”*). The road in this verse is therefore not an abstract moral order but the known way of the prophets, and its markers were visible to every audience that heard the sūrah.

**Who Walks It**

The Book never describes the path as a lane that carries people along; walking it is an ongoing act of a person's will. Sūrah al-Insān states the arrangement in a single line: *“We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful.”* (76:3), and Sūrah al-Muzzammil adds, *“So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord.”* (73:19). Sūrah al-Kahf gives the same choice in the strongest terms: *“˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve.”* (18:29). The straight path is guaranteed, but it is not enforced, which is what makes the enemy's activity possible in the first place. He was permitted to call people from the roadside; he was not permitted to block the road, and the verse under commentary is the charter that says so.

**The Ambush Language of Sūrah al-Aʿrāf**

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the same creature describing his strategy as an ambush: he will lie in wait on the straight path and come at people from front, back, right and left (7:17 — *“I will approach them from their front, their back, their right, their left, and then You will find most of them ungrateful.”*). That image fits what this verse establishes. An ambush assumes a road people are travelling, and the ambusher's whole business depends on traffic he cannot stop from passing. The Book also warns human beings against adopting the same technique in their own dealings, telling one community not to sit on every road, threatening and turning away those who believe, and not to try to make the way appear crooked (7:86 — *“And do not lie in ambush on every road—threatening and hindering those who believe in Allah from His Path and striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked.”*). Taken together, the passages describe the road as contested but never closed. The One who laid it has undertaken that the route remains open, which leaves the traveller, not the adversary, holding the decision.

## Verse 15:42

> you will certainly have no authority over My servants, except the deviant who follow you

**The Word for Authority**

The Arabic term translated *authority* is *sulṭān*, and it covers power, a claim, and a warrant. Its use here is exact: the creature is denied all three. He is not given power over the servants of God, he is not granted a claim on them, and no warrant exists for what he tries among them. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ records the statement in the very same form and attaches to it a promise of protection: *“˹Allah added,˺ ‘You will truly have no authority over My ˹faithful˺ servants.’ And sufficient is your Lord as a Guardian.”* (17:65). Al-Naḥl explains what the denial means for the person on the receiving end: *“He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.”* (16:99). The sentence is not a description of the enemy's weakness in general — he is given room, longevity and method — but of the boundary of his reach over God's own people.

**Whom the Authority Reaches**

The same passage in al-Naḥl continues by naming the group he does control: *“His authority is only over those who take him as a patron and who—under his influence—associate ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺.”* (16:100). The relation described there is patronage chosen by the human being, which is the exact point this verse makes when it says the followers are *deviant* rather than seized. The Book never presents the followers as innocent captives. Sūrah Sabaʾ reports the outcome of the enemy's expectation in the same terms, that most followed him except a group of true believers (34:20 — *“Indeed, Iblîs’ assumption about them has come true, so they ˹all˺ follow him, except a group of ˹true˺ believers”*). Sūrah al-Nisāʾ frames the contest as two alignments, believers fighting for the cause of God and disbelievers fighting for the cause of the devil, before adding that the devil's schemes are ever weak (4:76 — *“Believers fight for the cause of Allah, whereas disbelievers fight for the cause of the Devil.”*). His authority is real, and it operates through consent.

**Who Are the Deviant Who Follow?**

The Arabic word rendered *deviant* describes someone who has gone out of the way and stays there, and the phrase *who follow you* makes the relationship mutual rather than imposed. He offers; they come. Sūrah al-Anʿām describes the same configuration as a giving over: *“Allah leaves whoever He wills to stray and guides whoever He wills to the Straight Way.”* (6:39, ending). Sūrah al-Aʿrāf describes the ones who take devils as masters instead of God, thinking they are rightly guided (7:30 — *“He has guided some, while others are destined to stray. They have taken devils as their masters instead of Allah—thinking they are ˹rightly˺ guided”*). In every case the Book places the choice on the human side and the invitation on the enemy's side, which is what makes the distinction in this verse so pointed. Authority over the willing is conceded to him; authority over anyone else is denied.

**The Empty Promises**

The last element of the enemy's equipment is the one the Book names most often: what he offers does not exist. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states it plainly — *“Satan only makes them ˹false˺ promises and deludes them with ˹empty˺ hopes. Truly Satan promises them nothing but delusion.”* (4:120), and Sūrah Ibrāhīm reports his own speech of admission at the end, when he tells his followers that he had no power over them and that what he promised was nothing but illusion (14:22, substance — *“And Satan will say ˹to his followers˺ after the judgment has been passed, “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise.”*). The verse under commentary so completes a picture the Book draws in three strokes: he has no authority, he has no property rights over anyone, and what he trades in is a currency that does not exist. What remains is the choice of the person who follows, and to that the Book addresses itself for the rest of the sūrah.

## Verse 15:43

> and surely Hell is their destined place, all together

**The Appointed Meeting**

The Arabic word rendered *destined place* is a noun of appointment, the same kind of word a person would use for a fixed engagement with a time and a location. The creature who asked for a delay, obtained one, and pledged to mislead all of humanity now has an appointment entered on his own account. The Book uses this vocabulary for the final gathering throughout: Sūrah al-Qamar says of the mockers, *“Better yet, the Hour is their appointed time—and the Hour will be most catastrophic and most bitter.”* (54:46). There is a grim fitness in the arrangement. He was the one who wanted time extended; what the sentence confirms is that a date has been set for him and for everyone who joined him, and neither the date nor the destination is negotiable.

**A Destination Shared by the Tempter and the Followed**

The verse groups them. The pronoun covers the enemy and those who took his path, and the Book is explicit about that grouping in the parallel passages. Al-Aʿrāf records the sentence in the scene of expulsion: *“I will certainly fill up Hell with you and your followers all together.”* (7:18), and Sūrah Ṣād repeats the same words (38:85 — *“I will surely fill up Hell with you and whoever follows you from among them, all together.”*). Sūrah al-Mujādilah calls the followers the party of Satan and states the outcome of that party in a sentence built on loss (58:19 — *“Satan has taken hold of them, causing them to forget the remembrance of Allah. They are the party of Satan. Surely Satan’s party is bound to lose”*). The shared destination is the Book's way of saying that a leader and his followers are judged in the same ledger when the leadership is in wrongdoing. The one who planned the misguidance and the ones who chose it have arrived at the same appointment from opposite ends of the same road.

**What the Company of the Followers Means**

The Book never suggests that followers in this sense are passive. It describes the relationship as a choice made with knowledge, and it records, on the day of accounting, the recriminations that break out between the two parties. The followers will blame those who led them, and the leaders will disown the blame. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf reports the followers asking for a double punishment for those who led them astray, and Sūrah al-Baqarah records the misled followers saying that if they had a second chance they would disown their leaders as they were disowned (2:167 — *“The ˹misled˺ followers will cry, “If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us.”*). That future argument is exactly what the present verse forecloses. There is no distinction available at the appointment; the destination is one, and the party arrives together.

**Justice in a Shared Sentence**

A reader may ask how one destination can be just for a rebellious spirit and for human beings whose circumstances differed across centuries. The Book's answer is that the sentence is not identical for each person within the destination, and its own texts say so: the next verse describes gates and a portion assigned to each group, and other passages describe degrees of punishment. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states the principle at the scale of individuals: whoever brings a good deed will be rewarded tenfold, and whoever brings a bad deed will be punished only for one (6:160, first half — *“Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold. But whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished for only one. None will be wronged”*), while Sūrah al-Zalzalah says that every atom's weight of good and of evil will be seen. The shared place in this verse is therefore not a flattening of responsibility but the common end of a shared road.

## Verse 15:44

> It has seven gates, to each a group of them is designated.”

**A Portion Designated for Each Gate**

The second clause says that each gate has its allotted share of the company, and the Arabic verb suggests something written down and apportioned. The followers of the enemy are not poured into one undifferentiated mass; they arrive in groups, and each group is directed to its own entrance. The sūrah has already used this vocabulary of appointment and measure in its earlier verses, where every community has a written term and provision comes down in precise quantity. The same careful administration governs the last destination. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf supplies the corresponding principle for individuals when it says that a good deed is rewarded tenfold while a bad deed brings only its equivalent, and that no one will be wronged (6:160, second half — *“Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold. But whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished for only one. None will be wronged”*). The gates in this verse are where that principle becomes visible.

**Gates as Openings and as Barriers**

The Book uses gates for both mercy and severity, and the two uses illuminate each other. The arrogant are refused entry at the gates of heaven: *“the gates of heaven will not be opened for them, nor will they enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle.”* (7:40). The righteous arrive at gates already open, and the keepers greet them with peace and the command to enter and remain (39:73 — *“When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, “Peace be upon you!”*), while Sūrah Ṣād describes the gardens of eternity with their gates standing open for their people (38:50 — *“the Gardens of Eternity, whose gates will be open for them”*). At the end of the world, the sky itself becomes gates as it splits (78:19 — *“The sky will be ˹split˺ open, becoming ˹many˺ gates”*). A gate in this vocabulary is a boundary with an authority behind it. Whoever enters does so by permission, and the number of openings in this verse tells the condemned that their arrival, too, has been arranged rather than merely permitted.

**Faith in What No Eye Has Seen**

Everything described in this verse lies beyond human observation, and the Book presents such reports as a matter of belief rather than of verification. Sūrah al-Sajdah states the counterpart for the righteous in a verse that makes the same point from the other direction: *“No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do.”* (32:17). The Qur'an gives the unseen world in the detail that conduct requires and withholds the detail that curiosity wants. Muslims have read these verses for their warning and their encouragement, and the tradition has consistently discouraged tries to map the other world as though it were a place one could survey. The seven gates are named because the people who are assigned to them need to know that their destination is prepared.

**A Register That Assigns Every Place**

The second half of the verse speaks of groups designated to their gate, and the vocabulary of designation belongs to the Book's wider account of a record in which nothing living is left unaccounted for. Sūrah Hūd states the principle at the scale of the smallest creatures: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah. And He knows where it lives and where it is laid to rest. All is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.”* (11:6). The register that notes where a creature lives and where it is buried is the same register that decides where a person arrives at the end of the road. The assembly of the condemned in this verse is therefore not a crowd pouring through one opening; it is an ordering, and the ordering is the visible form of a record kept for each member of the company. Nothing about the destination is casual, and nothing about the arrival is left to chance.

## Verse 15:45

> Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs

**A Sentence That Turns the Sūrah Around**

After the gates of punishment, the verse declares what awaits the God-fearing, and it does so in a line whose structure mirrors the sentences about the enemy's party. There is a destination, a company and a place described by what grows in it. The Arabic word for the righteous here is the same noun used throughout the Qur'an for those who guard themselves against wrongdoing, and it is the word that opens the second sūrah of the Book: *“This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺.”* (2:2). The term names a practice rather than a status. The gardens and springs are given to people described by what they did with the warning they received, and the sūrah has just shown its audience two roads whose ends are now named in consecutive verses.

**Gardens and Springs as a Pair**

The pairing recurs verbatim elsewhere. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt opens its description of the reward with the same words, then adds what the people of the gardens are doing there: *“Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs, ˹joyfully˺ receiving what their Lord will grant them. Before this ˹reward˺ they were truly good-doers ˹in the world˺.”* (51:15-16). The Book's description of the gardens keeps returning to water, and the reason is not difficult to see. Its first audience lived in a landscape where water was the difference between life and death, and where a spring meant a settlement. Sūrah Muḥammad describes rivers of water that never changes, milk that never turns, wine that gives delight, and purified honey, then adds fruit of every kind and forgiveness from their Lord (47:15 — *“There they will ˹also˺ have all kinds of fruit, and forgiveness from their Lord.”*). The point is abundance without the decay that marks every good thing in this world.

**What "Amid" Says About the Life of the Garden**

The Arabic places them *in* the gardens and springs, not merely at them or beside them. The construction suggests a condition rather than a location: they are surrounded by the two things that made life good in their own experience, with no wall of danger and nothing to defend. The Book's descriptions of the rewarded state consistently emphasise this quality of being enveloped: in gardens under which rivers flow, in shade that does not shift, in a peace that is not interrupted. Sūrah al-Naḥl summarises the state by saying that they will have whatever they desire in gardens of eternity (16:31 — *“the Gardens of Eternity which they will enter, under which rivers flow. In it they will have whatever they desire. This is how Allah rewards the righteous”*).

**Who the Righteous Were in Practice**

The Book defines the term by description rather than by label, and the descriptions are concrete. Sūrah al-Baqarah names the people the Book guides: *“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them.”* (2:3), and continues with belief in what was revealed and in the hereafter. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān invites its readers to hasten toward a garden prepared for those mindful of God, and then describes them as people who give in ease and hardship and who restrain their anger. The word rendered *righteous* in the verse under commentary is the quality that runs through all of these: a person who takes the warnings seriously enough to adjust his conduct. What the gardens reward is therefore not an emotional state or a family name but a way of living that the sūrah's own audience had been invited to begin.

## Verse 15:46
> ˹It will be said to them,˺ “Enter in peace and security.”

**A Sentence Spoken Over Them**

The line is phrased in the passive, and the commentators discussed who says it. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī report that the greeting is spoken by God, while others, including al-Shawkānī, understand the angels as the speakers, since the Book elsewhere describes the keepers of the garden receiving its people at the gates with peace and the invitation to enter and remain (39:73 — *“When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, “Peace be upon you!”*). The ambiguity is not a defect in the verse; it removes the speakers from attention and leaves the words themselves in the foreground. What matters to the listener is that the command to enter comes from outside himself, that it is spoken as a welcome rather than a verdict, and that the person receiving it does nothing to earn the sentence at the moment he hears it. The promise is announced; he is invited across his own threshold.

**"Peace" as the First Word of the New Life**

*Salām* is the greeting of the next world in the Qur'an, and the Book repeats the fact often enough for it to function as a definition of the place. The people of the garden pray with the words glory and praise, exchange a greeting of peace, and close with praise of the Lord of all worlds (10:10 — *“in which their prayer will be, “Glory be to You, O Allah!” and their greeting will be, “Peace!” and their closing prayer will be, “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!”*). Believers are told that they will be greeted with peace where they dwell (14:23 — *“Those who believe and do good will be admitted into Gardens, under which rivers flow—to stay there forever by the Will of their Lord—where they will be greeted with “Peace!”*), that their greeting on the day they meet Him will be peace (33:44 — *“Their greeting on the Day they meet Him will be, “Peace!” And He has prepared for them an honourable reward”*), and that peace will be their greeting from the Most Merciful (36:58 — *“And “Peace!” will be ˹their˺ greeting from the Merciful Lord”*).

Even the name of the destination is built on the word: God invites to the Home of Peace and guides whom He wills to the straight path (10:25 — *“And Allah invites ˹all˺ to the Home of Peace and guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path”*). The first thing done to a person entering the garden is to be given the word that names the absence of every harm.

**"Security" as the Second Half of the Blessing**

The Arabic term for the second word describes safety from fear, and the pairing with peace covers the two ways life in this world goes wrong: conflict with others and the dread of loss. Sūrah Sabaʾ records the same double blessing spoken by the people of the garden themselves, that they have been settled in an everlasting home where they are touched by neither fatigue nor weariness (35:35 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who—out of His grace—has settled us in the Home of Everlasting Stay, where we will be touched by neither fatigue nor weariness.”*). The Book's earlier narrative supplies the contrast directly, since human beings were told after their mistake that they would find in the earth a residence and provision for an appointed stay, and that they would be enemies to one another (2:36 — *“You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.”*). What the verse under commentary announces is the settled end of that arrangement: no more enmity, no more eviction, and no further sentence pending.

## Verse 15:47

> We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts. In a friendly manner, they will be on thrones, facing one another

**The Removal Is Done to Them, Not by Them**

The sentence is spoken in the first person plural: we will remove. The people being described do not clean their own hearts of the resentment they carried; the removal is performed for them, and this points to something the Book assumes about bitterness, which is that it does not usually yield to a person's own determination. What is being described is not the forgiveness of a wrong, since the wrongs in question occurred among believers themselves, during a life in which loyalties shifted, families divided and quarrels were left unresolved. The verse states that the last trace of all of it will be taken away before the group settles into its home. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the same act in the same words when it describes the people of the garden and adds a detail: rivers flow beneath them as the removal happens.

**Bitterness and What Produces It**

The Arabic term covers rancour, the sour residue of an injury that has not healed, and the Book treats it as a serious fault rather than a reaction. It recommends no such thing as a good grievance. In the sūrah of deeds and their consequences, the prayer taught to the believers includes a plea that bitterness not be allowed into their hearts toward those who believe: *“Our Lord! Forgive us and our fellow believers who preceded us in faith, and do not allow bitterness into our hearts toward those who believe.”* (59:10), and the Qur'an names the envier's envy as one of the things a person must seek protection from (113:5 — *“and from the evil of an envier when they envy.”*). When the verse under commentary describes the removal of bitterness, it is describing the cure of a disease that the Book spends considerable effort preventing in this world.

## Verse 15:48

> No fatigue will touch them there, nor will they ever be asked to leave

**The End of Exhaustion**

The word rendered fatigue describes the weariness that follows exertion, the state in which a body has spent what it has and demands rest. The Book applies to the people of the garden a promise that this will never happen again, and it is a greater claim than it may appear to a reader who has not lived by labour. The Book's own picture of the world it is describing as finished includes sweat, effort and toll: the Qur'an tells human beings that they will find in the earth a residence and provision, which means work. It describes the journey of life in stages from infancy to old age and the weakness of the last stage. Sūrah Fāṭir records the people of the everlasting home stating the same blessing about themselves in almost identical words (35:35 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who—out of His grace—has settled us in the Home of Everlasting Stay, where we will be touched by neither fatigue nor weariness.”*), and the two verses together make the promise a fixed feature of the reward rather than an incidental detail.

**The Pairing of the Two Negations**

The two halves of the sentence cover the two ways that a good condition can fail: it can become burdensome, or it can be taken away. A person's life in this world may be good and still exhausting, and it may be pleasant and still precarious. The Book's description of the final rest denies both possibilities in a single line, and this method of describing the reward by negation runs through the Qur'an. Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah describes a garden in which no idle talk is heard (88:11 — *“where no idle talk will be heard”*). Sūrah al-Naḥl says its people will have whatever they desire (16:31 — *“the Gardens of Eternity which they will enter, under which rivers flow. In it they will have whatever they desire. This is how Allah rewards the righteous”*). Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt mentions that they will not be subject there to death other than the first, nor to torment. Each negation removes one of the anxieties the reader brings with him from his own life, and this verse removes the two that are hardest to shake.

**What the Verse Says About the Other Destination**

The Book describes the fire with the opposite pair of details, and the comparison is instructive rather than decorative. Sūrah Ṭā Hā says of the evildoer in the fire that he can neither live nor die (20:74 — *“Whoever comes to their Lord as an evildoer will certainly have Hell, where they can neither live nor die”*), and Sūrah Fāṭir says of the disbelievers in the fire that death will not be decreed for them so that they might die, nor will its torment be lightened for them (35:36 — *“As for the disbelievers, they will have the Fire of Hell, where they will not be ˹allowed to be˺ finished by death, nor will its torment be lightened for them.”*). Exhaustion without respite, permanence without relief: the two destinations are described with matching vocabulary and opposite outcomes, which is the Book's normal practice wherever it sets the two ends of human life side by side. A reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening verses about appointed terms and delays will see the logic. Everything in this world, including the punishment of the guilty, has a term; the world to come does not.

## Verse 15:49

> Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**The Difference Between the Two Names**

The two titles at the end of the sentence are not duplicates. The first, *al-Ghafūr*, describes forgiveness as the covering and removal of a wrong; the second, *al-Raḥīm*, describes mercy as the giving of good after the wrong has been dealt with. A person who is forgiven is cleared of a debt; a person who is shown mercy is treated as though the debt had never existed, and is given more. The Qur'an often pairs the two when it speaks of accepting those who turn back, as in the command to the believers to say to those who come to them, peace be upon you, for *“Your Lord has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. Whoever among you commits evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repents afterwards and mends their ways, then Allah is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”* (6:54). The verse under commentary supplies the same two names as a general announcement, with no conditions attached to the announcement itself.

**Who Are the Servants Being Addressed**

The possessive in the words *My servants* is a mark of honour in the Book, and its use here is deliberate. The men and women being told about forgiveness are the same people whose failures had been recorded in the previous verses and who knew what they had done. Sūrah al-Zumar speaks to exactly this audience in words that could be read as an expansion of this verse: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The Qur'an never treats the news of mercy as a reward for the spiritually accomplished. It is broadcast to the people most in need of it, and the reason is that the alternative, despair, is described elsewhere in the same Book as an accomplice to further failure.

**Mercy in the Qur'an's Account of God**

The Book speaks about God's mercy more often and more expansively than about any other relationship between Him and human beings. In the dialogue at Mount Sinai, God says of Himself, *“My mercy encompasses everything.”* (7:156), a claim the Qur'an does not make for any other attribute in the same wording. Sūrah al-Anʿām has God taking mercy upon Himself as a commitment, announcing that He will gather all people for the Day of Judgment in the same breath (6:12 — *“He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment—about which there is no doubt.”*). Sūrah Ghāfir names Him *“the Forgiver of sin and Accepter of repentance”* (40:3), and Sūrah al-Nisāʾ provides the simple equation for anyone who has wronged himself, that he should seek forgiveness and will find God All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (4:110 — *“Whoever commits evil or wrongs themselves then seeks Allah’s forgiveness will certainly find Allah All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*). The verse under commentary belongs to this body of statements, and it is the one the Prophet was told to carry to the servants themselves.

**The Hadith on the Precedence of Mercy**

The Prophet is reported to have related what God wrote about this subject: *“When Allah completed the creation, He wrote in His book with Him upon the Throne: Verily, My mercy prevails over My wrath.”* The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3194 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2751, agreed upon by both collectors, with the wording of the record written and kept above the Throne. The image is of a decision made at the beginning of creation and preserved where the creation cannot touch it. Whatever a person's fear of judgment may be, the Book and the reports that accompany it consistently present mercy as the attribute God has given precedence, and the sūrah's own sequence performs the same act: a description of punishment, followed immediately by the announcement that God is forgiving and merciful.

## Verse 15:50

> and that My torment is indeed the most painful

**Whom the Punishment Reaches**

The Book is consistent about whom the warning concerns, and it names them without ambiguity. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states that those who disbelieve in God's signs and in the meeting with Him are the ones with no hope in His mercy, and that they are the ones who will suffer a painful punishment (29:23 — *“As for those who disbelieve in Allah’s signs and the meeting with Him, it is they who will have no hope in His mercy. And it is they who will suffer a painful punishment”*). Sūrah Ghāfir's list of divine titles ends with the reminder that to Him is the final return (40:3 — *“To Him ˹alone˺ is the final return”*). The warning is not addressed to someone who has sinned and turned back; the Book's own stories include murderers, adulterers and persecutors who returned and were received. The torment described in this verse is what awaits the person who dies in his position rather than the person who fails and rises again, and the Qur'an's many invitations to return exist precisely because the second possibility remains open for as long as life lasts.

**A Sentence That Completes the One Before It**

The Book states the two names in verse 49 and, in the next breath, states the punishment, and the grammatical linkage makes them a single message. The Qur'an uses the same pairing elsewhere in words that are close to this passage: *“Know that Allah is severe in punishment and that He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”* (5:98). Sūrah Ghāfir lists the titles of God in a row, including the forgiver of sin, the accepter of repentance and the severe in punishment (40:3 — *“the Forgiver of sin and Accepter of repentance, the Severe in punishment, and Infinite in bounty.”*). Readers are not offered the mercy without the warning, and they are not left with the warning alone. What a person does with the two facts together, in the Book's teaching, determines whether the mercy reaches him, and the sentence about punishment exists to make sure the announcement of mercy is understood as a summons to turn rather than as a licence to continue.

**Why the Book Keeps Fear Alive**

A religion that spoke only of mercy would tell its followers exactly what they wanted to hear, and the Qur'an never takes that route. It describes people who mistook delay and prosperity for approval in its own earlier verses, and it treats the mistake as fatal. The Book therefore keeps the other pole in view, sometimes in a single verse, as in the warning that no one can be sure of escaping God's plan: *“Did they feel secure against Allah's planning? None would feel secure from Allah's planning except the losers.”* (7:99). The sūrah's reader has just been shown the destination of a creature who reasoned himself into rebellion, and the sentence about torment is placed where no one can skim past it. Fear in the Qur'an is not a psychological weapon; it is one half of the information a person needs to act.

**The Plainness of the Book's Language About Pain**

The Qur'an does not shelter its audience from the subject. It uses the vocabulary of pain and punishment without abstraction, without euphemism and without embarrassment, and the same Book that describes paradise in gardens and springs describes the fire in what a body and a soul will endure. Sūrah Ṭā Hā describes its inhabitants as neither living nor dying there (20:74 — *“Whoever comes to their Lord as an evildoer will certainly have Hell, where they can neither live nor die”*), and Sūrah Fāṭir adds that their torment will not be lightened (35:36 — *“As for the disbelievers, they will have the Fire of Hell, where they will not be ˹allowed to be˺ finished by death, nor will its torment be lightened for them.”*). The purpose of such plainness is not cruelty but accuracy. A person who has been given a clear account of what awaits persistence can no longer claim that the warning was vague, and the Book's own summary of the matter leaves the responsibility with the reader rather than with the messenger.

## Verse 15:51

> And inform them ˹O Prophet˺ about Abraham’s guests

**Who Abraham Was in the Book's Account**

The Qur'an names Abraham in dozens of places and describes him in terms that no other figure receives. He is called a model of excellence, devoted to God and perfectly upright (16:120 — *“Indeed, Abraham was a model of excellence: devoted to Allah, ˹perfectly˺ upright—not a polytheist”*), and utterly grateful for the favours he received, so that God chose him and guided him to the straight path (16:121 — *“˹utterly˺ grateful for Allah’s favours. ˹So˺ He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path”*). Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the distinction directly: *“And who is better in faith than those who ˹fully˺ submit themselves to Allah, do good, and follow the Way of Abraham, the upright? Allah chose Abraham as a close friend.”* (4:125). He is the figure in whom the Book anchors the religion of submission, the father of the line that produced the prophets of Israel, and the builder, with his son, of the sanctuary at Makkah. For an audience that took pride in descent, the choice of his story was exact.

**The Word for Guests**

The Arabic word is *ḍayf*, and it means guests in the most concrete sense: people who come to a person's home and are owed hospitality by the custom of the land. The same root appears elsewhere in the Book for the honour of being a guest and for the obligations that follow. The desert cultures of Arabia treated the arrival of a stranger as a test of the household, and the Qur'an's other telling of this story describes the visitors as *“Abraham's honoured guests”* (51:24), with the adjective supplied on the sūrah's own initiative. When the Book uses a word from the daily life of its audience, it is not decorating the narrative. It is placing a story about the unseen world in the frame of the most familiar human obligation, and asking the listener to judge the scene by the manners he already practised.

**The Guests Were Messengers**

The visitors were angels, and the same passage that calls them guests names them as messengers: *“And surely Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with good news ˹of a son˺. They greeted ˹him with˺, ‘Peace!’ And he replied, ‘Peace be upon you!’ Then it was not long before he brought ˹them˺ a ˹fat,˺ roasted calf.”* (11:69). They arrived in human form, and the Book does not describe the form in any detail, which has been the general practice of Muslim exegetes when handling such scenes. What the narrative stresses is the action: they came with a message, they were received as guests, and the occasion turned out to be an announcement about the future of Abraham's line and the fate of a city. Two worlds meet at a tent door, and the Book tells the encounter as a domestic story.

**The Other Tellings of the Visit**

The Qur'an narrates this visit in several places, and the details differ in ways that suit each context. Sūrah Hūd describes the greeting, the calf, Abraham's alarm when the guests did not eat, the good news of Isaac and the plea he made for the people of Lot (11:69-76 — *“We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.”*). Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt gives the scene in a few swift lines and then moves to the destruction of the cities (51:24-34 — *“and placed it before them, asking, “Will you not eat?”*). Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt introduces the same angels with the news before they reach Lot (29:31-32 — *“When Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with the good news ˹of the birth of Isaac˺, they said, “We are going to destroy the people of this city ˹of Sodom˺, for its people have persisted in wrongdoing.”*). Each telling selects the elements its sūrah requires, and none contradicts another. The passage under commentary chooses to introduce the guests without describing them at all, which obliges the reader to continue into the verses that follow to learn why they came.

## Verse 15:52

> who entered upon him and greeted ˹him with˺, “Peace!” He ˹later˺ said, “Surely we are afraid of you.”

**The Greeting That Opens the Story**

The visitors begin with peace, and this detail is preserved in every telling of the scene. Sūrah Hūd gives the exchange in full, including Abraham's reply in kind: *“And surely Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with good news ˹of a son˺. They greeted ˹him with˺, ‘Peace!’ And he replied, ‘Peace be upon you!’”* (11:69). The Book records the courtesy of two parties who do not yet know each other, and it records it approvingly, since the same Book commands its readers to greet one another and instructs them to answer a greeting with something better or at least equal to it (4:86 — *“And when you are greeted, respond with a better greeting or at least similarly. Surely Allah is a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner of all things”*). The scene begins with the smallest and most ordinary act in the culture of the audience, and everything that follows in the passage, an announcement of the impossible and a sentence of destruction, unfolds from a doorway opened to strangers.

**Why Abraham Was Afraid**

The fear that Abraham expresses in this verse has an explanation in the parallel account, and it is a culture's explanation rather than a psychological one. Sūrah Hūd says of the guests, *“And when he saw that their hands did not reach for the food, he became suspicious and fearful of them.”* (11:70). In the society of the sūrah's first hearers, a guest who refused the food he was offered was declaring hostility or bringing danger into the house. Abraham had prepared a meal, as the other account details, and the guests did not eat. His alarm is therefore not the dread of a timid man; it is the correct reading of a social signal, and it tells the reader how completely the visitors had entered the world of human custom while remaining outside it.

**Hospitality as an Act of Faith**

The Book's account of that evening is, among other things, a portrait of how a believer treats strangers. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt records what Abraham did: *“Then he slipped off to his family and brought a fat ˹roasted˺ calf, and placed it before them, asking, ‘Will you not eat?’”* (51:26-27). The phrasing is quick and practical, the behaviour of a man who does not debate whether the visitors deserve his attention. His character is described elsewhere in words that fit the action: *“utters gratitude for Allah's favours. ˹So˺ He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path.”* (16:121). The hospitality practised in this verse is the visible form of the gratitude the Book attributes to him, and the fact that the guests turned out to be angels is not what made the welcome correct.

**The Prophet's Own Fear When Guests Arrived**

Fear at the arrival of an unknown visitor was not limited to Abraham's story. Sūrah al-Kahf and other passages show the prophets reacting to the appearance of the unseen world when it came to them in disguise, and the Book records their alarm without criticism. The Qur'an's prophets are consistently portrayed as human beings who experienced what their hearers experienced: hunger, illness, laughter, grief, fear. When the Book's opponents demanded a messenger who was not a man, the answer given was that the messengers sent were men, eating in markets and walking the streets (25:7 — *“And they say ˹mockingly˺, “What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺?”*). The verse under commentary contributes to that picture. The friend of God stands at his own door, uncertain whether the strangers are a threat, and the record preserves the moment.

## Verse 15:53

> They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not be afraid! Surely we give you good news of a knowledgeable son.”

**The Reassurance Comes First**

Before the message, the angels calm their host. The Book's arrangement of the sentence is deliberate: the instruction not to fear precedes the announcement, and the announcement is introduced as the reason for it. Sūrah Hūd records the same order of events with the visitors explaining their mission at once: *“They reassured ˹him˺, ‘Do not be afraid! We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.’”* (11:70). A guest who has already alarmed his host owes him an account of himself, and the visitors give it. In a single verse they identify their nature, remove the cause of the fear and then deliver the news they came with. The sūrah's reader is being shown, in the space of two lines, how quickly the unseen world can move from threatening to gracious.

**Good News of a Son**

The promise is the central content of the verse and the reason the visitors were described in the sūrah at all. The child is not named here, and the omission has the effect of putting the emphasis on the wonder rather than on the individual. Other passages supply the name: Sūrah Hūd tells of the good news of Isaac and, after him, Jacob (11:71 — *“And his wife was standing by, so she laughed, then We gave her good news of ˹the birth of˺ Isaac, and, after him, Jacob”*), Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt says that Abraham was given the news of a forbearing son (37:101 — *“So We gave him good news of a forbearing son”*) and later of Isaac, a prophet. One of the righteous (37:112 — *“We ˹later˺ gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous”*), and Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt describes him blessed with Isaac and Jacob, with prophethood and revelation reserved for his descendants (29:27 — *“We blessed him with Isaac and ˹later˺ Jacob, and reserved prophethood and revelation for his descendants.”*). The sūrah holds the name back and gives the attribute instead, which is a habit of this Book: the reader is told what the gift was like before he is told what it was called.

**Why the Son Is Described as Knowing**

The Arabic adjective describes knowledge, and its use here carries more than a compliment. The child promised to an elderly man and a barren woman is described by a quality that belongs to the prophetic line rather than by strength, beauty or wealth, and the same adjective appears in the other telling of the same moment (51:28 — *“˹They did not eat,˺ so he grew fearful of them. They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not be afraid,” and gave him good news of a knowledgeable son”*). The Book's own summary of what Abraham's descendants received makes the connection: prophethood and revelation were reserved for his line (29:27 — *“We blessed him with Isaac and ˹later˺ Jacob, and reserved prophethood and revelation for his descendants.”*). A son is a future, and the verse defines the future in knowing rather than of inheriting. For an audience that measured a family's worth by its numbers and its herds, the description quietly moves the measure to what a person carries inside him.

**A Promise That Exceeds Nature**

The announcement is impossible by the arithmetic of the couple's age, and the Book does not evade the fact; it records Abraham's astonishment and his wife's laughter in the parallel accounts. What the verses stress is the authority behind the promise. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt gives the angels' reply to the couple's surprise: *“They replied, ‘Such has your Lord decreed. He is truly the All-Wise, All-Knowing.’”* (51:30). Sūrah Hūd puts the same point in the mouth of the visitors when Sarah exclaims in wonder: *“Are you astonished by Allah's decree? May Allah's mercy and blessings be upon you, O people of this house.”* (11:73). The promise to Abraham belongs to the same family of announcements as the births of John and of Jesus, each of them impossible by ordinary reckoning and each of them reported as an act of God.

Abraham's concern for offspring appears elsewhere in the Book, in his prayer for the city and in his request that his descendants be included in the covenant made with him (2:124 — *“˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”*).

## Verse 15:54

> He wondered, “Do you give me good news despite my old age? What unlikely news!”

**The Astonishment of a Human Being**

The verse records Abraham's reaction, and the reaction is surprise rather than doubt. The Book does not present the friend of God as a man who receives an announcement about his own household with composure; it presents him asking how such news can apply to him. The parallel account gives the fuller form of the question, in which he asks whether the visitors bring news of a son when old age has befallen him, and the Book preserves the exact wording of his incredulity. Prophets in the Qur'an are consistently written this way, as men who lived inside the conditions of the human body. Their faith is not displayed as the absence of ordinary feeling but as conduct maintained in the middle of it.

**Age and the Arithmetic of the Household**

Abraham's objection is not religious but biological. He is old, and the other account makes the situation plainer by recording his wife's age as well: she laughs when she hears the announcement, and later exclaims at the thought of bearing a child when both she and her husband are past the years for it (11:71-72 — *“She wondered, “Oh, my! How can I have a child in this old age, and my husband here is an old man? This is truly an astonishing thing!”*).

**The Book's Other Impossible Births**

The same pattern appears wherever the Qur'an describes a child promised against nature. Zachariah, told that he will have a son in his old age and that his wife is barren, exclaims, *“My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?”* and receives the answer, *“So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”* (3:40). Mary asks how she can have a child when no man has touched her. The Book reports each question without embarrassment and answers each with an act of God. Read together, the accounts establish a single principle: the announcement of a child in impossible circumstances is one of the recurring signs of the prophetic story, and the astonishment it produces is part of the sign rather than a defect in the recipient.

**Why the Narratives Keep the Details of Family Life**

The Qur'an could have reported simply that Abraham received good news. Instead it gives the meal, the alarm at guests who did not eat, the wife's laughter, the old age of both partners and the words they spoke in their surprise. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt adds the physical gesture of Sarah striking her forehead in wonder (51:29 — *“Then his wife came forward with a cry, clasping her forehead ˹in astonishment˺, exclaiming, “˹A baby from˺ a barren, old woman!”*), an ordinary human movement that no revelation needed to record and that the Book records anyway.

## Verse 15:55

> They responded, “We give you good news in all truth, so do not be one of those who despair.”

**The Reply to a Question About Possibility**

The visitors answer the astonishment directly, and their answer does not try to satisfy the arithmetic. They do not tell Abraham that the calculation is wrong, or that the years are fewer than he thinks, or that some physical change has occurred in him. They state the standing of the news itself: it is given in truth, which in this context means that it is certain, decided and beyond reversal. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt gives the same reply in the words of the angels when Sarah expresses her wonder, that such has the Lord decreed and that He is truly the All-Wise, All-Knowing (51:30 — *“They replied, “Such has your Lord decreed. He is truly the All-Wise, All-Knowing.”*). The answer to a question about how is therefore an answer about who, and the visitors let the how remain in the hands of the One who decreed it.

**The Command Not to Despair**

The second half of the verse carries an instruction, and it is stated in the negative form the sūrah has already used for Iblīs: do not be among a group. The Arabic warns him against joining a category of people rather than against a single act, and the Book keeps that distinction because despair is not a moment but a condition. The Qur'an treats it as a serious fault on the human side rather than a reasonable response to difficulty. Sūrah Yūsuf phrases the rule for Jacob's sons in words that leave no ambiguity — *“do not lose hope in the mercy of Allah. No one loses hope in Allah's mercy except those with no faith”* (12:87) — and Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt attaches the same condition to those who reject God's signs and the meeting with Him (29:23 — *“As for those who disbelieve in Allah’s signs and the meeting with Him, it is they who will have no hope in His mercy. And it is they who will suffer a painful punishment”*).

**Despair in the Book's Vocabulary**

The Qur'an's word for despair describes the cutting off of expectation, and the Book applies it almost exclusively to the person who has lost confidence in God's capacity or willingness to give. Sūrah al-Rūm observes the pattern in human behaviour at large, where a taste of mercy produces pride and a touch of hardship produces immediate despair (30:36 — *“If We give people a taste of mercy, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it.”*). Sūrah al-Zumar sets out the correct understanding of the matter in the address to the servants who have exceeded all limits, telling them not to lose hope in God's mercy, since He forgives all sins (39:53 — *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”*). The two attitudes the Book rejects are therefore extreme on either side of the middle, the presumption that treats mercy as a guarantee of impunity and the despair that treats the record as final.

**Hope as a Discipline to Be Practised**

The instruction in this verse is given to a man of profound faith, which means the Book treats hope as something that requires maintenance rather than something that comes naturally to a believer. The Qur'an characterises the people of the garden as those who abandon their beds to invoke their Lord with hope and fear (32:16 — *“They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them”*), placing the two attitudes together in the practice of prayer. The same pairing of the two names, forgiving and severe in punishment, which the sūrah announced a few verses earlier, is at work here: the believer fears what he deserves and hopes for what God has promised, and he holds both in his worship. The visitors' command not to despair is addressed to a man who was surely not despairing, which is the point, and it fixes the position each of them is to occupy.

## Verse 15:56

> He exclaimed, “Who would despair of the mercy of their Lord except the misguided?”

**Who the Misguided Are**

The Arabic word rendered *misguided* describes someone who has left the road and is wandering, and the Book uses it for the people whose error has become a condition rather than an accident. Sūrah Yūsuf makes the connection between despair and unbelief explicit, when Jacob warns his sons not to lose hope in the mercy of God, since no one does so except people without faith (12:87 — *“And do not lose hope in the mercy of Allah, for no one loses hope in Allah’s mercy except those with no faith.”*). Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states the reverse with equal clarity, that those who reject God's signs and the meeting with Him are the ones with no hope in His mercy, and that they will suffer a painful punishment (29:23 — *“As for those who disbelieve in Allah’s signs and the meeting with Him, it is they who will have no hope in His mercy. And it is they who will suffer a painful punishment”*). The equation in Abraham's sentence is therefore the Book's own: the despair of God's mercy is not a mood to be pitied but a symptom of lost direction.

**The Mercy No One Should Doubt**

The sūrah has already declared, in God's own voice, that He is the All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, and it has been careful to state that mercy alongside the warning of punishment. Abraham's statement applies the same doctrine to his own situation before the promised gift has arrived. Elsewhere the Book describes the scope of that mercy in terms that make despair unreasonable on any account: God tells Moses that His mercy encompasses everything (7:156 — *“But My mercy encompasses everything.”*), takes mercy upon Himself as a commitment (6:12 — *“Say, “To Allah!” He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful.”*). Addresses the servants who have exceeded all limits against their own souls with the instruction not to lose hope, since He forgives all sins (39:53 — *“He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*). A person who measures God's mercy by the size of his own record has made himself the standard rather than his Lord.

**The Two Errors the Book Corrects**

The sūrah has set the two names side by side, and the correction it applies is symmetrical. Presumption is the error that treats forgiveness as a certainty that requires nothing, and the Book warns against it in the sharpest terms: *“Did they feel secure against Allah's planning? None would feel secure from Allah's planning except the losers.”* (7:99). Despair is the error that treats the record as a verdict already issued, and the verse under commentary names it as the mark of the misguided. A believer is meant to live between the two, fearing the punishment the sūrah has described and hoping in the mercy it announced, and the practice of that balance is what the Book calls worship. The teaching is stated plainly because the two errors are so easy to fall into and so comfortable once entered.

**Why Abraham Was the Right Person to Say It**

The Book's portrait of Abraham makes this sentence consistent with everything else said about him. He is described as a model of excellence, devoted and upright, utterly grateful for the favours of his Lord, chosen and guided to the straight path (16:120-121 — *“˹utterly˺ grateful for Allah’s favours. ˹So˺ He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path”*). He is the man God took as a close friend (4:125 — *“And who is better in faith than those who ˹fully˺ submit themselves to Allah, do good, and follow the Way of Abraham, the upright? Allah chose Abraham as a close friend”*). He had asked for offspring and had been given a covenant whose fulfilment he had waited for across most of a lifetime. When the Book puts the sentence about despair in his mouth, it is letting the reader hear the position of a man whose own history had put the mercy of God to the test, and the answer is that the test had not moved him from it.

## Verse 15:57

> He ˹then˺ added, “What is your mission, O messenger-angels?”

**The Term "Messenger-Angels"**

The translation renders the word of address as *messenger-angels*, and the compound reflects the Book's usage. The visitors are not wanderers who happened to appear; they are envoys with a commission. Sūrah Hūd introduces the same group with the same title: *“And surely Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with good news ˹of a son˺.”* (11:69), and Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt uses it again when the same angels reach Lot (29:31 — *“When Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with the good news ˹of the birth of Isaac˺, they said, “We are going to destroy the people of this city ˹of Sodom˺, for its people have persisted in wrongdoing.”*). The Book describes angelic movement as commissioned and purposive, subject to command and dispatched for decreed matters, a point stated directly in the sūrah of the Night of Decree: *“That night the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every ˹decreed˺ matter.”* (97:4). The phrase in this verse places the men at Abraham's table inside that system of government.

**The Same Question in Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt**

The parallel account uses closely similar words: *“˹Later,˺ Abraham asked, ‘What is your mission, O messengers?’”* (51:31), and the sūrah then supplies the answer in detail, that the visitors have been sent to a wicked people to rain down stones of baked clay marked by the Lord for the transgressors (51:32-34 — *“marked by your Lord for the transgressors.”*). The telling in al-Ḥijr follows a different order and reserves the reason for the following verses. Reading the two passages together shows the sūrahs' method of telling one story twice: each fixes on the moment its own argument requires and trusts the reader to gather the rest elsewhere in the Book. In both cases the question is answered plainly, which is the difference between this scene and the exchanges the sūrah recorded earlier with the men who demanded signs and rejected every answer.

**A Prophet Asking a Question and Receiving an Answer**

The Qur'an records a number of occasions on which messengers and righteous people ask their Lord a question. Zachariah asks how he can have a son when he is old and his wife barren, and receives the answer that God does what He wills (3:40 — *“Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”*). Abraham asks for the mission of the visitors at his table and receives it. The Book's treatment of questions is divided by the asker's disposition: a question asked to learn has an answer, and a question asked to escape an obligation is met with a description of what is coming. The prophets of the Qur'an are shown asking, waiting and being answered, and the sūrah's first audience is placed in front of those scenes after being shown what happened to men who treated the message as a joke.

**Hospitality Given Before Identity Was Known**

Abraham had no idea who his visitors were when the meal was prepared. The Book makes that clear by the sequence of events, since his fear begins only after the food is refused (11:70 — *“And when he saw that their hands did not reach for the food, he became suspicious and fearful of them. They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not be afraid! We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.”*). His generosity was therefore offered to strangers without any assurance about their standing, and the sūrah places that fact in front of a community whose own welcome of the Prophet and his companions was, at that stage, thin. What the Book chooses to remember about the friend of God is not the size of his herds or the strength of his household but the calf he set before unknown guests and the question he asked them afterwards. Readers who follow the sūrah's sequence from its warnings about mockery to this scene are being shown the conduct that the mockers themselves were not practising.

## Verse 15:58

> They replied, “We have actually been sent to a wicked people

**The Same Word the Sūrah Used Before**

The Arabic word rendered *wicked* comes from a root that covers crime and the cutting off of bonds, and the sūrah has already used it earlier in its own account of the men who mocked the Prophet, where their disbelief is said to steep into the hearts of the wicked (15:12 — *“This is how We allow disbelief ˹to steep˺ into the hearts of the wicked”*). The visitors' answer therefore connects the town they are travelling to with the audience hearing the story. The Book's vocabulary is chosen to make that connection unavoidable: the same description that covers the people of Sodom covers the mockers of Makkah, not because their offences are identical but because both are committed against the same warning. A community reading these verses is being told that it recognises itself in the story's second half or it does not, and the recognition is left to the reader.

**Who the People Were and What They Had Done**

The Qur'an names the crime of the cities plainly and without euphemism. Lot rebukes his people: *“Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before? You lust after men instead of women! You are certainly transgressors.”* (7:80-81), and Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt adds further details in his rebuke, including the abuse of travellers and openness in their gatherings, to which his people's reply was to demand that the punishment be brought upon them if he spoke the truth (29:29 — *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?”*). Sūrah al-Naml records the same exchange, and Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ has Lot ask his people why they lust after men and leave the spouses their Lord created for them (26:165-166 — *“Why do you ˹men˺ lust after fellow men”*). The Book's account of the city is therefore not vague about what happened there; it is explicit about the practices and about the arrogance that accompanied them.

**A Mission to Destroy, Not to Preach**

The angels' errand differs from the mission of every prophet in the Book. Lot had been sent to his people to call them to God, and he had preached, warned and been threatened with expulsion for it (26:167 — *“They threatened, “If you do not desist, O Lot, you will surely be expelled.”*). The visitors sent in this passage arrive after that effort has failed, and their task is execution rather than invitation. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt states the mission in hardware: the messengers are sent to a wicked people to rain upon them stones of baked clay marked by their Lord for the transgressors (51:33-34 — *“marked by your Lord for the transgressors.”*). The distinction matters for the way the sūrah's audience understands its own situation. The warning they were hearing was the time of preaching, and the fate of the city illustrates what follows when that time runs out.

**Envoys Who Carry Decrees**

The verse's opening places the visitors in a chain of command: we have been sent. The Book's picture of the angels is consistently bureaucratic in the older sense of the word, with assigned duties, permissions and decrees. They descend by permission of their Lord for every decreed matter (97:4 — *“That night the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every ˹decreed˺ matter”*), and they do not act on their own initiative. In another scene, when the Prophet is asked by Gabriel about the timing of a certain matter, the answer given is that the angels descend only by the command of their Lord (19:64 — *“We only descend by the command of your Lord. To Him belongs whatever is before us, and whatever is behind us, and everything in between. And your Lord is never forgetful”*). The visitors in this verse therefore speak as officers of an administration rather than as spirits with their own agendas, and the sentence they deliver to Abraham is a report of the office they hold rather than an expression of opinion about the town.

Sūrah Hūd describes the command arriving and the cities being turned upside down, with stones of baked clay rained upon them, marked by the Lord (11:82-83 — *“When Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay”*).

His response, recorded elsewhere in the Book, is to plead for them (11:74-76 — *“Then after the fear had left Abraham, and the good news had reached him, he began to plead with Us for the people of Lot Truly, Abraham was forbearing, tender-hearted, and ever turning ˹to his Lord˺ ˹The angels said,˺ “O Abraham! Plead no more! Your Lord’s decree has already come, and they will certainly be afflicted with a punishment that cannot be averted!”*), and he is told that the decree has already come and cannot be averted.

## Verse 15:59

> As for the family of Lot, we will certainly deliver them all

**Why the Righteous Are Never Swept Away**

The Book presents the preservation of the righteous in these narratives as a matter of principle rather than of sentiment. When Abraham raises the case of the cities with his visitors, asking whether the righteous will be destroyed with the wicked, the angels give an answer that settles the point: *“He said, ‘But Lot is there!’ They responded, ‘We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.’”* (29:32). The exchange is the Book's own statement of the principle: judgment is by person, and the presence of a righteous man in a doomed city is answered with a rescue, not with an exemption for everyone else. The destruction of the wicked is not a natural disaster with indiscriminate effects; it is an operation with a passenger list.

**"All Together": the Totality of the Deliverance**

The final element of the sentence emphasises completeness, which is the same emphasis the Book uses when it describes the angels prostrating as a body earlier in the sūrah. When the time of destruction arrives, the rescue is not partial. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt records the operation from God's side with the same insistence on totality: the believers were evacuated from the city before the torment (51:35 — *“Then ˹before the torment˺ We evacuated the believers from the city”*). There is no account in the Book of a faithful man in these cities left behind because the destruction was arranged by district, and none of a household rescued except in fragments. What the visitors announce is a complete removal of everything the decree permits to survive, and one exception, which the next verse names.

**One Family Found in a City**

The Book's honesty about numbers appears in the same context as the promise of rescue. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt says that only one family was found there that had submitted (51:36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). The statement follows a description of destruction, and its position makes it a lesson about how few the people who accept a prophet's message can be, even after years of effort by a superior man. The sūrah's own audience in Makkah was small and under pressure, and the story of Lot's household is placed before them as evidence that the worth of a community is not measured by its size. What is being rescued in this verse is a single family, and the destruction that follows is a whole society.

**The Ruins That Remain as Evidence**

The rescue announced in this verse is completed in the verses that follow, and the Book attaches a consequence to the whole episode that survives to this day. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt closes its account of the cities with the words, *“And We did leave ˹some of˺ its ruins as a clear lesson for people of understanding.”* (29:35). The sūrah's own narrative of the Stone Valley, which it will reach later in the same chapter, leaves the same kind of trace in the landscape.

---

## Verse 15:60

> except his wife. We have determined that she will be one of the doomed.”

**Who She Was and What She Did**

The Qur'an names her offense in a passage that leaves no room for ambiguity. Concerning two women, it says: *“the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot. Each was married to one of Our righteous servants, yet betrayed them. So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever. Both were told, ‘Enter the Fire, along with the others.’”* (66:10). The betrayal described is not stated in detail in the Book, and the commentators explained it in the wife of Lot's cooperation with his enemies and her failure to keep faith with his mission. What the verse establishes is the principle the Book draws from the two cases: a marriage to a prophet carries no guarantee, and a household tie cannot substitute for a person's own loyalty to God.

**An Exception Inside the Promise**

The promise of rescue was stated as complete, and this verse reopens it to remove a single person. The placement is deliberate and painful: the exception arrives after the household has been gathered in the reader's mind as an unit, so that the loss is felt as a subtraction rather than a qualification. The Book keeps the same detail in every account it gives of the story, in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf (7:83 — *“So We saved him and his family except his wife, who was one of the doomed”*), Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ (26:171 — *“except an old woman, who was one of the doomed”*), Sūrah al-Naml (27:57 — *“So We delivered him and his family, except his wife. We had destined her to be one of the doomed”*) and Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt (29:32 — *“He said, “But Lot is there!” They responded, “We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.”*), and the consistency is itself the message. A reader who wants to know whether family membership secures salvation is given the answer four times, in the same words, in the same narrative.

**"One of Those Who Lagged Behind"**

The phrase appears in several tellings, with variations that the Book does not conceal. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf says she was one of the doomed, Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ describes her as an old woman who remained, and Sūrah al-Naml says she was destined to be one of the doomed. The wording in each case places her with the group that stayed rather than with the group that left. The sūrah's own account of the night journey makes the point explicit when the angels instruct Lot not to let any of his household look back, with one exception, and warn that she will suffer the fate of the others (11:81 — *“She will certainly suffer the fate of the others.”*). She is not described as an opponent of the message in so many words. She is described as someone who remained where she was.

**Kinship and the Limits of Rescue**

The story of Lot's wife is one of several in the Qur'an that sever the assumption that a family relationship carries a person into safety. Noah's son is refused the same rescue on the ark, and the Book records God's words to Noah when he appeals for him: *“O Noah! He is certainly not of your family—he was completely of unrighteous conduct.”* (11:46). Abraham's father is left outside the covenant he was given, and the Book states plainly that his intercession for him did not stand. In each case the relationship is real and the refusal is real, and the Book records both. The verse under commentary belongs to that pattern, and its place in a sūrah whose first audience counted its standing by lineage is not accidental.

## Verse 15:61

> So when the messengers came to the family of Lot

**Lot's Place in the Prophetic Family**

The Book identifies Lot as a messenger in his own right and places him in the same line as the patriarchs: *“And Lot was indeed one of the messengers.”* (37:133). His relationship to Abraham is described in the Book's narratives as a nephew who left with him and settled in a different land, since God says that Abraham was delivered along with Lot to the land that was blessed for all people (21:71 — *“Then We delivered him, along with Lot, to the land We had showered with blessings for all people”*). The two households in this sūrah are therefore connected by blood and by faith, and the verse's mention of Lot's family continues the theme of the household that has run through the preceding verses. The reader is being shown two houses in the same family, one receiving good news and one about to be rescued by the narrowest of margins.

**What His Arrival Cost Him**

The arrival of the visitors was not a pleasant surprise for Lot, and the parallel accounts say so. Sūrah Hūd reports that when the angels came to him he was distressed and worried by their arrival, and that he said, this is a terrible day (11:77 — *“When Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival. He said, “This is a terrible day.”*). Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt uses the same words for his state (29:33 — *“And when Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival.”*). The Book is describing a man who understood his city well enough to know what the appearance of unknown male guests would set off, and who had no means of protecting them. His distress is the response of a person who has been placed in a situation whose outcome he cannot control, and the Book records it before it records anything he did about it.

**Who the Visitors Appeared To Be**

The angels had come in human form, and the Book's narratives show the city's reaction as evidence of how they looked. Sūrah Hūd describes the men of the city rushing toward Lot and his guests, and Lot's plea that they are his guests and must not be humiliated (11:78 — *“And ˹the men of˺ his people—who were used to shameful deeds—came to him rushing.”*; 15:68 — *“Lot pleaded, “Indeed, these are my guests, so do not embarrass me”*). Nothing in the Qur'an describes the visitors' physical appearance directly, and the interpretive tradition understood from the story's circumstances that they came as young men whose looks drew the attention of a people with that reputation. What the Book gives the reader is not a portrait but a consequence: the text shows what a town did when strangers arrived, and the reader infers what those strangers looked like.

**The Reputation of the City**

The crimes of the place are given in the Book in Lot's own rebukes, and one of them is directly relevant to the scene. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt records him asking his people whether they lust after men, abuse travellers, and practise immorality openly in their gatherings, and the answer he received was a challenge to bring on the punishment if he were telling the truth (29:29 — *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?”*). The abuse of travellers is named among their offences, so the arrival of any guest in that city was a matter that put the host in danger. The visitors in this verse walked into a place whose reputation preceded them, and the narrative's tension is set up before they have finished their first sentence.

**The Book's Pace Through a Disaster**

The sūrah will narrate the city's end in a handful of verses, and it devotes a similar handful to its arrival. The compression is deliberate; the Book never lingers on the mechanics of destruction, and it never entertains its readers with the details of the crime. Everything superfluous is stripped away, and what remains is a sequence of actions: the visitors arrive, Lot is troubled, the city comes, the visitors state their errand, the family leaves, the decree falls. Nor does the sūrah name the town, though other passages make its identity clear by describing the cities turned upside down and the stones rained upon them (11:82 — *“When Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay”*).

## Verse 15:62

> he said, “You are surely an unfamiliar people!”

**A Host Reading His Guests**

The sentence is Lot's first recorded reaction, and it says nothing about danger or welcome. He tells his visitors that they are unknown to him, and the observation carries everything that follows in the scene. A small town knows its own people, and strangers stand out at once; the arrival of guests whose faces nobody recognised was a public event in a place with the reputation of Sodom. The Book's account of his words is shorter than the accounts in the parallel sūrahs, which describe him as distressed and worried by their arrival and as calling the day a terrible one (11:77 — *“When Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival. He said, “This is a terrible day.”*). The version here gives the reason before it gives the emotion, and the reason is simply that he cannot place them.

**What He Feared for Them**

The Book makes the danger explicit in the fuller account. Sūrah Hūd says that when the angels came to Lot he was distressed and worried by their arrival, and that he said, this is a terrible day (11:77 — *“When Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival. He said, “This is a terrible day.”*). Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt uses the same description of his state (29:33 — *“And when Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival.”*). His anxiety was not about his own safety. It concerned what his townspeople would do to guests under his roof, and the narratives show that his fear was accurate, since the men of the city were already on their way to him when the conversation resumed. The Book frequently presents righteous people as people who read their surroundings accurately, and Lot's unease is presented as understanding rather than cowardice.

**The Duties of a Host in That World**

In the society addressed by the Qur'an, the arrival of a guest placed the host under an obligation that outranked his own comfort and even his own safety. Lot's language in the verses that follow shows the weight he attached to it: he calls the visitors his guests and pleads with his people not to humiliate him, then begs them to fear God and not to disgrace him (15:68-69 — *“Fear Allah and do not humiliate me.”*). When the situation deteriorates further he says that he wishes he had the strength to resist them or could rely on a strong supporter (11:80 — *“He responded, “If only I had the strength ˹to resist you˺ or could rely on a strong supporter.”*). The Book records that admission without criticism, and the whole scene turns on the fact that hospitality, once accepted, could not simply be withdrawn when it became dangerous.

**A Host Who Cannot Protect His Guests**

The verse under commentary is the moment before a host's duties exceed his capacity. Lot stood alone in a city whose men were united in their practices and who had already threatened him with expulsion when he objected: *“They threatened, ‘If you do not desist, O Lot, you will surely be expelled.’”* (26:167). The Book's account says that in the whole city only one household had submitted (51:36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*), which means he had no party to call on for protection. His visitors were twice his number, unknown to him, and evidently unaware of the danger, and the narrative places him in the position of a man who must defend strangers against a town with nothing but his own standing.

## Verse 15:63

> They responded, “We have come to you with that ˹torment˺ which they have doubted

**The Warning His People Had Doubted**

The thing the visitors have come with is the punishment Lot had been warning his people about, and the Book's account of that warning is preserved in his rebukes. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt records him asking his people whether they practised their shameful deeds openly and being answered with a challenge: *“Bring Allah's punishment upon us, if what you say is true.”* (29:29). The Book's narration of the exchange shows both halves of the situation: a messenger stating what would come, and a people doubting him and demanding it be produced. The angels' reply in this verse uses their own position against them; what they had refused to believe was already on its way and standing in the street.

**Doubt as a Response to Warning**

The Qur'an treats doubt of this kind as a decision rather than an intellectual state. The people of Lot did not examine his claim and find it wanting; they challenged him, mocked him and threatened him with expulsion. Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ records the same reaction to Lot's preaching as it does to the other prophets, and it closes the passage by saying that the people denied the truth and will soon face the consequences of their ridicule (26:6 — *“They have certainly denied ˹the truth˺, so they will soon face the consequences of their ridicule”*).

**When Ridicule Meets Its Object**

The Book has a consistent way of describing what happens to people who laughed at warnings, and it uses the same verb in several places: they are overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule. Sūrah al-Naḥl states it plainly, that the evil consequences of their deeds overtook them and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule (16:34 — *“Then the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds overtook them, and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”*). The Book's own words for the fate of the earlier nations use exactly this formulation, and the arrival of the angels at Lot's door is one of the earliest instances of the pattern in the prophetic story. The verses surrounding this one will apply it in full detail, since the punishment reaches the city at dawn.

**Faith That Arrives Too Late**

The Qur'an records how communities behave when the warning descends upon them, and it states the rule without ambiguity. Sūrah Ghāfir describes a people crying out when they saw the punishment, declaring belief in God alone and rejecting what they had associated with Him, and the next verse gives the verdict: *“But their faith was of no benefit to them when they saw Our torment. This has ˹always˺ been Allah's way ˹of dealing˺ with His ˹wicked˺ servants. Then and there the disbelievers were in ˹total˺ loss.”* (40:84-85). The men of Sodom had asked for the punishment to be produced; when the visitors standing in Lot's house tell him what they have brought, the window for the answer they were hoping to avoid has already closed.

**The Question They Asked the Prophet**

The same challenge reappears in the Book as a question put to the Prophet by his own people, and the answer given to him is a sentence worth remembering beside this verse: *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺, ‘Is this true?’ Say, ‘Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape.’”* (10:53). The sūrah's readers in Makkah were being shown a community that had made that demand and received a reply, and the visitors' words in this verse are the reply.

## Verse 15:64

> We come to you with the truth, and we are certainly truthful

**The Doubled Language of Truth**

The sentence repeats its key word twice in one line. The visitors first say that they have come with the truth, then that they are truthful, and the repetition is not padding. The Book uses a doubled construction when a claim is likely to be doubted, and these words are addressed to a man whose message had been dismissed by his own people for years. Lot stood in a town that had ruled his warnings to be lies, and the envoys sent to deliver the sentence therefore give him the one assurance that mattered to him: what he had been saying was true, and the men saying it now were telling the truth. The sentence reads as vindication before it reads as a threat.

**Truth as the Event, Not Only the Statement**

In the Qur'an's usage, a thing is called true when it happens as described. The Book attaches the same word to the punishment of the earlier nations, to the resurrection, and to the promises made to the messengers, and in each case the meaning is that the event will certainly occur. Sūrah Yūnus gives the Prophet's answer to the men of Makkah who asked whether the warning was true: *“Say, ‘Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape.’”* (10:53). The truth the visitors bring to Lot in this verse is therefore the arrival of something already decreed, and the second clause of the sentence confirms the authority of the messengers who carry it. What they say about the future is not a forecast; it is a report.

**A Lone Warner in a Hostile City**

The Book's own summary of the city's population is stark: only one household there had submitted (51:36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). Lot had preached to a people who answered him with threats of expulsion (26:167 — *“They threatened, “If you do not desist, O Lot, you will surely be expelled.”*) and who demanded that he produce the punishment if he was telling the truth (29:29 — *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.”*). The verse under commentary arrives at the end of that history. The envoys do not ask him to keep preaching or to try again; they state what they have brought and what they are. In a story full of reversals, this is the one the sūrah's audience was meant to notice: a man who spent his life unheeded received the confirmation of his message through the destruction of his audience.

---

## Verse 15:65

> So travel with your family in the dark of night, and follow ˹closely˺ behind them. Do not let any of you look back, and go where you are commanded.”

**The Biblical Account of the Same Detail**

The story of the cities is told in the Bible as well, and the account in Genesis relates that Lot's wife looked back as they fled and became a pillar of salt. The Qur'an's telling preserves the prohibition and never describes the transformation, and Muslim exegetes discussed her fate by way of the destruction that the verses of this sūrah declare. The comparison is useful for a reader who wants to see how the Qur'an handles a shared narrative: it keeps the moral core, the warning and the escape, and it declines to supply the kind of detail that would turn the event into a spectacle. What remains in the Islamic telling is the instruction that the Book puts in the angels' mouths and the lesson a reader draws from a family that was told not to look.

**"Go Where You Are Commanded"**

The last clause leaves the destination unnamed, and the omission is deliberate. The family are not told that they are going to a particular town or to a particular relative's house; they are told to walk and to keep walking where the command places them. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ reports the outcome of that journey, saying that God delivered Abraham, along with Lot, to the land He had showered with blessings for all people (21:71 — *“Then We delivered him, along with Lot, to the land We had showered with blessings for all people”*). The instruction in this verse therefore trains its hearers in the posture the Book keeps asking them to hold: obedience to a command whose full design is not displayed in advance. A household walking through the night out of a city it had lived in for years is a picture of submission in its plainest form.

**Leaving a Place That Has Been Judged**

The verse treats departure as an obligation rather than a matter of preference. A person who recognises that a society has exhausted its term is not meant to stay and watch the ending. The Book's counsel in a different context makes the general principle explicit, asking those who claimed to be oppressed why they did not move to a land wide enough to live in (4:97 — *“When the angels seize the souls of those who have wronged themselves—scolding them, “What do you think you were doing?” they will reply, “We were oppressed in the land.”*). The point is not that people should abandon every difficult place, since the Book's own prophets stayed in theirs for decades.

**A Rescue Carried Out in Obedience**

Everything in this verse is instruction, and the next verse records the revelation of the decree behind it. The sūrah's account of Lot's rescue in another chapter puts both halves in a single line, describing those who were delivered before dawn as a blessing from God, and adding that this is how God rewards whoever gives thanks (54:34-35 — *“as a blessing from Us. This is how We reward whoever gives thanks”*).

## Verse 15:66

> We revealed to him this decree: “Those ˹sinners˺ will be uprooted in the morning.”

**A Decree, Not a Prediction**

The verse says that God revealed the matter to Lot, and the Arabic word used for it belongs to the vocabulary of decisive judgment. The same root appears throughout the Book for the settling of a case, the carrying out of a sentence and the completion of an affair, and its use here tells the reader that what is coming has already been resolved rather than merely foreseen. The Book maintains this distinction carefully: prophecy in its pages is not a forecast that could turn out differently, and the decrees given to messengers are reports of decisions already taken. The command to leave the city in the previous verse and the decree announced in this one are therefore two halves of a single act, and the household's departure is a consequence of the judgment rather than a hope of avoiding it.

**The Word for the Destruction**

The translation renders the fate of the city's people with a term of uprooting, and the image is of something cut off at its base and removed whole. The Book's accounts of the destruction use a range of such words: the cities turned upside down, a rain of baked clay, a blast, a storm of stones. Sūrah Hūd describes the event in the language of command arriving and the settlements being overturned (11:82 — *“When Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay”*), and Sūrah al-Qamar records a storm of stones unleashed upon them while the family of Lot were delivered before dawn (54:34 — *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot’s family, We delivered them before dawn”*). The vocabulary of uprooting in this verse emphasises totality: not a punishment that would leave the society able to continue in some reduced form, but the removal of the thing from the ground it stood on.

**The Timing: "In the Morning"**

The Book fixes the hour, and the parallel account supplies the exchange in which the angels tell Lot that the appointed time is the morning, asking him whether the morning is not near (11:81 — *“Their appointed time is the morning. Is the morning not near?”*). A specific hour is given to a man who has just been told to travel through the night, and the connection between the two instructions is exact: the family needed the hours of darkness to get clear of the place. Sūrah al-Qamar describes the same morning, saying that by the early dawn the people were overwhelmed by an unrelenting torment (54:38 — *“And indeed, by the early morning they were overwhelmed by an unrelenting torment”*). The Book's habit of dating the destruction of communities gives its warnings a quality that general talk of consequences does not have: a day, a hour, a term that arrives.

**The Difference Between Hearing and Believing**

Lot had preached to the city for years with no result. The decree revealed to him personally in the previous verses is the answer to his preaching, and it arrives in a form that leaves no room for doubt. The Book's pattern in such scenes is consistent: the messenger receives the certainty of his message, and the people receive the event. Sūrah al-Qamar notes that the warning had already been given and that the people disputed it (54:36 — *“He had already warned them of Our ˹crushing˺ blow but they disputed the warnings”*). So the sequence in the sūrah is a summary of the whole history of the mission: a lone warner, an audience that argued, a decree from God, and a departure under cover of night while the town celebrated in the dark.

## Verse 15:67

> And there came the men of the city, rejoicing

**The Word "Rejoicing"**

The Arabic describes pleasure and cheerfulness, and the Book uses the word elsewhere for joy of a proper kind, which makes its appearance here the sharper. The commentators explained the cheer as satisfaction at the news of the guests' arrival, since the men of the city were hoping to use them as they had used other travellers. Sūrah Hūd gives the same moment in different language, describing the men of the city, who were used to shameful deeds, coming to Lot rushing (11:78 — *“And ˹the men of˺ his people—who were used to shameful deeds—came to him rushing.”*). The two accounts agree that the approach was eager, and that eagerness was the sound of a society that had turned the abuse of travellers into an occasion for pleasure rather than shame, which is the specific accusation levelled against the city by its own messenger.

**The Behaviour of a City Without Shame**

The Book's descriptions of the cities of Lot are consistently about public conduct rather than private weakness. Lot's rebuke in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt lists openness in their gatherings among their offences (29:29 — *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.”*), and Sūrah al-Naml records the same scene in which the town's response to his preaching was to threaten to expel the one household that wanted to remain chaste (27:56 — *“But his people’s only response was to say, “Expel Lot’s followers from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”*). Nothing in the account describes secret wrongdoing that a community tolerated; what it describes is wrongdoing conducted as community business and defended when challenged. The crowd arriving at Lot's door in this verse is the outcome of that state of affairs, and it is presented as the normal operation of the town rather than an exceptional outbreak.

**A Warning Already Disputed**

The Book records that the city's end had been preceded by years in which Lot warned them, and that their response was argument and mockery rather than attention. Sūrah al-Qamar states the matter in one line: he had already warned them of the blow, and they disputed the warnings (54:36 — *“He had already warned them of Our ˹crushing˺ blow but they disputed the warnings”*). The arrival of the crowd in this verse is therefore not the cause of the punishment but the last episode in a history of refusal, and the rejoicing in the street is part of the evidence against them. The sūrah's readers in Makkah were hearing this story recited to men who had been treating the Prophet as a subject for jokes, and the parallel would not have needed stating.

**Rejoicing Just Before the Seizure**

The Book connects joy of this kind with the moment of judgment in more than one place. Sūrah al-Anʿām describes a people who became oblivious to the warnings that came to them, were given everything they wanted, grew proud of what they had received, and were then seized by surprise so that their cheer turned to despair (6:44 — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired.”*). The men of the city in this verse are in exactly that position: satisfied, unalarmed and busy with the thing they had long desired, on the night before a decree fell. The sūrah does not have to gloss the scene, because its readers have been taught by other passages how the Book treats a community that is enjoying itself at the wrong hour.

**The Road the City Sat On**

Cities of this kind in the Book are not hidden in the desert; they are placed on routes that people travel, which is part of what makes their ruin instructive. A later section of this sūrah states that their remains lie along a known road (15:76 — *“Their ruins still lie along a known route”*), and another passage tells the Prophet's own contemporaries that they pass by the town that was rained upon, and asks whether they have never seen its ruins (25:40 — *“Have they not seen its ruins?”*). The town in this verse, with its men pouring into the street, was for years a destination and a stopover for traders and travellers. The Book's judgement falls on a settlement whose habits were public knowledge to everyone who passed through its gates.

## Verse 15:68

> Lot pleaded, “Indeed, these are my guests, so do not embarrass me

**The Claim a Host Makes in His Own House**

Lot's first words are about hospitality, and the Arabic places the guests before everything else: these are my guests. In the society the Book addresses, the declaration was not a courtesy but a claim of protection. By naming the visitors as his guests, Lot was telling the crowd that any harm done to them would be his own humiliation and that his standing in the town was bound up with their safety. The Book's records of his words in the parallel passage use the same language and add a challenge: he pleads with his people not to humiliate him by disrespecting his guests and asks whether there is not even one right-minded man among them (11:78 — *“Is there not ˹even˺ a single right-minded man among you?”*). He is negotiating with the last currency he has.

**What the Appeal Revealed About the Man**

The verse shows Lot doing what a righteous man does when he is overwhelmed: he uses the tools available to him, in order, without abandoning the people he is trying to protect. He had already tried preaching, and he had been threatened with expulsion for it. He had been left, by the Book's own reckoning, with one household in the city that had submitted (51:36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). Now he stands at his door trying to buy time with an appeal to honour, and the sūrah records him doing it without any suggestion that his effort was misdirected or his position shameful. The Book's prophets are shown using the means at hand: Noah builds, Moses argues, the Prophet negotiates with the tribes, and Lot pleads with a mob.

**Hospitality in the Book's Moral Order**

The Qur'an treats the protection of guests as part of the conduct expected of a believer, and the Book's list of those who deserve a person's kindness includes the near neighbour, the distant neighbour, the companion by the road and the needy traveller (4:36 — *“And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, ˹needy˺ travellers, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession.”*). Abraham's evening with unknown visitors, described earlier in this sūrah, is the positive image of the same obligation, and Lot's night is its negative one. The contrast between the two households is deliberately sharp: in one, a stranger is fed and then asked his business; in the other, a town comes to take a stranger by force. The Book places both inside a single sūrah so that its readers can see how much of a society's health is contained in the way it treats a person it does not know.

**Guests Whose Identity the Host Never Learned**

Lot did not know who had come to him. The Book records that the visitors were angels, and that he only learned their errand later in the night, when they told him that they had been sent against his people. Abraham's encounter with the same party shows how far the obligation runs ahead of knowledge: he received them, greeted them, noticed that they seemed unfamiliar, and hastened to bring a roasted calf, standing over them and urging them to eat, and only then discovered that his hands had been serving messengers (51:24-27 — *“˹Then he said to himself,˺ “˹These are˺ an unfamiliar people.”*). When the visitors did not reach for the food, the Book records his alarm and their reassurance that they were angels sent to the people of Lot (11:70 — *“We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.”*). Hospitality in these narratives is owed to the guest as he arrives, and the household that honours a stranger may be entertaining more than it can see.

---

## Verse 15:69

> Fear Allah and do not humiliate me.”

**The Appeal Moves From Honour to God**

Having invoked the claim of a host, Lot turns to the authority of the One who made them. The two sentences are short and they are run together in the text, which fits the way a man speaks when he has only a moment to say what matters. The first appeal was to the sentiment of a town; the second is to its religion. The men he was addressing believed in God, whatever their conduct, and the sūrah has already shown that Makkan audiences who lived carelessly still believed in powers above them and assigned to God daughters and partners. Lot's plea took that belief and made it the ground of his request, asking them to fear the Lord they acknowledged rather than to be shamed before a neighbour.

**The Word for God-Fearing in the Sūrah's Own Vocabulary**

The term Lot uses is formed from the same root as the word the sūrah employed a few lines earlier for the people it promised gardens and springs, the ones mindful of God (15:45 — *“Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs”*). The Book's vocabulary of guarding oneself appears in both places, and the connection is exact: what Lot was asking of the crowd at his door was that they become, for one night, what the sūrah has just described as the condition of the blessed. The same word stands at the head of Sūrah al-Baqarah's description of the people the Book guides, those who believe in the unseen, establish prayer and give from what they have been provided (2:3 — *“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them”*). The sūrah therefore places the two uses of the term within a few verses, one in a description of the reward and the other on the lips of a prophet barring a doorway.

---

## Verse 15:70

> They responded, “Have we not forbidden you from protecting anyone?”

**Where the Men of the City Stood in the Book's Own Scale**

The Qur'an measures societies by their responses to messengers, and it records these responses with a set of recurring phrases: they rejected, they mocked, they demanded a punishment, they threatened expulsion, they disputed the warnings. This verse belongs to that vocabulary. The town's answer to its prophet is not an argument but a claim of authority: they had forbidden him, and the question they ask assumes he should have obeyed. Sūrah al-Naml records the same city's response in almost exactly the same spirit, when its people's only answer to Lot's rebuke was to demand that his followers be expelled from the land for wanting to remain chaste (27:56 — *“But his people’s only response was to say, “Expel Lot’s followers from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”*). In both texts the town's final argument is its right to decide who belongs.

**An Answer That Answers Nothing**

The crowd does not deny what they intend to do, and they do not engage with the two appeals Lot has just made. They reply with a reminder of an arrangement they claim was already settled: they had told him, on some previous occasion, to stay out of their way in a matter they regarded as their own. The form of the question is confrontational, and its effect is to move the discussion from what is right to what was agreed. The sūrah records the sentence in full because it shows precisely what a community does when a prophet's argument cannot be answered: it changes the subject from the deed to the jurisdiction.

**The Prohibition Every Warmer Is Given**

The claim made in this verse has a familiar shape in the Book's stories. The peoples who rejected their messengers rarely argued the point; they issued a boundary. Shuʿayb's townspeople threatened to drive him and his followers out of the land unless they returned to the local faith, and he answered by asking whether they would do so even against his will (7:88 — *“We will certainly expel you and your fellow believers from our land, unless you return to our faith.”*). In Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ the response to that same man was a demand that he stop or face expulsion (26:167 — *“They threatened, “If you do not desist, O Lot, you will surely be expelled.”*), and the people of another messenger told him they saw him as a bad omen and would stone him if he did not desist (36:18 — *“The people replied, “We definitely see you as a bad omen for us.”*). Lot's crowd speaks the same language, and it is the language of communities that have nothing to say to a prophet except where the borders of his activity lie.

## Verse 15:71

> He said, “O my people! Here are my daughters ˹so marry them˺ if you wish to do so.”

**The Last Offer a Host Can Make**

Having been told that he had no right to protect anyone, Lot makes an offer that places the decision back in his people's hands: his daughters are before them. The Book records the same proposal in Sūrah Hūd with the reasoning attached, where he presents them as pure for his people and asks them to fear God and not humiliate him by disrespecting his guests (11:78 — *“So fear Allah, and do not humiliate me by disrespecting my guests.”*). The offer is the extremity of a man who has exhausted argument and is now trying to divert a crowd with something he has the authority to give. The text reports it without approving it, and the Book's readers have long debated what exactly the offer entailed.

**Marriage as the Answer to the Crowd's Use of the Body**

The offer is significant because of what it answers. The city's offence was the use of other people's bodies without any bond, and Lot's proposal substitutes an union the Book regards as the proper setting for desire. He does not ask them to become ascetics and he does not pretend that the urges they came out with were imaginary; he asks them to redirect those urges into a form that would give the women a place in their households and the men a share in responsibilities. The sūrah's readers would understand how far the offer's frame sat from the town's habits. The Book elsewhere treats the bond it names as the home of tranquility between spouses, with compassion and mercy placed between them (30:21 — *“And He has placed between you compassion and mercy.”*), and the city's customs had made that arrangement irrelevant.

**The Failure of the Offer**

The next verse in Sūrah Hūd records the crowd's answer, in which they insist that he knows they have no need for his daughters and that he knows what they want (11:79 — *“They argued, “You certainly know that we have no need for your daughters. You already know what we desire!”*). Their reply is the moment the scene's logic closes. They are not interested in lawful partnerships, households or the standing of the women in question; they want the visitors, and they say so without apology.

**What a Prophet's Helplessness Teaches**

The Book records Lot's plea for strength that same night, when he wishes he had the power to resist his people or a strong supporter to rely on (11:80 — *“He responded, “If only I had the strength ˹to resist you˺ or could rely on a strong supporter.”*). That sentence, spoken by a messenger in the middle of a confrontation, is one of the most human moments in the Qur'an's prophetic narratives, and it is meant to be understood alongside the offer in this verse. The Book does not present the prophets as figures who never reach the end of their resources. It presents them as people who exhaust every lawful means, ask for help when they have none left, and are rescued by God beyond anything they could arrange. The daughters offered in this verse are the last item in that list of means.

---

## Verse 15:72

> By your life ˹O Prophet˺, they certainly wandered blindly, intoxicated ˹by lust˺

The Book swears by many things in the course of its addresses: by the sun and its brightness, by the fig and the olive, by the pen and what the scribes write, by the morning light. The commentators record the report from Ibn ʿAbbās that God never created or formed a soul dearer to Him than Muḥammad's, and that this is the only life the Book swears by.

**Intoxication Without Wine**

The word for intoxication in this verse belongs to the Arabic vocabulary of drink, and it is used in the Book for the state produced by wine. Sūrah al-Naḥl mentions the intoxicant obtained from the fruit of the palm and the vine alongside wholesome provision (16:67 — *“And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision. Surely in this is a sign for those who understand”*), and Sūrah al-Nisāʾ forbids approaching prayer while intoxicated until the speaker knows what he is saying (4:43 — *“O believers! Do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you are aware of what you say, nor in a state of ˹full˺ impurity—unless you merely pass through ˹the mosque˺—until you have bathed.”*), a verse that belongs to the stages by which drink was finally prohibited. The men of the city were not drunk. The Book applies the word to them because the condition it names is not the drink but what the drink does: it covers the judgement and hands the person over to whatever appetite has his attention. The town's appetite had done that work for it.

**Blindness That Was Not in the Eyes**

The sūrah pairs the intoxication with a word for blind wandering, and its readers would know that the Book uses blindness for the loss of understanding more often than for the loss of sight. A verse elsewhere states that it is not the eyes that grow blind but the hearts within the chests (22:46 — *“Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind”*). The crowd at Lot's door could see its visitors clearly, and it could see the man pleading with it; what it could not do was see the situation it was in, or the guests for what they were, or the hour in which all of this was happening. The Book's account of the scene therefore uses two metaphors that describe the same ruin from different sides: the judgement was covered, and the sight was gone.

**Why the Verse Comes After the Offer**

The oath is placed after Lot's offer of his daughters, and its position is part of its meaning. The crowd had just refused a lawful arrangement for the desire that had brought them out, insisting on what they wanted instead (11:79 — *“They argued, “You certainly know that we have no need for your daughters. You already know what we desire!”*). Their refusal is the evidence for the verdict in this verse: a preference that could not be argued for and would not be traded was not a decision, it was a condition. The Book's verdict on societies that behave in this way is not that they chose an evil and could have chosen better, though the sūrah's readers were invited to choose better; it is that the choosing faculty had been taken out of their hands by the practice of the thing itself.

**The Prophet's Vindication in a Sentence**

The oath by the Prophet's life sits in the middle of a story about Lot, and the parallel between the two men is assembled by position rather than argument. The Prophet was being mocked in Makkah by men who had demanded signs, called him had, and asked why angels were not sent (15:6-7 — *“They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?”*). The verse swears by his life in a passage that ends with a mockery-blind city being wiped out at dawn. Sūrah al-Qamar's account of the same narrative notes that the warnings had been disputed (54:36 — *“He had already warned them of Our ˹crushing˺ blow but they disputed the warnings”*), which is the exact description of the Prophet's experience in his own city. The oath in this verse therefore does two things at once: it pronounces on Sodom and it answers Makkah.

## Verse 15:73

> So the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them at sunrise

**One Sound as the Instrument of Judgment**

The verse names the means of the city's destruction with a single word: a blast, a shout, a cry that no wall could keep out and no arrangement could answer. The Book uses the same word for the ruin of several communities. It says that the blast overtook the wrongdoers so that they fell lifeless in their homes, and it says the same of the people of the thicket after Shuʿayb had warned them (11:67 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*; 11:94 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*). For the Thamud the Book speaks of one blast that left them like the twigs a fence-builder gathers, and Sūrah al-Ḥijr will use the very same word again for the people of the stone valley (54:31 — *“Indeed, We sent against them ˹only˺ one ˹mighty˺ blast, leaving them like the twigs of fence-builders”*; 15:83 — *“But the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them in the morning”*). The vocabulary is deliberately repetitious. A reader of the Book learns that God has no shortage of instruments and no need for elaborate ones.

**Sunrise: The Hour Promised to Lot**

By naming the sunrise, the verse closes a schedule that the reader has been following for several verses. The angels told Lot that the appointed time was the morning (11:81 — *“Their appointed time is the morning. Is the morning not near?”*), the decree revealed to him said the city's people would be uprooted in the morning, and now the blast falls at the rising of the sun. The Book's narratives of judgment are frequently this exact about time. Noah's flood has its beginning, the drowning of the pursuers has its moment, and the towns of Lot have their hour.

**The Last Night of a City**

Everything the reader knows about this night has been told in a handful of lines. The visitors came, the family was warned, the route was given, the decree was announced, the men of the town gathered at the door rejoicing, and their prophet's last appeals were refused with a claim about their rights over him. Sunrise is the first moment after that sequence, and the Book treats it as the natural end of the story rather than as an interruption. The Book has already said of another people that they were seized in the middle of the enjoyment of what they had been given (6:44 — *“But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair”*).

**The Cry and the Command Behind It**

The creation narratives of the Book make the connection between God's speech and the existence of things direct. Sūrah Yā-Sīn states that when God wills a thing to be, He simply says to it, "Be," and it is (36:82 — *“All it takes, when He wills something ˹to be˺, is simply to say to it: “Be!” And it is”*). The blast that overtook the towns is the same kind of utterance: a word with effects, spoken over a place that had been warned for years. This is why the Book's punishment scenes are so often wordless from the human side. There is no weapon described, no attacker, no army to resist. A sound arrives, and the city is no longer there. A reader who has absorbed the sūrah's earlier verses about the creation of the human being from clay and the guarding of the heavens has already been told what kind of world this is.

**Overtaken While Looking On**

The Book preserves another description of the destruction of the towns that places the people inside the event as spectators. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt records that a blast overtook a people while they were looking on (51:44 — *“Still they persisted in defying the commands of their Lord, so they were overtaken by a ˹mighty˺ blast while they were looking on”*), and the words are the same family as those used here. The detail matters because it removes every possibility of interpretation after the fact. The generation that was destroyed had watched its own end arrive, which is the opposite of the position of the sūrah's readers, who see the ruins of these towns on the road and have the whole account preserved for them. The punishment of the people was completed in a moment; the warning to those who came after them continues indefinitely.

## Verse 15:74

> And We turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down and rained upon them stones of baked clay

**Stones of Baked Clay**

The material named in the verse is not loose rock but clay that has been hardened by fire, and the Arabic term for it is rare enough to have occupied the lexicographers. Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt records the same material in the angels' own description of their errand, where they tell Abraham that they have been sent against a people to send upon them stones of baked clay, marked by their Lord for the transgressors (51:33-34 — *“to send upon them stones of ˹baked˺ clay”*). Sūrah Hūd adds the qualification that these stones were marked by the Lord of the Prophet and that they were not far from the wrongdoers of that later generation (11:83 — *“marked by your Lord ˹O Prophet˺. And these stones are not far from the ˹pagan˺ wrongdoers”*). The detail turns a natural disaster into an addressed sentence. The stones were not debris; they were a delivery.

**Rain, and What Rain Normally Means in the Book**

The Book frequently uses rain for mercy. It describes winds sent as good tidings ahead of God's grace, driving heavy clouds to a dead land so that water comes down and every kind of fruit is brought out (7:57 — *“When they bear heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land and then cause rain to fall, producing every type of fruit.”*). The towns of Lot receive a rain as well, and it is the exact inverse of that picture: water that revives, replaced by clay that destroys. The Book's earlier account of the same event calls down a dreadful rain upon the city, and the sūrah's own line about the ruins lying along a known route depends on the reader knowing what kind of shower fell there (25:40 — *“They have certainly passed by the city ˹of Sodom˺, which had been showered with a dreadful rain ˹of stones˺.”*; 15:76 — *“Their ruins still lie along a known route”*). The same element of creation that brings a dead earth to life is turned against a living community.

**An Overturning Witnessed by Nobody Nearby**

The towns had visitors the night before, and by sunrise those visitors had gone, walking through the dark with instructions not to look back. The Book preserves the outcome of the household's departure, saying that God delivered Lot's family before dawn as a blessing, and adds that only one family in the city had submitted (54:34 — *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot’s family, We delivered them before dawn”*; 51:36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). The judgement therefore fell on an emptied place apart from the one woman whose fate has been separated from the rest.

**The Architecture That Could Not Help**

The Book's descriptions of destroyed communities frequently list what the people had built or controlled. Sūrah al-Ḥajj joins ruined settlements with abandoned wells and lofty palaces in a single line, and describes societies destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, living on in total ruin (22:45 — *“Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces”*). The stone valley's people will be shown carving their dwellings out of the mountains, confident in what they had cut into the rock (15:82 — *“They carved their homes in the mountains, feeling secure”*). The towns of Lot may not have had such fortifications, but the sentence in this verse contains its own answer to every form of security: the ground on which a city stands is itself one of the things its inhabitants cannot defend.

**The Cities and the Guests Sent Against Them**

The Book's account of the night joins the visitors' errand to the sentence that followed them. When Abraham saw that his guests did not reach for the food he had brought, he grew uneasy, and they told him not to fear, adding that they had been sent only against the people of Lot (11:70 — *“We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.”*). The same day they walked into the doomed town and were received by a man who did not know what they were. By evening the household had been given a route and a hour, and by sunrise the place of these events was under its own rubble. The verse's opening word, "And We," keeps the acts in the hand of God even though the Book has shown three travellers walking into town the previous afternoon.

## Verse 15:75

> Surely in this are signs for those who contemplate

**The Faculty the Book Keeps Asking Its Readers to Use**

The command to consider, reflect, reason and remember runs through the entire Book, and the sūrah's refrain belongs to that larger campaign. Elsewhere the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are called signs for people of understanding (3:190 — *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason”*), and a passage in Sūrah Qāf states that in the account of the destroyed peoples there is a reminder for whoever has a mindful heart and lends an attentive ear (50:37 — *“Surely in this is a reminder for whoever has a ˹mindful˺ heart and lends an attentive ear”*). Sūrah al-Baqarah puts the matter differently when it reports the demand of those who wanted a sign they had chosen, and answers that the signs have been made clear for people of sure faith (2:118 — *“Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith”*). The Book's call is never to suspend reason; it is to use the faculties of observation and judgement on material that is already in front of the reader.

**The Aftermath Turned Into Instruction**

The verse steps out of the narrative and addresses the reader, which is one of the sūrah's recurring habits. Having described the family's departure, the decree's date, the crowd's arrival, the refusal of every appeal and the ruin of the cities, the text pauses to tell an audience what it has just received. What the account contains is not a story about the distant past but evidence directed at whoever is listening, and the Book says so in a single closing line. The pattern appears again a few lines later with slightly different wording, and again at the end of the sūrah's next case study. The repetitions form a refrain, and the refrain is addressed to people rather than to places.

**The Difference Between Knowing a Story and Being Addressed by It**

A reader of the Book knows these events already from other sūrahs, and the sūrah's customers had heard the account of Lot's people repeatedly. The verse therefore distinguishes two kinds of acquaintance. One is familiarity with a narrative about a nation that ended badly. The other is the posture of a person who takes the account as something said to him, weighing his own conduct against the pattern displayed. The Book calls the second of these contemplation, and it makes the difference the subject of the verse. In Sūrah al-Ḥijr the heedless attitude is described at the beginning, where the mockers are told to eat, enjoy themselves and be diverted by false hopes, and are promised that they will come to know (15:3 — *“˹So˺ let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by ˹false˺ hope, for they will soon know”*). The line in this verse stands at the opposite end of that same concern.

## Verse 15:76

> Their ruins still lie along a known route

**A Road That Outlived Its Cities**

The verse tells the reader where the evidence sits. What remains of the settlements is not hidden in an untravelled place or preserved in a temple archive; it lies beside a route that people used, which means the destruction of the towns was visible to anyone making the journey. Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt addresses the Prophet's own townspeople with the observation that they pass by the ruins of these cities day and night and asks whether they will not then understand (37:137-138 — *“and night. Will you not then understand”*).

**Geography as a Standing Witness**

The Book's ruined peoples are usually placed in lands that its first audiences knew, and the reason is that a ruin does not require an argument. Sūrah al-Ḥajj describes destroyed settlements lying in total ruin, alongside abandoned wells and lofty palaces, and credits none of them with mystery (22:45 — *“Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces”*). A traveller who came upon heaps of masonry where a town had been could hold both facts in his hands: the Book's warning and the empty space. The sūrah's readers are being asked to notice that their world contained such places, and that the account of what happened there and the shape of what remained agreed with each other.

**Two Kinds of Remembrance**

The verse invites a distinction the Book makes repeatedly. One kind of remembrance is the ordinary work of a community keeping up the memory of its ancestors. The other is the use of a trace as a warning, and the Book means the second when it points at these ruins. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states that a clear lesson for people of understanding was left behind at the site (29:35 — *“And We did leave ˹some of˺ its ruins as a clear lesson for people of understanding”*), and the phrase is a definition of what the sūrah is asking its readers to do with the road. The ruins are not there to satisfy interest in ancient events. They are there so that somebody standing where the town used to be can consider what bringing a community to that end.

**A Sign That Requires No Excavation**

The Book's method of proving its warnings is not the uncovering of new evidence. It assumes its audience is already familiar with a landscape of ruins and with the accounts attached to them, and it asks only for the use of faculties everyone has. Sūrah Yūsuf ends its long narrative with the observation that in the stories of the messengers there is a lesson for people of understanding, describing the account as confirmation of what came before it and a detailed explanation of all things (12:111 — *“In their stories there is truly a lesson for people of reason.”*). The verse in this sūrah adopts the same stance toward the traces on the road: everything necessary is available, and the only question left open is what the traveller does with what he passes.

**What the Book Does Not Ask of Its Readers**

Nothing in this verse directs anyone to visit the site, perform a rite there, or treat the ruins as objects of veneration. The Book's instruction is consistently about recognition rather than pilgrimage: it tells its hearers to travel through the land and observe what became of those who denied, and elsewhere it reports that a messenger was sent to every community with the same call (6:11 — *“Say, “Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers.”*; 16:36 — *“So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers”*). The trace on the road is a witness, not a shrine. A community that keeps its warnings attached to ordinary geography, to be encountered on the way to somewhere else, is not asking its people for ceremonies. It is asking them to notice.

## Verse 15:77

> Surely in this is a sign for those who believe

**Why a Sign Needs a Believer**

A sign points beyond itself, and pointing requires a reader who accepts that there is something to point to. A person convinced that the world is a sequence of accidents will look at a ruined city and register a ruin; a person who holds that the world is administered and that communities answer for their conduct will look at the same stones and read a warning. The Book states the principle positively when it says that the signs have been made clear for people of sure faith (2:118 — *“Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith”*), and it states the alternative when it describes those who have no knowledge demanding a sign on their own terms.

**Belief as the Condition of Perceiving**

The Book nowhere suggests that the evidence is thin or that the believer is asked to accept less than the facts allow. What it says is that the capacity to profit from the signs is connected to the state of the person who approaches them. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the declaration that God will turn away from His signs those who behave unjustly with arrogance in the land, adding that even if such people were to see every sign they still would not believe, and that if they saw the right path they would not take it (7:146 — *“If they see the Right Path, they will not take it.”*). The verse is the severest statement of the principle in the Book: perception is a gift, and arrogance is the disposition that forfeits it.

**Those Who Pass the Ruins and Expect Nothing**

The sūrah's audience was familiar with the sites described in the previous verses, and the Book tells them plainly what their reaction had been. Sūrah al-Furqān says of the same city that it was showered with a dreadful rain, asks whether the people had not seen its ruins, and then explains their indifference: they do not expect to be resurrected (25:40 — *“Have they not seen its ruins?”*). That final clause is the diagnosis the sūrah's refrain assumes. People who have decided that there is no accounting after death can walk past the rubble of a punished community without disturbance, because for them nothing was happening in that account. The sign is not hidden from such readers; it has simply been ruled out in advance.

**The Believers Named in This Sūrah**

The term appears at strategic points in the chapter. Lot's household is exceptioned from the destruction and the believers among them are said to have been evacuated from the city, and the Book records that God found in that place but one family that had submitted (51:35-36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). The stone valley's people will be told, in a few verses, that in their story there is a sign for the believers as well. The sūrah therefore keeps two categories of reader in view from beginning to end: those who mock and those who accept. Its narratives are arranged so that the outcome of each community illustrates which of the two responses actually described the world.

**A Lesson Left for People of Understanding**

The Book does not leave the impression that judgment happened in a sealed past. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states that ruins were left behind at the site as a clear lesson for people of understanding (29:35 — *“And We did leave ˹some of˺ its ruins as a clear lesson for people of understanding”*), which places the readers of later centuries inside the intended audience of the event. The line in this verse makes the same move in a single sentence.

## Verse 15:78

> And the residents of the Forest were truly wrongdoers

**Who Lived in the Forest**

The community named here is the one associated in the Book with a wooded territory and with the prophet Shuʿayb. Commentators identify the site with the region of Madyan, the Midianites, a people of northwestern Arabia whose livelihood came from trade, and some exegetes noted that the dwellers of the forest might be a distinct group from the Midianites themselves. The names are preserved in more than one place in the Book: the residents of the forest are listed alongside other destroyed peoples, and Sūrah Ṣād joins them with the people of Lot among the forces that had gathered against the messengers (26:176 — *“The residents of the Forest rejected the messengers”*; 38:13 — *“Thamûd, the people of Lot, and the residents of the Forest. These were ˹all˺ enemy forces”*). What the Book establishes beyond doubt is that they were a real community with a prophet sent to them.

**A Trading People, Examined in Their Ledgers**

Shuʿayb's message to his people is preserved across several sūrahs, and the thread that runs through all of them is commercial honesty. He begins with the worship of God and moves directly to the balances: the command to worship Allah and to avoid giving short measure and weight. The injunction to give full measure, weigh with an even balance, and not to defraud people of their property or spread corruption in the land (7:85 — *“So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order.”*; 11:85 — *“O my people! Give full measure and weigh with justice. Do not defraud people of their property, nor go about spreading corruption in the land”*). What the Book presents is not a list of unrelated offences. A community whose prosperity depended on the roads and the marketplace had a specific temptation available every day, and its prophet named the temptation.

**The Argument Shuʿayb Made for Honesty**

The Book records a remarkable sentence from him. After instructing his people to measure and weigh fairly, he tells them that what remains as lawful gain by God is better for them, and adds that he is not a keeper over them (11:86 — *“What is left ˹as a lawful gain˺ by Allah is far better for you if you are ˹truly˺ believers. And I am not a keeper over you.”*). The reasoning is that a person's true profit is what God leaves with him rather than what he can extract by manipulating a scale. The verse reframes the whole question the merchants were weighing in their minds: not how much can be obtained, but what portion of a livelihood can be trusted. The Book places this economic argument beside the appeal to worship because in its order of things the two belong together.

**Why Fraud Appears Among the Gravest Charges**

The severity with which the Book treats dishonest trade is displayed in the opening of Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn, where a woe is pronounced on those who take full measure when they buy but give less when they measure for others, and who are asked whether they think they will not be resurrected (83:1-4 — *“Those who take full measure ˹when they buy˺ from people”*). The sin named there is precisely the practice for which Shuʿayb's people were condemned. The Book's treatment of it is a warning against the assumption that the offences which harm hundreds of ordinary people quietly are less serious than spectacular crimes. A scale adjusted by a fraction takes something from everyone who comes to the counter, and the Book puts the practice inside the same category as the abuses of the cities of Lot.

**The Meaning of the Word "Wrongdoers"**

The sūrah calls this community unjust, and the term covers both directions of what they did. Injustice is done to the buyers and employees they shortchanged, and it is also done to the messengers whose guidance they refused. The Book uses the same word across its accounts of punished nations: the people of Lot are described as wrongdoers in the verse that follows their ruin, and the warning about the marked stones says that they are not far from the wrongdoers of later generations (11:83 — *“marked by your Lord ˹O Prophet˺. And these stones are not far from the ˹pagan˺ wrongdoers”*).

## Verse 15:79

> so We inflicted punishment upon them. The ruins of both nations still lie on a well-known road

**The Sentence Passed on a Trading People**

The verse states that punishment was carried out, and the Arabic verb carries the sense of a retribution that answers a wrong rather than an irritation that vents itself. In the case of the forest-dwellers, the same sentence is recorded with a detail the sūrah does not repeat here: the blast overtook the wrongdoers after their prophet and those who believed with him had been carried to safety by a mercy from God (11:94 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*).

**Two Peoples on One Road**

The commentators read the reference to "both nations" as the people of Shuʿayb and the people of Lot, joined here partly because they were neighbours in place and partly because their missions belonged to the same stretch of history. The sūrah had named the two communities within a few lines of each other and had already told its readers that the settlements of Lot's people lay along a route people travelled. The closing sentence now gathers the two cases into one statement about geography, which is the sūrah's way of insisting that the warning material was available and continuous rather than scattered and forgotten.

**Ruins Left As a Deliberate Lesson**

The Book is explicit that the survival of such sites is not an oversight. It says of one of them that a clear lesson for people of understanding was left behind, and it says of another that the homes of a destroyed people stand in complete ruin because of their wrongdoing, as a lesson for people of knowledge (29:35 — *“And We did leave ˹some of˺ its ruins as a clear lesson for people of understanding”*; 27:52 — *“So their homes are there, ˹but completely˺ ruined because of their wrongdoing. Surely in this is a lesson for people of knowledge”*). The remains in this verse belong to that same category. A civilisation that had been removed in a morning left behind walls, hollows and tracks, and the Book treats those leftovers as an instruction placed on the ground for whoever came later. Nothing about the arrangement is accidental, including the fact that the road stayed in use.

**The Prophet's Words and the Soil's Testimony**

There is a correspondence in these chapters between what the messengers said and what happened afterward. Shuʿayb told his people that they were in prosperity and that he feared for them the torment of an overwhelming day, and that a swift punishment would overtake those who harmed the sign they had been given (11:84 — *“I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day”*; 11:64 — *“And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s earth and do her no harm, or a swift punishment will overtake you!”*). The Book then records the arrival of exactly what he had described. The line in this verse closes the loop by pointing at the site where the words were spoken and the event occurred, so that the record and the route support each other. The Book's warnings elsewhere rely on the same pairing of statement and trace.

**One Instrument for Two Peoples**

Sūrah Hūd already described the destruction of Shuʿayb's people with the same word this sūrah used for the cities of Lot: the blast overtook the wrongdoers and they fell lifeless in their homes (11:67 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*; 11:94 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*). The vocabulary of judgment in the Book is narrow by design. Whatever the community, whatever its tools and industries, the end recorded for it tends to arrive as a single overwhelming event rather than as a gradual decline. The merchants who manipulated their scales and the men who mobbed a prophet's house received the same sentence from the same quarter, and the sūrah says so in a handful of words before turning to its third and most detailed survivor community.

## Verse 15:80

> Indeed, the residents of the Stone Valley also denied the messengers

**Thamūd and the Prophet Sent to Them**

The people of the stone valley are the tribe of Thamūd, and the prophet sent to them was Ṣāliḥ. The Book introduces him as their brother, a man from among them rather than a stranger, and his opening call is the one the sūrah's readers know from every messenger in the Qur'an: worship God, for you have no god besides Him (7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ.”*; 11:61 — *“You have no god other than Him.”*). What follows in the fuller accounts is a people already settled and prosperous, made successors to a previous nation and established in the land with palaces on its plains and homes carved into its mountains (7:74 — *“Remember when He made you successors after ’Âd and established you in the land—˹and˺ you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains.”*). The sūrah's single reference to their denial assumes all of that background.

**Whose Denial Is Meant**

The commentators discussed the plural in this verse, since Thamūd had one prophet rather than several. One reading takes the reference to be their denial of Ṣāliḥ himself, since rejecting a messenger is rejecting the message he carries and, in the Book's reckoning, the whole series of messengers behind him. The Book uses the same plural when it reports the story in another sūrah, saying that the people of Thamūd rejected the messengers (26:141 — *“The people of Thamûd rejected the messengers”*), which supports the broader reading.

**Denial Recorded as Conduct**

The Book does not describe the tribe's rejection as a difference of opinion. In the fuller accounts, the denial takes the form of an act: they hamstrung the she-camel that had been given to them as a sign, in defiance of their Lord's command, and then challenged their prophet to bring on what he had warned them about (7:77 — *“Then they killed the she-camel—defying their Lord’s command—and challenged ˹Ṣâliḥ˺, “Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are ˹truly˺ one of the messengers.”*; 11:65 — *“But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, “You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes—this is an unfailing promise!”*). The sūrah's line about denying the messengers is therefore a summary of behaviour that everyone in the audience could recall, including the part where the tribe's own leaders planned to destroy Ṣāliḥ and his family by night and cover the deed with an oath (27:48-49 — *“They vowed, “Let us swear by Allah that we will take him and his family down by night.”*). The Book's final word on their planning is that they made a scheme and God made a scheme while they were unaware (27:50 — *“And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware”*).

**The Lands the Meccans Travelled Through**

The territory of Thamūd lay on routes that the Prophet's townspeople used, and the Book's descriptions assume familiarity with the remains. Sūrah al-Fajr names Thamūd as the people who carved rocks in the stone valley (89:9 — *“and Thamûd who carved ˹their homes into˺ the rocks in the ˹Stone˺ Valley”*), and the commentators noted that the Arabs of the Prophet's lifetime knew these mountain dwellings from their journeys. The sūrah's audience therefore stood in the position of a people who had seen the trace and heard the account and could compare the two. The warning in this verse arrives with that geography behind it: the residents of the stone valley are not an anecdote being imported from a distant land but neighbours who had already been dealt with.

## Verse 15:81

> We gave them Our signs, but they turned away from them

**Signs Were Given, Not Merely Available**

The verse says that the signs were handed to the community, which is a stronger statement than saying that evidence existed in their surroundings. The Book's narratives of Thamūd are specific about what they received. They were shown proofs and wonders, and the accounts name a she-camel as the clearest of them: the Book calls it a clear proof from their Lord, and elsewhere it is described as a sign and as a test for the people (7:73 — *“A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you.”*; 11:64 — *“And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s earth and do her no harm, or a swift punishment will overtake you!”*; 54:27 — *“We are sending the she-camel as a test for them. So watch them ˹closely˺, and have patience”*). The tribe's problem was therefore not a shortage of information. What the verse records is an offer made plainly and refused persistently, and the tense of the refusal, which the Book puts in the form of a habit, says that the turning away was repeated rather than momentary.

**The She-Camel as a Sign With Terms Attached**

The sign given to Thamūd was unusual in that it came with a behavioural arrangement attached. Ṣāliḥ instructed his people to leave the animal to graze freely on God's land and to do her no harm, warning them that a swift punishment would overtake them otherwise (11:64 — *“And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s earth and do her no harm, or a swift punishment will overtake you!”*). The Book records a further provision that the water of the town was to be divided between her and them, each taking a turn to drink on an appointed day (26:155 — *“Ṣâliḥ said, “Here is a camel. She will have her turn to drink as you have yours, each on an appointed day”*; 54:28 — *“And tell them that the ˹drinking˺ water must be divided between them ˹and her˺, each taking a turn to drink ˹every other day˺.”*). A sign of that kind cannot be admired from a distance and left alone. It enters the daily economy of a town, requires an adjustment to habit, and puts every person who draws water on notice about what they owe to a creature they did not make.

**A Sign That Demanded Obedience Rather Than Admiration**

The Book's treatment of signs is consistently behavioural. Earlier in this sūrah, the mockers demanded a display from heaven as a condition of belief, and the answer they received was that God does not send signs for entertainment. Thamūd was given one, and the record of what happened shows what such an arrival can produce in a community that has already decided not to change. The Book reports that they hamstrung her and then used the moment to demand the punishment their prophet had warned them about, which is a refusal so complete that the sign itself became the occasion for a challenge (7:77 — *“Then they killed the she-camel—defying their Lord’s command—and challenged ˹Ṣâliḥ˺, “Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are ˹truly˺ one of the messengers.”*). The verse in this sūrah summarises the whole episode in the language of habit.

**The Vocabulary of Turning Away**

The Arabic term used here for turning away describes a deliberate movement of the back rather than a failure to notice, and the Book applies it to the response of whole communities to revelation. In this sūrah it has already described the mockers who asked why the Prophet had not brought angels, and the Book's accounts of previous nations rarely record an argument that was actually answered; the response to the messengers is a shrug, a dismissal, or a demand. Sūrah Fuṣṣilat reports the same tribe in the same terms, saying that they preferred blindness over guidance even after the way had been shown to them (41:17 — *“As for Thamûd, We showed them guidance, but they preferred blindness over guidance. So the blast of a disgracing punishment overtook them for what they used to commit”*), which is the mirror image of the verse before the reader.

**A Sign Set Before a People Who Knew Its Cost**

One detail in the accounts shows how little the tribe's decision depended on ignorance. After they killed the animal, Ṣāliḥ told them they had only three days left to enjoy their homes, and the Book calls that promise unfailing (11:65 — *“But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, “You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes—this is an unfailing promise!”*). The tribe then had a short window in which the consequence of their act was announced to them in advance. Nothing in the account suggests they used the interval to reconsider. The verse's summary of their conduct, that they were in the habit of turning away, covers the last three days of their history as much as the years that preceded them.

## Verse 15:82

> They carved their homes in the mountains, feeling secure

**A People Who Cut Their Dwellings Into Rock**

The verse describes the tribe's building as work performed on the mountains themselves. The Book returns to the image in several places: Ṣāliḥ reminds his people that they established themselves in the land, building palaces on its plains and carving homes into its mountains (7:74 — *“Remember when He made you successors after ’Âd and established you in the land—˹and˺ you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains.”*). Another sūrah has him ask them whether they will keep carving homes out of the rock with great skill (26:149 — *“to carve homes in the mountains with great skill”*); and a third names Thamūd as the people who cut the rocks of the stone valley (89:9 — *“and Thamûd who carved ˹their homes into˺ the rocks in the ˹Stone˺ Valley”*). The repetition is the point. A community's identity in these accounts is bound up with what it could do with stone, and the sūrah names the craft here as the setting for the confidence it is about to undermine.

**What the Chapter Has Said About Security Already**

The theme of misplaced safety runs through the sūrah. The mockers in Makkah asked for angels and were told that the divine messengers do not arrive as a spectacle; they were confident in their mockery while the sūrah told them that every community has a term that cannot be moved. Later in the chapter the Prophet will be told not to look longingly at the comforts given to some of the disbelievers, which is the same subject approached from the other side (15:88 — *“Do not let your eyes crave the ˹fleeting˺ pleasures We have provided for some of the disbelievers, nor grieve for them. And be gracious to the believers”*). The rock-cutters of this verse are the fullest picture in the chapter of what that confidence looks like when a whole society shares it, and the ruin that follows is the chapter's answer to it.

**The Instrument That Reaches Inside Stone**

The Book records the end of the tribe in more than one description. One account says that an overwhelming earthquake struck them and they fell lifeless in their homes (7:78 — *“Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them, and they fell lifeless in their homes”*); another says that a blast overtook them in the morning and that they lay in their dwellings as if they had never lived there (11:67-68 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*). The commentators harmonised the accounts by taking the punishment to have combined a convulsion of the ground with a tremendous sound. Either way, the outcome is the reverse of the tribe's calculation. The threat they fortified against reached them through the ground, through the air, and inside the walls they had cut, and their skill in stone contributed nothing to the day.

**A Craft That Forgot Its Giver**

The reminder attached to the tribe's building in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf ends with an instruction: remember Allah's favours, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land (7:74 — *“So remember Allah’s favours, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”*). That sentence turns the whole subject of architecture into a question about gratitude. The ability to quarry, plan and carve is a gift, and the Book's complaint against the tribe is not that they built well but that the building replaced the acknowledgment of the One who gave them the ground, the strength and the years to do it. The verse in this sūrah leaves the same question standing by saying nothing about their skill and everything about their sense of safety.

## Verse 15:83

> But the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them in the morning

**A Sentence the Chapter Used Before**

The words of this verse closely repeat the line that closed the account of the cities of Lot a few verses earlier, and the repetition is one of the sūrah's most deliberate effects. Two communities, two different histories, two different appearances, and the same sentence recording their end. The Book uses the same word for the destruction of Thamūd in other sūrahs, describing them as a people destroyed by an overwhelming blast (69:5 — *“As for Thamûd, they were destroyed by an overwhelming blast”*), and it warns the Prophet's contemporaries of a blast like the one that befell ʿĀd and Thamūd (41:13 — *“If they turn away, then say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I warn you of a ˹mighty˺ blast, like the one that befell ’Ȃd and Thamûd.”*). The vocabulary of judgment in the Book is narrow on purpose: it teaches its readers to hear one story behind many names.

**The Morning as the Book's Recurring Hour**

For the second time in the chapter, the ruin falls in the morning. The cities of Lot were sentenced to the morning, and their case was completed at sunrise. Thamūd's story records that the tribe was given three days from the killing of the she-camel, and then the Book says that when the morning came they lay lifeless in their dwellings (11:65-67 — *“But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, “You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes—this is an unfailing promise!”*). The hour carries a specific meaning in these narratives: night ends, the day of ordinary work begins, and the community that had gone to bed confident does not get to spend the morning it was counting on.

**The Earthquake and the Cry**

The fuller accounts describe the punishment of Thamūd in two ways that the commentators read as one event. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf says that an overwhelming earthquake struck them and they fell lifeless in their homes (7:78 — *“Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them, and they fell lifeless in their homes”*); Sūrah Hūd and this sūrah say that a blast overtook them (11:67 — *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes”*; 15:83). Taken together, the punishment was a convulsion of the ground accompanied by a tremendous sound, which explains how a people living inside stone was reached. The sūrah's own later verse will state the result in everything they had earned, and the two descriptions match: there was no place in the mountains to stand and nothing they had built that could be held onto.

**The Three Days They Were Given**

One of the details the sūrah assumes its audience knows is the window that preceded the event. After the she-camel was killed, Ṣāliḥ announced that the tribe had three days left to enjoy their homes and described that as an unfailing promise (11:65 — *“But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, “You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes—this is an unfailing promise!”*). A warning with a deadline is a peculiar kind of mercy. It removes any possibility of surprise and leaves the community a period in which the message could still be reconsidered. The Book records nothing about how those three days were spent except the end of them, and the verse in this sūrah covers the last morning in the phrase that had already been used for Sodom.

**The Last Morning of a Nation of Builders**

Almost every account of Thamūd includes something about their buildings, and this verse arrives to close the discrepancy between the architecture and the life it was meant to protect. A people who carved rooms out of mountains, who kept palaces on the plains, and who felt safe in what they had made, met an event that left their homes standing while removing their owners from them. What survived from that morning was the evidence later travellers would see, and the Book says of those who came after them that they pass by the ruins and are asked whether they will not understand (37:137-138 — *“You ˹Meccans˺ certainly pass by their ruins day and night. Will you not then understand”*). The verse is the moment the chapter's third case reaches its verdict, and the next line states what the verdict meant for the tribe's portfolio.

## Verse 15:84

> and all they achieved was of no help to them

**The Final Verdict on an Accumulation**

The verse closes the sūrah's third case by weighing what the tribe had built, earned and secured against the event that came for them, and the verdict is that none of it was any use. The Book's word here for what they achieved belongs to the vocabulary of acquisition, covering what a person gathers through his labour, whether that is property, prestige, or the skills by which he is known.

**The Same Verdict Given Elsewhere**

The Book repeats this judgement about destroyed nations in several places, and the repetitions show that the phrase is a statement of a general law rather than a comment on one tribe. It says that the gains of earlier generations were of no benefit to them, in a passage addressed to those who measure their position by prosperity (39:50 — *“The same had already been said by those ˹destroyed˺ before them, but their ˹worldly˺ gains were of no benefit to them”*). It tells travellers to look at the fate of peoples who were mightier and richer in monuments than those who came after them, and whose worldly earnings likewise helped them not at all (40:82 — *“They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land, but their ˹worldly˺ gains were of no benefit to them”*). The ruin narratives of the sūrahs all end in this same accounting, which is why the verse reads as a conclusion rather than a footnote.

**What Wealth Can and Cannot Do in the Book**

The Qur'an nowhere treats wealth as an evil in itself; it treats it as a trust, a test and a resource for good, and its complaints about the rich concern the use they make of what they have. What the Book denies is the capacity of accumulated goods to settle the questions that matter. It records the case of a people destroyed by a wind that left nothing visible but their ruins (46:25 — *“It destroyed everything by the command of its Lord, leaving nothing visible except their ruins. This is how We reward the wicked people”*), and it warns that no fortune can purchase an exemption from the term appointed for a community. The tribe of Thamūd had the strongest possible defences in the material sense, and the verse records the plain fact that defences are not a category that counted.

**What Would Have Helped**

The sūrah has been quietly supplying the answer in its earlier lines. The people who are described as inheriting gardens and springs, and as coming to God with submissive hearts, are not described by what they owned, and the only household in the city of Lot that survived was the one that had submitted (15:45 — *“Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs”*; 51:36 — *“But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*). The Book's scale of value is set out in a single pair of verses near its close: whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7-8 — *“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it”*). The tribe's problem was not the quantity of its achievements but the fact that none of them was the kind of deed that would answer the moment.

**The Pivot the Next Verse Makes**

What follows this verse in the sūrah is remarkable. The chapter has just concluded three accounts of destruction, and instead of adding a fourth it turns to a statement about the purpose of creation and the certainty of the hour, and then instructs the Prophet to forgive graciously (15:85 — *“We have not created the heavens and the earth and everything in between except for a purpose. And the Hour is certain to come, so forgive graciously”*). The reader who reaches that line carrying the weight of Sodom, the forest and the stone valley is given a different kind of instruction than he might expect. The verdict on what the nations achieved stands as a fact of history, and the response the Book asks of the messenger whose people were mocking him is not a demand for the same end to come upon them. The next verses take up that subject, and the chapter's closing section becomes a different kind of chapter altogether.

## Verse 15:85

> We have not created the heavens and the earth and everything in between except for a purpose. And the Hour is certain to come, so forgive graciously

**Creation Was Not Play**

The verse opens with a declaration that the whole order of things has a purpose, and the Book repeats it often enough for it to function as a foundation stone. Sūrah al-Dukhān states that the heavens and the earth and everything between them were not created for sport and that they were created for a purpose, though most of those addressed did not know it (44:38-39 — *“We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport”*). Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ puts the same sentence in nearly the same words (21:16 — *“We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport”*), and Sūrah al-Aḥqāf joins purpose with an appointed term, adding that the disbelievers were turning away from what they had been warned about (46:3 — *“We only created the heavens and the earth and everything in between for a purpose and an appointed term.”*). The statement arrives here immediately after three narratives of destroyed nations, which is where the argument is strongest: the world that dealt with Sodom and Thamūd so decisively is not a world running on accident.

**What It Means for a Creation to Be in Truth**

The Arabic term rendered as purpose belongs to the vocabulary of truth and reality, and the commentators explained the phrase as meaning that the world has been made real and meaningful rather than empty. Sūrah Āl-ʿImrān places the same thought on the lips of believers who reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth and conclude that none of it was made without purpose (3:191 — *“You have not created ˹all of˺ this without purpose.”*). Elsewhere the Book ties the created order to signs that are available to people of knowledge, saying that the sun and the moon were arranged in precise measures and that none of it was made except for a purpose (10:5 — *“He makes the signs clear for people of knowledge”*). The sūrah's readers are therefore not being offered a vague reassurance. They are being told that the same design which governs the rising of the sun governs the fate of human communities.

**The Hour Is Certain to Come**

The second clause states the certainty of the hour of judgment, and the Book attaches the nearness of that hour to the Prophet's own mission in several places. It tells those who ask about the timing that the knowledge belongs to God alone and that perhaps the hour is nigh (33:63 — *“People ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the Hour. Say, “That knowledge is only with Allah. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near.”*). It says that the Book was revealed with truth and the balance and that the hour may be near (42:17 — *“It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near”*); and it announces that the approaching hour has drawn near (53:57 — *“The approaching ˹Hour˺ has drawn near”*). The sūrah's readers who had just passed through accounts of communities destroyed in a morning have the point showed in front of them. A judgment that came to towns in the past is evidence of a judgment that is coming for the world.

**"So Forgive Graciously"**

The instruction that closes the verse is startling in its position. Having described the ruin of three societies and then declared the purpose of creation and the certainty of the reckoning, the text turns to the Prophet and tells him to forgive with beautiful forgiveness. This is not a contradiction of the judgments described in the chapter; it is a directive about the messenger's conduct in the period before the sentences fall. The Book gives him the same counsel elsewhere, telling him to pardon and bear with a people from whom he would always find deceit (5:13 — *“But pardon them and bear with them.”*) and to bear with his opponents and respond with peace (43:89 — *“So bear with them and respond with peace. They will soon come to know”*). The anger that the messenger felt at mockery is nowhere forbidden; the retaliation is.

**Forbearance as an Assignment Rather Than a Temperament**

Sūrah al-Maʿārij repeats the instruction almost verbatim when it tells the Prophet to endure the denial of his people with beautiful patience (70:5 — *“So endure ˹this denial, O Prophet,˺ with beautiful patience”*). The wording places forbearance among the duties of his commission rather than among the traits of his character, which matters for how the chapter is read. What the sūrah gives instead is an instruction to the Prophet to hold still, and the rest of the chapter shows what that stillness is made of.

Sūrah al-Ṣād reports the same statement with the opposite conclusion attached, saying that the heavens, the earth and everything between them were not created without purpose, as the disbelievers think, and adding a woe upon them because of the fire (38:27 — *“We have not created the heavens and earth and everything in between without purpose—as the disbelievers think. So woe to the disbelievers because of the Fire”*).

## Verse 15:86

> Surely your Lord is the Master Creator, All-Knowing

**Two Names That Ground the Command to Forgive**

The verse answers the instruction that precedes it. A messenger told to forbear graciously with a people who mock him is being asked to trust that the matter is in hands competent to settle it, and the line names the hands. The Creator made everything that exists, and the All-Knowing knows precisely what each person does and why. Between them, the two names close off the two anxieties that would otherwise make forbearance impossible for a person with a cause: the fear that no one will ever settle the score, and the suspicion that the offence has been misunderstood.

**Knowledge That Misses Nothing**

The second name declares that God's knowledge is complete, and the Book attaches it to the specific matters that grief and anger distort: what enemies say behind a person's back, what the disbelievers conceal, and what the hypocrites keep hidden. Sūrah Yā-Sīn tells the Prophet not to let the words of his opponents grieve him, on the ground that God fully knows what they conceal and reveal (36:76 — *“So do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Indeed, We ˹fully˺ know what they conceal and what they reveal”*). Another verse reassures him that the very complaints he hears are already known to God and that it is not his honesty that is being questioned but the signs of God being denied (6:33 — *“We certainly know that what they say grieves you ˹O Prophet˺. It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny”*). A messenger who knows that his Lord already has the full account is relieved of the need to defend himself.

**Comfort for a Messenger Who Carried Too Much**

The sūrah's earlier verses addressed the Prophet's distress without naming it, and this line names the remedy. Sūrah al-Kahf records the Book's concern that he might destroy himself with grief over a people who would not believe in the message (18:6 — *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message”*), and Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ opens with the same image of a prophet tormenting himself over their disbelief (26:3 — *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief”*). The Book's answer in each case is not that the grief is unreasonable but that the messenger is not the one who determines the outcome. The declaration that God knows everything is set beside the instruction to forgive for exactly this reason: the person who is certain that nothing is unrecorded can leave the account with the One keeping it.

---

## Verse 15:87

> We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran

**"And the Great Quran"**

The second item in the gift is the recitation as a whole, and the Book draws a distinction between the revelation given to Muḥammad and what had been provided to the communities narrated in the previous verses. Those peoples had received signs in the form of an animal in a pasture or an escape from a burning city; the Prophet's sign is a text that continues to be recited, memorised and carried across the world. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt makes the comparison explicitly, asking whether it is not enough for them that the Book has been sent down and is recited to them, and describing it as a mercy and a reminder for people who believe (29:51 — *“Is it not enough for them that We have sent down to you the Book, ˹which is˺ recited to them. Surely in this ˹Quran˺ is a mercy and reminder for people who believe”*). The sūrah's line about the great Quran is the same argument in compressed form.

**A Gift Named in the Middle of the Chapter**

The verse speaks about what the Prophet has been given rather than about what has been taken from him, and its position after a series of destruction narratives gives it the force of a contrast. The communities that surrounded their prophets with mockery and demands were destroyed; the Prophet of this sūrah is told that he has already received the thing that outweighs everything they asked him to produce. The verb used is a word of bestowal rather than of wages, which places revelation in the category of grace from the beginning. Nothing in the sentence suggests the gift was earned by the man who received it.

**What the Seven Often-Repeated Are**

The commentators gave two principal explanations of the phrase. One report takes it to mean the seven long sūrahs at the beginning of the Book, and another, followed by many of the commentators, takes it to mean the opening sūrah of the Qur'an, the Fātiḥah. The second reading was supported by the fact that the Fātiḥah consists of seven verses when the opening formula is counted, and by its use in each unit of the daily prayer, which is why it is called oft-repeated. Sūrah al-Zukhruf provides the other name the tradition applied to it, describing the Book as the mother of the Book in God's keeping, highly esteemed and rich in wisdom (43:4 — *“And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom”*).

**The Opening Sūrah as the Essence of the Recitation**

If the reference is to the Fātiḥah, then the gift named here is a passage of seven lines that contains the whole relationship between the servant and his Lord: praise, worship, petition. The request to be guided along the straight path, with the description of those who received guidance and those who did not (1:1-7 — *“Guide us along the Straight Path”*). The tradition's appreciation of the sūrah matches its content. It is a summary of the message in the form of a prayer, and the Prophet is reported to have spoken of it as a treasure no other scripture had contained. The sūrah's audience, hearing this verse recited, would have recognised the text in question immediately, since they heard it recited in prayer day and night.

## Verse 15:88

> Do not let your eyes crave the ˹fleeting˺ pleasures We have provided for some of the disbelievers, nor grieve for them. And be gracious to the believers

**A Command Addressed to the Eyes**

The verse begins with the gaze, and the choice of organ is deliberate. Comparison between one person's lot and another's happens first in the eye, before it becomes a settled dissatisfaction in the heart. The Book asks the Prophet not to let that moment complete itself: he is not forbidden to see the wealth of the Makkan leaders, since he lived among them, but he is forbidden to let his eyes stretch toward it.

**The Grief That Is Also Named**

The second prohibition concerns grief on behalf of the disbelievers, which the Book returns to in several sūrahs because it was a real feature of the Prophet's life. Sūrah al-Kahf asks whether he would destroy himself with sorrow over a people who refused to believe (18:6 — *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message”*), Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ opens with the same image (26:3 — *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief”*), and Sūrah Fāṭir tells him not to let his life be consumed by grief over those who would not be guided (35:8 — *“Are those whose evil-doing is made so appealing to them that they deem it good ˹like those who are rightly guided˺? ˹It is˺ certainly Allah ˹Who˺ leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides whoever He wills. So do not grieve yourself to death over them ˹O Prophet˺. Surely Allah is All-Knowing of what they do”*). The Book does not treat this distress as a fault in the messenger. It treats it as a weight he was not meant to carry, and the instruction to set it down follows the reminder that God knows everything they do.

**"Lower Your Wing to the Believers"**

The Arabic phrase translated as being gracious to the believers invokes the image of a bird sheltering its young under a lowered wing, and the Book uses it for tenderness between people. It applies the same language to the treatment of parents, where a person is told to be humble with them out of mercy (17:24 — *“And be humble with them out of mercy, and pray, “My Lord! Be merciful to them as they raised me when I was young.”*), and it repeats the phrase about the Prophet's community in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ, where he is told to be gracious to the believers who follow him (26:215 — *“and be gracious to the believers who follow you”*). The expression describes protection and warmth rather than mere politeness, and it identifies the people who are entitled to receive it.

**A Saying Attached to the Verse**

The commentaries on this passage preserved a report of the first caliph, Abū Bakr, who is said to have remarked that whoever has been given the Qur'an and then considers anything in this world better than what he has received has made a great thing small and a small thing great. The saying captures the logic of the verse exactly, since the Prophet is being told not to look at what others hold precisely because he has been entrusted with what is greater. The remainder of the chapter turns to the mockers who held that wealth and to the recitation that the Prophet was to keep offering until the end of his life.

## Verse 15:89
> And say, “I am truly sent with a clear warning”—

**A Commission in Three Words**

The line gives the Prophet the words with which to describe himself, and the description contains nothing that a ruler would put on a monument. He is a warner, and the warning he carries is described as clear, which means it is stated openly and can be understood by anyone who hears it. The Book's messengers are repeatedly given this same brief wherever they are presented, and the uniformity of the description is part of the message: no messenger in the Qur'an arrives to found a dynasty, collect tribute, or display a spectacle.

**The Same Sentence Across the Book**

The formula recurs in sūrah after sūrah, and readers who follow it discover a consistent line running through the Prophet's self-description. He is told to announce to humanity that he has been sent with a clear warning (22:49 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning”*). To tell those who demand signs that signs belong to God while his own commission is to warn plainly (29:50 — *“They say, “If only ˹some˺ signs had been sent down to him from his Lord!” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Signs are only with Allah. And I am only sent with a clear warning.”*); to say that what is revealed to him is that he is a warner (38:70 — *“What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.”*); to say that he is not the first messenger and does not know what will happen.

That he is only sent with a clear warning (46:9 — *“Say, “I am not the first messenger ever sent, nor do I know what will happen to me or you. I only follow what is revealed to me. And I am only sent with a clear warning.”*); to call people to flee to God with a plain warning (51:50 — *“So ˹proclaim, O Prophet˺: “Flee to Allah! I am truly sent by Him with a clear warning to you”*); and to tell those who ask about the hour that knowledge belongs to God alone while his own task is the warning (67:26 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “That knowledge is with Allah alone, and I am only sent with a clear warning.”*). The sameness is the point.

**A Warning Instead of a Retort**

The command to say these words follows a series of instructions about conduct: forgive graciously, do not envy, be gentle with the believers. This verse continues that sequence by giving the Prophet a sentence to speak instead of the angry reply he might have made. In Sūrah al-Zukhruf he is told to bear with his opponents and respond with peace (43:89 — *“So bear with them and respond with peace. They will soon come to know”*), and the warning announced here functions the same way. The messenger's answer to mockery is not a curse or a threat of his own making; it is a statement about the job he has been given.

**The Line Between Warning and Threatening**

The distinction is worth holding clearly, because the sūrah is full of destruction. A warning tells the hearer about a consequence that will follow from his present course, in time for him to change it. A threat is an announcement of what the speaker intends to do. The Prophet is given the first of these and never the second, and the Book's narratives of punishment distinguish the two stages: the messenger warns, the community refuses, and the consequence arrives from God rather than from the messenger's hand. The verse in this line places the Prophet firmly on the side of the warning, and the communities described earlier in the chapter illustrate what was waiting when the warning was disregarded.

## Verse 15:90

> ˹a warning˺ similar to what We sent to those who divided ˹the Scriptures˺

**Who Divided the Scriptures**

The commentators identified the people in question as the recipients of earlier revelations who broke up what they had been given, accepting the portions that suited them and setting aside the rest. The Book describes the practice repeatedly in its dealings with the People of the Book. The verse's reference to dividing the Scriptures gathers all of that into a phrase.

**Division as a Way of Handling a Book**

What the phrase describes is not disbelief in everything the revelation contains. It is the process by which a reader keeps the material he finds agreeable and discards or reinterprets the material that demands something of him. The Book's exposure of the habit is blunt: it asks the people of the earlier scripture whether they believe in part of the Book and reject the rest, and then demands an accounting from those who do (2:85 — *“Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?”*). The practice is presented as a falsification of the text by way of selection, since a revelation cannot be taken as guidance if its hearers are permitted to decide which of its claims belong to God.

**The Book's Claim to Be a Whole**

The Qur'an's warnings on this subject reflect its own insistence that revelation functions as a complete address. It asks whether its readers will not consider it carefully and states that if it had been from anyone other than God they would have found much contradiction in it (4:82 — *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies”*). Its internal consistency is presented not only as evidence of origin but as a reason the text cannot be sorted into accepted and rejected parts.

**A Community That Split Its Religion**

The Book applies the vocabulary of division to communities as well as to texts. It speaks of those who divided their faith and became sects, of a people who split their religion into fragments with each group rejoicing in what it had kept, and it tells the Prophet that he has no responsibility for such people and that their case rests with God (6:159 — *“Indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not responsible whatsoever for those who have divided their faith and split into sects.”*; 30:32 — *“˹like˺ those who have divided their faith and split into sects, each rejoicing in what they have”*). The association between dividing a scripture and dividing a community is not accidental in the Book's usage. A text taken apart cannot hold a people together, and a people who have agreed among themselves to ignore parts of the revelation will find reasons to part company over the rest.

**The Audience This Verse Was Aimed At**

The Makkans had a version of the same habit. They preserved the memory of Abraham and the sanctity of the sanctuary and used both as titles to distinction while refusing the message that claimed to restore the religion of Abraham; they found the accounts of vanished nations acceptable as entertainment and rejected the resurrection those accounts were meant to establish. The Book records their habit of describing portions of the revelation as the tales of the ancients and dismissing others as poetry or sorcery, which is a division of the recitation into categories of their own devising (16:24 — *“And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!”*; 21:5 — *“Yet they say, “This ˹Quran˺ is a set of confused dreams! No, he has fabricated it! No, he must be a poet! So let him bring us a ˹tangible˺ sign like those ˹prophets˺ sent before.”*). The verse names a practice the sūrah's audience would have recognised in their own behaviour.

It tells them that they distort words from their contexts and neglect a portion of what they were commanded to uphold (5:13 — *“They distorted the words of the Scripture and neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold.”*), it accuses them of rejecting some messengers and killing others (2:87 — *“Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others”*). It states plainly that those who accept some messengers and reject others have taken a position of their own invention rather than faith (4:150-151 — *“Surely those who deny Allah and His messengers and wish to make a distinction between Allah and His messengers, saying, “We believe in some and disbelieve in others,” desiring to forge a compromise they are indeed the true disbelievers. And We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment”*).

## Verse 15:91

> who ˹now˺ accept parts of the Quran, rejecting others

**Why the Book Insists on Being Read Whole**

The Qur'an's claim to internal consistency is the basis of its insistence that it be received as an unit. It asks its audience whether they will not reflect on it and states that if it had come from anyone other than God they would have found in it much inconsistency (4:82 — *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies”*). The argument runs from the whole to the parts: it is the absence of contradiction across the entire text that marks it as revelation, which means a reader has no basis for discarding sections while citing the book's authority for the sections he keeps. The verse's phrase about accepting parts and rejecting others is the practical denial of that claim.

The verse supplies the content of the previous line by describing what the dividers actually did: they took some of the revelation and turned away from the rest. The Book does not accuse them of denying that the recitation had come from God; it accuses them of treating it as a collection from which a person may select.

**The Accusations the Makkans Made**

The Book preserves the specific charges the sūrah's first audience brought against the text, and they were exactly the sort described in this verse. They called it a set of confused dreams, then said the Prophet had fabricated it, then said he was a poet, and then demanded a tangible sign like the ones brought by earlier prophets (21:5 — *“No, he has fabricated it! No, he must be a poet!”*). Elsewhere they described the recitation as legends of the ancients, and the Book records their advice to one another not to listen to it but to drown it out so that they might prevail (16:24 — *“And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!”*; 41:26 — *“The disbelievers advised ˹one another˺, “Do not listen to this Quran but drown it out so that you may prevail.”*). A community that produces four or five different explanations of the same text in the course of an afternoon is not examining it; it is looking for a way to file it away.

**Selection as a Verdict on the Text**

Any reader who accepts one portion of a book and rejects another is making a judgement about the author. The passages of the Qur'an that discuss the earlier scriptures make this implication explicit, asking the people of the Book whether they believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest. Warning them that those who behave this way will receive disgrace in this world and a severe punishment in the next (2:85 — *“Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?”*). The same logic applies to the recitation delivered to the Prophet. To decide that one section is divine guidance and another is not is to take a position on who is speaking, and the sūrah's readers are shown where that position leads.

**The Community That Divides Itself**

The Book links the mishandling of scripture to the fragmentation of communities, and it does so with the same vocabulary. It speaks of those who divided their religion and became sects, and it tells the Prophet that he is not accountable for them and that their affair rests with God, who will inform them of what they used to do (6:159 — *“And He will inform them of what they used to do”*). Sūrah al-Rūm gives the portrait in more detail, describing those who split their religion and became groups, each faction rejoicing in what it had retained (30:32 — *“˹like˺ those who have divided their faith and split into sects, each rejoicing in what they have”*). A text that has been divided among parties becomes a set of flags for those parties, and the rejoicing over inherited fragments is the last stage of the process the sūrah describes.

## Verse 15:92

> So by your Lord! We will certainly question them all

**An Oath Sworn by the Lord of the Prophet**

The Book swears by many things, and in this verse it swears by the Prophet's own Lord, which is the form used when a matter needs the strongest possible confirmation. The oath is directed at readers who were treating the reckoning as a distant possibility or a rhetorical threat, and the sentence that follows it is not a prediction offered for their consideration but a declaration of what will certainly take place. The sūrah's readers had already heard the Book swear by the sun, the moon, the pen and the dawn. Here the oath is narrowed to the relationship between the messenger and the One who owns him, which places the matter of the dividers in the hands of the only authority that concerns them.

**Everyone, Without Exception**

The word the verse uses for all of them is unrestricted, and the Book uses the same scope when it describes the judgment in other places. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states that God will question those who received messengers and will question the messengers themselves (7:6 — *“We will surely question those who received messengers and We will question the messengers ˹themselves˺”*), which removes any suggestion that rank or proximity offers an exemption. Sūrah al-Naḥl tells humanity that they will certainly be questioned about what they used to do (16:93 — *“And you will certainly be questioned about what you used to do”*), and Sūrah al-Takāthur says that on that day people will definitely be questioned about the pleasures they enjoyed (102:8 — *“Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about ˹your worldly˺ pleasures”*). The sūrah's audience, which included both the mockers and the believers, heard the same word applied to each of them.

**The Day on Which No Question Is Needed**

One verse in the Book states that on that day no human or jinn will need to be asked about their sins (55:39 — *“On that Day there will be no need for any human or jinn to be asked about their sins”*), and readers have noted the apparent tension with verses like this one. The resolution that the commentators offered is that the two statements describe different aspects of the reckoning. The questioning in this verse is not an inquiry conducted to get information, since nothing is hidden from the One asking. It is part of the process by which a person is made to confront what he did, which is why the Book can say in one place that the guilty will be questioned and in another that the asking is unnecessary. The account is complete either way; what the questioning adds is the confrontation.

**The Record That Leaves Nothing Out**

The Book's descriptions of the judgment include the production of a written account, and those descriptions explain how complete the questioning will be. Sūrah al-Kahf tells of the record being laid open, of the wicked in fear of what is written in it. Of their cry that it leaves out neither a small sin nor a great one, adding that they will find before them everything they did and that God wrongs no one (18:49 — *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it.”*). A person whose conduct has been recorded in that way is not going to be asked about matters that were uncertain. What the question will establish is the answer, not the facts, and every reader of the sūrah is included in the sentence.

## Verse 15:93

> about what they used to do

**The Subject of the Questioning Is Conduct**

The line completes the sentence begun in the previous verse and states what the questioning will be about. The Book does not say that people will be asked what they believed as an abstract proposition, or which school they followed, or what their community's customs were. They will be asked about what they did, and the phrasing places the entire matter on the ground of action. The sūrah has spent its narratives describing communities in what they were doing: crowding a street, measuring short, killing an animal, carving homes in mountains and feeling safe in them. The verse says that this is the material from which the reckoning is assembled.

**Deeds as the Book's Unit of Account**

The Book maintains this standard consistently. It says that each person will have only what he endeavoured toward (53:39 — *“and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards”*), and it states that whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7-8 — *“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it”*). The two verses together rule out both the notion that good intentions count without performance and the notion that a person's standing in a community determines his standing before God. The sūrah's narratives illustrate the same principle in history, where cities with impressive buildings and profitable trades were judged by their practices and not by their prosperity.

## Verse 15:94

> So proclaim what you have been commanded, and turn away from the polytheists

**The Command to Make the Message Public**

The first half of the verse directs the Prophet to declare what he has been commanded, and the verb carries the sense of open, unhesitating speech. A message that was to be delivered privately to individuals could have been given a gentler instruction; this line asks for a public declaration. The sūrah has been addressing a man whose opponents were ridiculing him in the streets and demanding that he produce a different kind of sign. The answer to those demands is an instruction to keep saying plainly what he had been told to say, which places the content and the manner of delivery outside his discretion.

**The Content Is Fixed by the Command**

The phrase describing the message as what the Prophet has been commanded places the whole of it under obedience. The Book states the principle generally when it says that it is not for a believing man or woman to have any choice in a matter that God and His Messenger have decided, and adds that whoever disobeys has gone far astray (33:36 — *“It is not for a believing man or woman—when Allah and His Messenger decree a matter—to have any other choice in that matter.”*). The Prophet's task in this verse is therefore not to edit or soften the material he carries. A messenger told to proclaim what he has been commanded is being asked to trust the content he was given rather than to improve on it, and the sūrah's narratives have illustrated what happens to communities whose warners were ignored.

**Turning Away From the Polytheists**

The second instruction is not a licence to abandon the message. The Book's commands to the Prophet about dealing with the idolaters have to be read together, and their common feature is that he is told to disengage from the contest rather than from the duty. He is told to bear with them and respond with peace (43:89 — *“So bear with them and respond with peace. They will soon come to know”*), and the Book describes the servants of the Most Compassionate as people who, when the ignorant address them improperly, answer with the word of peace (25:63 — *“The ˹true˺ servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk on the earth humbly, and when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace”*). The turning away in this verse is the abandonment of an argument that was never going to be settled by argument, and it comes in the same sentence as the command to keep proclaiming.

**Public Speech and Its Cost**

A public declaration in Makkah was not a neutral act. The men who mocked the Prophet held the city's standing, its trade and its sanctuary, and the message he was told to proclaim denied the basis of their authority. The Book records the reaction in the sūrah itself, where the mockers asked why he had not been given a treasure or accompanied by angels and addressed him as one had (15:6-7 — *“They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?”*). The command to keep proclaiming therefore required the Prophet to accept the reputation he was given, and the verse is one of the places where the Book shows its messenger being told to proceed without waiting for the situation to improve.

## Verse 15:95

> Surely We will be sufficient for you against the mockers

**A Promise of Sufficiency**

The verse answers the situation created by the instructions in the line before it. A man told to proclaim his message openly and to stop trading argument with his opponents would be in a difficult position without an assurance of protection, and the sentence supplies it in the form of a declaration that God will suffice for him against the people who mock. The Book uses the same language in a passage that places the promise beside a description of the threat: it asks whether God is not enough for His servant, notes that the opposition threatens the Prophet with other powerless gods. States that whoever God leaves to stray will have no guide (39:36 — *“Is Allah not sufficient for His servant? Yet they threaten you with other ˹powerless˺ gods besides Him! Whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide”*). The sufficiency promised in that verse and in this one is not a plan of escape; it is a guarantee about the outcome.

**The Enemy Named Precisely**

The opponents are described here as mockers, which is a more specific charge than general disbelief. The Book uses the word for the treatment of every messenger it reports, stating that no messenger ever came to a people without being mocked (15:11 — *“but no messenger ever came to them without being mocked”*). The sūrah's opening verses already described the mockers asking why the reminder had been sent to a man from their own town and calling him insane, and asking why angels had not been sent down with him (15:6-7 — *“They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane”*). The promise in this verse is therefore aimed at a particular kind of hostility: the ridicule that is meant to remove a person's standing rather than to answer his argument.

**How the Sufficiency Was Delivered**

The Book does not describe in this sūrah how the mockers were dealt with, and its restraint is characteristic. Other passages state that the disbelievers were awaiting the outcome and that the Prophet was told to wait as well, since they too were waiting (44:59 — *“Wait then! They too are certainly waiting”*), and the command to endure his Lord's decree is accompanied by the assurance that he is under watchful eyes (52:48 — *“So be patient with your Lord’s decree, for you are truly under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes. And glorify the praises of your Lord when you rise”*). What the reader knows from the record is that the men who had organised the ridicule did not succeed in silencing the message. The Book leaves the mechanism unspecified in this verse and lets the reader supply the history that followed.

**The Reassurances in the Book's Difficult Passages**

The Qur'an's consolations to the Prophet recur throughout the Makkan sūrahs, and they are usually attached to a specific distress. He is told not to let the words of his opponents grieve him (10:65 — *“Do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah. He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing”*), not to let his life be destroyed by sorrow over those who refuse to believe (35:8 — *“So do not grieve yourself to death over them ˹O Prophet˺.”*), and to remain patient with what they say while glorifying his Lord (50:39 — *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset”*). The line in this verse belongs to that family of statements, and it names the hostile activity it addresses. A reader who follows the sequence can see the sūrah moving from history to instruction and then to comfort, and the comfort is specific enough to be useful to anyone facing the same kind of opposition.

**Who the Mockers Were**

Ibn Kathir names them. Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq said the leaders of the mockers were five men, all elders and nobles among their people: al-Aswad ibn al-Muṭṭalib Abū Zamʿah, from Banū Asad ibn ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā; al-Aswad ibn ʿAbd Yaghūth, from Banū Zuhrah; al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, from Banū Makhzūm; al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, from Banū Sahm; and al-Ḥārith ibn aṭ-Ṭalātilah, from Khuzāʿah. When their harm and their mockery of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ went beyond bearing, this passage was revealed.

Ibn Isḥāq adds, on the authority of Yazīd ibn Rūmān from ʿUrwah ibn az-Zubayr or another scholar, that Jibrīl came to the Prophet ﷺ while he was making ṭawāf around the Kaʿbah and stood beside him; as each of these men passed, something was done to him. And Ibn Kathir records that the Prophet ﷺ had supplicated against al-Aswad ibn al-Muṭṭalib because of the pain and mockery he had caused — *"O Allah, make him blind and take the life of his son."*

Three things in that account are worth holding. The verse's promise is not addressed to an abstraction; it answers five identifiable men whose names the tradition preserved, which is why the comfort is not general reassurance but a statement about a known situation. The men were not street hecklers but the senior figures of their clans, so the opposition being answered here is the respectable kind — the mockery that carries social weight. And the supplication recorded against one of them is severe enough that it has to be read honestly: the Prophet ﷺ was not indifferent to being mocked, and the verse does not ask him to pretend otherwise. What it gives him instead is the assurance that he will be sufficed against them, which is a different thing from being untouched by them.

## Verse 15:96

> who set up ˹other˺ gods with Allah. They will soon come to know

**"They Will Soon Come to Know"**

The closing clause is one of the Book's most frequently used warnings, and its brevity is part of its effect. The verse does not say what they will come to know, because the answer is contained in everything the sūrah has said. Elsewhere the Book tells the disbelievers that they have denied the truth and will soon face the consequences of their ridicule (26:6 — *“They have certainly denied ˹the truth˺, so they will soon face the consequences of their ridicule”*), and it hands the timing of the reckoning to God alone while stating that its arrival is certain. The word rendered as soon belongs to a timescale that cannot be judged by human expectation; the sūrah has already used the same phrase when it told the mockers to eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by false hopes, warning them that they would soon know (15:3 — *“˹So˺ let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by ˹false˺ hope, for they will soon know”*).

**The Charge Behind the Laughter**

The verse identifies the religion of the men who mocked. They were not skeptics; they were worshippers who had added other authorities alongside God, and the Book calls that arrangement by the name it uses throughout: setting up partners. The Makkan pantheon consisted of deities whose standing was bound up with the town's status, its pilgrimage traffic and the prestige of its leading families, and the Prophet's message removed them.

**What "Setting Up" a God Involves**

The Arabic expression describes a person constructing an object of worship by his own act rather than receiving it from the One who created him. The Book puts the same phrase to work in its argument with the Makkans elsewhere, where it asks whether the idolaters have taken their own desires as the god they obey (25:43 — *“Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ the one who has taken their own desires as their god? Will you then be a keeper over them”*) and describes the man who takes his desires as his deity and whom God has left astray in knowledge (45:23 — *“Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god?”*), a passage that also warns those who follow their own desires to consider where their path is going. The point of the phrase is that worship of this kind begins in human preference, and the resulting object is a creature of the worshipper's convenience.

**The Difference Between Knowing Now and Knowing Then**

The men addressed in this verse had been given the opportunity to understand the message while there was time to change their conduct. The knowledge announced for them arrives at a point where it cannot be put to use, and the Book's judgment scenes consistently present the guilty as understanding their situation only when the account has been closed. The sūrah's earlier verses state that the disbelievers will wish they had been Muslims when they see the reckoning (15:2 — *“˹The day will come when˺ the disbelievers will certainly wish they had submitted ˹to Allah˺”*), a line that describes the same delayed recognition. The difference between the two kinds of knowledge is the whole difference between the two outcomes the Book sets before its readers.

## Verse 15:97

> We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say

**What They Were Saying**

The sūrah has preserved a sample of the speech in question. Its opening verses describe people who will wish they had submitted, and it goes on to record the mockers asking why the reminder had been sent down to a man from among them and calling him had (15:6 — *“They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane”*). The Book records their further demands in other sūrahs: that he produce a tangible sign, that a treasure be sent down to him, that angels accompany him, and their various labels for the recitation, from poetry to sorcery to ancient fables (21:5 — *“So let him bring us a ˹tangible˺ sign like those ˹prophets˺ sent before.”*; 25:5 — *“And they say, “˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening.”*). The verse's reference to what they say covers an ongoing campaign of public mockery that was aimed at the Prophet's standing in his own community.

**A Verse That Names the Prophet's Grief**

The Book here states that God knows the distress caused to the Prophet by the speech of his opponents, and the wording places the knowledge in the emphatic form used for matters that need no proof. The verse does not reproach him for the feeling and does not treat it as a weakness. It records that the grief exists, that it has a specific cause in what was being said, and that it is known to the One who is addressing him. The sūrah's consolation works by naming the wound rather than by arguing that it should not hurt, which is a pattern that runs through the Book's reassurances to its messenger.

**Grief Without Blame**

The Book treats the Prophet's sorrow as a natural response that has to be managed rather than a fault to be corrected. Sūrah al-Kahf asks whether he will destroy himself with grief over a people who refuse to believe the message (18:6 — *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message”*), and Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ opens with the same image of a messenger tormenting himself over their refusal (26:3 — *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief”*). The record of his prayer is preserved in another sūrah: "O my Lord! Indeed, these are a people who persist in disbelief" (43:88). What the Book never does in these passages is tell the Prophet that the people's conduct does not matter or that he is wrong to care about it.

**The Comfort of Being Fully Known**

The reassurance in this line lies in the knowledge it attributes to God. A person bearing a private burden often finds that the hardest part is being read wrongly by everyone around him, and the Book tells the Prophet that nothing he feels has escaped notice. Elsewhere he is told not to let the words of his opponents grieve him, on the ground that all honour and power belong to God and that He hears and knows everything (10:65 — *“Do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah. He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing”*), and another passage states that God is fully aware of what his opponents conceal and what they reveal (36:76 — *“So do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Indeed, We ˹fully˺ know what they conceal and what they reveal”*). The consolation is not that the situation is painless but that it is completely understood.

## Verse 15:98

> So glorify the praises of your Lord and be one of those who ˹always˺ pray

**The Remedy Prescribed**

The verse gives the Prophet an activity in place of the distress described in the line before it. The Book's counsel to a messenger under attack is to turn his attention upward in glorification and to place himself among those who pray, and the instruction is repeated in several Makkan sūrahs in nearly the same terms. Sūrah Qāf tells the Prophet to be patient with what his opponents say and to glorify the praises of his Lord before sunrise and before sunset (50:39 — *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset”*). Sūrah Ṭā-Hā expands the same command to include the hours of the night and the two ends of the day, so that the day itself is organised around remembrance (20:130 — *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say.”*). What the Book offers its messenger is a schedule.

**"Be One of Those Who Pray"**

The second clause is worded in a way that deserves attention. The Prophet is not told simply to pray; he is told to be among those who pray, which places him inside a company rather than in a private relationship with the instruction. The community that surrounded him in Makkah was small, poor and constantly under pressure, and the verse gives its leader the task of standing with it in prostration. The Book's picture of the prophetic life in these years is full of such company: a household, a few believers, and a practice of worship that continued in a city where the worship was not welcome.

The mockers aimed to make the Prophet look small, and the Book's remedy is an action in which he is placed on the floor in front of his Lord. Judged by the standards of Makkan society, prostration is the posture of a defeated man; in the scales of the revelation, it is the position of the one who is in the right place.

**The Shape of a Day Given to Worship**

The details supplied by other passages show that the practice commanded here was not confined to one time. The Book mentions glorification before sunrise and before sunset, the dawn prayer that is witnessed by the angels (17:78 — *“Observe the prayer from the decline of the sun until the darkness of the night and the dawn prayer, for certainly the dawn prayer is witnessed ˹by angels˺”*), devotion during parts of the night (76:26 — *“and prostrate before Him during part of the night, and glorify Him long at night”*), and the command to devote oneself to God wholeheartedly (73:8 — *“˹Always˺ remember the Name of your Lord, and devote yourself to Him wholeheartedly”*). Read together, these instructions describe a life that has been divided into portions of remembrance, and the verse in this sūrah is the general command from which those details follow. The Prophet's answer to the campaign against him was, among other things, a daily routine that his opponents could not interrupt.

## Verse 15:99

> and worship your Lord until the inevitable comes your way

**Why Death Is Called Certainty**

The Arabic word used in this verse names the reality that cannot be doubted, and the Book applies it to the arrival of what every person is promised. Sūrah al-Muddaththir puts the same phrase on the lips of the guilty when they describe what came to them at the end, saying that the certainty came to us (74:47 — *“until the inevitable came to us.”*). The Book's warnings about the afterlife are expressed in the language of knowledge that grows certain: those who were heedless will see the fire and then see it with the eye of certainty (102:5-7 — *“Again, you will surely see it with the eye of certainty”*). Calling death the certainty is a statement about the one event in a person's future that is not open to dispute, and the sūrah's instruction is anchored to it.

**The Horizon That Frames the Daily Practice**

Without this line, the practice described in the previous verse could be read as a technique for managing stress. With it, the practice is given an orientation toward the end of a life and the account that follows. The Book elsewhere states the purpose of human existence in a single sentence, declaring that jinn and humans were not created except to worship God (51:56 — *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me”*), and this sūrah's closing instruction is that declaration put into the form of a personal command to one man. The verse's brevity is what gives it reach: there is nothing in it that applies only to the Prophet's circumstances.
