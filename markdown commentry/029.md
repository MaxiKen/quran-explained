# Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt (Chapter 29) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Web of Delusion and the Crucible of Tribulation**

Al-ʿAnkabūt, "The Spider," is the twenty-ninth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 69 verses. While primarily a late Makkan revelation, classical authorities (including al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿAbbās) record that its opening eleven verses were revealed in connection with early events in Madinah or during the painful departures of the *Hijrah*, when converts were subjected to brutal familial and physical trials. It takes its memorable name from the striking ecological and theological parable in verse 41: *“The example of those who take allies other than Allah is like that of the spider who builds a house. And indeed, the flimsiest of houses is the house of the spider, if they only knew!”* Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt is the great psychological manual on *Fitnah*—the fiery testing and examination of human conviction—opening with the penetrating existential question that cuts through all superficial religious sentiment: *“Do the people think that they will be left to say, 'We believe' and they will not be tested?”* (29:2).

**The Structural Architecture and the House of Silk**

The movement of Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt weaves between existential diagnostic questions, prophetic endurance through time, and cosmic reassurance. The opening movement (1–13) defines the universal law of spiritual examination (*al-ibtilāʾ*): faith is not an inherited badge or an empty verbal claim; it is a metal that must be thrust into the furnace of life to burn away the slag of hypocrisy and prove the gold of sincerity. The second movement (14–40) conducts an unblinking historical survey of prophetic testing:
- Noah (14–15): Preaching for 950 years in an agonizing, slow struggle without visible societal transformation, yet persevering until the Deluge cleansed the earth and the Ark was preserved as a sign.
- Abraham (16–27): Thrown into the blazing fire by his own father and community, delivered by divine decree, and declaring: *“Indeed, I will emigrate to [the service of] my Lord; He is the Exalted in Might, the Wise.”*
- Lot (28–35): Bearing witness against the grotesque public depravity of Sodom, standing entirely alone until the angelic messengers arrived to execute divine judgment.
- Shuʿayb (36–37), ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the archetypal trinity of arrogance: Qārūn (plutocracy), Pharaoh (autocracy), and Hāmān (technocracy and propaganda), all seized in their sins—some struck by a violent windstorm, some by the screaming blast, some swallowed by the earth, and some drowned in the sea (29:40). The third movement (41–55) delivers the supreme parable of the Spider's Web: analyzing polytheism and reliance on worldly powers. It balances this with the command to recite the Scripture, establish the prayer—for *“indeed, prayer deters from indecency and wickedness”*—and declare that the remembrance of God is greatest (*wa-la-dhikru Allāhi akbar*, 29:45). The closing movement (56–69) comforts the oppressed with the vastness of God's earth, reminding them that exile is better than compromise (*“O My servants who have believed, indeed My earth is spacious, so worship only Me,”* 29:56), reminding all souls that every soul shall taste death (29:57), and concluding with the glorious divine promise of verse 69: *“And those who strive for Us—We will surely guide them to Our ways. And indeed, Allah is with the doers of good.”*

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm

**Three Letters Standing Before a Sūrah of Trial**

Alif, lām, and mīm are recited by their names, one after another, and together they make no word. They carry no subject, no predicate, no claim that a hearer can accept or reject. What follows immediately is a claim of exactly that kind: that people who say "We believe" will not be left unexamined (29:2). The order is deliberate. A sūrah about endurance under pressure begins with a sound whose meaning is withheld, and the first lesson taught is that revelation has depths the listener does not command. Twenty-eight other sūrahs open the same way, so this is a convention of the Book rather than an accident of one chapter. Faith made only of agreement with what has been explained has never been the demand; the demand is trust that keeps walking past the edge of the explanation. Three letters that cannot be assembled into a statement stand at the head of a sūrah whose theme is that a claim of faith is not assembled from words alone.

**What the Commentators Do With a Word They Cannot Read**

Four explanations are offered, each with a reason attached. One group leaves the meaning with God, holding that the text supplies no key and that guessing at divine speech is the weakest of methods; the strength of that position is its refusal to guess. A second group reads the letters as an oath, arguing from the Arabic habit of swearing by what is precious and from the many sūrahs that open with a sworn adjuration. The difficulty is grammatical: no oath particle stands in front of them. A third group expands them into divine names — alif from Allāh, lām from Laṭīf, mīm from Majīd — an explanation that fits the sound of each letter but has no transmitted authority behind it. A fourth group hears a summons to the proud speakers of Arabic: the Book that eludes their imitation is built from the same twenty-eight letters they use every day. Reasons settle the argument. The first reading is the safest, and the fourth explains the most, since it accounts for the placement of the letters in a sūrah concerned with evidence and for the Qur'an's repeated challenge to produce a like of it (2:23).

**Fourteen Letters Drawn From Twenty-Eight**

The arithmetic of the device is exact. These openings stand at the head of twenty-nine of the Book's one hundred and fourteen sūrahs, and they draw on fourteen distinct letters — precisely half of the Arabic alphabet. Alif-lām-mīm alone opens six sūrahs: al-Baqarah, Āl ʿImrān, al-ʿAnkabūt, al-Rūm, Luqmān, and al-Sajdah. The chosen letters are spread across the sound-classes of Arabic rather than concentrated in one family of consonants, so the selection cannot be reduced to a stylistic preference for a certain set of sounds. Something in the language is being singled out as a whole. A device that halves an alphabet and then repeats one combination six times behaves like a system, and systems in a revealed text invite explanation. What the explanation is remains the point at issue between the schools, and the disagreement has never been resolved by anything firmer than reasoning. The letters stand there as data, and the readers of every century have had to decide what to do with data that resists decoding.

**Four Consecutive Sūrahs With One Opening**

Sūrahs 29, 30, 31, and 32 open with the same three letters, and they stand next to one another in the written Book. Their contents diverge widely: one is built on trial, one on the turn of fortune between empires, one on a father's advice to his son, one on the prostration of creation. Identical openings serve arguments that share no subject, which tells against any reading in which the letters encode the theme of the chapter they introduce. A reader who wants to know why al-ʿAnkabūt begins this way has to reckon with the fact that it is not alone.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:2

> Do people think once they say, “We believe,” that they will be left without being put to the test

**The Doorway al-Tustarī Described**

The early ascetic al-Tustarī is reported to have said that affliction is a doorway between those who know God and God. The image is not of an obstacle to be resented but of an opening through which entry happens, and the report matches the shape of the verse it explains. Knowledge of God that has been paid for in patience has a different quality from knowledge held cheaply. The Prophet described the same law of proportion when he was asked which people are most severely tried: the prophets, then those nearest to them in rank, then the next best, and a person is tested according to his religion, so that trials continue until he walks the earth with no sin upon him (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2398, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). The report establishes a scale. Severity follows rank, which reverses the assumption behind the verse's question: the untested life is not the privileged life, and hardship is a sign of terms being taken seriously.

**Two Reports About One Complaint**

Commentators connect the verse to concrete grievances. In one report it was revealed about Mihjaʿ, a man from the entourage of ʿUmar, killed by ʿĀmir ibn al-Ḥaḍramī and remembered as the first Muslim martyr at Badr; his wife and parents were shattered by his death, and the verses came down explaining that death in the path of God was not the evidence of abandonment it appeared to be. In a second report, the complaint came from Muslims in Makkah who had failed to leave for Madinah, had been stopped by the idolaters when they tried, and were answered with these words before the tenth verse of the chapter descended.

**What the Verse Refuses to Say**

Careful reading is as much about the claims absent from a sentence as the ones present in it. Nothing here says God needs the information a trial would produce, and nothing says the outcome is in doubt; such questions belong to the verse that follows. Nothing says suffering is punishment. Nothing exempts the sincere, and nothing accuses the claimant of fraud — the error identified is a mistaken inference, not a lie. The Book elsewhere states the same principle in the language of purpose rather than rebuke: God created death and life to test which of people is best in deeds (67:2), and loss of life, wealth, and food are named as the instruments (2:155), attached to a promise of good news for those who hold firm (2:155). The question in this verse, then, is narrow and severe. It asks only whether a person expects to buy the destination without the road.

**The Congregation That Could Not Leave**

For the first Muslims in Makkah the test was not a figure of speech. A believer might be isolated from family, watched in his trade, and prevented from joining the community gathering in Madinah, and the Book treats the failure to emigrate as a grave matter: those who wronged themselves are asked by the angels what they were doing, and the only excuse accepted is genuine helplessness, with a promise of pardon for the truly oppressed (4:97–99). The same emigrants are named later in the Book among the forgiven: those who left their homes after being compelled, then struggled and persevered, have a Lord who is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (16:110). Taken together, the two passages frame the test as a real cost with a real remedy. The claim to faith made in Makkah was measured by what a person was prepared to lose in the open, and by whether he would try again after failing.

**The Same Rule Stated to the First Community**

The claim that believers must be proven before they are rewarded is not new with this chapter. The Book puts the question directly to the community of Madinah: did they suppose they would enter the Garden while God had not yet distinguished those who struggled and those who held firm (3:142), and it repeats the challenge in the account of the wilderness generation, where the shaking reached even the Messenger and those with him before help arrived (2:214). The three passages differ in audience and vocabulary while making one demand. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states the rule at the level of the individual claim, 3:142 states it at the level of the community, and 2:214 states it at the level of history. Placed together, they make it impossible to read the verses about trial as a lesson aimed only at the first generation, since the Book deliberately repeats the demand to each new audience that assumes the matter is settled.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:3

> We certainly tested those before them. And ˹in this way˺ Allah will clearly distinguish between those who are truthful and those who are liars

**A Rule With a Precedent Behind It**

The claim that believers are tried is given a history in this sentence, and the history is meant to comfort rather than threaten. Communities that came before — the followers of Moses, the followers of Jesus, and those who stood with the earlier messengers — met hostility for their allegiance and held their ground. The Book makes the same point twice elsewhere, once as a challenge and once as a scene: how could the people of Madinah expect to enter the Garden while God had not yet shown who among them would struggle and who would endure (3:142), and how could anyone imagine entering Paradise without the shaking that reached even the Messenger and his companions until they cried out for help (2:214). Placing this verse after the question of 29:2 turns an individual rebuke into a law of history. The Makkan believer under pressure is not the first person to discover that faith costs something; he is standing in a queue that stretches back through the prophets.

**Ṣidq: Truth as Firmness**

The Arabic word for truth in this verse carries a physical sense before it carries a moral one. *Ṣidq* belongs with firmness and solidity; a spear described as *ṣadq* is one that has been tested and does not bend. A *ṣādiq* is therefore not merely a person who says accurate things; he is a person whose whole state holds under pressure, whose speech matches the facts and whose behaviour matches his speech. The opposite term, *kādhib*, describes a claim that has nothing behind it. Setting the two words against each other after a mention of testing is exact, not decorative: the test is what reveals whether a man's words are load-bearing. The Book applies the same standard to the community as a whole when it orders believers to be mindful of God and to remain with the truthful (9:119), a command that makes truth a place a person stands in rather than a compliment paid to him. Speech is easy; standing is not.

**Trials That Are Written to the Servant's Credit**

Suffering endured in the path of God is not treated in the Book as silence that goes unrecorded. The Prophet is reported to have said that no weariness, illness, grief, sorrow, harm, or distress touches a Muslim, down to the prick of a thorn, without God removing some of his sins by it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5641; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2573), a report transmitted on the authority of Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī and Abū Hurayrah. The Qur'an states the same accounting principle for the fighters of Madinah: thirst, fatigue, hunger, or any step that angers the disbelievers is written down as a good deed for them, and every donation and every valley crossed is recorded, so that God may give them the best reward for what they did (9:120–121). The testing of this sūrah, then, is not a period in which a person is left to manage alone. It is a ledger. Every cost is entered, and nothing that was paid for the sake of God is lost in the transaction.

**One Event, Two Verdicts**

The distinction in this verse cuts in both directions at once, and the Book says so explicitly when it addresses the same principle to the community of Madinah: testing continues until God proves who among them struggles in His cause and remains steadfast (47:31). A single hardship therefore issues two findings on the same day — the truthful person is confirmed, and the liar is exposed — and neither finding is arbitrary, since both rest on what the individual actually did with the pressure. This is why the verse joins the two groups in one clause instead of dividing them into two sentences: one instrument, one day, and one ledger produce both results. The reader who wants reassurance from the sentence will not find it in the form of exemption. He finds instead the promise that nothing about the test is random, that the criteria are known in advance, and that the trait being measured is precisely the trait he claims to have.

That reading keeps the divine attribute intact and fits the Book's description of God separating the good from the evil so that no one is left guessing (3:179).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:4

> Or do the evildoers ˹simply˺ think that they will escape Us? How wrong is their judgment

**The Race Nobody Wins Against God**

The verb translated as escaping carries the sense of arriving first, of getting ahead of someone in a race. The evildoer of this verse pictures God as a pursuer with limited speed and himself as a runner with a head start. The Book answers that picture in another passage with the same root: those who disbelieve should not suppose that they are beyond reach, because they cannot make themselves uncatchable (8:59). The claim being ridiculed is not that God is weak; the evildoer would never say that. The claim being ridiculed is that God is slow, and that the interval between the deed and the accounting is his own territory, where he can arrange matters. Time is the thing he thinks he has won, and time is the thing he did not create. The race image fits the verse's ending, since a verdict of "how wrong is their judgment" is exactly what a spectator says when a competitor has misread the course.

**Prosperity Read as a Verdict**

The specific reasoning behind the mistake deserves to be stated, because the verse assumes it rather than spelling it out. Health, wealth, and long life arrive to the wrongdoer on schedule, and the silence of heaven is read as approval. The Book confronts that reading in several places. When a people became heedless of warnings, they were given everything they desired, and at the moment they took pride in what they had, the seizure came upon them suddenly (6:44). The disbelievers are told plainly not to think that an extended life is good for them, since the extension only increases what they will answer for (3:178). God's own words in another sūrah describe the mechanism from the inside: the end is delayed for a while, and the planning is flawless (7:183). Prosperity, then, is not a certificate of standing. It is an interval, and intervals end.

The error also has a social dimension. A family that prospers while breaking its word teaches its neighbours a lesson about what pays, and the Book's prophets argue against the inference rather than against the wealth, which is why so many of these exchanges end with a reminder about what the Hereafter holds.

**The Term That Cannot Be Advanced**

The interval the wrongdoer mistakes for immunity is not indefinite, and the Book states its rules with unusual firmness. Every community has an appointed term, and when it arrives no one can delay it by a moment or bring it forward (7:34). Death does not wait for anyone's business to be concluded: God never delays a soul when its appointed time arrives, and He is aware of everything people do (63:11). A person's whole calculation rests on a period he did not set and cannot extend. The verse's confidence, then, is not the confidence of a spectator who expects a future punishment; it is the confidence of someone who has read the schedule. The evildoer's escape would require the machinery to fail at exactly the point he needs it to fail, and the Book shows no such point anywhere in the system.

**What Kind of Judgement They Are Making**

The Book treats the verdict of 29:4 as an act of judging, and it has a specific vocabulary for rulings made without reference to revelation. Sūrah al-Māʾidah asks whether it is the judgment of ignorance they seek, and then asks who could be a better judge than God for people of sure faith (5:50). The evildoer of this verse is doing exactly that: he rules on his own case in his own favour, on the strength of his comfort, and he does it without a hearing. The Book's objection is not only that he reached the wrong verdict; it is that he assumed jurisdiction he does not hold. What follows in the chapter is a description of judgment on terms he did not set.

The phrase also exposes the disorder in the evildoer's reasoning. He has taken a delay in the proceedings as a verdict in his favour, and the rebuttal is not an argument about evidence but a statement about who holds the authority to decide.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:5

> Whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah, ˹let them know that˺ Allah’s appointed time is sure to come. He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**Hope With a Date Attached**

The verse addresses a desire and immediately attaches a schedule to it. To hope for the meeting with God is not a mood; it is an expectation of an event, and an event has a time. The sentence supplies the time twice over: the appointed term is coming, and the Coming is described as certain rather than possible. Hope in the Book is therefore never vague optimism. It is the state of a person who expects something specific and orders his affairs accordingly. The verb also carries a second shade in Arabic, that of eager anticipation, the leaning of a person toward what he awaits. Attach that anticipation to a fixed date and the practical result is a life paced by the calendar rather than by the mood of the day. The verse does not ask the hopeful person to feel more; it hands him the fact that makes hope reasonable and, in the same breath, makes delay unreasonable.

**What the Meeting With God Includes**

The phrase is compressed, and the commentators unpack it as the whole sequence that ends a life: the arrival of the angel of death, the resurrection of the body, the presentation of the record, and the receiving of the outcome. Nothing in that list is exotic, and nothing in it is optional. This is why the verse can be read as a description of the end of every human being rather than as a doctrine for the pious only. Every person will meet God; the question the sentence leaves open is what kind of meeting it will be. The hopeful person and the careless person walk the same road at the same uncertain pace, and the difference between them is entirely in how they spend the interval. Setting the phrase in that plain sense removes the mist that often gathers around religious vocabulary. Meeting God is not a metaphor for a feeling; it is the day the record is opened.

The meeting the Book describes is not an event a person learns about at a distance; it is the moment when the record of a life is placed before the one who lived it. What was concealed, postponed, and half-completed comes into view in the same instant, which is why the verse treats the appointment as something to prepare for rather than something to discuss.

**An Appointment Nobody Postpones**

The word rendered as appointed time is the same word the Book uses for the fixed term of every community and every soul. No one can push it back by a moment, and no one can pull it forward (7:34). No soul dies except by God's permission at a written time, and those who want the world get it while those who want the Hereafter get theirs (3:145). Death arrives on schedule even when the person has plans: God never delays a soul when its term arrives, and He sees everything His servants do (63:11). The comfort this offers is different from the comfort of being spared. It is the comfort of living inside a system that cannot be corrupted, where the strong cannot buy an extension and the weak cannot be cut short early. The person who hopes for the meeting therefore has nothing to negotiate and everything to prepare.

The image of a term fixed in advance removes urgency from an opponent's arsenal. A person convinced that the reckoning can be hurried or delayed has an argument to make; a person who understands the date as already written has only the interval in front of him, and that interval is what the verse asks him to fill.

**What Hope Requires of the One Who Holds It**

Hope in the Book is always attached to work, and the attachment is explicit. The Prophet is told to announce that whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord should do good and never associate anything in worship with his Lord (18:110). The verse under discussion assumes the same, because it presents hope as a stance toward a coming event rather than as a sentiment toward a distant one. Hope without a change of conduct would be a contradiction in terms, the expectation of a result in which one has no investment. What the sentence therefore does, in one move, is to give the hopeful person the reason for his discipline (the appointment is real), the motive for his patience (the appointment is not far), and the assurance that the one who set the appointment hears and knows. Two names at the end supply the guarantees; the whole verse supplies the deadline.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:6

> And whoever strives ˹in Allah’s cause˺, only does so for their own good. Surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of˺ His creation

**The Striver Is the One Who Profits**

The sentence states a rule of accounting in the plainest terms: the benefit of effort returns to the one who makes it. This is not a concession but a structural fact about the relationship between God and creatures. An act of devotion cannot add to God, and an act of rebellion cannot subtract from Him, so every deed lands where it was launched. The Book repeats the principle in different words: whoever works righteousness does so for his own soul, and whoever does evil does so against it (41:46), and the same bookkeeping is applied to the whole of a life with the reminder that people will return to their Lord afterwards (45:15). The practical effect of the verse is to strip away a common and comfortable illusion — that worship is a favour conferred on the deity. What worship changes is the worshipper, entirely and only, and the chapter says so before it asks anyone to exert himself.

**A God Who Cannot Be Enriched**

The second half of the verse gives the reason for the first: God is free of any need for the worlds. People stand in need of Him, while He alone is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy (35:15). Mūsā makes the same point to his people when he tells them that ingratitude on the part of everyone on earth costs God nothing, since He is Self-Sufficient and Praiseworthy in Himself (14:8). If the divine being had needs, then devotion would be a transaction and the creature would have leverage; the verse removes that possibility at the root. The Book never appeals to divine need to motivate obedience, because there is none to appeal to. What it appeals to instead is consequence: obedience heals and elevates the one who obeys, and transgression damages him, and neither outcome touches the One addressed. Ridding the relationship of need is what makes it a relationship of pure gift.

**No One Carries Another's Load**

Effort returns to the one who made it, however, and every individual arrives at the accounting alone, since no burdened soul will carry the burden of another (53:38), a statement repeated in the Book with an unusual insistence (17:15; 35:18; 39:7). The Qur'an says that each person will have only what they worked for (53:39), which closes the door on the idea that another's merit can be transferred as property. Intercession, kinship, and community are real in the Book, but they operate within this frame rather than around it. The verse under study sits inside the same architecture: the struggle is personal, the profit is personal, and the reason for both lies in a God who needs nothing from either side. A reader who takes that seriously will stop measuring religious effort by what it does for anyone else, including God, and start measuring it by what it is doing to him.

**The Profitable Trade Offered Elsewhere**

The Book frames this same effort as a bargain in which the benefit flows to the buyer, and it does so with the language of commerce. The Prophet is told to ask whether believers want to be guided to a trade that delivers them from a painful punishment — the trade of faith in God and His Messenger, and of struggling with wealth and life in His cause (61:10–11). The structure of the offer is important: everything on offer is profit, and nothing is owed to the other party. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt and Sūrah al-Ṣaff therefore state one rule in two idioms, one of the ledger and one of the market. Whoever enters either formulation for the sake of the beneficiary will misread both, and the verse under discussion leaves no room for the misunderstanding.

The Book fixes that standard explicitly: no soul is required to give more than it has been given, and hardship is followed by ease (65:7).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:7

> As for those who believe and do good, We will certainly absolve them of their sins, and reward them according to the best of what they used to do

**Absolution Comes Before Reward**

The sentence places the wiping away of sins ahead of the paying of reward, and the order is not accidental. In the Book's accounting, guilt is the obstacle that has to be cleared before anything can be received, so absolution comes first and recompense follows. The Arabic verb behind "absolve" belongs to the same root as the word for expiation, a term built on the idea of covering: a thing is blotted out rather than merely set aside. The promise is emphatic in form, an oath-like construction that leaves no room for condition or doubt. What this suggests is that the believers addressed here arrive at the reckoning with a record that is mixed — they have both faith and faults — and the verse tells them which way the balance is settled.

**Reward Measured by the Best, Not the Average**

The phrase "according to the best of what they used to do" introduces a generous principle. A person's deeds are not averaged; the standard applied is his best. The Book states the same measure elsewhere — those in Paradise have their misdeeds overlooked while the good they did is accepted (46:16), and God rewards people according to the best of their deeds, adding to it out of His grace, and giving without limit to whom He wills (24:38). The same promise, in nearly the same words, is attached to whoever does good whether male or female while believing: a good life in this world and a reward measured by the best of what they did (16:97). The generosity has a logic. If the criterion were the average, the smallest lapse would drag a whole life down; by using the best, the Book gives weight to what a person was at his highest, which is exactly what the testing was designed to reveal.

**What Happens to a Past Carried Into Islam**

The verse answers a real anxiety felt by converts in every generation: what happens to the years spent before faith. The Book is explicit that sincere entry into faith reopens the account. Those who emigrated after being compelled to renounce their religion, then struggled and held firm, are told that their Lord is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful (16:110). The command to announce that God forgives all sins is addressed to people who have exceeded every limit against themselves (39:53), and the Book elsewhere states that a deed done in ignorance is not the same as a deed persisted in after knowledge. Commentators differ on whether conversion wipes the record at the moment of belief or through the good deeds that follow it. The verse under discussion favours the second reading without denying the first: it joins belief and action in one condition, and then speaks of absolution. The believer who piled up offences before Islam receives both the covering and the new record, and the second is what the rest of his life is spent filling.

**Deeds That Outlast the World They Were Made In**

Why should any deed survive the dissolution of the world in which it was performed? The Book gives an answer when it says that wealth and children are the adornment of this life while the abiding good deeds are better with God in reward and in hope (18:46). Its sharpest statement of the principle comes where the Prophet is told not to invoke any other god, because everything is bound to perish except God Himself, to whom all matters return (28:88). A deed is a strange kind of object: it is produced in time, and it either attaches itself to something perishable — a reputation, a payment, a fear — or it attaches itself to the One who does not perish. The righteous action the verse rewards is the second kind. It has no expiry date, because it was not made for a market that expires. Whatever is done for the sake of the world goes when the world goes; what is done for God's sake is filed where decay cannot reach.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:8

> We have commanded people to honour their parents. But if they urge you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, then do not obey them. To Me you will ˹all˺ return, and then I will inform you of what you used to do

**A Duty Ranked With the Highest**

The Book places kindness to parents in the first rank of obligations, next to the worship of God alone. The covenant taken from the children of Israel opens with that order: worship none but God, then be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, and the needy (2:83). The decree given to the Prophet's community arranges the same pair, adding that a grown child must never say "ugh" to an aging parent, nor yell, but must speak respectfully (17:23). The Prophet was asked about the greatest sins and answered by naming the association of partners with God and undutifulness to parents in a single breath, then sat up straight and repeated the warning about false testimony until his companions wished he would stop (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5976). The placement is what matters here. Duty to parents is not a courtesy that sits below religion; it is one of the things religion is built on, and a verse that limits it has to do so with care.

**Saʿd and the Mother Who Refused to Eat**

Commentators connect the verse to Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ. When he embraced Islam, his mother Ḥamnah announced that she would take no food, no drink, and no shelter until her son abandoned the religion of Muḥammad and returned to the ways of his people. Days passed, and the family feared for her life, until Saʿd complained to the Prophet. The verses came down giving him the ruling he needed: accompany your parents in the world with kindness, but do not obey them in what contradicts God (Q, Ṭ, W; the same instruction appears in 31:15 and is tied by some commentators to the same event). The report shows how the general sentence lands in a real household, and it also shows the answer given to the child: he was not told to win the argument, and not told to despise his mother, but told what to do and what not to do.

**Courtesy Without Compliance**

The Book supplies the style in which a refusal is to be conducted. The parallel verse says to keep the company of such parents in this world courteously, while following the way of those who turn to God in devotion (31:15). Kindness here is not a ceasefire that lasts until the parents change; it is a standing obligation. The same principle governs relations with people outside the faith who are not at war with Muslims: God does not forbid Muslims from dealing kindly and fairly with those who neither fought them nor drove them from their homes, and He loves those who are fair (60:8). The line the verse draws therefore runs between belief and behaviour, not between affection and distance. A parent may be wrong about God and still be owed the visit, the support, and the respect — and the child who withholds those things has misread the ruling he was given.

**Justice That Can Be Testified Against One's Own Family**

The limit placed on obedience to parents does not cancel the obligation to deal fairly with them, and the Book states the two duties in one verse when it orders believers to stand firm for justice as witnesses for God even if the testimony goes against themselves, their parents, or their close relatives (4:135). The instruction is unusually demanding, and it is placed under the heading of justice rather than of worship. A person may be forbidden to follow a parent into idolatry and still be required to testify truthfully on that parent's behalf in a dispute. The two duties run on separate tracks, and the sūrah's instruction not to obey is not a permission to punish.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:9

> Those who believe and do good will surely be admitted by Us into ˹the company of˺ the righteous

**The Reward Described as Company**

The promise here is phrased in people rather than property. The believer is not only given a garden; he is placed among the righteous, and the Book treats that placement as the substance of the reward. In Sūrah al-Nisāʾ the same logic is spelled out: whoever obeys God and His Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by God — the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous, described as honourable company (4:69). Sūrah al-Raʿd adds family to the picture, promising Gardens of Eternity entered along with the righteous among a person's parents, spouses, and children, with angels coming in by every gate (13:23). Eternal life in the Book is therefore social by design. The blessed are not scattered through a landscape alone; they are gathered, and the gathering itself is what the verse names as the goal of a life spent believing and acting well.

**Who the Righteous Are**

The Arabic word rendered as "righteous" comes from the root of repair. A *ṣāliḥ* thing is sound, fit for the purpose it was made for; *iṣlāḥ* is the act of setting something right that had gone wrong. Its opposite, *fasād*, names corruption, the condition of a thing that no longer works as it should. Calling a group "the righteous" is thus not a compliment about their manners; it is a claim about their condition. They are people whose machinery is in order, whose appetites, fears, and ambitions are functioning under proper authority. This is why the Book can use the word as a class name in the same breath as prophets and martyrs (4:69): the categories overlap because all of them describe people who were sound where it counted. The promise in this verse is not only the enjoyment of Paradise but residence among people of that description, a company that lies at the opposite end of the scale from the corrupt.

**Being Brought In, Rather Than Getting In**

The verb form used here has God as its subject: "We will surely admit them" — the entry is something done to the believer, not something he accomplishes. Arabic can express arrival in several ways, and the Book chooses the causative for the reward while choosing the language of effort for the road. The two are consistent: the striving belongs to the servant, and the entry belongs to the Lord. Sūrah al-Zumar paints the scene at the end of that arrangement, with those who were mindful of their Lord being led to Paradise in successive groups until they reach gates already standing open, where the keepers greet them and welcome them in to remain (39:73). Nothing in the scene suggests that the travellers forced the gates. The doors are open before they arrive, and the welcome is spoken by others. The promise in this verse is that kind of arrival, prepared and granted.

**Where the Same Company Is Named Again**

The Book keeps returning to the same arrangement in other chapters. Those who obey God and His Messenger are placed with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous (4:69), and the verse that follows promises them a reward that needs no addition. In the Gardens of Eternity of Sūrah al-Raʿd, the righteous are joined by the righteous among their own families (13:23), which means the gathering is not a crowd of strangers sorted by merit but a reuniting of people who belong together. Taken with the sentence under discussion, the two passages show a consistent picture: the reward is described by its inhabitants, admission is by God's act, and the criterion is the pair of belief and good conduct that the sūrah has already used to define the tested believer.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:10

> There are some who say, “We believe in Allah,” but when they suffer in the cause of Allah, they mistake ˹this˺ persecution at the hands of people for the punishment of Allah. But when victory comes from your Lord, they surely say ˹to the believers˺, “We have always been with you.” Does Allah not know best what is in the hearts of all beings

**"We Have Always Been With You"**

The second face of the same character appears when the pressure reverses. With the arrival of help from God the man returns to the company he had been avoiding, and he does it with a claim about his history rather than an apology for his absence. The words are a bid for standing: nothing in them acknowledges the retreat that preceded them, because the retreat has been retroactively deleted. This is the transactional standard in its purest form. Allegiance is purchased when it is profitable and suspended when it is expensive. The community hears such a sentence constantly, and the Book's use of the plural — the victory belongs to the Lord, and the reclaimer speaks of "we" — marks the mismatch between the size of the claim and the size of the contribution that preceded it.

The claim is also revealing in its form. The speaker does not assert a faith of his own or invoke a doctrine; he asks to be counted among a group whose standing he is not sure of, and the Book records the sentence because it shows what such a person thinks protection consists of.

**The Report About Those Forced to March to Badr**

Commentators connect the verse to a specific grievance from the Makkan period. According to Ibn ʿAbbās the words concern believers the idolaters had forced to march with them toward Badr; when the matter worsened, some of them joined the enemy's ranks without excuse, and the Book later addressed precisely that case, asking what those who wronged themselves had been doing and whether God's earth was not wide enough to emigrate (4:97), with a clear exemption for the genuinely helpless (4:99), and a promise of pardon for those who emigrated after being compelled, then struggled and endured (16:110). The reports fix the first audience without closing the meaning. The condition they describe — loyalty adjusting to the temperature of events — is not confined to one battle or one century, and the verse is worded as a general case precisely so that it can travel.

**The Worshipper Standing on the Edge**

The Book gives this character a portrait of his own in Sūrah al-Ḥajj, and the lines are worth setting beside the verse: there are some who worship God on the verge of faith, content when good befalls them and relapsing into disbelief when a trial comes, losing this world and the Hereafter, which the Book calls the clearest loss (22:11). The man described in the sūrah under discussion is that figure in action. He did not decline to join the community; he joined it at the edge, so that he could step back whenever the ground moved. The Book's verdict on the resulting trade is stated in loss in both worlds, which is exactly the calculation the person had been trying to avoid.

The description also supplies the reason his position is dangerous. He has no settled commitment to weigh against the pressure, so each trial finds him already halfway to compliance, and the warning is addressed to people who have arranged their lives so that a single difficulty can decide them.

**The Man Described, and the Man Not Described**

A distinction has to be kept, since the sentence is capable of wounding the wrong reader. The case stated is a person whose belief was conditional from the start, whose support moved with the outcome and who covered the movement with a false claim about his record. It is not a description of the believer who panics, weeps under torture, or fails once and returns. The Book has space for the second: the wilderness community was shaken until the Messenger and the believers with him cried out asking when God's help would come (2:214), and those words are recorded as the speech of the faithful. What separates the two is the direction of the movement, not the presence of fear. The one who stumbles and comes back is inside the test, and the one who is only present when the outcome is settled was never inside it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:11

> Allah will certainly distinguish between those who have ˹sure˺ faith and the hypocrites

**The Same Audit, a New Pair of Names**

The sūrah has already announced that God will separate the truthful from the liars (29:3). This sentence runs the same procedure again with a different pair of terms: those who have sure faith and the hypocrites. The repetition is a form of tightening. The first statement sorted people by whether their claim was backed; this one sorts them by whether their conviction was firm. Between the two sits the man described in the previous verse, whose loyalty rose and fell with the news, and the verse is best read as his verdict. What the sentence promises is not punishment in the first place but clarity: the distinguishing will be done, and it will be done by God, whose knowledge of the interior cannot be contested.

**Faith That Does Not Doubt**

The phrase rendered as "sure faith" fixes the opposite condition with precision. The Book defines the true believers as those who believe in God and His Messenger and then never doubt, expending their wealth and their lives in the cause of God, and calls them the ones true in faith (49:15). Two elements are joined in that definition and in this verse: the interior absence of hesitation and the exterior expenditure that follows from it. Certainty in the Book is not a feeling of confidence; it is a settled state that makes a person spendable. The man in the previous verse lacked exactly that, and his condition under pressure proved the absence. The sentence here therefore separates two ways of handling the same claim: one man's belief is load-bearing and another man's is ornamental, and the difference becomes public the first time the structure takes weight.

**The Vocabulary the Book Reserves for This Condition**

The Book's later chapter on the same subject supplies the name that this verse's audience would have recognized the type by, and it opens with the charged word attached to it (63:1). The term's use in the Makkan period, before that chapter existed, is instructive: the Book is describing a condition rather than a party. Sūrah al-Ḥujurāt distinguishes the two registers in a single exchange, telling a group of nomads who claimed faith that they had not believed but had submitted, since faith had not yet entered their hearts (49:14). The distinction between claiming and holding is the one this verse draws.

**Why the Sentence Ends in Distinction, Not Fire**

The verse stops short of the punishment it clearly implies, and the restraint is instructive. What God promises here is the sorting itself — the exposure of the two groups as what they are. That is a mercy for the community, which needs to know whom to trust in matters of survival, and it is a mercy for the waverer, who is given the chance to see himself before the final accounting closes the file. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the destination of the unrepentant type and then immediately opens a door: the hypocrites are in the lowest depths of the Fire, except those who repent, mend their ways, hold fast to God, and make their devotion sincere, who will be with the believers (4:145–146). The Book's habit of attaching an exit to every verdict explains why this sentence issues a distinction and leaves the sentence of the sort closed.

**The Sūrah That Dedicates Itself to the Type**

One chapter of the Book takes its name from this failing and opens with the portrait complete: when the hypocrites come to the Prophet, they bear witness that he is the Messenger of God, and God, who knows the witness to be true of the man they address, bears witness that they are liars (63:1). A few lines later the cause is given — they believed, then abandoned faith, and their hearts were sealed so that they could not understand (63:3). Set beside the present verse, the later chapter supplies the anatomy of what is being distinguished here. The exterior form had never been the problem; the Book is full of people saying the words. What is being tested, in Makkah and in Madinah alike, is the part of a person that no exit from a burrow can protect. The community is promised a proof here rather than a purge, and the proof is conducted by the One whose knowledge of the interior no defense can obscure.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:12

> The disbelievers say to the believers, “˹Just˺ follow our way, and we will bear ˹the burden of˺ your sins.” But they would never ˹want to˺ bear any of the believers’ sins. They are simply lying

**An Offer Designed to Remove the Cost**

The proposal is elegant in its cruelty. Return to the ways of your ancestors, and the consequences of that return will be carried by us. Every element of the sentence is calculated: the believers are addressed as people who hesitate because they can see the price, so the price is taken away; departure from the community is dressed as a small step, so the step is described as "following"; and responsibility for the deed is transferred to someone else, where the Book says it cannot go. The offer is also evidence of what the disbelievers understood about the new faith. They knew that its followers believed in accountability, that they feared the Day, and that the fear was strong enough to hold a person inside a difficult commitment. Attacking that fear with a lie about the ledger was a strategy aimed precisely where the believers were strongest.

**The Burden That Cannot Be Moved**

The refusal given in the verse is grounded in a rule the Book states again and again: no soul will bear the burden of another (35:18), and no soul will be requited for anything but what it worked for (53:39). The same declaration appears in several chapters with the same unyielding shape, and it governs the whole economy of the Hereafter: the record belongs to the person whose deeds produced it, and it cannot be reassigned, sold, gifted, or inherited. Anyone promising to carry a stranger's sins is promising something outside the structure of the system he lives in. The Book does report that leaders of misguidance will carry an additional burden for those they misled while those who followed them still carry their own, so that the weight accumulates for the one who caused it — but nothing in that arrangement lightens the follower's load by a single sin.

**The Ransom That Will Not Be Taken**

The Book closes the same door from another direction when it describes the Day for which the offer would be owed. No soul will be of help to another on that Day; no intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help given (2:48), and the sentence is repeated almost word for word in the same sūrah (2:123). A ransom is what the proposal in this verse actually amounts to: someone offers to pay a debt that is not transferable, in a currency nobody accepts. The Book's insistence on the point, stated in two nearly identical verses, suggests that the temptation to look for a sponsor is permanent. People in every generation prefer a system in which someone else's merit can be arranged to cover their own account, and the Book announces the same answer each time: the account is yours.

Refusing a ransom also settles what the judgment is about. If the dispute could be closed by an exchange of goods, it would be a disagreement about property; the verse describes it as a question of truth and conduct, and the Book's account of the Hereafter never allows wealth to substitute for either.

**Why the Sūrah Uses the Word Liars**

The sūrah has already used this vocabulary as its central dividing line, promising a distinction between the truthful and the liars (29:3). The return of the word here is deliberate. The disbelievers' offer is a lie in the plain sense — the promise will not be kept — but it is also a lie in the sūrah's deeper sense, since it invites men to structure their lives on a claim that does not hold. The Book frequently joins the two senses, because the moral vocabulary of this chapter is not a set of compliments and insults; it is a description of what is load-bearing. A lie in this usage is anything a person can stand on that will not support him.

The proposal in this verse was not aimed at strangers; it was aimed at the community's own dissidents, and the Book records the anxiety behind such efforts in a warning to believers about yielding to a group among the People of the Book who would turn them back from belief to disbelief once they had accepted it (3:100).

The Book rejects this kind of transfer with a short formula in another sūrah, where the Prophet is told to tell the disbelievers that they will not be held accountable for the believers' misdeeds, nor will the believers be accountable for theirs (34:25).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:13

> Yet they will certainly ˹be made to˺ carry their own burdens, as well as other burdens along with their own. And they will surely be questioned on Judgment Day about what they used to fabricate

**Two Burdens on One Back**

The promise that closes the previous verse is answered here with what actually happens. The disbelievers will carry their own burdens, and with them burdens belonging to others — the weight of the people they misled and the falsehoods they taught. The Book's accounting is exact in both directions. Nothing owed by the followers is deducted from the followers, since no soul bears another's burden (35:18), and nothing owed by the leaders is overlooked because their crime was committed in public and through other people's hands. The image is not of punishment doubled by an arbitrary judge; it is of a ledger that records both the deed and its reproduction. A lie spoken once and adopted by a thousand people arrives at the reckoning as a thousand entries plus the original, and the man who issued it owns every copy.

**Fabrication as an Industry**

The final phrase names the sin precisely: they will be questioned about what they used to fabricate. The Arabic verb describes making something with effort, the way an artisan produces a finished object, and the Book applies it to religious inventions — doctrines, prohibitions, excuses, and the offer made in the previous verse. Fabrication is the right word because the product has no counterpart in reality. What these men manufactured was a religion that flattered its customers, and the terms of the previous verse show the method: an offer of immunity, tailored to the fear they knew their listeners carried.

**What the Followers Get to Say**

The Book records the scene from the other side as well. Those who followed will say to those who led that they were followers, and will ask whether the leaders can now bear any part of the Fire on their behalf; the leaders will answer that everyone must taste it, and the quarrel will end in mutual recrimination (14:21). Elsewhere the followers ask God to give their leaders a double punishment in the Fire (7:38). The request is denied and granted at once, since the Book has already explained that the leaders carry their own burden plus the burden of those they misled (16:25). Following another person into ruin does not transfer the follower's responsibility upward any more than leading transfers it downward. Each rank answers for its own share, and the extra weight on the leader is a penalty for what he added, not a discount for what the follower did.

**Fabrication and the Question of Authorship**

The Book uses one word for the manufacture of a religion, and it always attaches the word to a person rather than to a crowd. Whoever still fabricates lies about God after the truth has come is named as the real wrongdoer (3:94), and the Book's descriptions of the act elsewhere involve an individual claiming revelations he never received or claiming the power to produce the like of what God sent down (6:93). The scene in this verse is the audit of that authorship. What is placed on the scale is not the popularity of the doctrine or the sincerity of the followers but the identity of the maker, and the question put to him concerns what he put together. A doctrine that has spread through a town is still the work of the hands that designed it, and the Book's account of the Hereafter treats influence as a form of production.

**The Same Two Ledgers in Another Sūrah**

Sūrah al-Naḥl states the arrangement without imagery: those who mislead will bear their own burdens in full on the Day of Resurrection along with some of the burdens of those they misled without knowledge, and their destination is a wretched one (16:25). The phrase "without knowledge" is important, since it separates the deliberate manufacturer from the person who simply inherited a wrong idea. A man who knows what he is doing carries the heavier load; a man who passes on what he was handed is still asked about what he did with it. Between this verse and the passage in al-Naḥl, the Book has stated the complete rule: the follower keeps his own account, the leader keeps his own plus the interest of what he produced, and neither arrangement is negotiable on the Day of questioning.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14

> Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing

**The Mission That Outlasted Generations**

The Qur'an supplies the content of those centuries in Sūrah Nūḥ, where the prophet reports his own methods to his Lord: he called his people day and night (71:5), he preached to them publicly and privately (71:9), and each time he invited them to seek forgiveness they pressed their fingers into their ears, wrapped themselves in their garments, persisted in denial, and behaved with arrogance (71:7). Three modes of address, three rejections. Elsewhere the Book gives the results in a single line: none of your people will believe except those who have already believed (11:36). The record is important because it removes a comfortable assumption from the mind of anyone who measures a mission by its returns. By that standard Noah failed for nine and a half centuries. The Book treats him as one of the greatest of the messengers, which tells the reader what the actual standard is: delivery, endurance, and refusal to abandon the task.

**The Verdict Given Before the Water Came**

Before the flood, Noah receives an instruction that few preachers ever receive: stop grieving over what they are doing, because no one else among them is going to believe (11:36). The sentence would be crushing to a man who had been working toward a harvest, and liberating to a man who had been asked to keep planting. It also marks a boundary in the story. Until that moment the mission was open and the warning was live; after it, the matter was settled, and the remaining task was the construction of the vessel.

**Overtaken While Still Wrongdoing**

The sentence ends with a description of the state in which the water found them. The Book does not say the flood came after they changed their minds or that it interrupted a period of reflection. It came on them while they were doing the thing they had been warned about. This is the pattern the chapter has already laid down: the wrongdoer's confidence is built on the assumption that he will have time to arrange matters, and the Book answers him with a term that arrives without negotiation. Sūrah al-Qamar adds the mechanism, describing the gates of the sky opened with pouring rain (54:11) while the earth's springs gushed, so that the two waters met by a decree already issued. There is no weather in the account and no accident. What drowned a civilization was a decision that had been announced for centuries to the people it drowned.

**The Prayer That Ended the Reprieve**

Sūrah Nūḥ closes with a petition that marks the end of the opening. Noah asks his Lord not to leave a single disbeliever on the face of the earth, then gives the reason: if they are spared, they will mislead His servants and produce only wicked offspring and hardened deniers (71:26–27). The prayer is severe, and it comes from a man who had watched nine centuries of persuasion fail. What it shows is that the end of a period of respite is not an arbitrary act of power; the record was complete, and the request came from the person best placed to read it. The Book allows its prophets to reach that point but never before it, and it lets the reader see the full ledger first. The flood is presented as an answer to a question that had been asked for a very long time.

The figure of nine hundred and fifty years sits oddly against the human units of the surrounding verses, and the Book elsewhere warns its readers not to measure divine patience by the clock they carry: a day with your Lord is like a thousand years by your counting (22:47).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15

> But We delivered him and those in the Ark, making it a sign for all people

**Rescue Carried Out by the Same Hand**

The water that destroyed the wrongdoers carried the believers, and the Book makes no distinction between the two operations. One decree produced both outcomes, and the vessel that delivered its passengers was the instrument of their salvation only because God had ordered its construction. The Bible preserves a similar picture, and the Qur'an's account keeps the emphasis where it belongs: nothing in the story suggests that the saved escaped by navigation, foresight, or strength. They were inside a structure that floated because it had been designed to float by instructions their prophet had received (11:37). The scene therefore stands as the chapter's illustration of what deliverance looks like in practice — not a removal of people from danger, but a means of surviving inside it, prepared in advance by the One who intended to save them.

**Who Was Aboard**

The Qur'an gives a precise list of the passengers: Noah's family, except those against whom the decree had already passed, a pair from every species, and those who believed — with the last group described in the smallest possible terms, since none believed with him except a few (11:40). Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ uses the phrase for the fully loaded ark (26:119) to underline the same point: the vessel was full, and the freight was not the world's elite. Commentators discuss the number of believers aboard, offering figures that differ, and the disagreement over the count matters less than the ratio it implies between the people who boarded and the civilization that perished. The Book's point is that the survivors were few enough to be counted, and that they were identified by trust rather than by standing in the city they left behind.

The pairing of a family and a cargo is worth noticing, since the Book's account of the boarding order places the household of Noah before the animals and after the warning. The arrangements for the voyage required provisioning, stalling, feeding, and the management of creatures that could not be reasoned with, and the narrative records none of the practical difficulty. What it records instead is the line between those against whom the decree had already passed and those who were taken aboard, and the line runs through Noah's own household. A community that heard this story in Makkah would have understood the implication for its own families, since the pressure the sūrah has been describing divided households as well as cities. The ark is described in another chapter as fully loaded, and the word suits a vessel whose owner gathered exactly what had been assigned to him and left the rest to the water.

**The Ship That Stood as Evidence**

The account in Sūrah Hūd ends with the waters receding, the earth swallowing its rivers and the sky withholding its rain, and the ark coming to rest on Mount Jūdī (11:44). Commentators note that the vessel remained there for a long time, and some take the sign mentioned in this verse to be the ship itself, still visible after the flood as a monument to what had happened. The reading fits the Book's habit of leaving physical traces of its judgments so that later generations can walk to them. A rescue that leaves no mark becomes a story; a rescue that leaves a grounded hull becomes evidence. The Book does not need the remains to prove the account, but it reports them, and the reporting turns the deliverance into something people could see with their own eyes.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:16

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said to his people, “Worship Allah, and fear Him. This is better for you, if only you knew

**"Better for You," Said to People Who Disagree**

The reason attached to the command is a claim about benefit rather than an appeal to raw authority. Abraham tells his people that worship and guarding themselves are better for them, and the qualifier at the end — if only you knew — locates the obstacle in their knowledge rather than in his argument. The Book uses this idiom when it wants the hearer to weigh an option rather than flinch from a threat, and it uses it about worship specifically: believers are told to eat from the good things they have been provided and to give thanks to God if it is truly Him they worship (2:172). The reasoning is that the creature who lives in dependence on a provider is already in a relationship, and the command is an invitation to stop pretending otherwise. What Abraham offers his people is not a demand for surrender but a description of the arrangement they are already in.

**Abraham as the Book's Standing Witness**

The Qur'an returns to Abraham more than to any other figure of the ancient world, and it uses him as the proof that the new message is not a novelty. Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but a man of pure submission who was never a polytheist (3:67). The Prophet is commanded to follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who did not associate partners with God (16:123), and when the followers of other scriptures press their claims, the answer given is that the community follows the faith of Abraham instead (2:135). The Book even asks who could reject the faith of Abraham except a fool, noting that God chose him in this life and that he will be among the righteous in the next (2:130). Placing Abraham in this sūrah, immediately after the account of Noah's long effort, makes him the second witness to a single pattern: the tested prophet who stands alone against the customs of his town.

**How Abraham Argued**

The content of Abraham's reasoning appears across several chapters and always follows the same method. He is shown the wonders of the heavens and the earth so that his certainty can be complete (6:75), and he turns his face to the One who originated the heavens and the earth, upright and not one of the polytheists (6:79). He asks his father why he worships what can neither hear nor see nor benefit him at all (19:42), and the Book gives the same challenge in the mouth of the Prophet: how can anyone worship what can neither harm nor benefit him, when God is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing (5:76)? The argument is from evidence and from plain capacity. Objects that cannot perceive their worshippers cannot be relied on by them, and Abraham asks his people to notice the mismatch between what they call upon and what a god would have to be. The invitation to worship is therefore followed, later in the conversation, by a demonstration.

**What the Summons Cost Abraham's Hearers**

A town that organizes its calendar, its trade, and its standing around a shrine is not being asked to change a private opinion when a prophet tells it to worship God alone; it is being asked to accept a loss. Abraham's reasoning in this verse anticipates that, which is why he attaches a claim about benefit to a command that appears, on the face of it, to be a demand. The Book elsewhere records the resentment such a change produced, and it answers those who would reject the faith of Abraham by asking what kind of person would do so and noting that God chose him in this life and that he will be among the righteous in the next (2:130). The argument from benefit and the argument from honour are two ways of saying that what the town was being asked to surrender was already worth less than what it was being offered.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:17

> You worship besides Allah nothing but idols, simply creating lies ˹about them˺. Those you worship besides Allah certainly cannot give you any provision. So seek provision from Allah ˹alone˺, worship Him, and be grateful to Him. To Him you will ˹all˺ be returned

**The Argument From Provision**

Abraham then selects a single test case and presses it: the objects of worship cannot provide for their worshippers, so provision should be sought from the One who can give it. The Qur'an returns to this argument often, stating that those who worship besides God cannot afford anyone any provision from the heavens and the earth and have no power to do so (16:73). The reasoning is practical rather than metaphysical. A worshipper asks his god for what he needs — rain, food, children, security — and the man who carves or buys his god is petitioning something that depends on the same supply chain he does. The Book's test exposes the absurdity without mockery: address your requests to the source of the supply. Abraham's people were asking for provision from things that had never provided anything, and the sentence tells them where the warehouse is.

**The Nearest-to-God Defense, Answered**

The Book records the counterargument that idolaters in every era make, and it answers it directly. They say they worship these beings only so that they may bring them closer to God (39:3), and the Book replies that sincere devotion belongs to God alone and that those who persist in the claim are, in its own words, liars and deniers. The defence fails because a mediator has to own something to mediate with. An object that neither hears nor sees nor benefits cannot carry a request upward, and the Book's alternative is the one Abraham states in this verse: seek provision from God, worship Him, and give thanks to Him. The instruction removes the middle layer entirely and leaves the creature addressing his creator without an apparatus. Abraham is not denying the existence of a distant God; he is demolishing the office of the intermediaries his people have installed.

**Gratitude as the Only Honest Response**

The command to be grateful is placed at the exact point where the argument about provision ends, and the placement is deliberate. Gratitude is the proper reaction to a gift that has already been received, and it is also the test of whether the recipient understands where the gift came from. The Book pairs thanks with worship in one breath when it tells believers to eat from the good things provided for them and to give thanks to God if it is Him they truly worship (2:172). Ingratitude, by contrast, is presented as the characteristically human failure: people remember God when a need presses and forget Him when the pressure lifts. Abraham's three imperatives — seek, worship, thank — describe a life ordered around a single provider, and the gratitude is what keeps the order honest.

**Provision as a Subject of Worship**

The Book joins the supply of livelihood to the practice of worship in a way that is easy to pass over, and one verse states the connection with unusual directness: the Prophet is told to command his people to pray and to be constant in it, and then to say that God asks no provision of him, since it is God who provides for him, with the final outcome belonging to the righteous (20:132). The order in that sentence is the same as in the verse under discussion: the command to worship comes first, and the question of supply is answered by the One giving the command. Abraham's instruction to his people was that they seek provision from God and give thanks to Him, and the placement of the injunction inside a missionary speech tells the reader that the argument about food was, from the first, an argument about worship.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:18

> If you ˹Meccans˺ persist in denial, so did ˹many˺ communities before you. The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly.”

**A Sentence That Reads Two Ways**

This line can sit inside Abraham's address to his people, or it can be read as a break in the narrative in which the Book turns to address its own audience with a word of consolation. The commentators leave both possibilities open, and each reading gives the sentence a different speaker without changing what it asserts. In the first reading, Abraham finishes his case by telling his town that denying him will not make him the first rejected messenger, and that the man who speaks to them is responsible for the message rather than for their acceptance. In the second reading, the Prophet is told that the rejection he is meeting has a long history and that the assignment given to him was never the production of belief in other people's hearts. The shared content of the two readings is the principle that makes the sentence worth recording.

**The Messenger's Job Description**

The Book repeats the job description with unusual frequency, as if the community of preachers needed to hear it until it became reflex. The Prophet is told that if his opponents turn away, his duty is only to deliver the message, while God sees everything His servants do (3:20). In another chapter the same sentence is attached to the warning that the messenger is responsible for his duty while the audience is responsible for theirs (24:54), and it recurs in Sūrah Yā Sīn in the mouth of the messengers themselves, who add that their obligation is limited to clear delivery (36:17). Sūrah al-Talāq and Sūrah al-Shūrā state it again, and Sūrah al-Māʾidah puts the point with a divine name attached: the messenger's duty is delivery, and God fully knows what people reveal and what they conceal (5:99). The recurrence is the Book's way of vaccinating its preachers against despair.

**Delivery That Is Actually Clear**

The qualification at the end of the sentence does real work, since it prevents the exemption from becoming an excuse. What is asked of a messenger is not the mere production of sound; it is clear delivery, which the Arabic adjective covers in both senses: the message must be intelligible in its wording and it must get through to the person addressed. A confused presentation, a hidden text, or a claim announced to nobody does not satisfy the description. The Book's own messengers argue at length with their towns, answer objections, appeal to evidence, and adapt their methods, as Noah does when he moves between public and private address. The assignment excludes responsibility for results; it does not exclude effort in the telling, and the sūrah has just shown Abraham doing that work in his own city.

**The Deniers Who Came Before**

The sentence also supplies a precedent, and the Book uses the same move elsewhere when it gives a voice to the polytheists' argument that nothing would have been worshipped besides God had He not willed it, and answers that communities earlier than them made the same argument, then asks the pointed question of what the messengers' duty is if not clear delivery (16:35). Every generation in the Book believes its resistance is a novel and reasonable position, and every generation is shown that the reasoning has an ancestry. The comfort this gives a preacher is not sentimental. It is the comfort of a man who discovers that his difficulty has been experienced, described, and answered by people who worked in the same field before him, and the Book preserves those answers so that the discovery can be made in time.

The precedent also protects the audience from a particular illusion about their own moment. Every community in the Book treats its resistance as a considered response to a specific nuisance, and the Book's histories show that this reading did not survive the events that followed in every single case. The men of Makkah who dismissed the recitation as a curiosity could travel north and look at settlements that had dismissed similar messages, and the sūrah has already directed them to do exactly that. A person who has been shown that his reaction has a history is being told, gently, that he is not the first to hold the position and that the position has never carried a guarantee. The verse supplies the precedent before the judgment because the precedent is the last piece of evidence available to someone who has set aside the arguments.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:19

> Have they not seen how Allah originates the creation then resurrects it? That is certainly easy for Allah

**An Argument Built From What the Deniers Already Concede**

The question does not begin by asking whether God can raise the dead. It begins with the fact of creation, which the audience already affirms, and then moves from that fact to the conclusion it wants them to accept. This is the Book's standard method in disputes about the resurrection: find the premise both sides hold, and show that it already contains what the denier is refusing. The pagans of Makkah did not deny that God made the heavens and the earth; they assigned Him that role and then added partners to Him. Having granted the first creation, they had no ground left for doubting the second, and the sentence presses exactly there. The question in its opening words is not rhetorical decoration; it identifies a piece of evidence that the listeners can verify with their own eyes every day of their lives.

The verse also assumes that the audience has been looking at that evidence without drawing anything from it. The Arabic verb for seeing in this construction carries the sense of considering rather than merely perceiving, and the Book uses it when it wants to distinguish an observation that has been registered from one that has been thought about. A farmer in the Hijaz watched his fields come up after rain every year, and a herdsman watched calves and lambs arrive in season, and both of them could describe the process in detail without ever asking what it implied about their own end. The sentence is addressed to people in that position, and it treats their failure as a failure of inference rather than of observation. What follows from the evidence is not complicated: a maker who produced the first instance of a thing has demonstrated the only capacity the second instance requires. The sūrah presses the point without elaborating the biology, because the argument needs no more data than the audience already possessed.

**One Act, Two Occurrences**

The Arabic of the sentence describes God originating the creation and then bringing it back, and the Book repeats the pairing so often that it functions as a formula. He originates the creation and then resurrects it, which is even easier for Him (30:27), and the same sequence is given as the reason why the return to Him is a promise that cannot fail: He originates the creation, then resurrects it, so that He may justly reward those who believe and act well (10:4). The logic of the comparison is simple and airtight. If an agent can produce a thing from nothing, producing it a second time from existing material cannot be the harder operation. The Book chooses the easier word deliberately, since the objection it is answering is always a claim about difficulty, and it answers that claim by removing every possible ground for it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Travel throughout the land and see how He originated the creation, then Allah will bring it into being one more time. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything

**An Instruction to Walk and Look**

The verse does not hand its audience a doctrine to be accepted on authority; it sends them out of doors. The command to travel and observe fixes the kind of knowledge at stake, because the object of the investigation is not a report about the past but a process still running in every field and valley the traveler crosses. The Book's confidence in this method is remarkable, since it stakes a claim about the resurrection on what people can see for themselves in the ordinary world. Nothing in the sentence isolates religious knowledge from experience or sets the two at odds. What is being asked for is attention rather than assent, and the sentence assumes that a person who looks at the world with the question in mind will find that the question has been answered before he arrived.

**The Two Kinds of Journey in the Book**

Journeys in the Qur'an have two destinations, and both serve the same purpose. One journey leads the traveler to the ruins of destroyed communities so that he may see what became of people who denied the messengers — the Book issues that command in several chapters, sending people to look at the fate of the deniers (3:137; 6:11; 16:36; 27:69; 30:42). The other journey takes him out to examine the works of the Creator, which is the instruction given here. The two are complementary rather than opposed: one teaches what happens to the message that is refused, the other teaches who stands behind the message in the first place. Sūrah al-Rūm gives the remarkable observation that the earlier peoples were superior in strength, cultivated more land than the Meccans ever did, and came to their messengers with clear proofs (30:9) — the evidence of achievement is not an argument against judgment.

**What the Traveler Is Supposed to Notice**

The Book does not leave the observation vague. The worlds it points to include the renewal of dead ground by rainfall, the variety of plants and creatures that appear in different regions, and the emergence of human beings themselves from a beginning that no one witnessed. Commentators take the phrase "how He originated the creation" to cover the whole sequence by which life appears where there was none, which is exactly the phenomenon the resurrection repeats on a different scale. The traveler who walks with this question discovers that he is standing in the middle of a demonstration. Attention is the price of the knowledge; the facts are free and available to anyone who has the patience to look, and the Book regularly contrasts the person who sees with the person who merely passes through the same landscape.

The command to travel is also a command about company, since a journey puts a person among people whose ways of living he has not seen. The Book sends its audience out of the familiar circle of their own town and its inherited assumptions, and it does so at a point in the argument where the audience has been relying on the practices of its ancestors as a reason for its position. A traveler who moves through other communities observes how they build, plant, cook, bury their dead, and organize their markets, and he discovers that a great deal of what he had taken to be necessary is merely local. The evidence for the resurrection is universal rather than tribal, and the Book's instruction to walk the earth quietly removes the excuse of not having encountered the question before. What the traveler encounters in every direction is the same fact, which is why the verse can promise that the observation will settle the matter for him.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:21

> He punishes whoever He wills, and shows mercy to whoever He wills. And you will ˹all˺ be returned to Him

**What the Attribution to Will Does Not Mean**

A reader may hear the phrase "whoever He wills" as a description of arbitrary power, and the rest of the Book prevents that reading. The sayings collected outside the Qur'an report the divine statement that mercy prevails over wrath, and the Book itself declares that God has prescribed mercy for Himself and that His mercy encompasses all things (7:156). Punishment in the Qur'an is consistently attached to deeds, and the passages that attribute it to God's will never present it as random. Commentators explain the difference in the two clauses by noting that mercy is named in the Book as that which God has taken upon Himself as an obligation, while punishment is described as the consequence that follows when people insist on the course that produces it. The will in the sentence is therefore not the will of a capricious ruler but the will of a sovereign who has published His policy.

**The Return That Gives the Sentence Its Weight**

The verse closes with the same destination named in the previous two, and the repetition serves the argument. Punishment and mercy are not abstractions distributed from a distance; they are received at a meeting. The Book's standard formula is that people return to God and are then informed of what they did, and here the return is stated without the explanation attached, as though the audience already knows the procedure. What the short clause accomplishes is a shift from speculation to expectation. A person may argue about the attributes of God in the abstract, but the sentence places him on a road that ends in his own arrival, and the argument becomes a matter of preparation rather than definition.

The return also makes the sentence a statement about direction rather than distance. A person may travel far from the community he was born into, and the verse's claim is that the road ends in the same place for everyone, which is what makes the warning relevant to hearers who assumed that leaving Makkah settled the matter.

**The Same Pairing in the Book's Warnings**

The pairing used here appears whenever the Book describes the end of a community or a life. Mercy and punishment are named together in the passages about the destruction of earlier peoples, in the descriptions of reward and loss in the Hereafter, and in the closing lines of sūrah after sūrah. What the repetition establishes is that the Book has no interest in a single-sided portrait of the divine. A God who is only severe cannot be trusted with the weak, and a God who is only indulgent cannot be trusted with justice. The attributes are held together in this sentence the way the Book holds them everywhere: the same authority that raised the creation governs its outcome, and the two outcomes are distributed by a will that has already explained itself. A reader who wants a single rule for the arrangement will not find one, and the absence is deliberate, since a rule would let a person calculate with God instead of turning to Him. What the Book supplies instead is a description of the One who decides, and it anchors the claim about mercy in the declaration that His mercy encompasses everything (7:156), a statement broad enough to keep hope alive in the worst case and specific enough to be attached to deeds in the clause that follows.

**Mercy Described as an Obligation Taken On**

The Book avoids leaving mercy as an inference from God's character, and it states that He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful before it says anything about the accounting that follows (6:12). The same sureness runs through the two announcements placed side by side in another chapter, where the Prophet is told to inform God's servants that He is the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful, and that His torment is the most painful (15:49–50). The pairing is the Book's standard way of presenting the two outcomes: neither is an accident of temperament, and both are announced in advance so that a person can order his life accordingly. The verse under discussion uses the same structure, and it leaves both clauses standing without softening either one.

The two clauses also hold each other in check. A reader who leans on mercy alone loses the urgency the verse has just established, and a reader who fixes on punishment alone loses the invitation that follows, so the sentence leaves neither error available to him.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:22

> And you cannot escape Him on earth or in heaven. Nor have you any protector or helper besides Allah.”

**A Denial That Closes Both Directions**

The sentence removes every route of escape by naming the two regions that exhaust the geography available to a human being or an angel. There is no hiding place on earth and no exit into the sky; the Book states the same impossibility when it tells its audience that they can never escape God on earth and have no protector or helper besides Him (42:31), and the jinn themselves affirm that they know they cannot frustrate God on earth nor escape from Him into heaven (72:12). The wording is not a threat about capture but a description of the structure of existence. Anything that exists exists within the domain of its maker, and the idea of fleeing to a place outside that domain turns out to be an idea with no address. The Book strips the fantasy in a single line.

**Protector and Helper, Two Different Offices**

The pair of terms at the end of the verse names two distinct things. A protector is one who takes charge of a person, guards what belongs to him, and stands between him and harm; the word carries the sense of nearness and responsibility. A helper is one who advances a person's cause, lending strength so that a claim may succeed. The Book denies that either office exists besides God, stating that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Him alone and that people have no guardian or helper besides Him (2:107), and repeating the denial with the added note that He alone gives life and causes death (9:116). The denial is aimed at the two practical uses to which people put their gods: protection from harm and assistance toward advantage. Neither service is available from any source but the One being addressed.

**The Patronage the Sentence Takes Away**

For a believer under pressure, the practical temptation is not to worship a stone; it is to look for a patron. The powerful of Makkah could offer protection, a well-placed tribe could offer leverage, and a compromise could purchase safety. The sentence removes that entire market in one stroke, and it does so at the point in the chapter where the reader most needs the reassurance that follows from it. If no protector exists besides God, then no enemy can be protected against Him, and nobody who is held by Him can be lost through the incompetence of an ally. The verse's apparent severity is therefore a form of security. It tells the believer that the only power in the universe capable of helping him is one that cannot be alienated by a stronger party.

The removal is stated as a consequence rather than as a punishment. A person who has placed his confidence in patrons discovers that the relationship was never reciprocal, and the verse's earlier question about protection becomes, in this sentence, the loss of the assumption the question rested on.

**The Question Put to Those Who Plot**

The Book does not rely on the impossibility of escape as a bare fact to be accepted; it turns the fact into a question and leaves it with the people it concerns. The formulation in another chapter asks those who devise evil plots whether they feel secure that God will not cause the earth to swallow them, or that the torment will not come upon them in ways they cannot comprehend (16:45). The phrase about ways they cannot comprehend is the answer to the confidence of a clever man. Plots are designed against known dangers, and the Book's warning is that the arrival will not be on the list the plotter prepared. The verse under discussion supplies the geography of that impossibility; the question supplies the practical consequence for anyone still deciding how much confidence his arrangements deserve.

Putting the matter as a question also leaves the plotters to answer it themselves. The Book has used the same technique with the town's prosperity and with its rainfall, and each time the audience is handed the premise of an argument it would rather not finish.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:23

> As for those who disbelieve in Allah’s signs and the meeting with Him, it is they who will have no hope in His mercy. And it is they who will suffer a painful punishment

**The Hope They Gave Up**

The judgement here concerns hope, which the Book uses as a spiritual diagnostic. The sons of Jacob are told to search for Joseph and his brother and never to lose hope in God's mercy, because nobody loses hope in God's mercy except people with no faith (12:87). The verse under discussion presents that condition as settled: those who have rejected the signs and the meeting no longer expect mercy at all, and their punishment follows. The Book's other statements about mercy rule out the idea that this despair reflects a shortage on God's side. God declares that His mercy encompasses everything (7:156), so the emptiness described here is produced by the people who stopped expecting anything, not by a limit on what was available to them.

The Book's portrait of this condition gains detail from the way it is reached. Nobody decides one morning to abandon hope in God; the process described across the sūrah is gradual and consists of small refusals. A person dismisses a warning as an old story, then treats the messenger as an irritant, then finds the whole subject embarrassing in the company of his peers. Each step makes the next easier, and the end of the sequence is a settled inability to expect anything from God at all. The verse names the terminus rather than the road, which is why its verdict reads as a description of a state rather than a punishment imposed from outside. What the reader is shown is what the process produces when it has run its course, and the sūrah supplies the road in the verses that surround it.

**Denial Is Not the Same as Failure**

A line has to be drawn, because the verdict stated here concerns disbelief rather than sin. The Book addresses people who have sinned repeatedly with a command not to despair of God's mercy and a promise that He forgives all sins (39:53), and it tells those who believed, emigrated, and struggled that they may hope for God's mercy and that He is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful (2:218). Hope, in the Book's vocabulary, is available to the person who is still turning. What closes it is the settled refusal of the signs and of the meeting, which is a position rather than a lapse. The verse therefore condemns a stance, and it leaves the space around the stance unoccupied, so that nobody standing nearby concludes that the door has already shut behind him.

That distinction also protects a reader who has erred from concluding that his case is closed. Failure can be repaired within a lifetime, while the condition described here is a decision already made and left standing, and the difference between the two is what the closing sentence of the passage goes on to address.

**The Door Left Open for Whoever Turns**

The Book states what happens when the two denials named in this verse are retracted, and it states it in terms that leave no room for doubt: those who repent, believe, and do good works are the ones whose evil deeds God changes into good deeds, since He is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful (25:70). The transformation described is stronger than forgiveness, because the record itself is rewritten. Set beside the verdict of this verse, the promise defines the boundary between the two outcomes: what separates them is not the size of the offences but the direction a person is facing at the end. The sūrah will finish with a scene in which the town of Abraham answers an argument with fire, and it places this warning about despairing of mercy immediately before it, so that the reader meets the severity of the passage with the Book's wider invitation in view. The placement also fixes the standard the community is to apply to its own members: the door of return is never announced as closed, and the Book reserves the description in this verse for the people who have themselves stopped expecting anything. What the reader is meant to carry away is not a list of who may be excluded, but the recognition that the two things under discussion — evidence and accountability — are the two things he still has.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24

> But the only response of Abraham’s people was to say: “Kill him or burn him!” But Allah saved him from the fire. Surely in this are signs for people who believe

**A Town With Nothing Left to Say**

The verse reports the town's reply and marks it as the only reply they had. Abraham had laid out an argument in the previous verses: the idols they worshiped could not see, hear, provide, or benefit them, and the One who made them was the One to be worshiped. The Book records no attempt by the townspeople to answer any part of that case. What they produce instead is a decision about the man, and the decision is to remove him. This pattern recurs throughout the Book and is treated as a recognizable signal, since a position that cannot be defended in argument is defended by force. The sentence is therefore a verdict on the debate as much as on the men: the case was closed, and violence is what the losing side had left.

**The Two Options They Offered**

"Kill him or burn him" — the alternatives move from the ordinary to the spectacular. The Book fills in the scene in other chapters. In Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ the conclusion is recorded as an appeal to burn him to avenge their gods, if they must act at all (21:68), and in Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt they are described building a furnace and casting him into the blazing fire (37:97). The language of vengeance tells the reader what the idols meant to their worshipers: an insult to a god was a public injury, and the community's honour was at stake in the response. The Book is precise about the motive because the motive explains the scale. This was not a judicial proceeding against a heretic; it was the defence of a shrine, and the shrine could not defend itself.

The choice between the two methods is also a measure of how the debate had concluded. A killing would have been an act of private vengeance and could be argued about afterwards; a public burning was a demonstration, staged in front of the community, addressed to the gods as much as to the man. Commentators note that the town resolved the matter by consensus, which is the last resource of a society that has exhausted its reasoning. The sūrah has been tracking collective decisions throughout its narratives: a market that agreed on its scales, tribes that agreed to treat their messengers with contempt, a court that agreed to build a tower. In each case the agreement is presented as the crime, since it implicates the community in a decision no individual would have needed to defend alone. Abraham's town reached the same point, and the fire was the form its agreement took.

**The Fire That Was Ordered to Be Cool**

The rescue is reported in the fewest possible words here — God saved him from the fire — and expanded elsewhere with a command that has no parallel in the Book: the fire is addressed directly and told to be cool and safe for Abraham (21:69). The created element obeys its maker rather than its nature, which is the point the sentence makes about God's authority over the physical world. What burned everything else became, by decree, temperature and safety for one man. The Book's narration is careful not to describe Abraham performing a miracle of his own; he is the passive recipient of the act. When the same scene appears again, the outcome is stated in the plotters rather than the plot: they sought to harm him, and they were made the worst losers (21:70).

**The Same Scene in Another Sūrah**

Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ gives the scene with its dialogue intact: the people call for the fire to avenge their gods, God commands the fire to be cool and safe for Abraham, and the verse that follows reports that they sought to harm him and were made the worst losers (21:68–70). Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt supplies the means — a furnace prepared for the purpose — and the same conclusion that the plotters came out inferior (37:97–98). Three chapters recount one attempt from three angles, and none of them describes Abraham negotiating, defending himself, or escaping. The pattern is consistent with everything the sūrah has said about trials: the servant endures, God acts, and the outcome is recorded in a way that reminds the reader whose story it actually is. The town that could not answer a single argument is remembered for what it tried to do with fire, and the man it condemned is remembered as the one delivered from it. That is a lesson about the relative weight of arguments and force, and the sūrah has arranged for its readers to see both endings at once. The fire that was intended to close the argument became the evidence that settled it, and the town that demanded a demonstration received one it could not survive.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:25

> He said ˹to his people˺, “You have taken idols ˹for worship˺ instead of Allah, only to keep ˹the bond of˺ harmony among yourselves in this worldly life. But on the Day of Judgment you will disown and curse one another. Your home will be the Fire, and you will have no helper!”

**Religion as an Arrangement for Living Together**

Read this way, the verse illuminates a theme the chapter has been developing since its opening. The disbelievers of Makkah had offered the believers a bargain in which following the ancestral way would come with the burden of sin carried by others (29:12), and the waverer had claimed a share in the community whenever it profited him (29:10). Both cases show religion operating as a social arrangement rather than a conviction. Abraham's diagnosis goes to the same place: the gods of his people were the terms on which a town agreed to live with itself, and the harmony was the glue. He announces that the arrangement belongs to a life that ends, so the partners to it will not be able to enforce its terms when the life is over.

**The Day the Partners Fall Out**

The Book describes the collapse elsewhere with precision. Those who misled others will disown their followers on the Day when they face the punishment, and the bonds that united them will be cut (2:166); the followers will cry out that they wish for a second chance so they could disown their leaders as they themselves were disowned (2:167). A summary statement gives the rule in one line: close friends will be enemies on that Day, except the righteous (43:67). Affection that organized a society becomes the medium of mutual accusation once the society is finished. Abraham's announcement therefore concerns the future of a social world, and it carries the force of a prediction: the bond his people cannot imagine breaking has an appointment to break itself.

The scene also answers anyone who assumed that a shared cult guaranteed a shared loyalty. The Book picks the worst possible moment for the partners to turn on each other, which is the moment when loyalty would matter, and it stages the failure inside the Hereafter, where the consequences become visible to both sides at once.

**A Home and No Helper**

The sentence closes with the two terms the chapter used two verses earlier, and the echo is exact. A person has no protector and no helper besides God (29:22); the people who installed other gods in His place arrive at a fire with no helper at all. The Book is showing what happens when a denied fact becomes an experienced one. What they treated as their protection turns out to be their destination, and the allies they gathered in this world are absent from the scene. In both verses the vocabulary comes from the language of patronage and shelter, which is the currency in which the town of Abraham, like the town of the Prophet, conducted its business.

**The Friendship That Survives the Day**

The exception attached to the companion verse returns to the subject from the other side: close friends will be enemies on that Day, except the righteous (43:67). The Book does not conclude from the wreckage of corrupt friendship that all bonds dissolve. It concludes that bonds endure in proportion to what they were built on, and the sūrah will shortly report what became of Abraham's household after he left. An affection organized around idols is an agreement with an expiry date; an affection organized around God is not. The verse under discussion supplies the first half of that contrast, and the events that follow in the chapter supply the second.

The exception makes the passage more than a warning. A promise is placed inside the same speech so that the reader compares the two arrangements, and the bond that holds when the others fail is named: loyalty built on a shared submission rather than on a shared cult.

**Why a Manufactured Unity Always Comes Apart**

The Book sets one account of unity beside another and lets the reader compare them. The harmony Abraham describes was assembled around objects, and it lasted exactly as long as the interests that held it together. Against it the Book places the brotherhood it asks of believers, which it grounds in something else entirely: believers are told to hold firmly to the rope of God and not to be divided, and to remember that they were enemies until God united their hearts, becoming brothers by His grace, at the brink of a pit from which He saved them (3:103). The difference between the two arrangements is not the warmth of the participants. It is the foundation, and the Book's argument in this verse is that a foundation made of stone cannot hold a community together past the life that produced it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:26

> So Lot believed in him. And Abraham said, “I am emigrating ˹in obedience˺ to my Lord. He ˹alone˺ is indeed the Almighty, All-Wise.”

**The First Follower Named**

Lot's acceptance of Abraham's message is reported in three words, and the brevity is part of the point. Nothing is recorded about the arguments that convinced him or the doubts he weighed; the Book simply states that he believed the man standing beside him. Lot is identified elsewhere as a relative of Abraham, and the family connection makes the sentence more valuable rather than less, since the first person to accept a prophet is usually the one who has watched him most closely. The Book's habit of naming the early believers in its narratives — a wife, a nephew, a household — gives the reader a picture of the movement at its beginning: not a crowd persuaded by spectacle but a handful of people near enough to see what kind of man the messenger was. The verse's restraint is also a form of respect for Lot: he is given no speech here, only the act of believing, which is the whole of what the account requires of him at this point.

**Emigration as an Act of Worship**

The word the verse uses places the journey among the things a person does for God rather than among the things that are done to him. Commentators treat this as the first migration in the Book, and the pattern it establishes recurs in the lives of the prophets and in the experience of the first Muslim community in Makkah. Sūrah Maryam records the consequence in the same order the story requires: after he had left them and what they worshiped besides God, God granted him Isaac and Jacob, and made each of them a prophet (19:49). The reward in that verse is attached to the departure, not to the argument, and the connection is not accidental. Obedience that costs a person his city is the kind the Book treats as a test passed at a different level from the debates that preceded it.

The framing also gives the journey a purpose beyond survival. A family that moves only to avoid harm is driven by its enemies, while a family that moves for the sake of worship is carrying out an instruction, and the sūrah's own command to the believers in Makkah rests on the same distinction.

**What the Book Promises the Migrant**

The Book generalizes the promise that Abraham's decision implies: whoever emigrates in the cause of God will find many safe havens and abundant resources across the earth, and the one who leaves his home and dies while emigrating has his reward already secured with God, who is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful (4:100). Sūrah al-Naḥl describes the same people as those who emigrated after being persecuted, and promises them a good home in this world and a better reward in the next, naming patient endurance and trust in their Lord as their distinguishing marks (16:41–42). The Book never presents departure as a solution available in every case; the earlier reports in this sūrah acknowledge believers who were genuinely unable to leave. What it presents is a decision with an attached guarantee, and Abraham is the first to make it. The terms are stated in the plainest things the Book has at its disposal — shelter, provision, a reward that cannot be lost — which is exactly what a person weighing such a step needs to hear before he takes it.

**What Was Carried and What Was Left**

A migration is always a subtraction as well. Abraham walked out of a city where he had a father, a household, and a place in the argument, and the Book elsewhere places the wife of Lot among the examples of a house divided on the question of belief, naming her alongside the wife of Noah as a case of betrayal inside a prophet's own family (66:10). The example establishes that kinship does not carry faith with it, and this verse shows the same rule from the side of the man who leaves. He carried out of the town a decision and a nephew; what he left behind included people who could not be brought with him, and the Book records the loss in the same narrative as the promise, without softening either one. A reader who wants the reward of obedience delivered without subtraction will not find that arrangement anywhere in this chapter.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:27

> We blessed him with Isaac and ˹later˺ Jacob, and reserved prophethood and revelation for his descendants. We gave him his reward in this life, and in the Hereafter he will certainly be among the righteous

**Sons Described as Blessings, Not Wages**

The account of Abraham's departure is followed immediately by news of his family, and the Book presents the children as gifts rather than as payment. The wording matters, because the reader has just watched a man lose his town for the sake of God, and the risk of reading the next line as compensation is real. The Book says he was blessed with Isaac and later with Jacob, and its other accounts supply the same framing: the angels who came to him gave good news of a knowledgeable son and then said that this was what his Lord had decreed, adding that He is All-Wise and All-Knowing (51:28, 51:30). The birth announcements in the Book are always delivered in that register. What God gives is given out of generosity, and the man who receives it does not hold an invoice.

**Prophethood Reserved in the Line**

The clause that follows states the greater of the two gifts: prophethood and revelation were placed among Abraham's descendants. The Book elsewhere lists the line that carried this trust — Isaac and Jacob, then David, Solomon, Job, Joseph, Moses, and Aaron, all guided as Noah was guided, with the comment that this is how God rewards those who do good (6:84). Sūrah Maryam connects the two incidents directly, recording that after Abraham had left his people and what they worshiped, God granted him Isaac and Jacob and made each of them a prophet (19:49). The sequence makes a claim about how God answers a man's sacrifice: what He gives in return is not comfort but continuation, and the continuation is not property or power but the delivery of His message to later generations.

The Book's interest is in the arrangement rather than in the individuals who filled the offices. Prophethood is given where God chooses, and the fact that most messengers came from a single line is a statement about how guidance reached the world rather than a claim about the merits of a family.

**The Reward Granted Before Death**

The verse says the reward was given in this world as well as the next, and the commentators take that to cover the honours that were visible within Abraham's lifetime: guidance, acceptance, a good name, and the community of those who followed his way. The Book names one of those honours explicitly when it reports that after Abraham had fulfilled the commands given to him, God declared that He would make him a model for all people (2:124). A model is a position established in the world, not stored for later.

**A Lineage That Did Not Guarantee Anything**

The promise of prophethood in Abraham's descendants is not a statement about every person descended from him, and the Book makes that distinction elsewhere with care. When Abraham asked about his offspring, the answer he received was that the covenant would not extend to the wrongdoers (2:124), and another passage reports that both Abraham and Isaac were blessed while some of their descendants did good and others clearly wronged themselves (37:113). The gift described in this verse is therefore a provision for the line rather than a standing exemption for its members, and the Book treats the two as entirely different things. What Abraham was given was the means by which communities would be reached; what each of his descendants did with that inheritance was decided one person at a time.

**The Same Family Gift in Sūrah al-Anʿām**

Sūrah al-Anʿām gives the family blessing in its widest form, naming Isaac and Jacob and then listing the descendants through whom guidance continued, with the brief verdict that this is how God rewards those who do good (6:84). The list is long enough to make clear that the promise to Abraham was not the elevation of one household for its own sake. Through the line came prophets, scripture, and communities, and the Book treats the whole chain as the fruit of a single man's decision to leave a city in obedience. That is the sense in which the reward of this verse is given in this world: before any Hereafter, the goods of revelation were distributed among nations that Abraham would never see, and the account of them is credited to the man who walked out of Ḥarrān with a nephew and a sentence about his Lord. The verse therefore records two kinds of gift in one breath: children who would carry the message, and a name that would be honoured by the communities those children produced.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:28

> And ˹remember˺ when Lot rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people: “You certainly commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before you

**The Word Behind Shameful Deed**

Arabic has a term for acts that are ugly in themselves, and the Book uses it for serious sexual transgression more than for anything else. The word signals that the deed is repugnant to a person's own nature before any law is applied to it, which is why the Book can appeal to shame in a prophet's rebuke. Commentators describe the act in view as relations between men, and the Book states the charge without ambiguity in several chapters, noting that the men preferred other men over the wives that God had created for them (26:165–166), and describing the practice as transgression and ignorance (7:81; 27:55). The vocabulary of the Book at this point is direct and legal in tone rather than suggestive, and it attaches the offence to a violation of the order God established for the sexes.

The word also places the offence inside a category the audience already recognized. The Book uses the same vocabulary for sexual wrongdoing wherever it appears, and the effect is to remove any suggestion that the town's conduct was a local custom to be assessed on its own terms.

**The Difficulty of a Messenger Who Lives There**

Lot's position deserves a moment. He is not an outsider arriving with a warning; he lives in the place, his household is in it, and the men he addresses know him. The Book records his distress when the danger approaches his guests and the humiliation he fears from his own people (11:78), and it records their blunt reply that they have no need of his daughters and that he knows what they want (11:79). A prophet in his own town faces the hardest version of the assignment, because the audience knows his past and the message has to be carried without the authority that distance lends. That is the setting in which he says the sentence under discussion, and the Book presents him as a man doing his duty in a place that had run out of shame long before he spoke.

Living among them also shaped how the warning had to be delivered. A man who eats with his neighbours and trades in their market cannot address them as a stranger would, and the Book's account preserves a tone belonging to someone speaking to people he has known all his life.

**The Book's Category for Sexual Transgression**

The term used here for the shameful deed belongs to the Book's standard vocabulary for sexual offences, and its other uses show how the category is drawn. The Book tells believers not to go near adultery, describing it as truly a shameful deed and an evil way (17:32), and it uses the same word for the acts of Lot's people in the parallel accounts. The choice of term is significant for the argument of the verse. The Book's charge is not that the town broke a local custom or offended its neighbours; it is that the practice belongs to a class of acts that the Book treats as wrong in themselves, and the rebuke opens with the general category before it names the particular offence.

The category also explains why the Book reports the offence before it reports the sentence. What follows in the sūrah is a judgment, and that judgment becomes intelligible only once the reader understands what kind of act the town had adopted as a public practice.

**A Rebuke Repeated in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf**

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf reports the same moment in the same terms: Lot scolds the men of his people and asks whether they commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before, then names the preference for men over women and calls them transgressors (7:80–81). Two chapters preserve two versions of one speech, which is the Book's standard method of establishing that a warning was delivered and that its content is reliable. The repetition also protects the reader from treating the story as a curiosity of the ancient world. What the chapters report is a community that was reached, warned in plain language, and given time, and the rebuke that opens the account is the evidence that the warning was not vague. A messenger who itemizes the practices of his people has removed every possibility of a misunderstanding, and the Book records the itemization to close that door permanently.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29

> Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.”

**Three Charges in a Single Question**

Lot's rebuke now names three practices, and the list widens from a sexual offence to the conduct of a whole town. The first charge states the desire; the second concerns travellers; the third concerns what the people do in their own assemblies. Read as a sequence, the three describe a community that has damaged its members, its visitors, and its public life, and the Book presents them as parts of one condition rather than as unrelated vices. This is the chapter's most detailed portrait of a corrupt town before the narrative moves on to the fates of the earlier peoples, and the specificity is deliberate: the warning given to Sodom was about identifiable behaviour, not about a general atmosphere of sin that nobody could have been expected to recognize.

The question reduces the town's practice to three complaints, and each of them concerns conduct a visitor would notice within a day. Nothing in the charge depends on private belief or hidden intention, which is why the verse presents the case as something the audience already knew and had stopped objecting to.

**An Answer That Argues Nothing**

The town's reply contains no rebuttal. Instead the people demand that the punishment be brought forward if the warning is true, and the Book marks the tone as mockery. The same challenge is placed in the mouths of earlier communities: the people of ʿĀd tell their prophet to bring down what he threatens if he is truthful (46:22), and the people of Thamūd make the same demand of Ṣāliḥ. The Book treats the formula as a signature of a community that has stopped taking the warning seriously and wants an end to the preaching. It is also a confession of sorts, since the demand concedes that a punishment exists and that only its timing is in question. What the speakers ask for is a delay they believe will never expire.

What the sentence lacks is any attempt at justification. The townspeople do not deny the practices, cite a precedent, or claim the reports were exaggerated; they ask for punishment instead, which is the response of people who have run out of arguments and are relying on the weight of their position in the town.

**The Same Challenge in Sūrah al-Aḥqāf**

Sūrah al-Aḥqāf records the identical demand made to the prophet Hūd: when he warned his people, they challenged him to bring down what he threatened if he were among the truthful (46:22). The Book reports it as a recurring formula in its dialogues, which means the reader should recognize the shape of the sentence rather than treat it as a peculiarity of one town. Communities that have reached the end of their patience with a warning believe they have found a decisive test: if the messenger is right, the punishment will come, and if it never comes, the matter is settled in their favour. The Book's answer to that reasoning is not an argument but a history, and the histories it provides in this chapter are the record of what happened when the challenge was accepted.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:30

> Lot prayed, “My Lord! Help me against the people of corruption.”

**The Meaning of Corruption in the Book's Vocabulary**

The word Lot uses belongs to a root that means to spoil and to ruin, and the Book applies it to a wide range of damage. It is used of violence and bloodshed, of fraud in trade, of the ruin of crops and livestock, and of the general decay that follows a society's departure from what it was set up to be. Sūrah al-Rūm gives the widest statement, that corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned, so that they might taste the consequences of some of their deeds and perhaps return (30:41). The general principle running through these passages is that corruption is not only a private moral failing; it is a public condition whose effects spread beyond the people who produce it, and the petition of this verse names an entire community by that description.

The vocabulary also covers conduct that goes unpunished in a town's own courts. Cutting a road, robbing a caravan, and intimidating a visitor are corruptions of the security a settlement exists to provide, and the Book's word gathers those offences together with the moral disorder that accompanies them.

**Violence Hidden Behind the Language of Peace**

The Book's portraits of corrupters repeatedly show them describing themselves in the opposite terms. When they are told not to spread corruption in the land they answer that they are only peacemakers, and the reply comes back that they are the corrupters and do not perceive it (2:11–12). Elsewhere the same type is described as one who, once he gains power, travels the land to spread mischief and destroy crops and cattle (2:205). The sins named in those passages are concrete injuries to other people's lives and livelihoods, and the account of Sodom in this sūrah fits the category precisely: the town had made its streets unsafe and its guests fair game. Lot's prayer identifies the community by the damage it caused, which is why the request is intelligible even to readers who know nothing about the town's other practices.

The name given to the opponents in this prayer is the one the Book uses for those who break the order of a community from inside it, and its range covers fraud, injustice, and the corruption of worship. That breadth is the reason the prayer is defensible. Lot is not asking for the removal of a tribe he dislikes; he is naming conduct that makes life in the town impossible for anyone who will not join in, and the Book's accounts of the city supply the conduct. The verdict in the following verses is an answer to a statement about behaviour rather than a clash of loyalties.

**A Request for Help, Not a Demand for Punishment**

The wording of the petition is restrained, and the restraint is part of its authority. Lot says "help me against" the people, which is the language of a man who needs support, not of a prosecutor asking for a sentence. The Book's prophets are consistently presented in this posture, and the pattern protects them from the charge of vindictiveness: they do not take the judgment into their own hands and they do not itemize what their opponents deserve. What the prayer asserts is a fact about the situation rather than a wish about the defendants. Lot is powerless against a town that has refused every argument, and he asks the one who is not powerless to take charge of the affair.

The Book's prophets ask for relief rather than for the punishment of their neighbours, and the request is aimed at the trouble rather than at the people causing it. Noah had already used the same posture in an earlier account, asking to be helped rather than answered with fire, and the destruction that follows is reported as God's decision rather than as the prophet's petition granted. What the pattern shows is the limit on a messenger's role: he argues, he warns, he prays, and the verdict remains with the one being petitioned.

**The Same Prayer in the Mouth of Noah**

The Book records the same movement in Noah's career, where the prophet cries out to his Lord that he has been overwhelmed and asks for help (54:10). Two men, several centuries apart, with completely different audiences, produce petitions that could be mistaken for one another. The similarity is one of the Book's ways of showing that the difficulty is structural rather than personal. Every messenger who delivers a message that people reject arrives at the same wall, and the Book supplies the same instrument for getting past it, which is to ask for help rather than to get a hearing by other means. The prayers also mark the transition from the period of warning to the period of consequence, without giving the prophet any role in the transition itself.

Noah's case also shows how long the interval between a prayer and its answer can be. The Book reports his call and then the flood that followed it, with a long ministry of argument in between, so a reader who takes this verse as a demand for immediate action will find the pattern corrected by the earlier story.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:31

> When Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with the good news ˹of the birth of Isaac˺, they said, “We are going to destroy the people of this city ˹of Sodom˺, for its people have persisted in wrongdoing.”

**Angels Who Come as Guests**

The Book introduces these envoys elsewhere as guests who arrive at Abraham's home and whom he hastens to serve (15:51). The detail is remarkable because it places the heaviest sentence in the story in the setting of a meal. When the guests do not eat, their host grows afraid, and they reassure him and announce the birth of a knowledgeable son (51:28). The response of a human being to a supernatural visitation is thus recorded with ordinary texture: fear, hospitality, relief, and only then comprehension. The verses do not present Abraham as a man who read the situation immediately; they present a generous host who was frightened by his guests and then told what they had come for. The destruction of a city arrives in the middle of that domestic scene, which is the Book's way of showing that the two orders of event are administered without ceremony.

**The Grounds Stated for the Destruction**

The envoys do not announce a punishment without giving its basis: the people of the city have persisted in wrongdoing. The Book repeats this structure in the case of every destroyed community, and the pattern is not decorative, since it fixes responsibility for the outcome on the people themselves. When the chapter reports the fates of the earlier nations, it adds the explicit disclaimer that God would not have wronged them but that they wronged themselves (29:40). The word translated as wrongdoing belongs to the vocabulary of transgression and injustice, and the same realities are named in the passage about Lot's own rebuke: the deeds were shameful, they were committed openly, and they continued after repeated warnings. What the envoys announce is the conclusion of a process, not its beginning.

The sentence names the offence in the same breath as the verdict and gives it no elaboration. That brevity sets this account apart from those in which the Book lists the practices of a people at length, and the difference is instructive: where the offences are itemized, the audience is meant to recognize itself among them, and where they are named once, the point is simply that a reason existed.

**Abraham's Plea, Recorded in Another Sūrah**

Sūrah Hūd supplies the dialogue that the present verse anticipates. After the fear had left him and the good news had arrived, Abraham began to plead with God for the people of Lot (11:74), and the answer he received was that the pleading should stop because the decree had already come and the punishment could not be averted (11:76). The intercession is treated with respect rather than rebuked as impertinence; the Book records the plea and the refusal together, which shows that a prophet's appeal on behalf of people he cares about is an act of mercy rather than an error. What the refusal establishes is that intercession does not operate outside the schedule of justice, and that the reputation of a righteous man in a wicked town does not purchase an extension of time.

The habit is not decorative. A destruction reported without a reason would leave the reader with an act of force, and the Book's consistent practice is to name the offence before it reports the sentence, whether the offence is denial, fraud, or the abuse of a community's weakest members. Stating the grounds protects the reader's understanding of God at the same time that it instructs him about conduct. Every ruined settlement in this sūrah arrives with an explanation attached, and this one is no exception.

**The Same Visit in Sūrah Hūd**

Sūrah Hūd gives the encounter at length, with the guests arriving, the calf being served, the fear, the good news, the pleading, and the reply that the decree has come (11:69–76). Reading the two accounts together shows how much detail the Book withholds elsewhere and how much it repeats when the point requires it. The shorter version in this chapter keeps only what the argument needs: envoys, a good news, a doomed city, and the reason for its fate.

The longer report supplies what this verse compresses: the visitors arrive, they greet him with peace, and the meal he sets before them is declined, after which the fear described here takes hold of him. The two accounts are not variants of one another so much as a summary and its setting, and reading them side by side shows that the verse's brevity is a choice rather than a gap. The sūrah keeps the moment at its essential point, which is the arrival of guests whose errand has two parts.

The announcement reaches a man who has just been afraid in his own house, and the Book repeatedly places good news beside the anxiety it dissolves rather than after an interval of calm.

**Why the Grounds Are Always Stated**

The Book rarely announces a destroyed community without adding the reason for its destruction, and the practice has an obvious function: it prevents the reader from treating the event as an exercise of raw power. In this sentence the reason is the persistence of wrongdoing, which places the outcome at the end of a long process rather than at the start of one. The same pattern appears in the chapter's summary of the earlier nations, which will report the forms of seizure and then add that God would not have wronged them but that they wronged themselves (29:40). The consistent statement of grounds serves the reader's own situation as well, since a community that is not facing any such sentence can still examine itself by the same criteria.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:32

> He said, “But Lot is there!” They responded, “We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.”

**Rescue of a Family as a Unit**

The promise that follows names a household: Lot and his family will be saved. The Book does not treat salvation in the narratives as an individual transaction only, and the family of a prophet is gathered with him where the family has taken his side. Another chapter reports the same night from the city's perspective, saying that the believers were evacuated before the punishment and that only one family was found there that had submitted to God (51:35–36). The phrase is stark. A town that organized itself around shame produced one household that belonged to its prophet, and the rescue operation was designed around that single unit. The Book's account of the rescue therefore answers Abraham's question in the affirmative, with a precision that is greater than he had asked for.

**The Exception Stated Before the Escape**

The sentence also names the exception: his wife will not be saved, and she is counted among the doomed. The Book states the exception in every version of the story, and the repetition is deliberate, since the reader might otherwise assume that a prophet's household is automatically delivered. The woman is identified in the narratives as belonging to the people by her sympathies rather than by her marriage, and the accounts of the night add that she looked back or stayed behind when the others left. Sūrah al-Taḥrīm places her alongside the wife of Noah as an example for those who disbelieve, noting that both were married to righteous servants of God and betrayed them, and that the standing of their husbands was of no benefit to them (66:10).

Mentioning the exception in advance also keeps the rescue from being read as a divine miscalculation. The Book records the terms before the event so that a reader who knows how the story ends will not find a stray person inside the sentence, and the same care is visible in the account of Noah's household.

**The Vocabulary of Those Left Behind**

The Arabic word rendered as doomed carries the sense of being left behind or of being covered over, and commentators connect it with dust, both the dust that buried the towns and the dust on faces that the Book describes as belonging to the disgraced on the Day of Judgment (80:40). Whether the connection is taken as etymology or as a deliberate wordplay, the image is the same: the person who remained with the town when the town was finished. The term appears in the other accounts of this night without change (7:83; 15:60; 26:171), and the consistency of the wording suggests that the Book wants this detail fixed in the reader's memory. Being left behind is the fate that the whole narrative warns against.

The word also carries the sense of abandonment rather than of mere absence. Those described are not people who happened to be elsewhere; they are the ones who remained with the conduct after everyone who objected had gone, and the term places them at the scene of the offence.

**The One Household Found in the City**

Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt gives the outcome in two lines: the believers were brought out of the city before the torment, and only one family was found there that had submitted to God (51:35–36). The two sentences measure the town. A place that had developed a settled practice of violence, had defended it in public, and had mocked its warning produced a single household on the side of the messenger, and that household was taken out before the event. The chapter under study records the promise made in advance to Abraham, and the other chapter records its execution in the dark. Together they show the precision the envoys claimed for themselves when they told a worried old man that they knew better than he did who was inside the city.

The size of the exception also answers a complaint the sūrah anticipates. A community may ask whether a message can be true when so few accept it, and the account of a city with one believing household presents the question in its starkest form and leaves it with the answer that truth is not determined by attendance.

**An Accounting That Misses Nobody**

The reply to Abraham rests on a claim about divine knowledge that the Book states in a single line elsewhere: the peoples of the past will be given a full account with sure knowledge, because God was never absent from them (7:7). The envoys' answer in this verse is that same claim applied to one town on one night. Communities are not dealt with as masses whose individual members are indistinguishable; the distinction between a wrongdoer and a believer is made before the sentence is carried out, and the promise to Abraham about his nephew shows the distinction being made in advance of the event.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:33

> And when Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival. They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not fear, nor grieve. We will surely deliver you and your family—except your wife, who is one of the doomed

**The Two Griefs the Reassurance Addresses**

The words spoken to Lot are brief and they cover two distinct conditions: do not fear, and do not grieve. Commentators read the first as directed at the danger that has just walked into his house, and the second as directed at the fate that he already suspects is coming for his people, and for the member of his own household who is part of that people. The Book's reassurances to its prophets often address fear and sorrow as a pair, because the two conditions have different remedies: fear concerns what may happen, and grief concerns what cannot be undone. What the envoys offer is the removal of both, and they attach the offer to something concrete, which is a promise about who will be delivered.

The Book is careful elsewhere to separate a household's rescue from a claim about the beliefs of everyone inside it, and it names the wives of Noah and Lot as examples of people who were joined to prophets by marriage and still ended on the other side of the line (66:10). Nothing in this verse requires the reader to assume that every person carried out of the town shared Lot's convictions; what it reports is the saving of a household, with the exception named in the same sentence. The Book's accounts of families are consistently written that way, and the reader who assumes otherwise will find the exception incomprehensible.

**The Exception Repeated Across the Accounts**

The wife of Lot is named as an exception in every version of the story, and the Book's insistence on the detail has a purpose that is greater than biography. A reader who assumes that proximity to a prophet guarantees safety is being corrected in advance of the disaster, and the correction is made by God's own envoys rather than by an interpreter. Sūrah al-Taḥrīm states the principle directly, citing the wives of Noah and Lot as examples set for those who disbelieve: each was married to a righteous servant of God, each betrayed him, and the standing of the husbands was of no use to them against God (66:10). The exception in this verse is therefore a doctrine about the limits of kinship, delivered on the last night of a city's existence.

Two fears are being answered at once. Lot dreads what his own townspeople will do to guests he cannot protect, and he dreads the ruin of a place where he has lived his whole life among people he knew by name. The reply addresses both without separating them, since it tells him that the visitors are not his responsibility to defend and that the town's fate is already settled. A reader who takes the verse as a report of angelic procedure will miss the human shape of it, which is the shape of a man receiving two kinds of relief in one sentence.

**The Instructions Given in Sūrah Hūd**

Sūrah Hūd completes the scene with the practical orders that belong to the rescue: Lot is told to travel with his family in the dark of night, not to let anyone look back, and that the appointment is the morning, with the question added of whether the morning is not near (11:81). The instructions confirm everything the sūrah's account implies. The family was not delivered by remaining where it was and being shielded; it was delivered by moving, under cover of night, at the cost of leaving home. The sūrah under study keeps the promise and omits the journey, because the chapter's subject is the promise and the pressure that produced it. Taken together, the two accounts give the tested believer the same answer from two directions: the rescue is certain, and it does not come without a departure.

Announcing the outcome before the event is one of the Book's standing practices, and its purpose is visible in the effect it has on the person receiving it. Lot was told that his household would be saved while he was still standing in a room with men he had failed to protect, so that the reassurance arrived before his own eyes could confirm it. The same arrangement appears wherever the Book prepares a believer for a loss he cannot prevent, and it places the comfort on the side of a promise rather than on the side of the visible facts.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:34

> We are certainly bringing down a punishment from heaven upon the people of this city for their rebelliousness.”

**A Sentence Delivered to the Man Inside the City**

The words are spoken to Lot on the night the envoys arrive at his door. He has just been told that his household will be taken out, and the same visitors now describe what will happen to the place he is leaving. The Book places the announcement in the middle of the rescue rather than after it, which means the last night of the city was spent by its one believing household knowing exactly what was coming. Nothing in the account suggests that Lot argued, negotiated, or tried to warn the town a final time.

**The Word Behind the Punishment**

The Arabic term rendered as punishment also carries the senses of defilement and of plague, and the commentators weigh all three. The Book uses the same word for the filth of Satan from which God purifies the believers (8:11), which supports the reading of defilement, and it uses it for the inflictions sent upon the Egyptians, which supports plague. Most commentators take the reference here as the rain of baked clay stones that the other accounts describe falling on the towns (11:82), or the storm of stones thrown at the people of Lot (54:34). The disagreement is not idle: on the second reading the punishment mirrors the crime, since a community that defiled what God had made clean is answered with defilement of its own kind.

The verdict arrives with no interval attached. Lot had been arguing with his town for years, and what the visitors bring is the closure of a dispute already made and refused, which is why the announcement reaches him as a settled matter rather than as a warning to pass along.

**The Morning That Was Already Appointed**

Another chapter supplies the timing. Lot is told to travel with his family in the dark and not to look back, with the exception of his wife, and the messengers add that the appointment is the morning and ask whether the morning is not near (11:81). The question is one of the Book's small, devastating touches: the answer is obvious to everyone except the people who have no idea the appointment exists. The destruction of the town therefore has a time, a cause, and a fixed boundary, and the account of it proceeds with the calm of something scheduled rather than the fury of something sudden. For the reader under pressure in Makkah, the sequence carries a promise about the accuracy of divine appointments in general.

Fixing the morning also rules out an escape under cover of darkness. The visitors give Lot a time rather than an event, which tells him how long the interval will hold and prevents him from treating the announcement as a general prediction, and the same practice appears in the accounts of Noah and of the tribes that were destroyed.

**A Word That Covers Plague, Filth, and Stones**

The Arabic term for the punishment carries a range of senses, and the Book uses it across them. It appears among the inflictions sent upon the Egyptians, where they pleaded with Moses to have the torment removed and pledged to believe and to let the Israelites go (7:134), and it appears in a passage where God sends rain to purify a community and to remove from them the whispers of Satan (8:11). Defilement, plague, and punishment therefore share one word, and the commentators note that the range is not accidental. The Book's judgments are often described in the vocabulary of cleansing, and the sentence against a town that had made a public practice of defilement uses a term that carries the removal of filth within it.

The range of the term is also the reason this sūrah's version of the story can be read without identifying one instrument. The Book leaves the physical description to other chapters and keeps what matters for the argument here, which is that the announcement was made in advance and that the people had heard it.

**The Same Crime and Sentence Elsewhere**

Sūrah al-Ḥijr recounts the same night with the same means — the cities overturned and stones of hardened clay rained upon them — and Sūrah al-Qamar describes a storm of stones sent upon the people of Lot (15:74; 54:34). The Book's repetition of the scene across chapters serves the sūrah's purpose here: a reader following the argument about trial and judgment encounters the same event from several angles, each time with the cause attached. Nothing in any account presents the destruction as an unexplained act of power. Every version names the crime, records the warning, and notes the deliverance of the household that believed, which is precisely the pattern the chapter has been teaching since it asked whether people expect to be left untested.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:35

> And We did leave ˹some of˺ its ruins as a clear lesson for people of understanding

**A Sign Whose Meaning Had To Be Recorded**

A ruin is a poor witness on its own. Walls do not announce whether a town was abandoned because its wells failed, because a trade route shifted, or because a fire swept through, and travelers in every century have supplied their own explanations for the empty settlements they passed. The sign this verse mentions therefore required an interpreter, and the Book supplies the interpretation in the same narrative that records the destruction. That is the practical function of the accounts collected in this chapter: they convert a landscape into a testimony by attaching a cause to an outcome. For the reader who never visits the site, the account is the sign, and its clarity does not depend on archaeological reconstruction. What was preserved for people of understanding was preserved twice, in the ground and in the text, and the second preservation is the one that reaches everyone.

The Book repeatedly directs its listeners toward the ruins of earlier nations, and the sūrah's later verses will say that the dwellings of ʿĀd and Thamūd are evident to the Meccans as they travel. Elsewhere the Book asks whether its audience does not pass by the remains of the people of Lot in the morning and at night, and it treats their failure to reflect on what they see as the strangest part of the whole matter (37:137–138). The instruction is not to feel sorrow at a pile of stones; it is to connect the stones to a warning that is currently in circulation. A ruin becomes a lesson only when someone standing in front of it asks what produced the emptiness, and the Book assumes that the question is within reach of any traveler with a functioning mind.

**Ruins That Stand Beside Blind Hearts**

The Book pairs the physical ruin with an inner blindness, and the pairing sharpens what this verse means by people of understanding. One passage describes the societies destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving total ruin behind them along with abandoned wells and lofty palaces, and then asks whether the readers have not travelled so that their hearts may reason and their ears may listen, adding that it is not the eyes that go blind but the hearts in the chests (22:45–46). The sign of this verse is therefore addressed to an organ that a journey cannot repair by itself. A traveler can stand in front of an abandoned town and see only broken masonry, and the Book treats that outcome as a condition of the heart rather than a shortage of evidence.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:36

> And to the people of Midian ˹We sent˺ their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah, and hope for ˹the reward of˺ the Last Day. And do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”

**A Third Community Added to the Roll**

The chapter has moved from Abraham and Lot to a people living further north, and the pattern continues: a messenger is sent, he states his case in a sentence or two, and the outcome is reported. Midian is named in the Book as the people of Shuʿayb, and scholars connect their descent to Midian the son of Abraham, which supplies one reason the chapter places this account after the story of Abraham and his nephew. The sequence of the sūrah is not chronological in any ordinary sense; it is thematic. A community known for a particular kind of dishonesty is added to a list that includes towns destroyed for violence, and the reader is meant to see the common structure behind the different offences. The same warning, the same refusal, and the same end.

**"Their Brother," Not a Stranger**

The Book describes Shuʿayb as the brother of the people he addresses, and the expression recurs for the messengers of this period. It means that he came from among them, spoke their dialect, and knew their trade. The Book uses the word to close off an excuse: nobody could claim that a foreigner had misunderstood the local customs, and nobody could dismiss the warning as the intrusion of an outsider with no stake in the community. The phrase also makes the rejection heavier, since the man being mocked at the market was someone the merchants had grown up with. The sūrah has already shown the same dynamic in Abraham's town and in Lot's, and Midian repeats it.

The word also settles the question of a messenger's social position. Shuʿayb was not a foreign official bringing a policy for the town to adopt; he was a man whose family lived there and whose warning therefore implicated him in whatever the outcome would be, and the Book's use of the family term keeps that stake visible.

**Corruption, the Same Word From Lot's Prayer**

The third command returns to the term the sūrah used when Lot asked for help against a people of corruption. The Book applies the word to the specific malpractice of Midian, and the fuller version of Shuʿayb's warning states the charge in economic language: give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, and do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order (7:85). The pairing is deliberate. Fraud in the scales is not treated as a business irregularity but as damage to the land itself, because a market that cannot be trusted corrodes everything that depends on it. The sūrah is showing the reader how wide its central term reaches.

The word's appearance in both stories also keeps the sūrah's charges connected. What Lot called corruption in the conduct of his townspeople is the same disorder the Book finds in fraudulent scales, and the shared vocabulary ties the two offences together as failures of one covenant rather than as separate categories of crime.

**A Messenger Who Knew Their Business**

The Book sends prophets out of the communities they address, and in Shuʿayb's case the messenger understood the trade he was rebuking. The fuller account preserves his warning with the reason attached: he tells the people of Midian that he sees them in prosperity and that he fears for them an overwhelming day, then names the short measure as the thing he fears it for (11:84). The sentence combines affection with alarm, which is a register the Book uses for messengers speaking about their own towns. Shuʿayb was not denouncing an abstraction called fraud; he was describing the practices of shops he had grown up with and the households that would be ruined by what was coming, and the warning keeps both facts in view at once.

**The Trade the Book Is Willing to Argue About**

The specifics of Shuʿayb's case are preserved in other chapters, and they show how far the Book is prepared to go into the details of commerce. He tells his people to give full measure and weigh with justice, and warns them not to defraud people of their property or spread corruption in the land (7:85). Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ repeats the instruction twice, ordering full measure and no loss to others, and forbidding fraud and corruption in the same breath (26:181; 26:183). The Book attaches these rules to the creed rather than to a code of trading practice, and it treats a short weight as an act of misguidance rather than a civil matter. Midian was warned about the scale, and the warning carries the same weight as a warning about worship, which is the measure of how seriously the Book regards a market that cheats its customers.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:37

> But they rejected him, so an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them and they fell lifeless in their homes

**A Verdict Stated in One Clause**

The whole response of Midian to its messenger is compressed into the words "they rejected him," and the report of their end follows immediately. The Book does this repeatedly in this chapter, and the compression is a judgment in itself. No extended dialogue is preserved, no argument is presented, and no excuse is offered on the town's behalf; the record shows only the decision and its consequence. The reader who wants the details of Shuʿayb's preaching has the fuller accounts in other chapters, where he argues about weights and measures and warns about a punishment that will fall on a lying marketplace (7:85; 11:85). What the sūrah keeps is the essential move, because the chapter's subject is not the content of one prophet's sermon but the pattern that repeats across the nations.

What the clause leaves out is as telling as what it contains. There is no inventory of the town's possessions, no description of its buildings, and no account of survivors; the report is narrowed to a verdict and its execution, and the sūrah's habit of ending these narratives at the moment of seizure keeps the reader's mind on the cause.

**The Same Sentence Given to Two Nations**

The description of this destruction is identical in wording to the account of the Thamūd, who were struck down after rejecting Ṣāliḥ and were found lifeless in their dwellings (7:91). Two nations, two messengers, two distinct crimes, one sentence. The Book's use of the same formula for both fates is a way of teaching the reader that the differences between the offences do not matter at the level of consequence.

**The Ground Itself as the Instrument**

An earthquake is a peculiar choice for a merchant people, and the mismatch carries a lesson for a town that measured its security in warehouses and roads. The Midianites depended on settled land, caravans, and stable thoroughfares; their prosperity was possible only because the earth held still under their feet. What ends them is the failure of that assumption. Sūrah Sabaʾ asks whether the people have not seen everything that surrounds them of the heavens and the earth, and whether God could not cause the earth to swallow them or drop deadly fragments of the sky upon them, adding that in this is a sign for every servant who turns to God (34:9). The earth's steadiness is a gift that is spent without being noticed.

**Nobody Was Destroyed Before Being Told**

The Book states its rule about destruction in terms that leave no room for the tragedy of the uninformed: God would never destroy a society for its wrongdoing while its people are unaware of the truth (6:131), and He would never destroy a society until He had sent a messenger to its capital, reciting His revelations to its people (28:59). The second passage adds the condition that the destruction follows on persistence in wrongdoing. Midian therefore fits the rule rather than escaping it: a messenger had lived among them, they had heard the warning about their scales, and the earthquake is presented as the end of a process that began with instruction. The sentence reports a seizure, but the Book's wider statements show what stood between the warning and the tremor.

Stating the rule this way also explains why the Book's messengers spend so long arguing. The warning is not a formality that precedes an inevitable sentence; it is the condition on which the sentence is held back, and the length of Noah's mission, the patience shown to Pharaoh, and the years Lot spent among his neighbours are all applications of the same policy.

**The Identical Sentence in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf**

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf gives the Midianite destruction in exactly the words this verse uses, noting that an overwhelming earthquake seized them and that they were found lifeless in their dwellings (7:91). Sūrah Hūd gives the same end for Thamūd and Midian in adjacent passages, which is why commentators treat the two destructions as a matched pair. The Book's habit of preserving one formula for these events means that a reader who has learned the pattern can predict the ending of any story he encounters, and the predictability is the point. The chapter is not narrating tragedy for its own sake; it is teaching a rule that its audience is expected to apply to itself.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:38

> And the people of ’Ȃd and Thamûd ˹met a similar fate˺, which must be clear to you ˹Meccans˺ from their ruins. Satan made their ˹evil˺ deeds appealing to them, hindering them from the ˹Right˺ Way, although they were capable of reasoning

**Two Tribes the Audience Had Seen For Themselves**

The sūrah now names two Arabian peoples rather than distant nations, and it addresses the Meccans directly about them. ʿĀd and Thamūd were ancient tribes of the peninsula, sent the prophets Hūd and Ṣāliḥ respectively, and their ruined settlements lay along the caravan routes the audience travelled for trade. The Book's argument changes register here. Abraham, Lot, and Shuʿayb belonged to lands the Meccans had heard of; ʿĀd and Thamūd belonged to places they had personally ridden through, and the chapter says so in the plainest terms by telling them that their fate is evident from their dwellings. Evidence that can be visited is being placed in front of people who had already visited it without looking.

The choice of these two peoples also avoided an objection the audience could have raised. Their remains lay within reach of the caravan routes, so a Meccan trader could look at what was left of them, and a messenger arguing from a distant legend would have invited a very different response.

**Ruins as an Argument From Geography**

The Book repeatedly converts travel into evidence and treats the failure to draw conclusions from landscape as a form of blindness. The same ruins are addressed in another chapter, where the Meccans are told that they pass by the remains of the destroyed towns day and night and are asked whether they will not then understand (37:137–138). The question presumes that the physical remains of a civilization ought to be enough to force the inquiry, since a settlement does not empty itself. The sūrah's audience had a working explanation available for the ruins they saw — old places, abandoned for reasons of trade or water — and the Book's challenge is that the explanation leaves out the one fact the ruins were preserved to convey.

The Book treats the landscape as part of its evidence rather than as scenery. Empty settlements, abandoned wells, and fallen walls appear in its arguments repeatedly, and the sūrah's audience travelled past them for a living, which is why the verse can present the ruins as material that was already familiar.

**The Warning That Is Aimed at the Listener**

The direct address to the Meccans sharpens the whole passage, since the audience being invited to consider the ruins was on the same road with a messenger of its own. The sūrah has been assembling a list that ends with a general verdict, but it takes care before that verdict to put the reader inside the story. The same observation appears in the verse from Sūrah Sabaʾ that calls the surrounding heavens and earth a sign for every servant who turns to God (34:9). What separates a person from the ruins of ʿĀd is not a different nature and not a different level of intelligence; it is whether he draws the conclusion available to him while he still has time to act on it.

The warning also reaches a reader with no ruins near his house. The Book leaves the story in place so that the conduct behind the destruction can be recognized wherever it appears, and the sūrah has spent many verses describing that conduct in the audience's own town.

**The Ruins That Sat on the Trade Route**

The Book does not leave the remains of these peoples to be discovered by archaeologists; it locates them on the roads its audience used. The ruins of the destroyed tribe are described as still lying along a known route (15:76), and the Book says of the cities that were showered with stones that the Meccans had certainly passed by them and asks whether they had not seen the ruins, adding that those travelers did not expect to be resurrected (25:40). The argument of this verse and the argument of those passages are one argument. The evidence was not buried in a distant land or hidden in an archive; it stood beside the caravan tracks in the form of empty settlements, and the only step the Book required was the step of asking why they were empty.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:39

> ˹We˺ also ˹destroyed˺ Korah, Pharaoh, and Hamân. Indeed, Moses had come to them with clear proofs, but they behaved arrogantly in the land. Yet they could not escape ˹Us˺

**Korah and the Wealth That Went Into the Ground**

The Book gives Korah's story in another chapter, where he is a man of enormous fortune who is reminded that his wealth is a test and answers by claiming it was given to him for his own merit. His end is reported in a single sentence: the earth swallowed him along with his house, and there was no one to help him against God, nor could he help himself (28:81). The detail of the house matters because it ties his fate to the wealth it contained. What he had accumulated as security became the wreckage in which he was buried, and the assets that made him formidable in the city were irrelevant in the space of an instant. The Book's verdict on his prosperity is not that it was evil; it is that he mistook it for protection.

The wealth of Korah is described elsewhere as belonging to a man who attributed his fortune to his own knowledge, and the ground's treatment of him answers that claim directly. The verse under discussion keeps the burial at the level of a fact, since the sūrah's interest lies in the collapse rather than in the argument that preceded it.

**Pharaoh's Tower and Hāmān's Labour**

The role assigned to Hāmān in the Book is precise. He is the official ordered to light a fire and build a tower so that Pharaoh could look upon the God of Moses and, in the fuller accounts, so that he could be proclaimed a deity to the public (28:38). The project is a good illustration of the problem the chapter has been describing. Imperial power in the Book is not only cruel; it is absurd, spending the labour of a nation to prove a claim that no structure can support. Pharaoh's real objection to Moses was never about evidence, since the proofs were displayed before him, and the tower is what his position produced when evidence failed: an architectural argument, built by the man standing beside him in this verse. The Book's portrait of the court is completed by the fact that the project required the labour of a nation and produced nothing usable. It is one of the few places in the scripture where a government's most formidable undertaking is described as an exercise in vanity, and the reader is meant to notice how much of the state's power was spent on a claim that the state could not make true.

**"They Could Not Escape Us"**

The closing clause uses the exact word the sūrah employed earlier when it told its audience that no one escapes God on earth or in heaven (29:22). The repetition ties the two passages together and closes the distance between the imperious and the ordinary. A ruler with a treasury, an army, and a tower is in the same position as any other creature before the decree, and his apparatus cannot delay it by a day. The Book's reports of these regimes are consistently unimpressed by their scale, and the phrase reminds the reader that the empires that survive in the record do so only as material for the argument. The next verse gathers the verdicts of the whole chapter into a single statement.

The phrase closes the case against all three at once. None of them escaped, and the sentence draws no distinction between the man with the treasure, the man with the army, and the man with the building contract, which leaves the reader facing a limit that no kind of power can negotiate.

**Korah's End Retold in Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ**

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ reports Korah's destruction with a detail that belongs beside this verse: the earth swallowed him and his home, and the verse notes that those who had envied his position the day before were struck the next day by the realization that God enlarges provision for whomever He wills or withholds it (28:81–82). The observation attaches the story directly to the sūrah's later theme about provision, and it shows that the punishment did its work on the survivors as well. Korah's wealth had made him a model to be envied, and his end made him a warning in the same streets, which is the pattern the whole chapter follows: the ruin becomes a lesson for those still in a position to reconsider.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40

> So We seized each ˹people˺ for their sin: against some of them We sent a storm of stones, some were overtaken by a ˹mighty˺ blast, some We caused the earth to swallow, and some We drowned. Allah would not have wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves

**One Verdict, Four Methods**

The verse gathers the whole catalogue of destroyed nations into a single sentence and reports four methods of seizure. The variation is the striking feature. No two communities were ended in exactly the same way, and yet each is described as having been taken for its own sin. The Book has been building to this summary for many verses, and its purpose is to prevent the reader from attaching the lesson to a particular disaster. Fire from the sky, a cry that overwhelms, the ground opening, and water closing over a host are different events, but they are described here as one act performed by one authority, distributed according to circumstances that the verse says are known.

The list also shows that the Book does not treat the manner of destruction as the point of the account. What the four cases share is the verdict, and the variety of instruments—water, wind, sound, and a swallowing earth—keeps a reader from attaching the lesson to any one of them.

**The Refrain That Repeats Across the Book**

The Book attaches the same disclaimer to nearly every account of national destruction, and its recurrence is a deliberate protection of the reader's understanding of God. The same sentence closes the story of the people in the wilderness, who were told that the evildoers did not wrong God but wronged themselves (2:57), and it appears in the punishment of the tribes and the overturned cities, where the Book states that God would have never wronged them but that it was they who wronged themselves (9:70). Five nations, five methods, one explanation, repeated in identical words. The repetition is the Book's way of ensuring that no reader draws the conclusion the disbelievers were accused of drawing elsewhere in the sūrah — that the disaster was arbitrary.

The repetition also serves a teaching purpose, since a community that hears the same clause at the end of story after story will eventually attach it to its own situation. The Book's habit of repeating a verdict in identical words makes the lesson portable, and this sūrah uses the technique at the point where the list of nations finally stops.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

> The parable of those who take protectors other than Allah is that of a spider spinning a shelter. And the flimsiest of all shelters is certainly that of a spider, if only they knew

**The Shelter Built by the Thing That Needs It**

The image is exact, and its force lies in a detail the reader supplies without being told. The spider does not find its house; it spins one out of its own body. Everything the web is made of comes from the maker, and the structure offers no protection against anything heavier than itself. Applied to the people described in the verse, the picture says that the protectors they have chosen are not external powers they have enlisted but arrangements they have produced. The Book's criticism in this chapter has been consistent about that: idols that cannot provide, patrons who will disown their clients, wealth that sinks with its owner. What all of them share is that the security is manufactured by the person who feels secure.

**What the Sūrah Means by a Protector**

The term rendered as protector names the person or power to whom one attaches oneself for support, and the Book uses it across a wide range of commitments. It criticizes those who take other human beings as their patrons, those who take devils as allies, and those who take idols as their reliance. In the Book's vocabulary the office belongs to God alone, while in the human realm it belongs to the Prophet and to the community of believers who stand together. The sentence therefore does not only condemn those who bow to statues. It addresses anyone who has arranged his safety around a source that is not God, whether that source is a state, a patron, a fortune, or a reputation, and it asks what such a shelter is actually made of.

**Why the Image Is a House Rather Than a Wall**

The Book chose the picture of a dwelling rather than a weapon, and the choice carries its own argument. A house is what a person builds to be at rest, and what he trusts to be there when he returns. The web is genuinely a house by that definition: it is spun to catch, it shelters the spider, and it is precisely where the spider lives. The problem is not that the shelter is fake but that it is unequal to the pressures that will arrive. The sūrah has just spent many verses reporting those pressures, and the reader who has followed the fates of five nations knows what the weather eventually does to a structure of that kind.

A wall is judged by what it can repel; a house is judged by what it can hold. The comparison in the verse therefore tests the idolatrous arrangement on the second question rather than the first. What the town had built was a religion that organized its calendar, its trade, and its standing, and the assertion of the verse is that none of it would hold up the thing it was meant to support.

**The Wider Use of the Image in the Book**

The spider's web became a standard image in the Book's later interpretation for everything a person builds without God at the centre: religious systems invented by human beings, acts of worship performed for the approval of an audience, and ideologies that promise what they cannot deliver. The generalization is not a stretch, since the Book states the underlying principle elsewhere when it says that the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord are like ashes scattered by the wind, with no power over anything they have earned (14:18), and it says of certain deeds that they have come to nothing (2:217; 3:22; 5:53). What the image adds is the reason: the structure was never designed to bear the load.

The same concern for durability runs through the Book's other statements about work that is not grounded in what is true, and those statements ask one question of every enterprise a person undertakes: what will it be worth once the conditions that made it convenient have ended? Read beside them, the spider's web is a member of a family rather than a curiosity, and it is the member chosen for a chapter addressed to people who had built their whole standing on an inherited cult.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:42

> Allah surely knows that whatever ˹gods˺ they invoke besides Him are ˹simply˺ nothing. For He is the Almighty, All-Wise

The complaint against the town's devotion is put in knowledge rather than of power, which is a more damaging charge. A weak ally who has been told what is needed can still be useful, whereas a thing that cannot be informed of the request cannot respond to it at all. Every act of worship is a claim to have been heard, and the verse removes that claim by naming the one party in the relationship who is described as knowing everything. The Book's argument against the idols therefore does not depend on what they could do; it depends on what they were.

**The Idols That Cannot Hear**

Other chapters supply the reasoning that stands behind this brief verdict. Abraham asks his father why he worships what can neither hear nor see nor benefit him at all (19:42), and the Book tells the Prophet to ask the disbelievers how they can worship besides God those who can neither harm nor benefit them, when God alone is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing (5:76). The incapacity is total: no perception, no agency, no effect of any kind, whether for good or ill. The argument is not that the idols are weak deities but that they do not belong to the category of agents at all, and the verse's claim that they are nothing is the conclusion of that argument stated in its shortest form.

A person who relies on a thing that has no knowledge of him has placed his affairs in a relationship that runs in one direction only. The verse states the fact as part of an argument that runs through the whole sūrah: worship is a transaction with a party who hears, and the Book's practice of answering supplications is unintelligible without a hearer. When God is described as knowing everything, the sentence quietly removes the alternative, since the objects of the town's devotion were being asked for things they could not be informed of.

**A Sentence That Reads Differently in Different Mouths**

The verse is addressed to a Prophet engaged in a dispute, and its confidence is that of a man who has been told the outcome. For the audience in Makkah it functioned as a warning, since it names the fate of everything they were relying on. Read by later communities, it keeps its edge, because the objects of invocation change while the structure of the mistake does not. What the Book consistently supplies in such passages is not an argument that the reader must win in a debate but a fact that the reader may verify in the ruins of the nations described earlier in the chapter. The next verse explains why so few readers act on what the parables show them.

**The Difference Between an Object and a Distraction**

One more distinction belongs to this verse, and it concerns the attitude of the people described. The Book does not say they deny God; the sūrah has already reported that the idolaters acknowledged Him as the maker and the provider while associating others with Him in worship. Their problem was not a missing creator but a crowded ledger, and the sentence's word "besides Him" identifies the specific error. What they call upon in addition to God turns out to be nothing, and the addition is what the verse is examining. It is a narrow charge, and it is sharper for being narrow: everything they have added to their devotion has no counterpart in existence, and the One they acknowledge beside their additions knows precisely that. The Book makes the same case elsewhere when it tells the Prophet to ask the idolaters whether they are informing God of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth (10:18), and when it invites them to invoke those they claim as divine and observe that they have no power to remove harm from them or transfer it to another (17:56). The additions were real only as activities, and activities without objects leave the ledger exactly where it was.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:43

> These are the parables We set forth for humanity, but none will understand them except the people of knowledge

**Parables Offered to Everyone, Understood by Few**

The sentence makes a distinction that this chapter has been circling since its opening: the material is public and the comprehension is not. The parables are set before humanity without restriction, and the same image which the spider's web provides is available to any hearer. What divides the audience is not access but recognition, and the Book locates the difference in knowledge rather than in intelligence or social standing. The chapter's first verses raised the same question about people who say they believe and expect to be left untroubled; the sūrah's method throughout has been to place the evidence in the open and let the response reveal what a person is.

The verse also presents the parables as public property. They are set before humanity rather than reserved for a school, which is why the Book's images draw on spiders, flies, mountains, and rain instead of technical vocabulary, and why a failure to profit from them is treated as a fault in the audience rather than a shortage in the material.

**The Parable as a Test of the Hearer**

A comparison in the Book does more than illustrate; it sorts. The reader who accepts the image of the spider's web must also accept what it says about the reliability of his own arrangements, and that acceptance is where most readings stop. The same structure appears in the Book's wider use of comparisons, and the Book says of itself that God is not ashamed to set forth a parable of a gnat or something smaller, adding that He by this leads many astray and guides many (2:26). The verse is one of the clearest statements in the Book that revelation is not neutral in its effects. Its content can be heard by anyone, and its function is to separate those who take the point from those who do not.

**The Adornment of a Full Heart**

The Book treats understanding as something that depends on the condition of the person who receives it, and it names the obstruction elsewhere with the language of rust and seals. It says of the hypocrites that their hearts were sealed so that they could not comprehend (63:3), and it warns that those who persist in rejecting a sign after it has been explained lose the capacity to benefit from signs in general. The classification in this verse is not arbitrary, then; the Book presents knowledge as a capacity that grows with use and atrophies with neglect. A person who has already decided what he will accept before he hears an argument is not in a position to understand the argument, whichever argument it is.

Knowledge described as adornment also implies visibility. A person whose understanding has settled into his character carries it in his dealings, his speech, and his patience, and a word connected with beauty suggests an effect meant to be noticed by others rather than kept private.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:44

> Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. Surely in this is a sign for the people of faith

**Creation Described as a Deliberate Act**

The Arabic phrase rendered as "for a purpose" is built on the word for truth, and it can be read in more than one way: that the heavens and the earth were created with truth as their foundation, or that they were created to manifest the truth. Commentators record both, and the Book states the same idea in several sūras with the same construction (39:5; 45:22; 46:3; 64:3). What the phrase excludes is the alternative the audience would have found in the intellectual air of the time: that the world is an accident, or a game, or the by-product of forces with no intention behind them. The verse asserts a maker, a purpose, and a design, and it does so in the same chapter that has just compared human arrangements to a spider's web.

The wording also rules out the notion that the world assembled itself or that its order is accidental. The Book's verbs for creation carry the sense of measuring and proportioning, which is why the argument from design in this verse can end in a call to reflect rather than in a demand for bare assent.

**The Purpose the Book Specifies**

Other passages supply what the purpose is, and they are consistent. God created the heavens and the earth for a purpose so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed, and none will be wronged (45:22). Sūrah al-Rūm pairs the purpose with an appointed term and notes that most people deny the meeting with their Lord (30:8). The same chapter that reports the creation in truth describes the night and the day wrapped around one another and the sun and moon running to a fixed term (39:5). Taken together, the Book's statements make the creation purposeful in two directions at once: it exists for a term, and it exists so that what is done inside it can be accounted for. The verse's brevity assumes that its audience has heard those statements.

**The Fragility That Belongs to the Sign**

The context supplies a further layer. The verse stands immediately after the parable of the spider's web and immediately before the command to recite and pray, and its subject is the created order that the web belongs to just as much as the spider does. The Book states elsewhere that everything is bound to perish except God Himself (28:88), and that the created world is not permanent but runs to a determined end. A sign that is itself passing away can still testify to something that does not pass, and the sūrah has been making exactly that point since its opening verses about the frailty of what people build. The heavens and the earth are a sign because they point past themselves.

**Looking Inward at One's Own Composition**

Sūrah al-Rūm adds the most personal version of this line of reasoning when it asks whether people have not reflected upon their own being, and then states that God created the heavens and the earth and everything in between for a purpose and an appointed term, adding that most people deny the meeting with their Lord (30:8). The appeal moves from the sky to the self, and the purpose named is again the meeting. This is where the sūrah's argument has been heading throughout: the creation is not only evidence of power but an arrangement with a schedule, and the reader is inside the arrangement. The verse under discussion calls that arrangement a sign for people of faith, and the sūrah's next command tells the believer what to do with what the sign shows him.

Looking inward and looking outward are joined in one argument. A person who examines the design of his own body finds the same proportioning he sees in the sky, and the Book often places the two witnesses side by side so that a reader who doubts one still has the other in front of him.

**A Reminder That Outlasts Every Generation**

Because the sign described here is the created order rather than a particular miracle, its audience is not limited to the people who witnessed the events of this chapter. Each generation inherits ruins it did not watch being ruined, but it inherits the heavens and the earth complete, and the verse attaches its appeal to that inheritance. The Book states the consequence of ignoring it in another passage that says most people are in denial of the meeting with their Lord even while they live inside a creation that runs to an appointed term (30:8). The sentence under discussion supplies the counterweight: for people who believe, the same arrangement that others take for granted works as a sign, and it keeps working as long as the arrangement stands.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45

> Recite what has been revealed to you of the Book and establish prayer. Indeed, ˹genuine˺ prayer should deter ˹one˺ from indecency and wickedness. The remembrance of Allah is ˹an˺ even greater ˹deterrent˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you ˹all˺ do

**The River at the Door**

The Prophet described the effect of the five daily prayers in a comparison that has been preserved with its chain and its collection: he asked his companions whether a man with a river at his door who bathed in it five times a day would retain any dirt, and when they answered that no trace would remain, he told them that this is the likeness of the five prayers, with which God erases sins (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 528, narrated by Abū Hurayrah). The image is chosen for its ordinariness. Nobody would call a bath a heroic act, and the Prophet's point is that the benefit lies in the repetition of something unremarkable at a fixed address. What wears away a person's failings in this account is not intensity but return.

The image also makes the standard of purity ordinary rather than heroic. A person is asked to bathe in water he can already see, which means the instruction assumes a resource at hand rather than an exceptional effort, and the same assumption runs through the worship the sūrah asks of its audience.

**The Sanctity of the One Who Prays**

The Book states in another place that God accepts only from the reverent (5:27), and the earliest readers of this verse drew the same line between the act and its fruit. Al-Ṭabarī, commenting on this verse, records from Ibn ʿAbbās that the one whose prayer does not restrain him from indecency and wrongdoing gains nothing from God through it but distance, and he records the same verdict from al-Ḥasan and Qatāda. Al-Baghawī carries the saying from Ibn Masʿūd and Ibn ʿAbbās in a fuller form — prayer contains a stopping point and a restraint against disobedience, and the one unrestrained by it gains only distance — and adds a harsher formulation from al-Ḥasan and Qatāda, that such a prayer turns against the person offering it. He also records from Jābir, with its chain of narration, a report that a man told the Prophet of someone who recited the Qur'an all night and then stole in the morning, and received the answer that his recitation would restrain him.

Ibn Kathīr supplies the fact-check rather than another chain. He lists the versions that raise this wording to the Prophet and concludes that the soundest of them all are the ones stopping at the Companions and the Successors, naming Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan, and Qatāda; and through al-Aʿmash from Mālik ibn al-Ḥārith he reports that Ibn Masʿūd, told of a man who prayed at great length, answered that prayer benefits only the one who obeys it, then recited this verse. Al-Qurṭubī preserves the same exchange in a shorter form. The verse itself remains the canonical statement of the principle and is enough for the reader's purposes. What all of these formulations share is a refusal to separate the act from its fruit, and the Book never treats worship as a transaction that can be completed in isolation from the life that contains it.

The protection is also stated in a way that does not depend on the worshipper's reputation. What restrains him is the act itself rather than the standing he has built up, and generations of readers have taken from the sentence the reassurance that a person's worship counts for something even when the rest of his record is uneven.

**What Recitation Contributes**

The command to recite placed at the head of the verse gives the believer a supply of content for the inner life. The Book describes itself as a healing and a mercy for the believers (17:82) and as guidance for the conscious (2:2), and the meaning of what is recited is what makes the deterrence of the previous clause intelligible. A person who recites a passage about the fates of towns that answered their messengers with mockery carries the argument with him into the rest of his day. The sūrah's historical review, which ends in this verse, was not assembled for the reader's information; it was assembled so that it could be recited, and this sentence tells him to keep reciting it.

The recitation also supplies the content that prayer then expresses. A community that recited these verses knew what it was asking for and what it was committing itself to, and putting reading beside prayer treats the text as the working material of the devotion rather than as a preliminary to it.

**Prayer That Is Work Rather Than Relief**

The Book never presents the prayer as a pause from the obligations the sūrah has been describing; it presents it as the resource that makes them possible. Believers are told to seek help through patience and prayer, with the added note that this is a burden except for the humble (2:45), and the Book's list of the qualities of successful believers opens with those who humble themselves in prayer (23:1–2). The connection to this sūrah is direct. A community being told to hold a claim under pressure, to refuse a parent's wrong instruction, and to leave a city that will not allow worship is not being handed a ritual for its spare hours. It is being handed the discipline that keeps those other duties standing, and the verse places recitation and prayer at the head of the instructions for exactly that reason.

On any reading the verse refuses to leave prayer as an isolated duty and connects it to a state that continues outside it, which is why the Book elsewhere gives the command to establish prayer for the sake of remembrance (20:14).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46

> Do not argue with the People of the Book unless gracefully, except with those of them who act wrongfully. And say, “We believe in what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is ˹only˺ One. And to Him we ˹fully˺ submit.”

**An Instruction About Method, Not Retreat**

The verse does not forbid discussion with Jews and Christians; it regulates the manner and then hands the Muslim a formula to use. Disagreement is assumed, and the assumption is realistic, since the community that received this chapter lived alongside communities that held different scriptures. What the sentence excludes is the kind of argument that treats an opponent as an adversary to be defeated, and the Book elsewhere gives the fuller instruction to invite with wisdom and kind advice and to debate only in the best manner (16:125). Commentators explain the phrase as requiring that evidence be drawn from God's proof and that the tone remain good even when the other party's tone does not. The rule is stated at the level of method because the method is what the Muslim controls.

The method also sets the terms on which a believer's own case is to be made. Someone who argues in this manner is not required to win, only to present what the two sides share and to state his position clearly, and the instruction assumes that his opponent may still refuse after the argument has been put.

**What Muslims Are Told to Say**

The formula given in the verse is a statement of shared ground: we believe in what was revealed to us and in what was revealed to you, one God for both, and to Him we submit. The same declaration appears in a fuller form earlier in the Book, where believers are told to affirm the revelation given to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets, making no distinction among any of them (2:136; 3:84). The wording is carefully constructed. It does not concede that the two revelations are identical, and it does not deny the scriptures of the other community either. It affirms a common object of worship and a shared posture of submission, and it leaves the differences to be discussed under the rule of the first sentence.

**Submission as the Common Noun**

The final phrase of the formula returns to the root of the word Islam, describing the believers as those who submit themselves completely to God. The Book uses the same vocabulary for Abraham, who was neither a Jew nor a Christian but a man of pure submission and no polytheist (3:67), and its point in doing so is that the category existed before either of the two later communities acquired its name. What the Muslim is told to announce, then, is not the novelty of his religion but its continuity with the submission that the prophets practised. This framing is the reason the sūrah can address the People of the Book as parties to a shared history rather than as strangers, and it sets the terms on which the disagreement is to proceed.

The choice of a common noun also gives the reader a way to describe both communities without flattening the difference between them. What they share is a posture toward God; what distinguishes them is the record each accepts, and the verse leaves that second question to the argument it recommends.

**The Prophet's Instruction About Reports**

The manner prescribed in this verse found practical expression in a rule the Prophet gave his companions. When some Jews recited the Torah in Hebrew and explained it in Arabic to the Muslims, he told them not to believe the People of the Book or to disbelieve them, but to say that they believe in God and in what has been revealed (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4485, narrated by Abū Hurayrah). The rule is a discipline for the mind as much as for the tongue. Anything brought from another scripture falls into one of three classes — consistent with what God has revealed, contradictory to it, or unclassifiable without independent verification — and the Prophet's instruction distinguishes the three without permitting either credulity or blanket rejection.

**Where the Two Communities Actually Agree**

The formula given in this verse is short because the shared ground it names is short, and the Book develops the same list elsewhere without altering it. One God, one line of messengers, one final accounting, and one posture of submission: those are the points on which the two communities agree, and every one of them is a matter the Book treats as fundamental. The disagreement that remains is real, and the verse's rule deals with it by requiring argument rather than war, which is only possible because the parties already share something to argue from.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47

> Similarly ˹to earlier messengers˺, We have revealed to you a Book ˹O Prophet˺. ˹The faithful of˺ those to whom We gave the Scriptures believe in it, as do some of these ˹pagan Arabs˺. And none denies Our revelations except the ˹stubborn˺ disbelievers

**One Method of Revelation Across the Ages**

The verse opens by placing the revelation given to the Prophet in a sequence rather than presenting it as an unprecedented event, and the Book does this wherever it discusses the earlier scriptures. The community of Madinah is told in another chapter to affirm what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets, without distinguishing between any of them (2:136). What arrives with the Qur'an is therefore described as the continuation of a procedure God has been running for centuries, and the sentence reminds the reader that the People of the Book addressed in the previous verse are not outsiders to the story but earlier recipients of it.

**Two Communities That Believe**

The verse names two groups who accepted the Book: faithful members of the communities that had received earlier scriptures, and some of the pagan Arabs among whom the Prophet lived. The first group is attested in other passages that describe People of the Book believing in what was revealed to the Prophet and in what was revealed to them, humble before God and refusing to trade His revelations for a fleeting gain (3:199). The second reference gives the reader a measure of the audience in Makkah and Madinah, where the response was mixed and the verse says so plainly by naming only some of them.

**The Believers Who Recognized What They Already Had**

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ reports the reaction of those who had been given knowledge before the revelation reached them: when it is recited to them they declare that they believe in it, that it is the truth from their Lord, and that they had already submitted before it arrived (28:53). Sūrah al-Māʾidah describes the same recognition in more physical terms, with the eyes of the believers overflowing with tears at what they recognize of the truth, and their prayer that they be counted among the witnesses (5:83). These passages matter for the sūrah's argument because they show that acceptance of the Book was not a decision made without antecedents. The people described had been preparing for it by their own reading of what they had been given.

What those believers accepted they had already received in part, which means their faith was an act of recognition rather than of conversion to a stranger's claim. The Book treats such recognition as costly in its own right, since a scholar who endorses a message from outside his community loses standing with the people who trained him.

**Denial as a Stubborn Act**

The final clause states that nobody denies the revelations except the stubborn disbelievers, and the Arabic term carries the sense of covering and of wilful resistance rather than simple unbelief. The sūrah will repeat the same verdict a few verses later in slightly different words, replacing the disbelievers with the wrongdoers (29:49), and the variation suggests that the Book is describing a condition rather than a demographic group. Denial in this vocabulary is an act performed by someone who has been shown something, which is why the Book consistently denies that its evidence was withheld. The verse therefore does not say that most people accept the revelation; it says that those who reject it do so while resisting what they have understood.

**The Same Claim in Sūrah al-Baqarah**

Sūrah al-Baqarah gives the widest version of the declaration, listing the prophets and the books by name and requiring belief in all of them without distinction (2:136). The placing of that verse is significant: it comes in a passage addressed to a community being drawn into disputes about religious genealogy, and it answers the disputes by refusing to rank the messengers. The verse under discussion makes the same move in compressed form. What it establishes is that the Book given to the Prophet belongs in a library rather than in a market stall, and that the community which holds it is joining a conversation that began long before its own appearance. That framing shapes how the community is expected to conduct itself in the disputes that follow, and it explains why the sūrah can move from a rule about courteous argument directly into a report about who accepted the message.

The Book describes its own relation to the earlier scriptures with a phrase that is easy to read past: it says that what was revealed to the Prophet came with the truth as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority over them, before it orders him to judge by what God has revealed and not to follow their desires (5:48).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48

> You ˹O Prophet˺ could not read any writing ˹even˺ before this ˹revelation˺, nor could you write at all. Otherwise, the people of falsehood would have been suspicious

**The Unlettered Prophet**

The Book elsewhere calls him the unlettered prophet, describing him as the figure foretold in the Torah and the Gospel, who commands what is right, forbids what is wrong, permits the good and prohibits the foul, and lifts the burdens his followers had carried (7:157). The term is significant in the argument of this verse because it is a public fact rather than a private claim. His tribe knew his occupation and his habits; his companions had grown up around him; his opponents were the first to catalogue his history when they tried to find a more comfortable explanation for the recitation. A claim about literacy is exactly the kind of claim that an audience in one city is qualified to check, and the Book makes it in front of the audience best placed to contradict it.

The description also closes an avenue the audience would otherwise have used. Had the Prophet been a reader of earlier books, the accusation of copying could have been pressed with some plausibility, so the verse builds its argument on forty years spent among them without producing or reading a text.

**The Suspicion That Never Formed**

The verse states what the accusation would have been if the facts had been different. Had the Prophet been a reader of earlier books or a writer of documents, the charge would have been that he had assembled his material from the scriptures of the Jews and the Christians, and the Book records that charge being made anyway. Sūrah al-Furqān reports the allegation that these are ancient fables which he has had written down and which are rehearsed to him morning and evening (25:5). The answer supplied by this verse is the state of the man's own hands and eyes. The accusation presupposes a technical capacity he did not have, and in a market town where gossip travels faster than caravans, a detail of that kind could not have been concealed from people who had every motive to expose it.

**The Book the Prophet Could Not Have Written**

The closing phrase of the verse prepares the reader for what follows, where the Book is described as clear revelations preserved in the hearts of those who have been given knowledge (29:49). The contrast runs through both verses: the revelation is not carried in a document the Prophet composed, but in the memory of a community, and its transmission does not depend on the literacy of one man. The Book states elsewhere that it has been made easy to remember (54:17) and that it is a reminder and a clear recitation rather than poetry (36:69). What the Prophet could not write, a whole community learned to recite, and the two facts are connected in the sūrah's argument about where the Book came from.

**The Teacher They Invented Instead**

The Book records the accusation that replaced the older one. When the charge of authorship was removed by the Prophet's circumstances, his opponents produced a different explanation: the Book says that they claimed nobody was teaching him except a human being, and the answer given is that the man they point to speaks a foreign tongue while the recitation is in eloquent Arabic (16:103). The reply does not rest on the Prophet's integrity or on his companions' denials; it rests on a difference between two languages that anyone in the audience could hear. This is the same method the verse under discussion uses. In both cases the Book takes the accusation seriously enough to answer it with a fact the accusers could verify, and in both cases the fact concerns something the community had already observed for decades.

The invention also explains why the Book answers it with an observation rather than a denial. Anyone in the town could check whether the Prophet read, and the reply rests on what his neighbours had already seen, which keeps the argument at the level of public knowledge rather than private assurance.

**Witnesses Who Could Have Refuted Him**

The strength of this argument is that it was made to the one group on earth best equipped to disprove it. The Prophet's opponents in Makkah knew his childhood, his trade, his travels, and his habits; they had every reason to produce a single witness who had ever seen him read a sheet of writing or copy a line from a scroll. The Book makes its claim in front of them, and the historical record preserves no rebuttal, only the accusation that the material came from old stories recited to him morning and evening.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49

> But this ˹Quran˺ is ˹a set of˺ clear revelations ˹preserved˺ in the hearts of those gifted with knowledge. And none denies Our revelations except the ˹stubborn˺ wrongdoers

**A Book Carried in Memory Rather Than on a Shelf**

The sentence answers the previous verse's argument about writing by stating where the revelation actually lives. The Qur'an is a set of clear verses preserved in the hearts of those who have been given knowledge, and the image is precise: the Book is housed in people rather than in a manuscript that could be lost, altered, or monopolized. The Book elsewhere says of itself that it has been made easy to remember, and it repeats the question of whether anyone will take heed (54:17). What the verse describes is the mechanism by which a text survives without a printing press or an archive: a community that carries it in its chests, recites it in its gatherings, and transmits it to its children.

**Who Has Been Given Knowledge**

Two readings of the phrase appear in the commentaries. One takes it as a reference to the Muslims who memorized, recited, and interpreted the Qur'an, and it fits the Book's repeated use of the term knowledge for the understanding that comes with faith. The other takes it as a reference to those among the People of the Book who found the signs of the Prophet in their own scriptures and recognized him. Both readings have support in the surrounding verses, which have just reported that faithful People of the Book believe in the revelation. The two groups are not mutually exclusive, and the sūrah has already described scholars of earlier scriptures joining the community of those who submit.

**A Community That Cannot Be Dispossessed**

A reader in the position of the first audience, being pressured to abandon a religious claim in a city that opposed it, would hear something further in this verse. The Book does not reside in a building that can be seized or a document that can be burned; it resides in the memory of the believers, and no authority in Makkah had jurisdiction over that. The sūrah has already told its audience that the protection they need cannot come from any creature, and this verse shows the corresponding asset: a text that travels with its carriers. The community's possession of revelation is not threatened by its political weakness, and the verse makes the observation without any appeal to resistance or to material strength of any kind.

**A Heart Opened and a Heart Hardened**

The Book's location of the revelation in the hearts of the knowledgeable connects to a wider contrast it draws between two conditions of the same organ. One passage asks whether those whose hearts God has opened to submission, so that they are enlightened by their Lord, can be like the misguided, and then pronounces woe on those whose hearts are hardened at the remembrance of God, calling them clearly astray (39:22). The verse under discussion describes the first condition in its settled form. The verses are not only stored inside people; they are held in hearts that have been prepared to receive them, and the difference between a community that carries the Book and one that has heard it and forgotten is described in the same passage as the difference between light and hardness.

**The Book's Own Account of How It Is Kept**

The claim that the revelation is preserved in the hearts of knowledgeable people connects to a wider set of passages about how the Book is held. The Book says of itself that it has been made easy to remember (54:17), describes itself as a reminder and a clear recitation rather than poetry (36:69), and says that God has taken responsibility for collecting and reciting it (75:17–18). The mechanism these verses describe together is one in which the community's memory is not an accidental vessel but a designed feature of the revelation's survival. The verse under discussion states the result of that design: the signs are not locked in an archive that a hostile power could seize but carried by people who recite them in their homes and in their prayers, which is precisely the practice the sūrah will command a few verses later. A community whose only asset is a text in its memory is difficult to disarm, and the Book presents that arrangement as one of its own provisions rather than as a fortunate accident of history.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:50

> They say, “If only ˹some˺ signs had been sent down to him from his Lord!” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Signs are only with Allah. And I am only sent with a clear warning.”

**A Demand That Returns in Every Chapter**

The request recorded here appears again and again in the Book, which suggests that it was a settled feature of the dispute rather than a passing remark. The disbelievers repeatedly call for a miraculous sign to confirm the messenger: they ask why no sign has been sent down to him (10:20; 13:7), they demand that he produce a spring from the ground and a garden of palms and grapes (17:90–91), and Sūrah Ṭā Hā records them demanding a sign from his Lord even while noting that they had already received the clear confirmation of earlier scriptures (20:133). The Book gathers all these challenges into the single sentence of this verse, and its answer is equally compact. The demand was not new, and neither was the response to it.

**"Signs Are Only With God"**

The phrase places the signs in the treasuries of the unseen, and the Book states the point more fully elsewhere in the instruction given to the Prophet to say that he does not claim to have God's treasuries or to know the unseen, and that he only follows what is revealed to him (6:50). The sentence therefore refuses a promotion. The messenger is not offered the status of a wonder-worker whose arrival can be certified by performance; he is given a message and told to deliver it. The Book's insistence on this division of labour runs through its accounts of every prophet, and it protects both the messenger and the audience from a relationship built on spectacle rather than on submission.

**Why the Signs Were Not Simply Provided**

The Book gives its reasoning in another chapter: nothing prevents God from sending the requested signs except that earlier peoples denied them, and it cites the example of Thamūd, which was given a she-camel as a clear sign and wrongfully rejected it, adding that signs are sent only as a warning (17:59). The logic is uncomfortable and precise. A demonstration that arrives on demand, and is refused, leaves the audience with nothing except a heavier account to settle, and the Book treats the withholding of further spectacle as restraint rather than as withholding of evidence. The signs already provided were of that kind: the she-camel was clear, and the people who rejected it did so after seeing it.

**"I Am Only Sent With a Clear Warning"**

The title the verse assigns the Prophet is one the Book repeats in many places: a warner, and nothing more in the way of status (22:49; 35:23; 38:70; 46:9; 67:26). The adjective attached to the warning in this verse describes both its content and its tone. The message is clear, which means it does not require a wonder to be understood, and its function is to alert rather than to compel. A warner who could force acceptance would not be warning anyone; he would be operating a gate. The Book's conception of the prophetic office keeps the messenger's role within the bounds of announcement, and the sentence under discussion is one of its most economical statements of the boundary.

The reply also defines the messenger's jurisdiction. He delivers what he was given and answers for the delivery, while the production of signs rests with the one who chose when to send them, and the closing verses of the sūrah depend on that division being understood by the people making the demand.

**The Evidence That Was Already in Circulation**

The demand in this verse is answered elsewhere by pointing to what the audience was already holding. The Book asks those who want a sign whether the clear verses they have received are not sufficient for people of firm faith (2:118), and it presents the revelation itself as the standing demonstration that accompanies the messenger wherever he goes. The verse before us stops short of naming that answer, and the sentence that follows it in the sūrah supplies the reply in full. The pause between the two is where the argument lives. A community that has been reciting a book for years is in a different position from an audience that has heard nothing, and the Book treats its own recitation as the sufficient sign for the situation.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:51

> Is it not enough for them that We have sent down to you the Book, ˹which is˺ recited to them. Surely in this ˹Quran˺ is a mercy and reminder for people who believe

**An Answer That Replaces Rather Than Refutes**

The demand for signs is met here with a question about whether the audience has been given enough already, and the sentence nominates the Book as the sufficient proof. The move is characteristic of the sūrah's method throughout: instead of arguing about the withheld miracle, the Book points to the object that is already in the room. The recitation was not a private document held by the Prophet; it was delivered in public, heard by the community, and available to opponents who could quote it back to him line by line. The verse treats that availability as the heart of the answer. A sign that can be examined, memorized, and disputed in the open is a stronger claim on an audience than a spectacle that ends when the spectators go home.

**Why Recitation Matters to the Argument**

The wording draws attention to the fact that the Book is recited to them, and the oral delivery is not incidental. The audience is not required to accept a message secondhand or to consult a scholar who has seen a manuscript; the words arrive in their own language, in their own gathering places, in the voice of a man they know. The Book makes the same point when it describes itself as an explanation for people and as something made easy for remembrance (54:17), and when it insists that the Prophet was not a poet whose phrasing could be dismissed as art (36:69). The verse's argument depends on that plainness. The sign is its content, and the content is delivered without an intermediary that could corrupt it.

Mercy answers a need and a reminder answers a lapse, and one recitation is described from both directions in a single sentence. One hearer receives what he has been missing while another is told what he already knew, and the Book refuses to sort its audience into those categories in advance.

**A Remedy, a Guide, and a Mercy**

The Book names its own contents in a single sentence that this verse's language echoes: it announces to humanity that a warning has come from their Lord, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide, and a mercy for believers (10:57). The four terms describe what a recitation does rather than what it contains. A warning alerts, a cure repairs, a guide directs, and a mercy relieves, and each of them is offered as the effect of hearing the text rather than of reading about it. The sūrah's claim that the recitation is sufficient for its audience is thus a claim about its function. Verse after verse, the Book presents itself as the working instrument of guidance, and the demand for an additional sign is answered by pointing to an instrument that is already in use.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:52

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Sufficient is Allah as a Witness between me and you. He ˹fully˺ knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And those who believe in falsehood and disbelieve in Allah, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”

**Why a Witness Is the Right Category**

The dispute in Makkah was not primarily about astronomy or arithmetic; it was about whether the man speaking was telling the truth about what he had received. A witness is exactly what such a question requires, and the Book is careful to name one whose competence no party can question. The verse goes on to give the ground of that competence: God knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth, which means He has full access to the fact at issue and to the motives of everyone discussing it. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the same sufficiency in the context of the Prophet being denied, noting that God bears witness to what He has sent down, that He sent it with His knowledge, and that the angels also bear witness, while God alone is sufficient (4:166).

**"Between Me and You"**

The phrase is forensic, and it fixes the two parties to the disagreement precisely. One man brings a message and a community denies it, and there is no third party on the ground with jurisdiction to determine who is right. The Book records the same impasse elsewhere when the disbelievers tell the Prophet flatly that he is no messenger, and he is told to answer that God is sufficient as a witness between him and them, along with those who have knowledge of the scripture (13:43). The addition in that verse is instructive, since it shows that the witness in this arrangement is not silent: people who have studied earlier revelations count as testimony in the Book's understanding of evidence, and the sūrah has already reported that some of them believed.

**The Vocabulary of Loss**

The final word of the verse comes from the language of trade, and the audience addressed was composed largely of merchants who would have felt the weight immediately. A person described as being in loss has seen his capital disappear; in the Book the term describes a life spent on goods that turn out to have no value at the settlement. Sūrah al-Kahf states the principle in its sharpest form when it reports the complaint of those who discover that their works have been scattered, and the sūrah under discussion has already described false protectors as a spider's web. The two images agree: what the losers purchased with their lives could never have held, and the bill for it is presented at the only moment when the purchase cannot be reversed.

The register is drawn from the market the audience knew best. Profit and loss describe outcomes a trader measures in a ledger, and the Book uses that vocabulary for the Hereafter because the calculation involved is of the same kind: something given up in the present against something received later.

**The Prophet as a Witness in His Turn**

The Book assigns the Prophet the same office the verse assigns to God, and it does so in passages that define his mission: he has been sent as a witness, a deliverer of good news, and a warner (33:45; 48:8). The two uses of the word are connected. In this life the messenger serves as a witness over the community that received him, and the Book describes a moment when every community will produce its witness and the Prophet will be brought as a witness over his own (4:41). The verse under discussion names God as the witness between the two parties in the present dispute, which places the Prophet's testimony and his opponents' denial inside a record that is already complete. The sentence does not need to argue about who is right, because the administration of the case has been described from both ends.

**The Difference Between a Witness and a Referee**

One further distinction belongs to the sentence, and it concerns what a witness is for. A witness does not settle a dispute at the moment he is named; he establishes what happened, and the settlement comes later. The Book names God as witness here and elsewhere without promising that the disagreement in Makkah would be resolved in the Prophet's lifetime, and the arrangement tells the reader where the outcome is stored. The community being addressed is not asked to win; it is asked to conduct itself in the knowledge that the record is being kept by someone who cannot be misled.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53

> They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the punishment. Had it not been for a time already set, the punishment would have certainly come to them ˹at once˺. But it will definitely take them by surprise when they least expect it

**The Challenge That Recurs in the Book**

The demand for the punishment to be produced on the spot is one of the most frequently reported features of the disbelievers' speech in the Qur'an. They ask when the threat will come to pass if the message is true (10:48; 21:38), some of them go so far as to pray for stones to fall from the sky or for a painful punishment to overtake them if the claim is genuine (8:32), and the Book records the same attitude toward the Hour in Sūrah al-Shūrā, where those who disbelieve in it ask to hasten it mockingly while the believers are wary of it, knowing it to be the truth (42:18). The verse under discussion gathers this behaviour into one sentence and answers it, and the answer is not an argument but a fact about time.

The recurrence also tells the reader that the demand is not a feature of one audience's temperament. It reappears with the same logic in several chapters, which suggests a standing tendency in settled communities to measure a message by the speed of its results.

**The Appointed Term and Its Uses in the Book**

The phrase rendered as a time already set appears throughout the Qur'an in several senses, and the commentators list them: the span of a human life, the term set for the sun and the moon, the appointed end of the heavens and the earth, the deadline for a debt, and the moment fixed for a punishment. Its use here belongs to the last category, and Sūrah Nūḥ makes the point in the voice of a prophet: God will forgive and delay a people's end until the appointed time, and when the time set by God comes it cannot be delayed, if only they knew (71:4). The term is therefore a fixed quantity in the Book's understanding of history, recorded before the events it governs, and it is not a quantity that the challenged party can influence by shouting.

**Surprise as the Method of Arrival**

The verse's last clause states that the punishment will take them suddenly while they are unaware, and the Book applies this description to the ruin of communities across several chapters. It reports the people who were seized by surprise after their complaints had been converted into plenty (7:95), and it says of a heedless people that their provisions were poured out until, at the moment they took pride in what they had been given, the seizure came upon them unawares (6:44). The pattern is not incidental. A warning that arrives on the day the warning was issued would function as a transaction; a warning that arrives on a different day tests whether the listener took the message seriously in the meantime.

The manner of arrival also disposes of any expectation that the sentence can be scheduled. People who ask for judgment in this life usually assume they will know when it is coming, and the Book answers with the image of an arrival that finds a town unprepared, which removes the interval they were counting on.

**Patience Commanded With a Fixed Routine**

The Book answers the demand for haste not with an argument about timing but with a schedule. The Prophet is told to be patient with what they say and to glorify his Lord before sunrise and before sunset, in the hours of the night, and at both ends of the day, with the promise that he will be pleased with the reward (20:130); the same opening command is repeated with a shorter list in another chapter (50:39). In each case the answer to provocation is a routine of worship rather than a rebuttal. The verse under discussion shows the demand for an early sentence being made; the passages about patience show what the Prophet was told to do while the demand stood unanswered. What the impatient were asking for would have ended the interval in which worship was still possible.

**How Believers Are Supposed to Read the Same Delay**

The verse is addressed to a Prophet who had been asked to speed up the judgment, and the correction applies to his own impatience as much as to the mockery of his opponents. The contrast drawn in Sūrah al-Shūrā is precise: those who disbelieve ask to hasten it, and the believers are afraid of it precisely because they know it is real (42:18). The two attitudes are separated by knowledge rather than by temperament. What they ask for is deliverance, and what they receive is a schedule. Nor does the verse treat the mockery as a small matter, since the attitude it describes is the practical form of a settled decision: a person who jokes about the judgment has already placed himself outside the conversation the judgment is meant to provoke, and the sūrah has spent many verses describing what happens to communities that reached that stage.

The Book treats the inference as the central error of the heedless, and it states the correction in another chapter with unusual directness: if God punished people immediately for what they have done, He would not have left a single living being on the earth, but He delays them for an appointed term, and when the term arrives He sees His servants (35:45).

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:54

> They urge you to hasten the punishment. And Hell will certainly encompass the disbelievers

**Encompassing as the Opposite of Escape**

The verb used here describes being closed in on all sides, and it fits the sūrah's earlier treatment of the same theme with precision. Two verses were given earlier to the impossibility of flight: people cannot escape God on earth or in heaven, and they have no protector or helper besides Him (29:22). The fire that encompasses them is the answer to every attempted exit, and the Book's descriptions of the final state consistently use this vocabulary of enclosure. What the disbelievers in this chapter have been doing throughout is looking for arrangements that would place them outside the reach of judgment — patrons, wealth, deliberate demands, and delays. The sentence states the outcome of all of them at once.

Encirclement also removes the spatial reasoning people use when they estimate a danger. A fire ahead can be avoided by moving back and one below can be escaped by climbing, but a thing that encompasses has no outside from which to approach it, and this word leaves the challengers nowhere to put themselves.

**Why Hell Is Named Where the Audience Asked for Proof**

The mockery in the previous verses asked for a demonstration, and the Book grants the request in the only form that suits the moment. A fire described in advance is a warning; a fire experienced is a fact, and the people demanding a fact are told which one they will receive. The Book's habit of answering a challenge with the reality behind it appears in several chapters, and the exchange it recounts in Sūrah al-Anfāl is the clearest: the people pray for stones or a painful punishment if the message is true, and the Book records the prayer without comment. What the sentence under discussion adds is the certainty that the prayer is already answered in a manner and at a time that the speakers did not choose.

**A Fire That Reaches the Inside**

The Book describes the punishment of the Hereafter in a way that rules out any reading of it as an external event with no interior. One chapter names it as God's kindled Fire, which rages over the hearts (104:6–7). The detail is exact: the organ that carried the mockery, the refusal, and the appetite is the organ described as burning. The verse under discussion threatens a fire that scorns the faith, and the Book's own description of that fire locates its work in the place where the choice was made. This is the reason the sūrah keeps tying the outcome to what people concealed and what they intended. The punishment described in the Book is not an accident that happens to a body; it is the consequence of a condition that began in a person's interior and was never repaired.

**What the Believers Were Told About the Same Fire**

The Book does not leave the image one-sided, and it places the corresponding promise in the vicinity of the same warnings. Those who believe and do good are told that they will be housed in elevated mansions in Paradise with rivers flowing beneath them, to remain there forever, and the Book calls that the excellent reward of those who work righteousness and who patiently endure while putting their trust in their Lord (29:58–59). The pairing is the sūrah's method of stating its two certainties in one place. The fire and the gardens are both described as attainments that no one can avoid reaching, and the only variable in the account is the direction a person faces while he lives. The two promises stand in the same chapter within four verses of one another, which is the Book's usual proportion: the warning is stated plainly and the invitation is placed where a reader who has been shaken by the warning can still find it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:55

> on the Day the punishment will overwhelm them from above them and from below their feet. And it will be said, “Reap what you sowed.”

**The Encirclement Given a Shape**

The previous verse stated that the fire would encompass the disbelievers; this one describes the enclosure in spatial terms. Punishment arrives from above their heads and from beneath their feet, and no direction is left open. The Book uses the same construction elsewhere when it describes a torment that comes from above and from below, and the image belongs to its wider vocabulary for a situation that cannot be escaped by movement. The people addressed in this sūrah had spent its argument looking for exits through patrons, wealth, and demands for proof. The sentence gives them a picture of the moment when every exit is closed at once, and it does so without any description of fire or smoke. The geometry does the whole of the work.

**Why the Two Directions Are Named**

The Book's historical punishments in this chapter came from exactly these two directions, and the correspondence is not accidental. The towns were overturned and rained upon from above; Korah was swallowed from below; floods came up and storms came down. The verse therefore presents the final punishment as a summary of everything the reader has already witnessed in the sūrah's narratives, and it treats the earlier judgments as previews of a single event. A community that had read about stones from the sky and the opening of the earth now finds both directions occupied at once, and its own body standing between them.

The two directions also cover the two ways people imagine escape. One group plans to cross a border, and another counts on the solid ground beneath its feet; naming the sky and the earth removes both options without an argument, since the pair describes the whole of the space in which a person could hope to hide.

**"Reap What You Sowed"**

The sentence that closes the verse is a harvest saying, and the Book's use of agricultural imagery for the consequences of deeds appears throughout. The Qur'an states that every soul will be paid back for what it has done (45:22), and it addresses the people of the fire with the command to taste what they used to do in several places (32:14). What the harvest maxim adds is the interval between planting and reaping, which is precisely the interval the sūrah has been discussing in the mockery of its opponents. The disbelievers had asked for the punishment to be hurried, as though the harvest could be pulled forward by the laborers. The answer given here is that the field belongs to the owner, and the crop arrives when it is ripe.

**The Sentence Heard by Everyone Standing There**

The words of this verse are addressed to the people who mocked the warning, but they are spoken aloud in the hearing of everyone present, and that public character belongs to the scene. The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter consistently include the condemned party's own admissions, and the effect of those admissions is to remove the possibility of a defence at the only moment when a defence would matter. The voice that says "reap what you sowed" is not answering an argument; it is describing a harvest in front of the people who planted the field. The sūrah's method of argument in this world has been to present evidence and let the audience decide, while the world to come operates by disclosure, and the verse marks the boundary between the two methods.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:56

> O My believing servants! My earth is truly spacious, so worship Me ˹alone˺

**A Call That Treats Believers as Servants**

The verse changes address completely, and its opening is one of the most affectionate constructions in the Book: the One who speaks calls the believers His servants. The word chosen is the same one used for every creature, but attached here to faith it becomes a title of honour, and the plural possessive fixes a relationship rather than a status. The command that follows is deceptively short — worship Me — and the whole force of the sentence lies in the clause between the address and the command, which supplies a fact the audience needed: the earth is spacious. In a chapter that has documented the pressure on believers from every angle, the instruction is not to endure the pressure where it stands but to recognize that the ground underneath them is not confined to one city.

**The Earth's Width as a Practical Answer**

The verse was reportedly revealed in connection with Muslims who could not practise their religion openly in Makkah and who were advised to emigrate to Abyssinia, where they would be free to worship. The report fits the situation the sūrah has described from its opening, where the pressure on the first community included confinement, intimidation, and the prevention of migration itself. The Book's answer to that condition is not a promise that the pressure will lift but an assertion about geography. A person whose worship is impossible in one place is told that the earth does not consist of that place, and the sentence treats the discovery of this fact as a matter of obedience rather than a concession to weakness.

The reply is also unusually practical for a chapter so concerned with the Hereafter. Instead of asking the believers to endure a specific harm, the verse points them to a place where the harm does not exist, which is the Book's standard advice to a community whose worship has become impossible where it lives.

**The Question the Angels Will Ask**

The seriousness of the instruction is stated in another chapter where the angels seize the souls of those who have wronged themselves and ask them what state they were in. When they answer that they were oppressed in the land, the angels reply by asking whether God's earth was not spacious enough for them to emigrate in, and the verse reports their destination with the phrase that it is an evil journey's end (4:97). The passage continues with an exemption for those who genuinely cannot find a way out, describing helpless men, women, and children, for whom the Book allows the hope of pardon (4:98–99). Read together with this verse, the two passages establish both halves of the rule: the earth's width is a fact to be acted on, and anyone truly trapped inside a narrower space is not held responsible for what he cannot do.

**A Command That Cannot Be Framed as Retreat**

The Book never presents migration in these passages as a defeat. It is described as a transfer of operations to ground where the work can proceed, and the reward attached to it in the Book is stated in both worlds. Sūrah al-Naḥl promises those who emigrated after being persecuted a good home in this life and a better reward in the hereafter, and describes them as people who endured patiently and placed their trust in their Lord (16:41–42). The two clauses are the answer to the objections the reader might raise. The first makes the move materially possible, and the second names the dispositions that make it a form of worship rather than an escape.

**The Earth as a Place Given to the Righteous**

The Book treats the habitable earth as something granted to a particular kind of inhabitant, and it states the arrangement twice: Moses reassures his people that the earth belongs to God alone, that He grants it to whoever He chooses of His servants, and that the outcome belongs to the righteous (7:128), and another passage reports that God decreed in the Scriptures that His righteous servants shall inherit the land (21:105). The command to emigrate in this verse sits inside that promise. A believer who leaves a hostile place is not abandoning a territory to its owners; he is moving within an earth whose title belongs elsewhere, and the sūrah's instruction to spread out in it is the practical form of the assurance. The spaciousness of the land is mentioned because the entitlement is not what the residents assume it is.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:57

> Every soul will taste death, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned

**The Fact That Makes the Command Reasonable**

The sentence supplies the reason for the instruction that precedes it. A believer told to leave his city for the sake of his worship might reasonably ask what remains of the life he is leaving behind, and the verse answers before the objection is formed: the life is leaving by itself. Death is stated as a universal fact rather than as a threat, and the Book repeats the formulation in several chapters with almost the same words, so that it functions as a fixed element of the reader's mental furniture rather than as a warning directed at particular people. What the sentence establishes is a level floor. The migrant and the man who stayed, the persecutor and the persecuted, the king in his tower and the slave in the quarry all arrive at the same appointment.

**No Exemption and No Bargain**

The universality is stated without qualification, and the Book reinforces it elsewhere in terms that leave no room for negotiation: every being on earth is bound to perish, and only the Lord, full of majesty and honour, remains (55:26–27). The sūrah has already addressed a community that placed its confidence in wealth, patrons, and power, and it has described their arrangements as a spider's web. The sentence now removes the one asset that all those arrangements were designed to protect. A person who trusted in his own longevity is told that his longevity is a fixed quantity, and no fortress alters the schedule: wherever a person may be, death will overcome him even if he is inside a fortified tower (4:78).

**The Return That Follows the Tasting**

The second half of the verse moves from the event to its consequence, and the wording keeps the two connected. The soul does not simply stop; it returns to the One who made it, and the return is stated in the plural address that gathers every person into one destination. The Book's full formula elsewhere attaches the reward to this return: every soul will taste death, and each will receive its full payment on the Day of Judgment, and whoever is spared the fire and admitted to the garden has prospered, while the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment (3:185). The verse's brevity assumes that formula, and it compresses the whole of the hereafter into two clauses so that a person can carry them.

The return also settles the question the sūrah has been arguing about. A community told that the dead are finished has no reason to weigh its conduct, and the verse closes that position by placing a destination at the end of every life, including the lives of those who were driven from their homes.

**Death as the Appointment Nobody Skips**

The Book presents death as an appointment that cannot be anticipated or postponed by the person it concerns, and it makes the point by rejecting the fantasy of escape: say that the death being fled will inevitably meet its subject, after which he is returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen and informed of what he used to do (62:8). The wording of this verse sits in that same register. Every soul is to taste death, and the sentence is stated as a fact of creation rather than a threat aimed at anyone. What the Book adds elsewhere is the destination that follows the appointment, and it is that sequel which gives the verse its function in the sūrah: it tells a community weighing the cost of emigration that the one thing they cannot bargain about with anyone is the date being written down for them.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58

> ˹As for˺ those who believe and do good, We will certainly house them in ˹elevated˺ mansions in Paradise, under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness!˺—

**A Promise Framed as a Residence**

The reward is announced in the vocabulary of housing, and the choice is deliberate in a chapter that has just instructed believers to leave their homes for the sake of their worship. The Book does not say the emigrants will be compensated with gardens; it says they will be settled in elevated chambers, and the verb carries the sense of giving a person a place to stay after a period of movement. Sūrah al-Naḥl uses the same language for the reward of this life when it promises those who emigrated after being persecuted a good home, while noting that the reward of the hereafter is greater (16:41). The sūrah under discussion moves directly to the greater one, and it describes a dwelling that no hostile authority can take from its occupants.

The order of the two sentences is part of the instruction. A promise offered before a warning reads as an inducement, while the same promise placed after one reads as an assurance to people who have already been told what their position costs. The Book follows that sequence whenever it addresses a community under pressure, and it does so here because the audience had just been commanded to leave the ground they were standing on. What they received first was the difficulty of the command; what followed was the address they were moving toward.

**Chambers Raised One Above the Other**

Commentators note that the mansions promised here are described in another chapter as elevated chambers built one above the other with rivers flowing beneath them, and the Book attaches a guarantee to that description: it is the promise of God, and God never fails in His promise (39:20). The architecture is worth pausing over. Elevation in the Book is consistently associated with rank and with security from what lies below, and the chambers of this verse are described as elevated within a garden rather than as a tower in a landscape. The image answers the sūrah's earlier picture of the spider's house directly. Where a person's shelter was once a structure he spun for himself, it is now a residence prepared for him, and the two are as different in origin as they are in permanence.

**The Rooms That Are Seen From Within and Without**

The Prophet described these dwellings in a report transmitted on the authority of ʿAlī and graded ḥasan by al-Albānī: in Paradise there are rooms whose outside can be seen from within them and whose inside can be seen from without, and when a man from the desert asked who they were for, he answered that they are for those who speak good words, feed others, fast regularly, and stand in prayer at night while people sleep (Sunan al-Tirmidhī, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). The description is unusual in its emphasis on visibility. The rooms are not hidden behind walls but transparent, which fits a dwelling whose occupants have nothing to conceal, and the qualifications attached in the report are the practices of people who had spent their lives on exactly that.

The verb the verse uses for the reward's condition is the ordinary word for labour, and it covers effort that produces nothing visible for a long time. A community scattered by pressure, working at trades in towns where it had no standing, is exactly the audience the sentence fits, and the promise attaches to the effort rather than to the result. Nothing in the wording requires the worker to see the outcome of his work, which is the assurance a migrant household most needed.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:59

> those who patiently endure, and put their trust in their Lord

**Patience as the Discipline of the Long Interval**

The Arabic term covers more than the endurance of pain. It describes holding oneself in check, and the Book applies it to the waiting that occupies most of a believer's life. The emigrants addressed earlier in the sūrah were waiting before they left and waiting after they arrived; the community under pressure in Makkah was waiting for relief that might be years away. The Book's instruction to seek help through patience and prayer in another chapter is addressed to believers who are being asked to remain steady without a schedule (2:153). The quality named in this verse is what makes that interval survivable, and the Book attaches a promise to its company rather than to its results.

**Trust in God as the Ground of the Patience**

The second quality explains how the first is possible. The Arabic term describes entrusting one's affairs to another and relying on him to manage them, and the Book uses it for the believer's relationship to God in the specific context of provision and outcome. Sūrah al-Ṭalāq states the connection in one verse: God provides for a person from sources he could never imagine, and whoever places his trust in God finds Him sufficient, since God achieves His purpose and has set a destiny for everything (65:3). Trust in this understanding is not optimism about circumstances; it is a decision about who is handling them. The sūrah has already told its readers that the earth is spacious and that death is certain, and the verse's two qualities are what a person needs to act on both facts at once.

**Why These Two Define the Migrant**

Commentators observe that the believers described here are set against the disbelievers of the preceding verses, who demanded that the punishment be hastened and mocked the delay. The contrast is exact. One group cannot tolerate the interval and tries to force the outcome forward; the other endures the interval and leaves the outcome with its Lord. Sūrah al-Naḥl describes the same people with the same pair of words, saying of those who emigrated and were persecuted that they endured patiently and placed their trust in their Lord (16:42). The two dispositions therefore mark the community that accepted the cost of leaving home, and the Book presents them as the alternative to impatience rather than as an additional set of obligations.

**Patience That Is Not Passivity**

The Book's usage prevents the misunderstanding that patience means doing nothing. The same chapters that praise endurance describe the emigrants packing up and moving, the fighters standing their ground, and the Prophet consulting his companions before a decision and then placing his trust in God (3:159). What the quality removes is not action but anxiety about the outcome, and it is precisely what allows action to be taken under threat. A person who believes that the result depends entirely on his own arrangements has a reason to avoid risks; the person who has entrusted the result has a reason to accept them. The verse's two qualities therefore support the practical obedience described throughout the sūrah, from keeping the prayer to leaving a city.

**The Company This Pair Joins**

The Book attaches these dispositions to some of its highest descriptions. God is with the patient (2:153), He loves those who trust in Him (3:159), and the promise of assistance follows the command to persevere rather than to prevail (8:46). The verse under discussion places patience and trust at the end of a chapter that has argued about evidence, judgment, and the fate of nations, and it does so without adding any other condition. What the sūrah leaves with its reader is a definition of the person who receives the mansions described in the previous verse: someone who waits without demanding, and who acts without owning the result.

The Book keeps a roll of people whose lives make the same argument. Abraham leaving his father's house, Moses leaving Egypt, the youths leaving a city of idolaters, and the believers leaving Makkah all stand in that line, and the verse places its audience inside a company whose members had already survived the choice it asks of them.

**Remembering God in Every Posture**

The Book asks for remembrance in circumstances that leave no room for a scheduled appointment: believers remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth (3:191), and the instruction given to the community after battle is to remember God in the same three postures, then to establish prayer properly once they are secure (4:103). The two verses together show why the sūrah attaches patience and gratitude to the promise of a home. Neither quality can be practised only in a mosque or only in a moment of quiet. They are the practices that survive a journey, a night in a strange village, and a season of waiting, and the Book's pairing of the two with the reward of the righteous is an instruction about what to do while the reward is still pending.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:60

> How many are the creatures that cannot secure their provisions! ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ provides for them and you ˹as well˺. He is indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**An Argument Drawn From the Animal World**

The verse turns to a category of creatures that have no reserves, no stores, and no arrangements for the future, and it treats their survival as evidence. The Arabic word used here covers everything that moves on the earth, and the point made about them is precise: they do not carry their own provision and they do not gather what they need in advance.

**The Provision Guaranteed in Another Chapter**

The Book states the same principle explicitly in another place: there is no moving creature on the earth whose provision is not guaranteed by God, and God knows where it lives and where it is laid to rest, with everything written in a clear record (11:6). The verse under discussion supplies the observation that leads to that conclusion, and the two belong together. What the Book asserts is not that provision is abundant or that creatures never go hungry; it is that the responsibility for supply has been taken up by someone who knows every creature's location and needs. The sentence under study adds the personal application immediately, since it names the readers along with the animals: God provides for them and for you.

The Book states the guarantee elsewhere as a rule about creation rather than as a comfort reserved for believers. Provision is assigned to every creature, and this verse's argument turns on that breadth, since it invites its audience to observe animals that store nothing and do not starve.

**The Trust That Sends the Bird Out**

The Prophet described the same mechanism in a report preserved in two collections: were people to rely on God with the reliance He deserves, they would be given provision as the birds are given it, going out in the morning with empty stomachs and returning in the evening with full ones (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2344; Sunan Ibn Mājah 4164, on the authority of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb). The comparison is often read as an argument for inactivity, and the wording prevents that reading, since the birds leave the nest. What the report commends is the departure without the stockpile. The trust lies in going out with an empty stomach, and the provision lies in the return, and the two halves of the day belong to the same animal.

**The God Who Provides as the Book Describes Him**

Other chapters fill out the title used here. God is described as the Supreme Provider, Lord of all power, ever mighty (51:58), and the Prophet is told in another passage that his Lord does not ask him to supply anyone, but supplies him, and that the outcome belongs to the righteous (20:132). The Book's argument in these verses is not that effort is unnecessary but that the effort is not the source. A person works, plants, trades, and travels, and the result arrives through arrangements no one controls. The verse's question about the creatures that cannot secure their own provision is a way of stating that fact in a form nobody can dispute, and the sūrah's audience would have seen the birds on every rooftop in the city.

**The Book Refuses to Separate Children From Provision**

The Book treats anxiety about food as one of the enduring temptations in matters of family, and it addresses it with a prohibition: parents are told not to kill their children for fear of poverty, with the assurance that God provides for them and for the parents, and the act is called a heinous sin (17:31). The order of the promise in that verse is telling, because the children's provision is mentioned before the parents'. The verse under discussion makes the same argument in the context of migration. A household that moves for the sake of its religion does not move into a vacuum, and the Book's pattern is to name the fear, forbid the act it produces, and attach the supply to the obligation. Believers are being asked to trust a provider whose record, in the Book's account, begins with the creatures that cannot negotiate.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:61

> If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, “Allah!” How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺

**A Question the Opponents Answer Themselves**

The sentence instructs the Prophet to put a question to the very people who were disputing with him, and the answer is supplied in advance because it was never in doubt. The idolaters of Makkah did not deny a supreme creator; they acknowledged that the heavens and the earth belonged to God and treated the idols as intermediaries or subordinates rather than as rivals to the maker. The Book records the same admission in several chapters, where the disbelievers confess under questioning that the earth and those on it belong to God and that He is the Lord of the seven heavens (23:84–86), and where they are asked who provides from the sky and the earth, who owns hearing and sight, and who conducts every affair, with the answer again being God (10:31). The sūrah builds its case on ground the other side has already conceded.

**What Subjecting the Sun and Moon Adds**

The phrase about the sun and the moon introduces a further element: not merely creation but arrangement for human benefit. The two great bodies move on courses that make agriculture, travel, and the marking of time possible, and the Book treats that serviceability as evidence of an intention toward the people who benefit from it. Elsewhere it lists the subjects of this arrangement — the night wrapped around the day, the sun and moon running to a fixed term — and treats each as a mark of the One who set them going (39:5). An audience that had already accepted the creator was therefore being asked to notice that the same agent had also organized the world for their use. The confession being sought was, in other words, already larger than the question that produced it.

**The Contradiction Inside the Confession**

The verse's astonishment is aimed at a specific inconsistency. The people it describes affirmed God as creator and provider, and then worshiped objects they knew had produced nothing. The Book repeats this diagnosis in several places and always with the same puzzled tone, asking the idolaters why they are not mindful after giving the right answer (23:85), why they are so deluded (23:89), and whether they will not then fear Him (10:31). The charge is not that the Meccans were atheists. It is that they held a belief whose consequences they refused, and the sūrah treats the refusal as the real subject of the dispute.

The contradiction is also a matter of timing. The confession is made in this life while the benefit is being enjoyed, and the complaint is that the acknowledgement arrives at the point where it costs nothing, since the same people recognize their lord in difficulty and forget him once the danger has passed. The theme of believing in God without obeying Him runs through the Qur'an, and the sūrah has already touched it in its portrait of the wavering man who accepted the community's faith and abandoned it under pressure.

A community that answers correctly in its own words has supplied the premise of the case against it, and the Book's arguments with Makkah frequently proceed that way, letting the opponents state the fact and then drawing the consequence.

**Gratitude as an Exchange God Has Announced**

The Book does not leave gratitude without a stated return. Believers are told that if they remember God, He will remember them, and are urged to be thankful rather than ungrateful (2:152), and a proclamation is reported in which God declares that gratitude will bring increase while ingratitude brings severe punishment (14:7). The argument of this verse closes on that arrangement. The question put to the audience about who sends down water and controls hearing and sight is not a quiz; it is the preamble to a claim about what is owed, and the Book's wider statements show what the owing looks like in practice. What the benefactor asks for is acknowledgement, and the punishment for withholding it falls on the one who withheld it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:62

> Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants. Surely Allah has ˹full˺ knowledge of everything

**A Statement About Distribution**

The verse concerns the distribution of provision, and it makes no attempt to present that distribution as a reward for merit in this world. The Book repeats the sentence in a number of chapters, and in every instance it uses the same pair of verbs: God outspreads provision for whom He wills and straitens it for whom He wills (13:26; 34:36; 42:12; 39:52). The statement is not a complaint about unfairness, and it is not an explanation of poverty; it is a fact about administration. Since the previous verse established that the same God is acknowledged by the disbelievers as the creator of the sun and the moon, the sentence now extends the argument from creation to provision. The one who arranged the universe for human benefit is the one who rations out what each person receives.

The statement also covers the timing of distribution. What a person receives at a given moment may be less than he wants or more than he needs, and the arrangement is described as measured in both directions rather than as a single rule about plenty.

**The Two Verbs and the Shape of a Life**

The Arabic verbs describe the widening and narrowing of a supply, and the metaphor suggests a hand that opens and closes. The Book applies it to nations as well as to individuals, and it treats the alternation as a feature of existence rather than as an occasional intervention. Commentators note that this pair of verbs serves as the pivot of these final verses, moving from God as creator to God as sustainer, and that the audience's own experience confirmed the description: the same households that had prospered in one season found their trade narrowed in the next. What the verse delivers is a framework for interpreting that experience, and the framework excludes both the assumption that wealth signals God's approval and the assumption that poverty signals His abandonment.

**The Warning Against Reading Wealth as Favour**

The Book's fuller statements make the point with unusual sharpness. Sūrah Sabaʾ records the Prophet being told to say that his Lord gives abundantly or sparingly to whom He wills, adding that most people do not know, and then stating that it is neither wealth nor children that bring a person closer to God, but belief joined to good conduct, which earns a multiplied reward and security in elevated chambers (34:36–37). The same chapter reports that whatever is spent in charity will be compensated by the best of providers (34:39). Taken with this verse, the passages establish that material condition is not the measure of a person's standing, and they do so by naming the actual measure in the same breath.

The warning also runs in the other direction. A person who receives less than his neighbours may read his condition as a sign of rejection, and the verse denies him that conclusion as firmly as it denies the wealthy man his comfort.

**Knowledge That Closes the Question**

The sentence ends with the statement that God has full knowledge of everything, and its placement answers the objection that a varied distribution invites. Human observers see a person's circumstances and not his ability, his responsibilities, his trials, or his capacity to be tested by abundance and by scarcity alike. Sūrah al-Shūrā attaches the same knowledge to the same statement of distribution, describing God as holding the keys of the treasuries of the heavens and the earth and giving abundantly or sparingly as He wills while knowing all things (42:12). The verse therefore does not merely assert a distribution; it asserts a distribution administered by someone who knows what each recipient can carry.

The word for knowledge here carries the sense of full awareness rather than of information stored on file. What the sentence rules out is the possibility that the distribution is careless, and the denial is aimed at the complaint of a person who has begun to suspect that the arrangement has no author paying attention to it.

**Wealth, Poverty, and the Test They Carry**

The Book nowhere treats either condition as a verdict, and this sentence is one of the reasons. Elsewhere it states that God expands provision for some and restricts it for others precisely so that the difference may function as a trial, and it warns that people who receive abundance may conclude that they have been honoured when the increase is in fact a delay (89:15–16). The verse under discussion leaves the purpose unnamed, and the omission is significant: it does not tell the reader which condition he is in, only who assigned it. That restraint matters for a community that had members on both sides of the divide. The merchant of Makkah who had lost his trade to pressure and the believer who had prospered were given one sentence to apply to two different situations, and the sentence fit both because it concerned the administrator rather than the arrangement.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:63

> And if you ask them who sends down rain from the sky, giving life to the earth after its death, they will surely say, “Allah!” Say, “Praise be to Allah!” In fact, most of them do not understand

**A Second Question With the Same Answer**

The sūrah returns to the method of the previous interrogation and selects a different piece of evidence. The questioner is told to ask who sends rain and revives the earth, and the answer is again supplied in advance, because the idolaters were as ready to acknowledge this as they were to acknowledge the creation of the heavens and the earth. The move is a narrowing of the argument. Creation is a claim about the distant past that no one in the audience had witnessed; the rain is an annual event that every farmer, herdsman, and traveler in the Hijaz watched with attention. By choosing the rainfall, the verse follows the Book's pattern of grounding its case in what the audience can verify from the previous season.

**Rain as the Book's Standing Proof of Resurrection**

The Book uses the revival of dead ground by water as its most frequent illustration of the resurrection, and it does so in chapters throughout the Qur'an. God drives the winds and sends the clouds to a lifeless land, causes rain to fall, and produces every kind of fruit, and the verse concludes that He will bring the dead to life in the same manner (7:57). Another chapter states it with equal directness: He gives life to the earth after its death, and so will you be brought forth from the graves (30:19). The rain is described as a provision for God's servants, and the verse adds that the emergence from the graves is similar to the emergence of the crops (50:9–11). The sign chosen in this verse therefore carries the whole argument for the Hereafter that the sūrah has been making since it sent its readers out to observe the beginning of creation.

**"Most of Them Do Not Understand"**

The verse closes by naming the defect, and it uses the language of understanding rather than of knowledge. The distinction matters in a chapter that has already told its readers that the parables of the Book are grasped only by people of knowledge. The idolaters of Makkah possessed the information, gave the correct answers to the questions, and lived as though the answers were not true. What they lacked was the capacity to connect what they affirmed with how they acted, and the Book treats that gap as a form of incomprehension rather than a shortage of data. The same diagnosis appears in the chapters that report similar interrogations, and it is the reason the Book's arguments in these passages are built from admissions rather than from new evidence.

The clause also explains why the argument has to be made at all. People who understood what their own answer implied would not need the question, and the Book's closing verdicts often name a failure of comprehension exactly where the facts have been conceded and their meaning has not.

**Rain Described as a Mercy With a Mechanism**

The Book describes rainfall in detail and then draws the conclusion from the detail. One passage follows the winds as they raise vapour, form clouds, spread them or pile them as God wills, until the rain falls on whom He wills and the servants rejoice (30:48), and the next lines ask the reader to observe the impact of God's mercy in giving life to dead earth and to conclude that the same power can raise the dead (30:50). The verse under discussion compresses that sequence into a single image of water sent from the sky reviving the land. The image is chosen because it is the one process everyone in the audience watched every year without being able to sponsor, stop, or explain. It is also the reason the sūrah can use the earth's revival as the standing proof of the resurrection, since the two events are described with the same verb.

**The Rain That Nobody Can Buy**

One reason the rainfall serves the argument so well is that no person can produce, divert, or purchase it. Wealth in Makkah could arrange caravans, hire protection, and build storehouses, and the sūrah has just told its readers that the distribution of that wealth is itself administered by God. The rain belongs to a category in which human arrangements are plainly absent, and the audience lived in a land where the arrival of the rainy season decided which households ate. The Book uses the image repeatedly for the resurrection because the correspondence is exact and visible: what falls from the sky does not come with an invoice, and it brings a dead landscape back to life in a season that no one controls. Acknowledging the sender of such a gift, the verse implies, is the smallest possible consequence of an admission the opponents had already made.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:64

> This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But the Hereafter is indeed the real life, if only they knew

**The Two Words for Play**

The Arabic term rendered as play covers idle activity undertaken for its own sake, and the second covers amusement, diversion, and everything a person uses to pass the time. The Book uses the pair for this world in more than one place, and Sūrah al-Ḥadīd gives the fuller catalogue: play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children, illustrated by rain that causes plants to grow to the delight of the planters, after which the vegetation dries, turns yellow, and is reduced to chaff (57:20). The image and the vocabulary agree. What the Book objects to is not pleasure but preoccupation, and the picture it paints is of a field that receives real labour and produces something that blows away.

The pair also describes two kinds of activity rather than one. Diversion is what a person picks up to pass an hour, and play is what he does with others to fill a season, so the two words together cover a life organized around amusement.

**"The Real Life"**

The Book's use of the word life for the Hereafter implies that the present existence is a preparation rather than the thing itself, and Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the matter bluntly when it says that whoever is spared the fire and admitted to the garden has triumphed, while the life of this world is nothing but the delusion of enjoyment (3:185). The expression is precise. A delusion of enjoyment is not the absence of enjoyment; it is pleasure that carries the impression of permanence while being temporary. The sūrah under discussion has been reporting the fates of nations who organized everything around such pleasure, and the sentence arrives at the end of that history with a claim about which of the two lives deserves the name.

The phrase also tells the reader where the word life properly applies. The Book does not deny that the present existence is real; it denies that this existence is what the word means when it is used without qualification, which is a claim about priority rather than about existence.

**Not a Condemnation of the World's Goods**

The verse does not forbid the use of what God has provided, and the Book says elsewhere that people should take their adornment and eat and drink while avoiding excess, since God has not prohibited the good things He created for His servants (7:32). Commentators reading this sentence stress that the fault lies in attachment rather than in possession, and that the Book's complaints about wealth are always about what a person does with it and what he trusts in it. The sūrah's merchant audience was not being asked to abandon trade; it was being told what trade is worth relative to what it can be exchanged for, and the sentence states the exchange rate without apology.

The Book's own statements about provision make it clear that the goods themselves are not the problem. Food, spouses, children, and safe homes are described elsewhere as gifts, and the verse's complaint falls on the use of those gifts as an end that leaves no room for the giver.

**The Verse's Word for Knowledge**

The sentence ends with the phrase "if only they knew," and the qualification places the problem in the same faculty the previous verse identified as missing. The information being withheld from the people described is not a fact about the world but a fact about proportion, and the Book treats the recognition of proportion as a form of knowledge rather than as a moral achievement. A person who understands what the two lives are does not need to be persuaded to prefer one over the other; the preference follows from the understanding. The Book's argument in these verses is therefore addressed to the reader's assessment rather than to his appetite, and the complaint registered in the clause is that the assessment has not been made at all.

The word also keeps the sentence from being a complaint about intelligence. The people described are not short of information; they are short of the use of it, and the verdict falls on a failure a reader can correct in this life rather than on a limitation he cannot.

**A Better Comparison Offered to the Doubters**

The Book puts the comparison into a question that its audience is invited to answer: is this present life better, or the Garden of Eternity that the righteous have been promised as a reward and a final destination (25:15)? The verse under discussion assumes the same contrast and states one side of it — that the life of this world is nothing but amusement and diversion. What the other passage supplies is the second term of the comparison, and with it the reason the sūrah's claim is not simply a denial of the world's value. The Book does not ask its readers to pretend that present enjoyments are unpleasant. It asks them to weigh them against something described as eternal, and it leaves the arithmetic to the reader, which is why the form is a question rather than a verdict.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:65

> If they happen to be aboard a ship ˹caught in a storm˺, they cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion. But as soon as He delivers them ˹safely˺ to shore, they associate ˹others with Him once again˺

**The Most Honest Moment of a Person's Life**

The verse describes a scene that the Book returns to in several chapters, and its power lies in the psychology it records. A person on a boat in a gale, with waves coming from every side and the hull about to fail, abandons his usual arrangements entirely. The Arabic phrase rendered as sincere devotion means that the prayer is offered to God alone, with no partner added, and the Book notes that this purity appears precisely when every other recourse has been removed. The scene is presented without irony. What the drowning sailor feels and does is correct, and the Book lets the reader see that the capacity was there all along.

The phrase used for the prayer in this moment is one the Book applies to worship offered to God with nothing added, and its appearance on a sinking ship is the point of the verse. Monotheism, in this account, is not a doctrine that requires instruction before it can be practised; it is the position a person takes when he is stripped of alternatives and left with the truth. The passengers in the story were not reciting a creed they had been taught, and several of them had spent years arguing against the doctrine in the marketplace. Their conduct in the storm shows where their reasoning actually settled when their lives were at stake, and the Book records the moment because it settles the dispute in the opponents' own voice. What the verse examines is not the sincerity of the prayer but the agreement it constitutes.

**Why the Sea Produces This Response**

The Book chooses the sea for a reason, and it states the reason in another chapter: when the ships are overcome by a gale and the passengers assume they are doomed, they cry out to God alone, promising to be grateful if He saves them (10:22). A storm at sea strips a person of every intermediate defense. There is no tribe to appeal to, no patron to invoke, no market to flee to, and the promises of the sea are limited to the wood beneath his feet. The sūrah has been reporting the world's other supports throughout — idols, wealth, patrons, and towers — and the verse now presents a situation in which all of them are simply absent. What remains is the appeal to God, and the Book's point is that the appeal is made without difficulty.

**The Return to Shore and the Return to Shirk**

The second half of the sentence reports what happens on land, and the change of conduct is as abrupt as the change of weather. Once delivered, the same people associate others with God again. The Book states the same pattern with variations in several places, describing those who forget every deity they normally invoke when hardship strikes at sea and turn away once they are safe, with the comment that human beings are ever ungrateful (17:67). Sūrah Luqmān reports that when the waves cover them like mountains they call upon God with pure devotion, and that only some of them remain relatively grateful after being delivered (31:32). The variation and the constancy of the pattern across chapters indicate that the Book is describing a general condition rather than one incident.

**The Gratitude That Did Not Follow**

The promise made in the storm is reported in another chapter, where the passengers pledge to be grateful if they are saved (10:22), and the sūrah under discussion notes what actually followed. The Book treats ingratitude as the characteristic human failing in these passages, and it names it in connection with precisely this scene. What the sentence describes is not a lapse of memory but a deliberate resumption of a practice that the person had abandoned under pressure and therefore knew to be false. The next verse takes up the conduct of the delivered passengers and states what their future holds, and the sūrah's final verses turn to the safety of the city they returned to.

Their conduct after the rescue is what the verse emphasizes, and it follows the same timeline as the confession: the appeal was made at the moment of greatest dependence, and the relapse came immediately after the relief. Nothing in the account suggests a period of reflection or a gradual drift, which makes the return to the old practices look like a decision rather than a lapse.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:66

> So let them be ungrateful for all We have given them, and ˹let them˺ enjoy themselves ˹for now˺! For they will soon know

**An Imperative That Concedes Rather Than Commands**

The sentence is phrased as permission, and the Book uses this construction when it has finished arguing and hands a people over to the consequences of what they have chosen. The form is not an invitation and not an encouragement; it is the removal of further resistance, and the Book employs it in other chapters with the same effect, telling the Prophet to leave to God those who reject the message and to let them laugh a little before they weep at length (9:82; 68:44). What the verse concedes to its subjects is exactly what they had been asking for throughout the sūrah: the freedom to continue as they were without further interruption. The tone is calm, and the calmness is what makes the sentence one of the more sober addresses in the chapter.

The verb also tells the reader how the Book handles people who have already decided. It does not pretend that the opponents will obey, and it does not withdraw the command, so the sentence holds both facts at once and leaves the responsibility for choosing with the people addressed.

**Enjoyment With an Expiry Date**

The permission to enjoy themselves carries a clause that limits it, and the clause is the whole weight of the sentence. "For now" is the translation's rendering of a word that marks a defined and short interval, and the Book uses the same term when it describes the portion of the disbelievers as brief and their end as painful (16:117), and when it says that the life of this world compared to the hereafter is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment (13:26). The permission therefore grants nothing beyond what was always going to be available, and it names the boundary in the same breath. What the listener is given is the pleasure of a season with the season's end attached.

The Book uses this short clause at the close of several warnings, which gives it a settled function. It marks the point where argument stops, since the hearers have been given the case and have answered it with mockery, and it leaves the outcome to be discovered rather than debated further.

**"They Will Soon Know"**

The verse ends with the Book's standard closure for those who refuse a warning, and the formula is used across the Qur'an: they will soon know, or they will come to know what they were warned about (37:170). The phrase has two functions. It declares the certainty of the outcome, and it refuses to state the schedule. Both parts matter in a chapter whose opponents had been demanding that the punishment be brought forward, since the answer they receive is neither a date nor a denial but an assurance that knowledge will arrive. The Book's eschatology consistently uses the language of disclosure for the moment of reckoning, and this short clause is one of its most compact statements of that principle.

The clause also leaves the form of the outcome unspecified. Nothing in the sentence says what shape the knowledge will take, and the restraint suits the sūrah's habit of letting the reader fill in what the Book has already described elsewhere.

**The Gift That Becomes Evidence**

The sentence draws on a pattern the Book describes in several chapters, where divine provision continues to flow to a people who reject the provider until the moment of seizure arrives. Sūrah al-Anʿām reports that when a community became heedless of the warnings, they were given everything they desired, and when they took pride in what they had been given they were seized by surprise (6:44). The permission in this verse and the mechanism in that one are two descriptions of the same interval. What looks like tolerance in the present is the time in which the account accumulates, and the Book names that accumulation for what it is rather than letting it pass as good fortune.

What they withheld was not a matter of courtesy, since the Book treats the acknowledgement of a gift as the recognition of its giver. By enjoying the provision while denying the provider, the people described here turned every benefit they received into evidence against themselves, and the verse names that consequence without adding a threat to it.

**The Difference Between Patience and Neglect**

The sentence raises a question that runs through the whole Book: how the delay of judgment should be understood by the people who are not its subjects. The verse's permission is given to the ungrateful, and the sūrah has already warned its own readers against reading the delay as an endorsement. What the believer is told elsewhere is that the long interval is the space in which a person can still turn, and the Book elsewhere describes God giving respite until a fixed term and then seizing (22:48). The same fact that hardens one party is the opportunity of the other, and the verse's tone takes the two possibilities into account at once.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:67

> Have they not seen how We have made ˹Mecca˺ a safe haven, whereas people ˹all˺ around them are snatched away? How can they then believe in falsehood and deny Allah’s favours

**A Sanctuary in a Violent Country**

The verse turns from the sea to the ground beneath the audience's feet, and it asks them to consider a fact about their own city. Makkah sat in a region where tribes raided one another and where settlements were emptied by violence, and it stood outside that pattern. The Book treats the town's immunity as an arrangement rather than a coincidence, and it elsewhere calls the site a safe sanctuary established for its inhabitants (28:57). The people being addressed had grown up inside that safety and had never known anything else, which is precisely the condition the verse is designed to disturb. Its question presses them to see a feature of their lives that habit had made invisible.

The Book treats the safety of the town as a fact its audience could verify hour by hour. Caravans were plundered on the roads around them, families were carried off in raids, and the contrast between the town's immunity and its neighbours' exposure was the first thing any traveller noticed, which is why the argument could be put as a question.

**The Provision That Came With the Safety**

The protection was not the only gift attached to the place. The Book records that the sanctuary received fruits of every kind as provision from God, brought by surrounding communities, and it adds that most of the people did not know this favour (28:57). A town in a barren valley with no agriculture and no trade goods of its own was fed because its status drew the caravans and their cargoes, and the whole arrangement rested on the sanctity of the site. The verse under discussion names the safety and leaves the markets unstated, and the two passages together cover both halves of the city's livelihood. What both are saying is that the prosperity of Makkah was a grant rather than an achievement.

**The Book's Vocabulary for the Wrong of Ingratitude**

Elsewhere the Book states that the security of a community is linked to its conduct, and it warns that God would never change a people's state of favour unless they changed their own state of faith (13:11). The verse under discussion does not threaten the sanctuary with removal, and it does not need to; it simply asks how long an arrangement can hold when the beneficiaries deny its source. The Book's treatment of the destroyed towns earlier in the chapter gives the reader the pattern in full, and the sūrah's placement of this verse near its close means the question arrives after that pattern has been established. What is left is for the inhabitants of the safe place to ask the question about themselves.

**Why the Sūrah Ends Where It Began**

The chapter has taken its reader through Noah, Abraham, Lot, Shuʿayb, two Arabian tribes, an Egyptian court, and a ruined civilization, and it now returns him to his own street. The Book uses this movement in several chapters: the lesson is brought home at the point where it can no longer be assigned to other people. The sanctuary of Makkah is the last piece of evidence named in the sūrah, and it is the one the audience could verify without traveling anywhere. Its conclusion is a question about two commitments held at the same time, and the verses that follow describe the people who hold them and the people who do not.

**Refuge Taken in a Place of Safety**

The Book treats the search for a protected place as an act of faith rather than a failure of nerve, and it records the prayer made by migrants who did exactly that: the youths who took refuge in the cave asked their Lord for mercy from Himself and for right guidance through their ordeal (18:10). The question in this verse — do they not see that We have made a secure sanctuary while people around them are being taken away — belongs to the same theme, and the sūrah has already commanded the believers to emigrate when their own town becomes uninhabitable. What the sanctuary proves, in the Book's argument, is that security is granted rather than produced. A town that enjoys it while denying the giver of it is enjoying a benefit whose source it has publicly rejected, and the verse's rebuke is aimed precisely at that inconsistency.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:68

> And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them? Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the disbelievers

**A Question That Claims the Highest Rank of Wrongdoing**

The Book states the same comparison in several chapters, and it always assigns the superlative to the same offence: fabricating lies about God or rejecting the truth after it has come (6:21; 6:93; 39:32). The form of the question is a challenge to the imagination rather than an assertion, and the answer it expects is that no one exceeds this offence in gravity. The reasoning behind that ranking is worth stating. A person who commits an ordinary wrong damages something created; a person who invents a religious claim about God damages the framework by which right and wrong are known at all, and he does it while quoting the highest authority in existence as his warrant.

The severity comes from what the offence denies rather than from the words used. A person who invents a claim about God has set himself up as a source of religious authority, and every practice built on his invention inherits the falsehood, which is why the Book keeps this offence in a category of its own.

**What Fabricating Against God Covers**

The phrase covers a wider field than deliberate forgery. The Book applies it to those who claim revelations they never received, to those who say they can produce the like of what God has sent down (6:93), and to those who attribute partners to God and then present the association as a divine arrangement. In the sūrah's own context, it applies to the men who invented a religion that promised immunity from the consequences of sin and sold it to their neighbours. The fabrication the verse names is therefore not confined to speech; it includes the system of claims by which a community is persuaded to live, and the Book treats the manufacture of such a system as a charge against its makers rather than against their victims.

The phrase also covers the subtler form of the offence. A person adds a rule, a prohibition, or a permission to the religion and attributes the addition to God, and the severity here falls on every claim to have been told something that was never told.

**The Mercy That Was Offered First**

The sūrah has already stated, in its earlier verses, that those who believe and do good have their offenses removed and are rewarded according to the best of what they did (29:7), and that the destruction in the historical passages never came without warning. The verdict in this verse follows the same order of events: the truth arrives before it is rejected, and the offer remains open while a person lives. The Book never presents the punishment in these chapters as a first move, and the phrase about arrival is the guard against that misreading. What the reader is being shown is the end of a process whose earlier stages the chapter has described in detail, and the question about fittingness is asked at the point where the process has run its course.

The order of the two clauses also shows what the messenger's work was for. The warning had to reach a people before the sentence could describe them, and the Book treats the years of argument and recitation as the period in which that condition was being met.

**The Same Sentence in Sūrah al-Zumar**

Sūrah al-Zumar carries an almost identical sentence: who then does more wrong than those who lie about God and reject the truth after it has reached them, and is Hell not a fitting home for the disbelievers (39:32)? The recurrence of the wording across chapters indicates that the Book treats this combination — invention and refusal — as the definitive form of the offence it has been describing throughout the sūrah. What the two passages share is the phrase about arrival, and its repetition across different audiences and different eras shows that the standard is not tailored to one community's circumstances. The same charge was available to be made against any people who received a messenger and answered him with a fabricated alternative.

The parallel also shows that the charge is not aimed only at the first audience. The same sentence appears wherever a community organizes itself around a claim that has no basis, and the Book expects its readers to recognize the pattern in their own surroundings as well as in the history it reports.

**Sincere Devotion Against Public Opinion**

The Book states the standard of worship in a form that anticipates resistance from the crowd: call upon God with sincere devotion, even to the dismay of the disbelievers (40:14). The phrase about the dismay of the disbelievers is the reason the verse under discussion places the fabrication of lies beside the denial of the truth. Between these two positions stands a person who has privately understood the case but will not say so publicly, and the Book's instruction that devotion be offered to God alone, whatever the audience thinks, addresses exactly that person. The sūrah has been dealing with a community that found its standing in an inherited cult; the sentence about the sincere and the deniers clears away both the originators of the doctrine and the crowd that keeps it going by not objecting.

## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69

> As for those who struggle in Our cause, We will surely guide them along Our Way. And Allah is certainly with the good-doers

**The Chapter's Closing Promise**

The sūrah ends with a sentence that answers the question it opened with. Its first verses asked whether people would be left untested once they claimed to believe, and its last verses promise guidance to those who struggle. The two statements belong together, and the order matters: the struggle is named first, and the guidance follows it, so that the reward described is attached to the effort rather than offered in place of it. The Book's placement of this promise at the end of a chapter full of ruined nations gives the reader a comparison to carry away. Every community described earlier in the sūrah had the same access to guidance and treated it as an inconvenience, and the final sentence describes what becomes of those who treat it as a road.

The promise also answers a question the sūrah has left open since its earliest verses. The chapter opened by describing tests that reveal what people are made of, and it closes by describing what the tested receive, so the two ends of the sūrah address the same reader at different stages of the same journey.

**What the Struggle in View Consists Of**

The term rendered as struggle covers exertion of every kind in the service of God, and the sūrah's own narratives illustrate the range. Abraham argued with his people, was threatened with fire, and emigrated. Lot preached to a town that mocked him. Noah spent centuries repeating a message to audiences that refused it. The believers of Makkah endured pressure, and some of them left their homes. The Book's usage does not restrict the term to warfare, and chapters elsewhere apply the word to striving against the disbelievers with the Qur'an itself (25:52) and to the pursuit of what brings a person closer to God (5:35). What unites the cases is the direction of the effort rather than the specific form it takes.

The struggle also has a definite object. It is directed at God rather than at a cause, a leader, or a community, and the phrasing keeps effort from becoming a performance staged for other people's approval, since the party being addressed already knows what the effort cost.

**The Company of Those Who Do Good**

The sentence closes with the assurance that God is with the good-doers, and the Book attaches this companionship to several qualities in different chapters, including patience (2:153), trust (3:159), and the avoidance of evil together with doing good (16:128). What the phrase contributes here is a relationship rather than a reward. The sūrah has described the world's false protectors throughout its length and compared them to a spider's web, and its final words name the one company that does not fail, offered to people whose conduct has made them eligible for it. The promise is stated in the present tense, which places the companionship in the life of the reader rather than in the hereafter alone.

The closing description also keeps the promise from becoming a private transaction. Those who strive are guided into the company of people who do good, so the reward described includes company as well as direction, and the sūrah ends on a community assembled out of a scattered audience.

**Why the Sūrah Ends With Struggle Rather Than Victory**

The Book could have closed the chapter with a description of the reward, and the sūrah has already described it a few verses earlier in the chambers of Paradise. Instead it ends with the effort itself and with the assurance of guidance. The choice reflects the situation of the community receiving it, which was being asked to hold a position under pressure with no visible prospect of relief. What such a community needs is not a description of the end but the certainty that the road it is walking has an owner and a destination. The sentence supplies both, and it does so without promising that the pressure will lift in this life, which is the one promise the sūrah's earlier verses taught its readers not to expect.

This ending was also useful to a community whose circumstances had not changed. A chapter closing on victory would have left the believers in Makkah waiting for an event that had not arrived, while a chapter closing on the struggle itself gave them work that was already available on the day they heard it.

**The Path That Requires Walking First**

The Book connects guidance to effort in the opposite order from what a reader might expect: the promise follows the struggle rather than preceding it. A verse in the same register joins three practices under one hope, describing those who recite the Book of God, establish prayer, and spend from what they have been given, secretly and openly, as people who may hope for a trade that will never fail (35:29). The ending of the sūrah makes the same move when it says that God guides those who strive for His sake. What the Book asks for is not certainty before action but action in the direction of what has been understood, and the guidance described at the close of this chapter is presented as the consequence of a road already being walked rather than as the condition for stepping onto it.
