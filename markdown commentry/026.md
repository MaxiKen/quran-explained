# Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ (Chapter 26) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Prophet's Anguish and the Challenge of Rhetoric**

The historical climate of ash-Shuʿarāʾ reflects the profound psychological grief of the Prophet ﷺ over the persistent stubbornness of his people. Verse 3 opens with touching intimacy: *“Perhaps, [O Muḥammad], you would kill yourself with grief that they will not be believers.”* The Quraysh were dismissing his divine message as mere *shiʿr* (poetry) or the inspired ravings of a had bard (*kāhin*). In pre-Islamic Arabia, poetry was the ultimate weapon of psychological warfare, political propaganda, and tribal self-aggrandizement. By naming the sūrah after the poets, the Qur'an confronts this cultural idolatry head-on: demonstrating that while the poet is driven by fickle emotion, vanity, and verbal deception, the Prophet speaks eternal, unchanging truth anchored in moral reality and divine revelation brought down by the *Trustworthy Spirit* (*al-Rūḥ al-Amīn*, 26:193 — *“which the trustworthy spirit ˹Gabriel˺ brought down”*).

**The Sevenfold Prophetic Drama**

The architecture of ash-Shuʿarāʾ is structured with extraordinary literary symmetry. The central body (10–191) unfolds seven successive historical dramas:

1. Moses and Aaron (10–68): Confronting Pharaoh's court, Pharaoh reminding Moses of his childhood upbringing in the palace, the staff turning into a serpent, the white hand, the contest with the sorcerers who instantly fall prostrate in faith upon seeing the staff swallow their illusions, the parting of the sea into towering walls of water, the rescue of the Israelites, and the drowning of the Egyptian hosts.

2. Abraham (69–104): Debating his father and people about the futility of idols that cannot hear or benefit, uttering the glorious confession: *“The Lord of the worlds, who created me, and He guides me. And it is He who feeds me and gives me drink. And when I am ill, it is He who cures me...”* (26:78–80), and praying for a *tongue of truth* among later generations and an untarnished heart on the Day of Judgment.

3. Noah (105–122): Enduring the contempt of the aristocratic elders who mock his impoverished followers, steadfastly proclaiming: *“I am to you a trustworthy messenger... My reward is only from the Lord of the worlds.”*

4. Hūd (123–140): Confronting the giants of ʿĀd, who built lofty monuments on every high place for sport and seized people with unrestrained cruelty.

5. Ṣāliḥ (141–159): Warning the skilled stonemasons of Thamūd who carved secure palaces into cliffs, presenting the she-camel as a public test of water rights, and their catastrophic doom after hamstrung rebellion.

6. Lot (160–175): Confronting the public sexual transgression and assault of his townsmen, saved with his family except his complicit wife.

7. Shuʿayb (176–190): Rebuking the commercial corruption, fraudulent scales, and highway robbery of the forest-dwellers (*Aṣḥāb al-Aykah*), who were crushed by the shadow of the canopy cloud (*Yawm al-Ẓullah*). The epilogue (192–227) brings the sūrah to an electrifying climax: affirming that the Qur'an was brought down by the *Trustworthy Spirit* upon the Prophet's heart in clear Arabic tongue, foretold in the scriptures of the ancients, and contrasting this divine speech against devils who whisper into the ears of wandering poets.

## Verse 26:1

> Ṭâ-Sĩn-Mĩm

**The Same Pattern Marks Several Chapters**

These three letters are not an isolated ornament. The same sequence opens al-Qaṣaṣ, while a related two-letter opening begins al-Naml. That distribution creates a link among chapters without making any of them dependent on a secret code. The shared sound can prepare the ear for a common setting, whereas each chapter then unfolds its own argument. Here the letters are followed immediately by a declaration about the clear Book. The placement matters: whatever the letters signify, their role is not to replace the Book's guidance but to lead into it.

**Sound Comes Before Explanation**

Recited letters have a physical presence. Ṭā, Sīn, and Mīm arrive as distinct sounds, with pauses between them, before the listener has received a proposition. This gives the verse an unusual rhythm. It asks the ear to attend before the mind begins to classify. Such an opening suits a Qur'an received through recitation, where cadence, repetition, and the movement from sound to sense all carry part of the communication. The letters are not a poem, but their arrangement reminds the hearer that revelation is heard as well as read.

---

## Verse 26:2

> These are the verses of the clear Book

**The Book Makes Its Meaning Available**

The demonstrative phrase points to what follows and to the revelation being recited as a whole. These are not private impressions or riddles reserved for a spiritual elite. They are āyāt, signs that point beyond themselves while remaining verbal statements that can be heard, remembered, examined, and acted upon. Calling the Book clear places responsibility on both sides of communication. The message has a form capable of being understood, and the hearer must not replace listening with a search for excuses.

**Mubīn Both Clarifies and Is Clear**

The Arabic adjective mubīn can describe something evident, and it can also describe something that makes another thing evident. Both senses fit this verse. The Qur'an is clear in its own wording and clarifying in its work. It brings hidden motives into view, distinguishes worship from imitation, and exposes the consequences that power tries to conceal. The same root appears in descriptions of manifest signs elsewhere in the Qur'an, including 12:1 — *“Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the clear Book”* and 43:2 — *“By the clear Book”*, so the word carries more than a compliment about literary style.

This double force changes how the Book should be read. A reader does not approach it only to collect information. The text also interprets life: Pharaoh's authority becomes a form of injustice, Abraham's questioning becomes a test of inherited worship, and a prophet's apparent defeat becomes part of a larger judgment. The Book makes things clear by giving them their true names and relations. It tells a person what he is seeing and what that sight requires of him.

## Verse 26:3

> Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief

**Compassion Has Become Dangerous to the Messenger**

The verse addresses the Prophet's sorrow with a warning so strong that it imagines grief consuming his life. The Arabic verb behind tormenting oneself, bākhiʿ, can carry the sense of destroying or wasting the soul. It does not portray a mild disappointment. The Prophet sees people turning away from the truth he has brought, and his concern for their rescue presses inward upon him. His pain comes from mercy, not from wounded pride. He wants them to escape a loss whose seriousness he knows.

**The Verb Gives the Grief a Physical Shape**

A related warning appears in 18:6 — *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message”*, where the same concern is raised after people reject the message. The repetition does not accuse the Prophet of a moral fault. It gives him companionship and relief: his sincerity is known, and he is not asked to force the result. The Qur'an can praise a messenger's care while teaching him that care must not turn into despair. This balance is central to the verse's pastoral force.

---

## Verse 26:4

> If We willed, We could send down upon them a ˹compelling˺ sign from the heavens, leaving their necks bent in ˹utter˺ submission to it

**A Sign Can Silence Argument Without Create Faith**

People often imagine that one more miracle would settle every dispute. This verse corrects that expectation. A sign capable of bending every neck would silence opposition for a moment, but it would not necessarily create love of truth or a durable willingness to obey. The Qur'an elsewhere records people asking for signs and then resisting after signs arrive. The problem is not always a shortage of information. It can be a refusal to accept the authority that the information carries.

That is why the verse speaks of a sign from the heavens without naming one. The nature of the event is less important than its effect: it would remove the possibility of ordinary resistance. By withholding such compulsion, God leaves room for a response that reveals the heart. The audience is not abandoned to darkness; it is addressed through a clear Book and through signs that invite understanding. What remains is a morally significant decision rather than a forced display of submission.

---

The Book states the same truth from the other direction: if God had willed, everyone on earth would have believed, and the Prophet is told not to grieve over those who will not (10:99 — *“Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers”*; 12:103 — *“And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are”*), and if He sent down the angels and made the dead speak and gathered everything before them they would still not believe unless God willed it (6:111 — *“Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign ˹they demanded˺, they still would not have believed—unless Allah so willed.”*). The verse's word is the point: the sign would bend the necks, and forcing assent is not the aim of the message, which the sūrah's opening has already put in the language of compassion — the Prophet is nearly killing himself with grief over their refusal (26:3 — *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief”*).

## Verse 26:5

> Whatever new reminder comes to them from the Most Compassionate, they always turn away from it

**Mercy Stands Behind the Warning**

The reminder comes from al-Raḥmān, the Most Compassionate. That divine name changes the emotional color of the verse. The people are not being abandoned to their error; mercy is sending them another warning. Even the announcement of judgment is an act of kindness when it gives a person time to turn before the consequence arrives. Turning from the reminder therefore means turning away from an offered mercy, not merely declining a piece of information.

The name also blocks a false picture of revelation as hostility. A warning may be painful because it exposes guilt, but pain does not make it cruel. A physician's treatment can sting while serving recovery; in a greater and more serious way, a divine reminder can disturb a person to rescue him from self-deception. The audience's conduct is tragic for that reason. They are not being chased by an enemy. They are refusing the hand that calls them back to life.

**Gradual Revelation Gives Time to Respond**

The term rendered new can also carry the sense of something recent or newly occurring. Revelation came over time rather than arriving as one complete book at one instant. Qur'an 17:106 — *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace. And We have sent it down in successive revelations”* connects that mode of delivery with recitation in stages, and 25:32 — *“The disbelievers say, “If only the Quran had been sent down to him all at once!” ˹We have sent it˺ as such ˹in stages˺ so We may reassure your heart with it. And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace”* records the objection that the revelation should have come all at once. The gradual form allowed an answer to events, a strengthening of the community, and repeated opportunities for correction.

---

## Verse 26:6

> They have certainly denied ˹the truth˺, so they will soon face the consequences of their ridicule

**Denial Turns a Warning Into an Appointment**

The verse moves from turning away to a firmer verdict: they have denied the truth. Turning away can look like avoidance, but denial gives the avoidance a settled conclusion. They have judged the message false and treated that judgment as permission to mock it. The response from revelation is not another try to win a debate. It is a declaration that consequences are coming. A person may postpone an encounter with truth, but postponement does not cancel the encounter.

The time word soon should be heard beside the human tendency to measure urgency by comfort. If no punishment appears immediately, the denier may think the warning has failed. The verse rejects that calculation. Divine timing can leave room for warning, repentance, and the completion of a people's record, yet the appointed consequence remains near in relation to a life that is brief. Nearness is moral as well as chronological: every act of ridicule is already shaping the state in which the person will meet its reality.

**Mockery Does Not Refute What It Targets**

The Qur'an returns to this reversal in 39:48 — *“And the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds will unfold before them, and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”* and 45:33 — *“And the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds will unfold before them, and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”*, where deeds once treated with contempt become present before those who committed them. These passages support a consistent principle: mockery is not a shield against consequence. It can even deepen the harm because it trains the heart to resist correction. Ignorance may seek knowledge, and doubt may seek evidence. Contempt closes the door before either process can begin.

---

## Verse 26:7

> Have they failed to look at the earth, ˹to see˺ how many types of fine plants We have caused to grow in it

**The Earth Offers a Sign Within Reach**

After warning about denied revelation, the verse directs attention to the ground beneath the audience. They do not need to travel to a distant age or wait for an extraordinary event. The earth is already displaying generation, variety, color, nourishment, and renewal. The question is not whether plants exist, but whether people have looked in the manner that turns sight into understanding. Observation becomes reflection when it asks who gives the earth its ordered abundance and why such abundance should be treated as morally empty.

The verb of looking can include deliberate consideration, not a passing glance. A person may see a field every day and never allow it to challenge his assumptions. The verse interrupts that familiarity. It asks the audience to notice quantity and kind together: many forms grow from one earth, yet their variety is not random confusion. The sign is available to ordinary experience, which makes neglect of it harder to excuse.

**Growth Makes Power Visible Without Spectacle**

Other Qur'anic passages place vegetation beside rain and the reviving of the earth, including 50:7 — *“As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and produced in it every type of pleasant plant”* and 20:53 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for ˹all of˺ you, and set in it pathways for you, and sends down rain from the sky, causing various types of plants to grow”*. The repeated comparison is not a lesson in agriculture alone. It establishes a pattern: the One who brings life from apparently inert ground has the power to restore people after death, and the visible cycle trains the mind to accept that larger claim. The earth's sign is therefore both present and forward-looking.

---

## Verse 26:8

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**Most Is Not All**

The statement that most would not believe leaves room for a believing minority. That small word prevents the verse from turning observation into a claim that every person responds in the same way. Evidence can be shared while responses differ. Some people see the same earth and become grateful; others limit their sight to immediate use or dismiss the question before it disturbs them. The majority's refusal does not make the sign weaker, and the minority's faith does not make it a private invention.

**Repeated Signs Establish a Pattern of Proof**

The earth is one member of a larger Qur'anic argument. The Book, prophetic history, and the natural world address different powers of attention, but they converge on one source of authority. Qur'an 41:53 — *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth. Is it not enough that your Lord is a Witness over all things”* speaks of signs in the horizons and within people, while 12:105 — *“How many signs in the heavens and the earth do they pass by with indifference”* describes signs passing before people even as they turn aside. These cross-references show that the verse is not making a narrow claim about botany. It is naming a pattern in which creation repeatedly presents evidence.

A pattern can be ignored one instance at a time. The ignored instances do not disappear; they accumulate into a record of refusal. This is why the verse's short conclusion is sober rather than surprised. Most do not believe, even after the sign has been placed in reach. The problem is not that the world is silent. It is that attention can become selective enough to hear only what leaves desire undisturbed.

---

## Verse 26:9

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**Might and Mercy Complete the Sign**

The refrain joins two names that people often separate. Al-ʿAzīz, the Almighty, means power that cannot be overcome, while al-Raḥīm, the Most Merciful, means care directed toward those who receive it. The earth's sign is not governed by blind force or by kindness without authority. It comes from a Lord strong enough to create, judge, and protect, and merciful enough to warn before judgment and to open the path of return.

**Mercy Includes a Warning**

People may imagine mercy as the removal of every painful consequence. The Qur'anic pairing corrects that idea. Mercy gives life, provision, guidance, time, and opportunities to repent. It also warns a person about the path he is choosing. If he persists after warning, the resulting judgment does not disprove mercy. It shows that mercy is not approval of wrongdoing or an agreement to erase moral reality.

The name al-Raḥīm is especially fitting for the prophet's mission because a messenger brings a form of care that demands a response. A warning can feel severe while protecting a person from a greater harm. The Prophet's grief arises because he understands this, while his audience treats the warning as an insult. The refrain places their dispute under a larger truth: the One who warns them is both able to judge and willing to forgive.

---

## Verse 26:10

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord called out to Moses, “Go to the wrongdoing people—

**The Story Begins With a Command**

The verse opens Moses' account not with his birth or his first miracle, but with a summons. God calls, and the call gives the prophet a direction: go. Prophethood is therefore not a self-appointed project built from private insight. Moses is sent by a Lord who knows the people he must face and the task he must carry. The imperative makes the mission concrete. Revelation will not remain an inward experience on a mountain; it will become an encounter with a society organized around wrongdoing.

The command also creates a contrast with the opening verses of the sūrah. The Prophet's grief over disbelief could make the mission feel like a personal struggle with an unresponsive crowd. Moses' story shows that such resistance belongs to the history of revelation. The messenger is called into the very place where rejection is expected. His courage will not come from favorable conditions, but from the One who sends him.

**Wrongdoing Names a System, Not Only a Person**

The people Moses is told to approach are described as ẓālimūn, those who act with ظلم, wrongdoing that places things out of their proper place. The label is plural before Pharaoh is named. That wording prevents the story from reducing injustice to one villain's private temper. A ruler can command, but officials, customs, beneficiaries, and silent witnesses can help an unjust order endure. The mission addresses the whole field in which oppression operates.

**The Address Recalls Earlier Missions**

Moses' summons is expanded in 20:9–48 — *“Has the story of Moses reached you ˹O Prophet˺”* and 28:29–35 — *“When Moses had completed the term and was travelling with his family, he spotted a fire on the side of Mount Ṭûr. He said to his family, “Stay here, ˹for˺ I have spotted a fire. Perhaps from there I can bring you some directions or a torch from the fire so you may warm yourselves.”*, while 79:15–19 — *“Has the story of Moses reached you ˹O Prophet˺ His Lord called him in the sacred valley of Ṭuwa ˹commanding,˺ “Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺ And say, ‘Would you ˹be willing to˺ purify yourself and let me guide you to your Lord so that you will be in awe ˹of Him˺?’”* also recalls the call in a shorter form. The differences among these accounts are not contradictions. Each passage selects the detail needed for its immediate argument. Here al-Shuʿarāʾ is preparing a series of prophetic confrontations, so the opening stresses the wrongdoing of the audience and the divine origin of the mission.

---

## Verse 26:11

> the people of Pharaoh. Will they not fear ˹Allah˺?”

**The Ruler's Name Does Not Absorb Individual Responsibility**

Calling them Pharaoh's people could tempt them to hide behind the ruler. A subject may say that orders came from above, a courtier may say that policy was already established, and a crowd may say that everyone accepted it. The verse refuses that shelter. Belonging to a political order does not remove the need for personal taqwā. Each person must decide whether he will help injustice, profit from it, remain silent before it, or resist it within the limits God has given.

**Earlier Calls Use the Same Moral Beginning**

The wording recalls other prophetic summonses in which fear of God comes before obedience. Moses is later told, in 20:43–44 — *“Go, both of you, to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺ Speak to him gently, so perhaps he may be mindful ˹of Me˺ or fearful ˹of My punishment˺.”*, to approach Pharaoh and speak gently while inviting him to remembrance and fear. Gentleness there does not weaken the indictment here. It shows that the purpose of warning is to awaken, not to satisfy anger. A hard truth can be carried in measured speech.

The repeated beginning also links the mission to the basic call of every prophet. Before detailed law, political reform, or debate over signs comes the recognition that God is Lord and that human action is answerable to Him. Pharaoh's people are not exempt because their customs are old or their ruler is strong. Their first duty is the first duty of every community: guard yourselves from injustice by returning to the Lord.

---

## Verse 26:12

> He replied, “My Lord! I fear that they will reject me

**Moses Answers the Command With Honest Fear**

Moses does not pretend that the assignment is easy. His first concern is that the people will deny him. The Arabic language of denial here includes calling a messenger false, refusing his authority, and treating his warning as an invented claim. Moses knows that a person who has built power on a settled story will not welcome the one who exposes that story. His fear is therefore a sober reading of the audience, not an excuse to escape the task.

This honesty makes the prayer part of obedience. Moses does not run from the command or answer with a boast about his own courage. He brings his weakness before the Lord who sent him. A servant can accept a mission while acknowledging the real obstacles within it. Faith is not the denial of fear; it is the refusal to let fear become the final authority. The verse gives language to that middle place between panic and bravado.

**Other Accounts Show the Same Need for Assistance**

In 20:25–28 — *“Moses prayed, “My Lord! Uplift my heart for me and make my task easy and remove the impediment from my tongue so people may understand my speech”* Moses asks for an expanded heart, ease in his task, and clear speech. Those requests illuminate the fear stated here without replacing it. The mission is inwardly demanding because it requires a servant to stand before a ruler while remaining free of the ruler's frame. Moses needs more than information. He needs steadiness, clarity, and the ability to make the message understood.

---

## Verse 26:13

> And ˹so˺ my heart will be broken and my tongue will be tied. So send Aaron along ˹as a messenger˺

**The Mission Presses on Heart and Speech**

Moses describes two connected obstacles. His breast will become constricted, and his tongue will not move freely. The first image concerns inward capacity: anxiety narrows the space in which a person can think, bear pressure, and remain composed. The second concerns outward delivery: a message may be true, yet the speaker may struggle to express it under threat. The verse treats both conditions as relevant to service. A messenger is not a disembodied voice; fear can touch his body, emotions, and speech.

The translation's broken heart captures the force of the image, while the Arabic points to a chest made tight. Commentators differed over whether Moses had a physical speech difficulty, a psychological inhibition, or both. The Qur'an does not settle the medical question, and the verse does not require a diagnosis. Its clear point is that Moses anticipates a real impediment to carrying out a grave public duty. He asks for help instead of hiding the impediment behind an image of perfection.

**A Tongue Can Be Tied by More Than Anatomy**

Speech becomes difficult when a person is frightened, but Moses' concern may also recall the long-standing difficulty mentioned in 20:27 — *“and remove the impediment from my tongue”*. Some reports and commentators imagined a physical cause; others understood the language as a prayer for inward ease and persuasive clarity. The stronger common ground is more useful than an invented certainty: Moses feared that his speech would fail to serve the message as it should.

---

## Verse 26:14

> Also, they have a charge against me, so I fear they may kill me.”

**Moses Names a Real Political Danger**

The final concern is not imagined embarrassment. Moses knows that a charge lies against him and fears that Pharaoh's people may kill him. The charge refers to the Egyptian who died when Moses intervened in a fight involving an Israelite, an event recounted in 28:15–21 — *“So Moses punched him, causing his death.”*. The memory has a legal and political life beyond the original moment. A ruler who wants to silence a messenger can turn an old incident into a reason for execution.

Moses therefore understands the mission at two levels. He may be rejected as a messenger, and he may be attacked as a man with a vulnerable past. Tyranny often combines those tactics. It questions the speaker's character so that the message need not be answered, then uses the accusation to justify force. By mentioning the charge before he goes, Moses shows that he is not confusing faith with a denial of danger.

**A Wrong Act and a Prophetic Mission Must Be Distinguished**

Moses does not present the Egyptian's death as irrelevant. His fear acknowledges that the event has moral and legal weight. Yet a past act does not automatically make every later word false. The question is how the act is understood, repented of, and judged, not whether a servant must remain silent forever after having been in need of forgiveness.

---

## Verse 26:15

> Allah responded, “Certainly not! So go, both of you, with Our signs. We will be with you, listening

**God Answers Fear Before Giving Further Instruction**

The response begins with a firm denial: certainly not. Moses has feared rejection, speech failing, and death, but God does not allow those fears to become the conclusion of the mission. The answer is brief because the servant needs assurance before he needs more detail. Divine reassurance does not argue that the danger is imaginary. It declares that the danger will not defeat the purpose for which Moses is being sent.

The force of the answer also corrects Moses' estimate of what he can carry alone. He had described several weaknesses, and God answers them with a command that includes Aaron. The reassurance is therefore not empty encouragement. It is attached to a changed situation: both brothers will go, and they will go with signs. Fear is met by companionship, evidence, and divine oversight.

**The Plural of Signs Gives the Mission Breadth**

Moses and Aaron are sent with God's signs, not with personal eloquence alone. The plural points to the signs that will support their claim, including the miracles associated with Moses and the public demonstration before Pharaoh. These signs are not tricks designed to win a contest. They are marks that identify the source of the mission and expose the weakness of Pharaoh's claim to ultimate authority.

---

## Verse 26:16

> Go to Pharaoh and say, ‘We are messengers from the Lord of all worlds

**The Mission Requires a Direct Audience**

Moses and Aaron are told to go to Pharaoh and speak to him. The command does not allow them to keep the message at the level of private advice or indirect criticism. Pharaoh's power governs the order that has harmed the Children of Israel, so the ruler must hear the claim that stands above his rule. This directness is not a license for rudeness. It is clarity about where responsibility is concentrated.

The plural pronoun in we are messengers also matters. Moses is not presenting himself as a solitary reformer with a personal program. Aaron stands with him, and both are servants of a Sender. Their authority is derived, not self-created. They do not ask Pharaoh to admire their personalities or accept a new political faction. They carry a command from the Lord who owns authority over Pharaoh and over those Pharaoh has oppressed.

**Lord of All Worlds Leaves No Space for Rival Lordship**

The title Lord of all worlds, Rabb al-ʿālamīn, is an universal claim. It reaches beyond one nation, dynasty, or sacred geography. A lord gives existence, sustains it, and has the right to command it. If God is Lord of all worlds, Pharaoh cannot be ultimate lord of Egypt, and no ruler can turn a portion of creation into a private domain beyond divine judgment.

---

## Verse 26:17

> ˹commanded to say:˺ ‘Let the Children of Israel go with us.’’”

**The Universal Claim Reaches the Enslaved**

The command now gives the first concrete consequence of God's lordship: Pharaoh must let the Children of Israel go with the messengers. The demand is not an abstract dispute about names. It concerns people held under a power that treats them as a resource. Divine sovereignty becomes visible in the release of those whom an earthly ruler has claimed as his own.

The phrase with us indicates movement out of Pharaoh's control. Moses and Aaron are not asking to become privileged intermediaries within the same system. They are calling for the community's departure from the arrangement that has confined it. The destination is not developed in this verse; the immediate point is that Pharaoh has no rightful ownership of the Israelites. A human ruler cannot convert a people created by God into permanent property.

**Letting Go Means Ending a Claim of Ownership**

The Arabic command has the sense of sending away or releasing. It is therefore stronger than a request for a holiday or a temporary suspension of labor. Pharaoh must relinquish his hold. The demand exposes the moral meaning of the political order: its power depends on refusing the Israelites the freedom to leave and to serve the Lord outside Pharaoh's control.

---

## Verse 26:18

> Pharaoh protested, “Did we not raise you among us as a child, and you stayed several years of your life in our care

**Pharaoh Answers a Divine Claim With a Personal Account**

Pharaoh does not begin by answering the title Lord of all worlds or the demand about the Israelites. He turns to Moses' childhood. His question presents the ruler's household as the place that raised and protected the man now standing before him. The tactic is personal and political at once. If Moses can be made to feel indebted, Pharaoh may avoid answering the charge brought against the state.

The account has a factual basis. Other passages describe Moses being taken into Pharaoh's household after infancy and growing under the protection of the palace. Yet a true fact can be used to draw a false conclusion. Care received during childhood does not give a ruler ownership of the adult conscience, and a household's role in preserving a child's life does not turn the child into an eternal subject of its benefactor.

**Upbringing Is Not a Permanent Title Over the Adult**

Pharaoh's wording treats years in his environment as though they settled Moses' allegiance forever. That assumption confuses nurture with lordship. A child depends on adults, but dependence in childhood does not erase the Creator's claim over the person who grows. Moses can acknowledge what occurred without accepting Pharaoh's conclusion about whom he must obey.

---

## Verse 26:19

> Then you did what you did, being ˹utterly˺ ungrateful!”

**Pharaoh Turns Moses' Past Into a Weapon**

The phrase what you did leaves the deed unnamed in the immediate speech. Everyone in the court is expected to know the incident: Moses struck an Egyptian during a quarrel, and the man died. By using a vague reference, Pharaoh can invoke the shame of the event without giving the circumstances a fair hearing. The omission is rhetorical. It lets the ruler control the frame and makes the accusation sound larger than the facts he is willing to state.

**A Past Wrong Must Be Faced, Not Exploited**

Moses is not presented as having no history of error. The event in 28:15–21 — *“˹One day˺ he entered the city unnoticed by its people. There he found two men fighting: one of his own people, and the other of his enemies. The man from his people called to him for help against his foe. So Moses punched him, causing his death. Moses cried, “This is from Satan’s handiwork. He is certainly a sworn, misleading enemy.”* was serious, and his later response shows awareness that he had wronged himself. A truthful account does not erase the deed to protect a respected servant. At the same time, acknowledging a wrong does not require accepting Pharaoh's use of it as a reason to preserve oppression.

This distinction is central to justice. An accuser may identify a real fault and still use it dishonestly. The validity of the charge must be separated from the purpose for which it is raised. Pharaoh wants the court to discuss Moses' past so that no one discusses the Israelites' present condition. A just listener examines both: what Moses did, and what Pharaoh is doing now.

---

## Verse 26:20

> Moses replied, “I did it then, lacking guidance

**Lacking Guidance Describes a Moment of Error**

The translation's lacking guidance renders a phrase that can also be understood as being among those who were astray or not yet guided to the right course. Moses is not claiming that he deliberately chose disbelief. The setting in 28:15–16 — *“˹One day˺ he entered the city unnoticed by its people. There he found two men fighting: one of his own people, and the other of his enemies. The man from his people called to him for help against his foe. So Moses punched him, causing his death. Moses cried, “This is from Satan’s handiwork. He is certainly a sworn, misleading enemy.”* indicates a moment in which he acted under confusion and then recognized the need for forgiveness. The wording presents the deed as an error of judgment before prophetic guidance had been given to him.

This nuance matters. Human beings can be wrong without every wrong being the same kind of rejection of truth. A person may act impulsively, misunderstand a situation, or intervene with an effect he did not intend. Such distinctions do not remove responsibility. They make responsibility more accurate. Moses' answer neither glorifies the act nor accepts Pharaoh's try to define his whole character by it.

**The Reply Refuses Pharaoh's Moral Trap**

This is how a person should answer a manipulative accusation. Do not deny a real wrong merely because an enemy mentions it. Do not accept a false conclusion merely because it is attached to a true fact. Examine the connection between the fact and the judgment. Pharaoh's care of Moses, Moses' past act, and the Israelites' oppression are related in the ruler's speech, but they do not form the moral chain he claims.

---

## Verse 26:21

> So I fled from you when I feared you. Then my Lord granted me wisdom and made me one of the messengers

**Moses Answers the Threat With a Plain History**

Moses does not deny that he fled. He names fear of Pharaoh as the reason. The admission is important because Pharaoh has tried to turn the past into a weapon. Moses refuses both concealment and exaggeration. He left the reach of the court because he feared that the ruler would kill him, and that flight became the setting in which God prepared him for a different return.

The flight was not a celebration of fear or a permanent rejection of responsibility. It was a human response to an immediate threat. The fuller account in 28:21–22 — *“So Moses left the city in a state of fear and caution, praying, “My Lord! Deliver me from the wrongdoing people.” And as he made his way towards Midian, he said, “I trust my Lord will guide me to the right way.”* describes Moses leaving Egypt in fear and then turning toward Midian while asking God for right direction. That detail keeps the event within a moral journey. Moses leaves danger, but he does not leave God. His movement away from Pharaoh becomes movement toward dependence.

**Flight Can Be Protection Before It Becomes Preparation**

A person may need to leave a place where a powerful wrongdoer can harm him. Such departure is not automatically cowardice or betrayal. Moses' flight preserves his life at a time when he is not yet equipped to confront the court. The same God who later commands him to return first allows a period outside Egypt. Protection and mission are not opposites; protection can be part of how a mission is made possible.

---

## Verse 26:22

> How can that be a ‘favour,’ of which you remind me, when ˹it was only because˺ you ˹have˺ enslaved the Children of Israel?”

**Moses Rejects a Benefit Built Beside Oppression**

Pharaoh has presented Moses' upbringing as a favor that should purchase loyalty. Moses answers by exposing the moral cost hidden behind that claim. How can the palace's care for one child be called a favor when the same ruler has enslaved an entire people? The question does not deny that Moses received shelter or food. It denies that those private benefits can be weighed without the public wrong that made Pharaoh's rule what it was.

The contrast is deliberate. Pharaoh remembers the years he gave Moses; Moses remembers the Israelites whom Pharaoh held. One view narrows gratitude to a personal relationship with the ruler. The other asks how a ruler treats a whole community. Justice requires the wider view. A kindness toward an individual cannot cleanse a system that degrades many others.

**A Private Benefit Cannot Cancel a Public Crime**

Pharaoh's argument would be persuasive only if personal care erased communal harm. It does not. Moses can acknowledge that he grew up in the palace and still say that the palace's rule was unjust. These truths do not contradict each other. The household may have preserved one child's life while the state oppressed the Children of Israel. Both facts must be judged, not forced into a single flattering story.

---

Moses' reply is the sūrah's compressed account of his own childhood: he was raised in Pharaoh's palace as an infant placed in the river, and the favour was given when he was an enemy of the house that nursed him. The sūrah of the Stories tells the same sequence at length — the child returned to his mother, carried to the palace, and the household of Pharaoh taken as an enemy and a grief (28:7–9 — *“And ˹it so happened that˺ Pharaoh’s people picked him up, only to become their enemy and source of grief. Surely Pharaoh, Hamân, and their soldiers were sinful”*), and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā adds the details of the basket and the sister (20:37–40 — *“‘Put him into a chest, then put it into the river.”*). Moses' argument is that a benefit given in the course of an oppression cannot be presented as a favour to the oppressed, and the verse turns the reminder back on the one who gave it.

## Verse 26:23

> Pharaoh asked, “And what is ‘the Lord of all worlds’?”

**Pharaoh Turns a Universal Claim Into a Challenge**

Pharaoh's question comes after Moses has invoked the Lord of all worlds. On the surface, it asks for clarification. In context, it also tests whether the messenger's Lord can be made to appear vague, distant, or irrelevant to Egyptian power. The title has already placed Pharaoh under a higher authority, so the question is not harmless curiosity. It is a challenge to the source of the command that he release the Israelites.

The expression Rabb al-ʿālamīn carries more than the idea of a creator who began the universe and then withdrew. Rabb includes the one who owns, sustains, orders, and brings a thing toward its proper state. The worlds are all created realms, not only one nation or one court. Pharaoh asks what this title means because accepting its scope would unsettle his own claim to rule.

**The Question May Be Sincere in Form but Hostile in Purpose**

Exegetes differed over Pharaoh's intention. Some treated the question as a request for the essence or exact nature of God. Others held that Pharaoh already understood enough to know that Moses was challenging his authority, and that he asked only to mock or provoke. The immediate setting supports the second reading more strongly. A ruler who presents himself as supreme is not approaching the title as a humble seeker; he is putting the messenger on trial before his court.

---

## Verse 26:24

> Moses replied, “˹He is˺ the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, if only you had sure faith.”

**Moses Answers the Question Through God's Works**

Moses does not try to contain God in a definition of essence. He points to the heavens, the earth, and all that lies between them. This is not a retreat from knowledge. It is a disciplined way to speak about the Creator through the created order that Pharaoh can see and cannot claim to have made. Divine lordship is made known through sustaining and governing the total field of existence.

The answer also avoids making God a rival within the universe. Pharaoh's authority belongs to a place, a court, and a historical moment. The Lord named by Moses is the Lord of the realms above and below and of the interval joining them. He is not one power among other powers. Everything Pharaoh uses to display grandeur already depends on the Lord whose scope surrounds it.

**Heavens and Earth Enclose Every Human Claim**

The pair heavens and earth forms a comprehensive frame. Whatever Pharaoh can point to as evidence of his rule lies inside that frame. His palace, river, army, ancestry, and land are not outside God's ownership. The words do not need to list every created thing because the pair marks the whole visible order, while everything in between closes the gaps that a ruler might try to reserve for himself.

---

## Verse 26:25

> Pharaoh said to those around him, “Did you hear ˹what he said˺?”

**The Question Polices the Court's Loyalty**

Those around Pharaoh are not neutral listeners. Their position depends on the ruler, and his question tests whether they will mirror his response. Did you hear what he said carries an unspoken command: hear it as absurd, and show that you belong to the side of Pharaoh. The court's members must decide whether loyalty means repeating the ruler's performance or facing the truth that performance avoids.

**Mockery Replaces an Answer**

Pharaoh's question contains no argument about the heavens, the earth, or the meaning of lordship. It signals that he has chosen performance over reply. A speaker can use ridicule when a claim threatens a settled position, because a joke may make the claim feel socially unsafe to consider. The audience then learns that agreement with the messenger would mean joining the target of the joke.

This tactic is different from honest disagreement. An honest critic identifies a premise, offers a reason, and accepts the possibility of correction. Pharaoh instead asks his courtiers to react to Moses' words as though their strangeness were already a refutation. The court is not being invited into inquiry; it is being recruited into a social defense of the ruler's authority.

---

## Verse 26:26

> Moses added, “˹He is˺ your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.”

**Moses Brings Lordship From the Cosmos Into the Family**

After speaking of the heavens and earth, Moses makes the claim personal. The Lord is not only the ruler of distant realms; He is your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers. The words address Pharaoh and his court directly. They cannot place God far away and treat the message as a theory about a remote universe. The One who sustains creation also sustained the people standing in that court and the generations from which they came.

**The Pronoun Your Removes the Court's Distance**

The word your carries a directness that the universal description alone might not. Moses is not speaking about a lord relevant only to the Israelites. The Lord addresses Pharaoh's life, Pharaoh's ancestors, and Pharaoh's own position. The ruler is included in the created order he pretends to command. He cannot dismiss the message as a foreign theology brought by an outsider.

This is also why the answer can be confronting without being sectarian. Moses does not say that the Lord is merely the patron of one tribe. He says the same Lord is related to the ruler and his forefathers as Creator and Sustainer. The claim creates a shared ground that Pharaoh may reject but cannot honestly call irrelevant. The Lord's authority does not depend on Moses' ethnicity or on Pharaoh's permission to enter the conversation.

---

## Verse 26:27

> Pharaoh said ˹mockingly˺, “Your messenger, who has been sent to you, must be insane.”

**Pharaoh Answers a Claim With a Diagnosis**

Pharaoh does not examine Moses' statement about the Lord of the forefathers. He labels the messenger insane. The accusation is a familiar way to avoid an argument: if the speaker can be declared irrational, his words need not be answered. The judgment is presented as though it were obvious, but the verse marks its mocking tone. Pharaoh is not offering a careful account of Moses' condition. He is trying to make the court dismiss the message before it reaches their conscience.

Calling a prophet mad also reverses the moral situation. Moses speaks coherently about creation, ancestry, and authority, while Pharaoh is the one who claims supremacy and defends oppression. The label allows the ruler to make his own extreme position appear normal. A court trained to trust Pharaoh may accept the diagnosis because accepting it protects the order from which the court benefits.

**Your Messenger Creates Distance From the Claim**

Pharaoh says your messenger. The possessive can sound as though Moses belongs to the people around him rather than speaking to Pharaoh. It turns a divine commission into a factional matter: they have their messenger, and Pharaoh has his authority. This wording is a political move. If Moses can be treated as the representative of a troublesome group, his message need not be judged as a claim about every human being.

---

## Verse 26:28

> Moses responded: “˹He is˺ the Lord of the east and west, and everything in between, if only you had any sense.”

**Moses Answers Mockery With a Wider Horizon**

Pharaoh has tried to make the claim about Moses' mind. Moses does not defend himself with an emotional protest. He continues describing the Lord. The east and west name the two horizons through which the sun's course is observed, and everything between them completes the scope. The ruler's court is one point within this ordered world, not its center.

This answer has a different rhythm from the previous description of heavens and earth. It moves across the daily experience of sunrise, sunset, direction, and changing light. Pharaoh can see his kingdom as a stable possession, but the horizons announce a world in motion under a power that no palace controls. The Lord's sovereignty is displayed in the order that frames every human activity.

**The Repetition Is a Deliberate Expansion**

The repeated descriptions also teach how religious knowledge grows. One image may show God's total creation, another His command over direction and time, and another His relation to human generations. None exhausts the Lord, but each blocks a false limitation. Moses is not trying to provide a finite portrait. He is giving signs by which a finite mind can recognize dependence.

---

## Verse 26:29

> Pharaoh threatened, “If you take any other god besides me, I will certainly have you imprisoned.”

**When Argument Fails, Pharaoh Reaches for Prison**

Pharaoh's response marks a change in method. He has questioned the Lord of all worlds, mocked Moses' answer, and heard the claim extended across the horizons. Now he threatens imprisonment. The move reveals that the dispute was never a neutral search for information. When reason does not restore the ruler's control, coercion becomes the substitute for a reply.

The threat is conditional: if you take another god besides me. Pharaoh frames worship of the true God as a crime against the state. The sentence exposes his political theology. He treats his own authority as entitled to decide whom a person may worship, then uses the prison to enforce that claim. A jail sentence cannot disprove God's lordship; it can only show what Pharaoh is willing to do when people refuse his false supremacy.

**Prison Turns Belief Into a Political Offense**

Pharaoh's threat is not limited to a debate about Moses' private conviction. He says that Moses will be imprisoned if he acknowledges another god. That condition makes worship a public matter because Pharaoh has made himself the center of public allegiance. The ruler wants the court to believe that the danger lies in Moses' devotion rather than in the ruler's demand that conscience submit to him.

---

## Verse 26:30

> Moses responded, “Even if I bring you a clear proof?”

**Moses Tests Whether Pharaoh Will Accept Evidence**

Moses does not answer the prison threat with a counter-threat. He asks whether the threat would remain even if he brought a clear proof. The question places evidence between the ruler and his punishment. If Pharaoh is genuinely concerned that Moses is making a false claim, a manifest sign should matter. If Pharaoh intends to silence any challenge to his authority, then no amount of evidence will make him release the threat.

The wording is carefully framed. Moses does not say that a sign will force Pharaoh to believe. He asks whether it will alter Pharaoh's judgment. A proof can establish a claim while leaving a proud person free to reject it. The question therefore tests not only the existence of evidence but the ruler's moral willingness to follow evidence where it leads.

**Mubīn Means Evidence That Makes the Matter Plain**

Clarity does not mean that every opponent will accept the sign. It means that the sign performs its evidentiary role. Pharaoh will be able to see what is shown and will have to decide how to describe it. The later accusation that Moses is a magician will not erase the event; it will reveal the strategy used to avoid its implication. A clear proof can be resisted, but it cannot honestly be called invisible.

---

## Verse 26:31

> Pharaoh demanded, “Bring it then, if what you say is true.”

**Pharaoh Accepts a Test on His Own Terms**

Pharaoh responds to Moses' question with a demand to produce the proof. His condition sounds fair: if the claim is true, let it be showed. Yet the tone remains adversarial. He is not asking how to understand a sign or what obedience would follow from it. He is challenging Moses to perform before the court, as though the truth of a divine mission were a spectacle Pharaoh has authority to commission.

The demand still matters because it brings the question into public view. Moses has asked whether clear evidence would change Pharaoh's judgment, and Pharaoh gives the proof a place in the encounter. Even a hostile ruler can be made to state a standard that later exposes his refusal. Once the sign appears, the court will have to explain why it does not count, rather than pretending that Moses offered nothing.

**A Sign Is Not a Performance for Royal Approval**

The court may still try to judge the sign by the ruler's categories. If it appears impressive, Pharaoh can call it magic; if it threatens his position, he can call it treason. Moses cannot control those labels. His duty is to present the sign faithfully and allow its meaning to confront the ruler's claim. The proof serves revelation, not the prestige of the stage on which it appears.

---

## Verse 26:32

> So he threw down his staff and—behold!—it became a real snake

**The Sign Connects Moses' Past and Present**

A staff belongs to the life Moses lived outside the palace. The object links the prophet before Pharaoh with the man who spent years away from Egypt. God takes an item associated with humble work and makes it serve a public mission. This reversal is part of the sign's meaning. The court expects authority to arrive dressed in royal symbols; revelation brings authority through a servant whose instrument looks ordinary.

**The Staff Sign Answers the Threat Without Violence**

Pharaoh has threatened imprisonment. Moses does not attack the ruler or try to seize the court. He presents a sign. The contrast matters. The messenger answers coercion with evidence, not with a rival use of force. The event is powerful, but Moses' role is declarative: he displays what God has given him and lets the ruler confront its meaning.

The staff also recalls the long preparation of Moses' life. The years of fear, travel, and work were not disconnected from the moment of confrontation. The object in his hand is evidence that the messenger has come from a different setting than Pharaoh's court. Yet the sign shows that humble origins do not limit divine choice. God can make the tool of a shepherd confront the symbol of a kingdom.

---

## Verse 26:33

> Then he drew his hand ˹out of his collar˺ and it was ˹shining˺ white for all to see

**The Second Sign Addresses the Human Body**

After the staff becomes a serpent, Moses draws out his hand and it appears shining white to the observers. The two signs use different registers. The first transforms an object Moses carries; the second appears through Moses himself. The court sees that the messenger is not relying on a single strange event that can be dismissed as a property of the staff. God's signs accompany the servant in more than one form.

The whiteness is described as visible to those who watch. It is not a private sensation or an inward assurance that only Moses can report. The court is given another public mark. This matters because Pharaoh's challenge required evidence that could be judged. Moses' body becomes the place through which the sign is shown, yet the sign points beyond Moses. The messenger is a bearer of power, not its owner.

**The Bright Hand Is a Sign, Not an Injury**

The passage does not present the hand as diseased or damaged. Its whiteness is a luminous sign, associated in parallel accounts with the hand appearing bright to observers. That detail matters because a visible change in the body can be interpreted as illness, weakness, or danger. The Qur'anic description directs attention to radiance and clarity rather than decay.

---

## Verse 26:34

> Pharaoh said to the chiefs around him, “He is indeed a skilled magician

**Pharaoh Reclassifies a Sign to Protect His Rule**

The chiefs have seen the staff and the hand, but Pharaoh does not ask what these signs mean. He gives them a label: Moses is a skilled magician. The accusation preserves the court's worldview by placing a divine sign inside a category the ruler can claim to understand and control. If Moses is only a magician, Pharaoh need not consider the possibility that a higher Lord has sent him.

The word skilled or knowing makes the charge sound informed. Pharaoh is not calling Moses a foolish trickster. He grants him expertise while denying his commission. That combination is politically useful. It lets the ruler acknowledge the force of what the court saw, yet explain it as a human technique that poses a threat to the kingdom. The label is an interpretation chosen for its usefulness, not a conclusion established by examination.

**The Chiefs Are the Court's Instruments of Consensus**

The address to the chiefs around him shows that Pharaoh is managing an inner circle. The malaʾ are the notables, leaders, and advisers whose influence helps shape public policy. Their position gives them a role beyond private opinion. When the ruler names Moses a magician before them, he is setting the official language that the court and the cities will repeat.

---

## Verse 26:35

> who seeks to drive you from your land by his magic. So what do you propose?”

**Pharaoh Recasts Liberation as Expulsion**

Pharaoh's accusation moves from Moses' supposed skill to its alleged political purpose. He says Moses wants to drive the Egyptians from their land. The claim changes the subject. Moses has demanded the release of the Children of Israel, but Pharaoh presents the demand as an attack on the whole population. A request that threatens an unjust arrangement is made to sound like a plan to dispossess everyone.

The phrase your land is carefully chosen. Pharaoh speaks to the chiefs as though the land belongs to them collectively and Moses is an outsider trying to take it. That language hides the Israelites' presence and labor while presenting the ruling group as the only legitimate public. It turns a question of oppression into a question of ethnic or national defense. Once that frame is accepted, Pharaoh can call resistance to slavery patriotism.

**A Tyrant Uses Fear of Loss to Preserve Control**

People who benefit from an existing order may tolerate injustice while they believe their own security is safe. Pharaoh activates their fear of losing land, status, and identity. He does not ask the chiefs to consider whether Israel is oppressed. He asks them to imagine Egyptians being expelled. Fear of imagined dispossession becomes a reason to ignore actual domination.

---

## Verse 26:36

> They replied, “Let him and his brother wait and dispatch mobilizers to all cities

**The Chiefs Choose Delay Instead of Judgment**

The chiefs do not answer whether Moses' signs are true. They advise Pharaoh to let him and Aaron wait while the state gathers specialists from the cities. The proposal creates time, but not for reflection. It delays the demand for Israel's release and moves the court toward a public contest that can be managed by royal power. The issue of revelation is turned into an event the government can schedule.

The verb behind let him wait can carry the sense of postponing, holding back, or delaying. Some commentators understood it as a request to keep Moses and Aaron confined for a while, while others took it as a proposal to postpone the confrontation. The latter fits the immediate wording better: the chiefs are arranging the collection of magicians, and the next verses describe the gathering. In either case, the purpose is containment while Pharaoh prepares a response.

**Delay Can Be a Form of Resistance**

Not every refusal is spoken as a clear no. A ruler may ask for another committee, another inquiry, or another performance until the original demand has lost its urgency. Pharaoh's court uses delay in this way. The Israelites remain under control while officials organize a spectacle. Time is presented as prudence, but the delay protects the existing order.

---

## Verse 26:37

> to bring you every skilled magician.”

**Pharaoh's Court Mobilizes Expertise Against Truth**

The chiefs want every skilled magician brought to Pharaoh. The word every gives the plan an air of completeness. If the kingdom can assemble its best practitioners, it can make Moses' sign appear ordinary or defeat it in public. The court is not content with one rival performer. It wants a national search for the greatest available expertise, as though quantity and reputation can settle the source of a miracle.

This response reveals how power treats knowledge when knowledge threatens power. Expertise is not invited to investigate the event freely. It is recruited to defend a conclusion already chosen. The magicians are valuable because they can support Pharaoh's interpretation, not because the court is willing to follow whatever the evidence shows.

**Skill Is Real but Not the Same as Prophetic Authority**

A person can be highly trained and still work within the limits of created means. Skill can manipulate appearances, influence attention, and produce fear. It cannot make the practitioner independent of God or turn a human claim into divine authority. Pharaoh's court confuses technical mastery with the right to define reality. The verse prepares the reader to see that expertise must itself be judged by its source and purpose.

---

## Verse 26:38

> So the magicians were assembled at the set time on the appointed day

**The Court Fixes Truth Into a Public Appointment**

The magicians are gathered at a set time on an appointed day. Pharaoh's response to Moses has become a scheduled event, with a place in the kingdom's calendar and a public expectation attached to it. The wording gives the confrontation order and visibility. It will not be a hidden exchange where the court can later alter the story. The chosen time makes the encounter a test before witnesses.

The Qur'an elsewhere identifies the day as a festival day when the people would gather after the sun had risen high. That detail in 20:59 — *“Moses said, “Your appointment is on the Day of the Festival, and let the people be gathered mid-morning.”* explains why the appointment could draw a large audience. The court selects a time that is socially available and symbolically useful. A religious or civic gathering becomes the stage on which Pharaoh hopes to defend his authority.

**A Set Time Removes the Excuse of Confusion**

The gathering at an appointed time allows the people to prepare and attend. No one need claim that the signs were shown in a hidden corner or that the magicians were brought without opportunity to see the event. Public timing increases the evidence available to the audience. It also increases responsibility. People who attend cannot later say that the confrontation was merely rumor.

---

## Verse 26:39

> And the people were asked, “Will you join the gathering

**The Court Turns a Religious Dispute Into a Public Event**

The people are asked whether they will join the gathering. The passive wording leaves the speaker unnamed, which fits a public mobilization carried through officials and announcements. The invitation sounds ordinary, but it draws the population into a contest arranged by the state. Attendance will make the confrontation appear communal rather than limited to Pharaoh and two messengers.

**Joining the Crowd Is Not the Same as Judging the Claim**

The question invites attendance, not conviction. A person may come out of curiosity, fear, habit, or hope of seeing justice. Presence alone does not show agreement with Pharaoh. This distinction matters because institutions often count attendance as consent. A crowd at a state event can contain spectators who have not accepted the state's interpretation.

The people therefore retain a responsibility after arriving. They must not let the size of the gathering decide what is true. A public event can offer evidence, but it can also use noise, excitement, and group identity to prevent careful thought. The invitation creates access to the scene; it does not remove the need for judgment.

---

## Verse 26:40

> so that we may follow the magicians if they prevail?”

**The Crowd Is Invited to Follow Victory Rather Than Truth**

The purpose of the gathering is stated plainly: the people may follow the magicians if they prevail. The condition makes victory the measure of allegiance. Instead of asking whether Moses is a true messenger or whether the Israelites deserve freedom, the public is asked to wait for a winner and attach itself to that winner. A religious and moral question is reduced to a contest of outcomes.

This framing favors Pharaoh before the contest begins. The magicians already represent the ruler's side, and their success will be presented as a public vindication. The people are not told that they might follow Moses if his sign prevails. The missing alternative reveals the limits of the invitation. It is not an open search for truth; it is a managed expectation of royal victory.

**The Omitted Alternative Exposes Propaganda**

Commentators noticed what the sentence leaves unsaid: no one proposes following Moses if the magicians fail. That omission may reflect the political risk of acknowledging Moses as a possible guide. If the crowd were invited to follow whichever claim proved true, Pharaoh's authority would already be placed below the evidence. The court cannot allow that possibility to be named.

---

## Verse 26:41

> When the magicians came, they asked Pharaoh, “Shall we have a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?”

**The Specialists Enter as Paid Participants**

The magicians arrive and begin with a question about reward. Their first concern is not the truth of Pharaoh's accusation or the meaning of Moses' signs. They want to know what they will receive if they win. That detail places the contest inside an economy of power. Pharaoh has summoned their skill, and they expect the ruler to value it materially and politically.

The question does not by itself prove that every magician is corrupt. Payment for useful work can be legitimate. The moral issue is the purpose for which the skill is being hired. Here the requested performance is meant to protect a tyrant's claim and to discredit a messenger. The reward question exposes the conditions under which expertise can become a servant of power: the practitioner looks first to the patron's payment rather than to the truth of the matter.

**Reward Reveals the Court's Understanding of Success**

The magicians ask about winning, not about clarifying the evidence. Their language assumes that the encounter will be judged like a competition. Success means defeating the rival before the crowd, and the prize belongs to the side that controls the outcome. This is the court's frame entering the mouths of the specialists.

---

The magicians' question about a reward is put before they have shown anything, and the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf records the same exchange with the addition that the reward they named was nearness to the king (7:113–114 — *“The magicians came to Pharaoh, saying, “Shall we receive a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?” He replied, “Yes, and you will certainly be among those closest to me.”*). Their standing reveals what the contest was about: not truth but employment, and the sūrah's staging turns on it, since the same men who asked for a wage end by refusing the wage and everything it could buy. The verse's note that they came as a body of specialists also fixes the scene: Pharaoh assembled the best craft his country had against a shepherd's staff.

## Verse 26:42

> He replied, “Yes, and you will then certainly be among those closest to me.”

**Nearness Here Is First a Political Rank**

The phrase among those closest to me is best read in the immediate context as nearness to Pharaoh's court. The magicians have asked what reward they will receive, and the ruler answers with a promise of privileged standing. Some commentators also heard a spiritual or symbolic sense in nearness, but the political meaning fits the exchange most directly. Pharaoh is not offering closeness to God.

**Pharaoh Pays With Access to Power**

Pharaoh's answer promises more than a fee. Victory will bring the magicians close to him. In a royal court, proximity is a form of power. Those who stand near the ruler receive attention, protection, influence, and opportunities unavailable to ordinary people. Pharaoh therefore buys the magicians' loyalty with the currency of status as well as money.

The promise shows how a tyrant sustains an official story. He does not rely only on threats. He rewards people who can make his claim appear credible. The magicians are offered a place within the ruling circle if they defeat Moses, so their personal advancement becomes tied to the court's interpretation of the signs. Their skill is being converted into an investment in Pharaoh's authority.

---

## Verse 26:43

> Moses said to them, “Cast whatever you wish to cast.”

**Moses Gives the Magicians the First Move**

Moses tells the magicians to cast whatever they wish to cast. He does not impose a narrow choice or ask them to perform only within conditions favorable to him. The invitation gives them the first move before the public. Confidence in God's sign allows him to let the opposing side display its full craft rather than trying to win through procedure.

**A Fair Hearing Can Expose an Unfair Interpretation**

Moses' openness makes the later accusation against him easier to judge. If he had prevented the magicians from performing, Pharaoh could claim that the result was produced by unequal rules. By inviting them to cast whatever they choose, Moses gives the public a complete comparison. The court will have no honest basis for saying that its experts were silenced.

This is an important form of courage in public argument. Let an opposing claim be stated strongly enough that it can be examined. Do not weaken it first and then congratulate yourself for answering it. Moses allows the magicians to present their best case. The sign will not be protected by hiding the rival performance; it will be understood by seeing what the rival performance cannot do.

---

## Verse 26:44

> So they cast down their ropes and staffs, saying, “By Pharaoh’s might, it is we who will surely prevail.”

**The Magicians Fill the Stage With Imitation**

The magicians cast their ropes and staffs. The plural objects create a wide display, not a single private trick. Their craft is arranged for the eyes of the crowd, and their words accompany the performance with a declaration of victory. The scene combines material technique, public confidence, and political allegiance. They do not simply cast; they cast while announcing what the audience is supposed to conclude.

**The Objects Become Instruments of Political Religion**

Ropes and staffs are used to make a ruler's claim appear victorious. Their performance is therefore not religiously neutral. The court has arranged the contest to defend Pharaoh's authority, and the magicians openly identify that authority as the source of their confidence. Objects that could have remained tools of craft become instruments in a campaign against Moses' message.

This pattern appears whenever skilled work is pressed into the service of a sacred political image. The tool itself may be harmless, but the use made of it can support falsehood. The magician who knows how to influence sight now helps the state influence judgment. The crowd must decide whether the spectacle deserves allegiance or whether it is being used to conceal the moral question about Israel.

---

## Verse 26:45

> Then Moses threw down his staff, and—behold!—it devoured the objects of their illusion

**The Objects of Illusion Are Not the Same as the Objects Themselves**

The verse says what the magicians made falsely, not that their ropes and staffs ceased to exist as physical objects. Their craft produced an appearance that affected perception. The staff of Moses consumes the objects as they appear within the contest, undoing the false presentation and leaving the audience without the illusion that had filled the stage.

**The Miracle Defeats the Court's Political Story**

Pharaoh's claim was that Moses used magic to drive the people from their land. The sign now turns the accusation against the court's chosen explanation. If the magicians' objects are the thing that is consumed, then the court cannot honestly say that Moses' staff is simply another device in the same contest. Its political story has lost the ability to contain the event.

This defeat is more than a loss in spectacle. The court had offered the people a choice between Pharaoh's magicians and Moses as rival performers. The staff reveals that the real conflict is between divine truth and the deception used to protect Pharaoh. The political frame collapses because the sign refuses to remain inside it.

---

## Verse 26:46

> So the magicians fell down, prostrating

**Prostration Reverses the Court's Hierarchy**

The royal court had arranged the contest so that everyone would look toward Pharaoh's chosen experts. Now those experts turn away from Pharaoh's authority and lower themselves before the Lord whose sign has prevailed. The movement is political as well as devotional. The people who were meant to confirm the ruler's power become witnesses that a higher power has appeared.

Prostration also reverses the visual language of the event. Pharaoh stands as the ruler who expects others to obey, while the magicians fall. Yet their falling is not humiliation in the sense of defeat by a human rival. It is honor because it places the body in the right relation to God. The court sees that submission to God can be dignity, while service to a false lord can be degradation even when it brings promotion.

**The Plural Shows a Shared Recognition**

The verse speaks of the magicians together. Their response is not presented as one unusual individual breaking ranks while the rest continue. The group recognizes the truth of the sign and acts in concert. That shared response is important because Pharaoh had assembled them as a collective authority. The same collective now becomes a collective witness against the court's interpretation.

---

## Verse 26:47

> They declared, “We ˹now˺ believe in the Lord of all worlds—

**The Magicians Name the Lord Their Sign Has Revealed**

After prostrating, the magicians declare belief in the Lord of all worlds. Their confession identifies the source of the sign and breaks with the court's account. They do not say merely that Moses has performed the stronger technique. They recognize that the event points beyond technique to the universal Lord already proclaimed by the messenger.

**Belief Is a Change of Allegiance**

Their words are spoken before the same court that expected them to secure Pharaoh's rule. Belief therefore has a social cost. The magicians are not adding a private spiritual opinion to their professional identity while leaving their public loyalty unchanged. They are naming a Lord whose authority includes Pharaoh, the court, and the people gathered for the contest.

This is why Pharaoh will treat the confession as rebellion. If the magicians had merely admitted that Moses' sign was unusual, the ruler might have negotiated the explanation. They have done more. They have accepted the authority represented by the sign and placed it above the authority that hired them. Faith becomes visible when it changes whom a person obeys and whose interpretation he accepts.

---

## Verse 26:48

> the Lord of Moses and Aaron.”

**The Magicians Identify the Universal Lord Through His Messengers**

The magicians complete their confession by naming the Lord of Moses and Aaron. They do not leave the universal title at the level of an unnamed power. The signs have shown them that the Lord proclaimed by the brothers is the Lord of all worlds. This identification ties divine authority to the messengers who brought the claim without confusing the messengers with God.

The wording also confirms that Moses and Aaron speak together under one source. Pharaoh had tried to reduce their mission to a personal disturbance, but the magicians now recognize a shared commission. Their confession validates the relation between sign and messenger. What they saw did not point to a force detached from a message. It authenticated the Lord who sent these two servants.

**Naming Moses and Aaron Rejects Pharaoh's Interpretation**

Pharaoh called Moses a magician and implied that Moses' power came from a human teacher. The magicians' confession rejects that interpretation at its root. They do not say that Moses has mastered a superior school of magic. They call God the Lord of Moses and Aaron, placing the brothers on the side of servanthood and Pharaoh on the side of false authority.

---

## Verse 26:49

> Pharaoh threatened, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? He must be your master who taught you magic, but soon you will see. I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, then crucify you all.”

**Permission Language Exposes Pharaoh's Fear**

The demand for permission sounds confident, but it gives away fear. If the magicians' belief were harmless, Pharaoh would not need to control the moment at which it occurred. He fears that their public recognition will influence the crowd and weaken the royal interpretation. Permission is therefore a way to delay and contain truth until the ruler can decide how it may be spoken.

**Pharaoh Invents a Conspiracy to Avoid the Sign**

He claims that Moses is their master who taught them magic. This explanation has a political advantage. It makes the magicians' recognition look planned from the start, so the ruler can dismiss their testimony as collusion. The court no longer has to ask why trained practitioners saw a difference between their craft and Moses' sign. It can say that Moses recruited them into a plot.

The accusation is contradicted by the sequence of the story. The magicians arrived seeking reward and confidently invoked Pharaoh's might. Their prostration came after the sign, not before it. Pharaoh knows the sequence, but he chooses a story that protects his power. Conspiracy language is useful when a ruler cannot refute witnesses honestly. It changes a spontaneous recognition into an alleged crime.

---

Pharaoh's answer to their faith is the threat of mutilation and crucifixion, and the same scene is given twice more in the Book: in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where he accuses them of plotting to take the city (7:123–124 — *“Pharaoh threatened, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? This must be a conspiracy you devised in the city to drive out its people, but soon you will see I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, then crucify you all.”*), and in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where he calls them to be certain that he is their lord the highest (20:71 — *“Pharaoh threatened, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? He must be your master who taught you magic. I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, and crucify you on the trunks of palm trees. You will really see whose punishment is more severe and more lasting.”*). His claim that they believed without his permission states the political logic of the chapter: the state treats conscience as a licence it issues. The magicians' answer, which follows in the next verses, is that they will not prefer him to what has come to them.

## Verse 26:50

> They responded, “˹That would be˺ no harm! Surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return

**Return to the Lord Makes Courage Rational**

Their reason is that they will return to their Lord. This belief gives death a destination and history a final judge. Pharaoh can decide what happens to them in the court of Egypt, but he cannot prevent their return to the One who created them. The threat remains serious in the world, yet it is no longer ultimate.

The statement also corrects Pharaoh's claim to ownership. The ruler speaks as though the magicians' bodies and beliefs belong to him. They answer that their final belonging is to God. Return means that life is not a possession held permanently by the palace. It is a trust that comes from the Lord and goes back to Him, whether the ruler approves or not.

**No Harm Does Not Mean No Responsibility**

The phrase no harm can be misunderstood if separated from its context. It does not teach that oppression is harmless or that people may abuse one another because the Hereafter exists. Pharaoh's punishment remains evil, and the ruler remains accountable for it. The magicians are speaking about what the threat can ultimately do to their relationship with God, not excusing what Pharaoh intends to do.

---

## Verse 26:51

> We really hope that our Lord will forgive our sins, as we are the first to believe.”

**Hope Is Directed to Their New Lord**

They say our Lord. The possessive expresses belonging, but not ownership in Pharaoh's sense. God is their Lord because He created, guided, and will judge them; they are His servants because they return to Him. The phrase also marks the personal change in their allegiance. Pharaoh had promised to bring them near, but the magicians now seek forgiveness from another Lord.

This is why their hope can survive the ruler's threat. Pharaoh can withdraw his reward and harm their bodies, but he cannot close the path to the Lord they name. Their relationship with God is not a privilege granted by a court. It is the truth of their existence, newly recognized and publicly confessed.

**First Can Mean Earliest or Foremost**

The expression first to believe has been understood in several ways. It may mean the first among the Egyptians to accept this sign, the first of Pharaoh's people to believe in Moses' message, or the first sorcerers to make the confession. In another usage, first can carry the sense of being foremost rather than earliest in time. The immediate setting favors a local meaning: they are the first in this public contest to respond openly.

---

## Verse 26:52

> And We inspired Moses, ˹saying,˺ “Leave with My servants at night, for you will surely be pursued.”

**The Exodus Begins With a Timed Divine Command**

After the public confrontation and Pharaoh's response, God directs Moses to leave with His servants at night. The command is precise about both the companions and the time. Moses is not asked to improvise a departure from a powerful state. He receives guidance that turns a dangerous escape into an act of obedience.

**My Servants Restores the Israelites' True Identity**

Pharaoh has treated the Children of Israel as labor, property, and a source of political risk. God calls them My servants. That name overturns the ruler's classification. Their deepest identity is not the status assigned by the palace. They belong to God, and the journey out of Egypt begins when that belonging is acted upon.

The phrase also defines the purpose of the departure. They are not leaving merely to exchange one earthly authority for another. They are being led out as servants of God, people whose lives and worship are no longer to be controlled by Pharaoh's claim. Freedom has a direction. It moves them from a false ownership toward the rightful Lord, whose service is not enslavement but care, guidance, and moral responsibility.

---

The command to leave by night is given in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā with the added instruction to strike a dry path through the sea, with no fear of being overtaken and no anxiety (20:77 — *“Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned ˹of drowning˺.”*). The sūrah of the Poets gives the departure first and the pursuit after it, so that the sea-crossing arrives when the danger is greatest, and the sūrah of the Heifer records the same events as the deliverance God reminds the community of (2:50 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes”*). The Book's account is set within the death of the firstborn for the households of Egypt, which is why Pharaoh's household released them.

## Verse 26:53

> Then Pharaoh sent mobilizers to all cities

**Pharaoh Responds to Departure With State Mobilization**

As soon as the Israelites leave, Pharaoh sends mobilizers through the cities. The ruler's response is organized and immediate. He does not treat the departure as a lawful release that should be accepted. He uses the administrative network of the kingdom to turn the exodus into a pursuit. The same state that gathered magicians now gathers forces against a community seeking freedom.

The verb sent shows that Pharaoh's power works through agents. A tyrant may speak as though he alone acts, but his commands depend on officials who carry them into towns and roads. Mobilization distributes responsibility across the system. Each officer may claim to be obeying orders, while the combined action serves the ruler's refusal to release the Israelites.

**Mobilization Conceals the Moral Question**

The simple demand had been to let the Children of Israel go. Pharaoh's mobilization avoids answering whether he has the right to keep them. Once the pursuit begins, the question is recast as security: how should the state recover those who have left? The ruler's force makes possession look like law and escape look like rebellion.

---

## Verse 26:54

> ˹and said,˺ “These ˹outcasts˺ are just a handful of people

**Pharaoh Minimizes the People He Fears**

Pharaoh describes the departing Israelites as a small band. The wording is meant to reduce them before the pursuit begins. If they are only a handful, then their departure can be framed as a minor disturbance that the state will easily correct. The ruler's contempt tries to make his mobilization look proportionate even though the people he is chasing are vulnerable and unarmed compared with his host.

The Arabic term translated outcasts or small band can suggest a remnant separated from a larger body. That sense adds a political edge. Pharaoh speaks as though the Israelites are a leftover group with no independent weight. He does not name them as a people with a history and a divine claim. He defines them by their reduced status within his order.

**Counting People Can Become a Tool of Dehumanization**

Numbers can be used honestly to plan protection, or dishonestly to decide whose lives matter. Pharaoh's handful is not a neutral estimate. It is a rhetorical device that makes the Israelites sound too few to deserve attention and too weak to have legitimate aims. The same population can be called insignificant when the ruler wants to ignore it and dangerous when he wants to justify pursuit.

---

## Verse 26:55

> who have really enraged us

**Pharaoh Describes the Oppressed as the Offenders**

Pharaoh says that the departing people have enraged him. The sentence shifts attention from what he has done to how he feels. The Israelites' movement away from enslavement is presented as an insult to the ruler, while the oppression that caused the movement disappears from his account. Anger becomes evidence of the victims' guilt rather than a reaction that the ruler must examine.

**Enragement Is Not Proof of Wrongdoing**

A ruler may feel anger when his authority is challenged, but anger cannot serve as a legal judgment. The question should be what the Israelites have done and whether Pharaoh has a right to pursue them. Instead, the ruler offers his emotional state as a reason for mobilization. His people are expected to accept that because he is enraged, the departing community must be guilty.

This substitution is dangerous because it removes evidence from the discussion. A person can be sincerely furious and still be wrong. Injustice often becomes more violent when the wrongdoer treats his anger as a moral certificate. Pharaoh's statement asks the cities to join his emotional interpretation before they ask whether the Israelites have committed a crime.

---

## Verse 26:56

> but we are all on the alert.”

**Pharaoh Reassures the Court With Military Readiness**

Pharaoh describes his people as a host on the alert. The statement is meant to answer the smallness of the Israelites with the strength of Egypt. A handful may flee, he implies, but the kingdom is prepared to respond. The language turns military readiness into reassurance, asking the court and the cities to feel secure because the ruler has forces, equipment, and command.

**Military Confidence Conceals Spiritual Weakness**

The host is ready in the worldly sense, but it is not ready for what God has decreed. Pharaoh's court measures the coming encounter by numbers, equipment, and speed. Divine planning operates on another level. The host can be organized perfectly and still be unable to prevent the fulfillment of God's promise to the Israelites.

This contrast does not make preparation worthless. The Qur'an does not praise carelessness. It exposes the error of treating preparation as sovereignty. A force can prepare means, but it cannot command outcomes that belong to God. Pharaoh's readiness will make the eventual defeat more revealing because it cannot be blamed on disorder or lack of resources.

---

## Verse 26:57

> So We lured the tyrants out of ˹their˺ gardens, springs

**God Draws the Tyrants Out From Their Security**

The verse turns from Pharaoh's boast to God's action. The tyrants are brought out from gardens and springs, the settings of luxury that helped sustain their confidence. Their departure is described as something God caused, not as a victory won by the pursuing army. Pharaoh believes he is moving by his own decision, yet the narrative places his movement within a divine plan.

The wording translated lured can also be understood as made to leave or brought out. Commentators considered whether the phrase stresses God's direct removal of the Egyptians from their possessions or the desire placed in their hearts to chase the Israelites. Both readings fit the contrast. Pharaoh's army leaves the security of its wealth because it thinks pursuit will restore control, while God is drawing it toward the judgment prepared for its arrogance.

**Luxury Becomes a Trap When It Feeds Pride**

Gardens and springs are signs of provision, but the tyrants treat them as evidence that their power is secure. The beauty and abundance around them create a world in which consequences seem distant. They can imagine that the palace, farms, and water will remain theirs because they have learned to confuse possession with permanence.

---

## Verse 26:58

> treasures, and splendid residences

**The List Moves From Nature to Human-Made Splendor**

The verse completes the list of what the tyrants leave behind: treasures and splendid residences. The earlier gardens and springs represented cultivated abundance; these items represent wealth stored and status displayed. The sequence moves from the resources of the land to the structures and possessions that society builds around them. Pharaoh's world contains every visible sign by which a ruling class measures success.

**Splendid Residences Cannot Make a Tyrant at Home**

A residence is more than a building. It represents belonging, rank, memory, and the right to inhabit a place without fear. The tyrants' splendid residences embody the social order they have built. When they are brought out from them, the narrative shows that home is not secure when it is founded on the dispossession of others.

This does not mean that beautiful homes are blameworthy. The moral issue is the relationship between splendor and oppression. A residence becomes part of the indictment when its comfort depends on a system that denies another people freedom. The verse names the buildings because the buildings are not outside the judgment of the order that produced them.

---

## Verse 26:59

> So it was. And We awarded it ˹all˺ to the Children of Israel

**Inheritance Here Is a Divine Transfer of Status**

Commentators differed over how literally to understand the inheritance. Some took it to mean that the Israelites came to have the Egyptians' actual wealth. Others emphasized a transfer of worldly station and the reversal of power, while some reports connected the wealth with what the Israelites took by God's command when they left. The wording supports a real reversal, but the larger Qur'anic account cautions against forcing every detail into a single material scene.

The strongest conclusion holds together possession and status. God stripped the tyrants of what made them appear secure and gave the oppressed a share in the dignity and resources from which they had been excluded. Whether every residence was occupied immediately is a secondary question. The central truth is that Pharaoh's order was not the final owner of Egypt's good things.

**The Transfer Is Judgment, Not Mere Reward**

The Israelites are not described here as receiving wealth because wealth itself proves their virtue. The transfer occurs within judgment on Pharaoh. The tyrants lose what they used to support domination. The Israelites receive what God's decree assigns them. This is a moral reversal in history, not a doctrine that material possession always marks divine favor.

---

## Verse 26:60

> And so they pursued them at sunrise

**The Pursuit Begins in the Fullness of Day**

The Egyptians pursue the Israelites at sunrise. The timing gives the chase a clear visual setting. The Israelites have left at night under God's command, while the pursuing force moves when daylight makes tracking easier. Pharaoh's host is no longer searching through darkness. It advances with the confidence that visibility, numbers, and speed will return the fugitives to its control.

Sunrise also creates a contrast between the two movements. The Israelites depart quietly under divine instruction; the Egyptians follow openly under the authority of a ruler who has refused to release them. Night protected the vulnerable community, while day reveals the scale of the force behind it. The verse is brief because the pursuit itself is the point: Pharaoh's refusal has become physical and direct.

**Daylight Does Not Give the Pursuers Divine Sight**

This distinction continues the chapter's contrast between appearance and truth. Pharaoh's people have watched signs in the court and still chosen a false interpretation. Now they see the fleeing community and read the sight as an opportunity to recover property. The light of day cannot correct a moral blindness that the ruler has taught them to share.

---

## Verse 26:61

> When the two groups came face to face, the companions of Moses cried out, “We are overtaken for sure.”

**The Pursuit Becomes a Visible Trap**

The two groups now meet in direct view of one another. The Israelites have the sea before them and Pharaoh's host behind them. The distance that allowed hope and movement has vanished. The companions see the situation through its immediate physical facts: one route seems blocked, the other is occupied by a powerful army. Their cry expresses the conclusion that those facts appear to demand.

The verse names two groups rather than repeating every identity. The Egyptians are the pursuing host, while Moses' companions are the people who left under God's command. The simple arrangement makes the danger clear without dramatizing it. A community that has escaped oppression is now confronted by the full machinery of the oppressor. The night departure has become a morning crisis.

**Fear Speaks in the Language of Certainty**

They say they are overtaken for sure. Fear often turns an interpretation into a fact. The companions do not say that the army is near or that their options are narrow. They state the outcome as settled. Their bodies are still alive and their journey is still under God's plan, but fear has narrowed their view to the apparent impossibility in front of them.

---

## Verse 26:62

> Moses reassured ˹them˺, “Absolutely not! My Lord is certainly with me—He will guide me.”

**Moses Rejects Despair Without Denying the Army**

Moses begins with an emphatic refusal: absolutely not. He does not accept the companions' conclusion that the pursuit has ended their hope. His answer is not a claim that the Egyptians are far away or that the sea is easy to cross. He rejects the meaning they have attached to the danger. The army may be near, but it is not the lord of the situation.

**My Lord Makes Faith Personal and Relational**

Moses says my Lord. The phrase is personal without being private. He knows the Lord who called him, supported him before Pharaoh, and led the Israelites out at night. That relationship gives him a basis for confidence when ordinary evidence points toward defeat. He does not rely on a general idea that some power might help. He relies on the Lord who has already acted in his mission.

The possessive also helps the companions. Moses is not claiming a special deity unavailable to them. The Lord who is with him is the Lord who brought the people out and who has commanded their journey. His personal trust becomes a public reminder that divine care has not stopped at the edge of the sea. The prophet's certainty is meant to steady the community, not to glorify the prophet.

---

## Verse 26:63

> So We inspired Moses: “Strike the sea with your staff,” and the sea was split, each part was like a huge mountain

**Guidance Becomes a Concrete Command**

The promise that God would guide Moses now becomes an instruction: strike the sea with the staff. Moses is not asked to reason his way through the barrier or to wait for a natural opening. The command joins a familiar instrument to an extraordinary result. The staff that served him in ordinary work becomes the means through which God opens a path for the trapped community.

**The Sea Is Described Without Being Named**

The Qur'an does not identify the exact body of water. Muslim and Jewish tradition often associates the crossing with the Red Sea, but the revealed verse itself stresses the miracle rather than the geographical label. Leaving the name unspecified keeps attention on God's power and on the deliverance of the people, not on a detail the text has chosen not to settle.

This restraint is important in interpretation. A traditional identification may help readers imagine the scene, but it should not be presented as though the verse supplied a modern map reference. The certain facts are enough: Moses was commanded to strike the sea, it split, and the divided water stood like mountains. The miracle's meaning does not depend on claiming more geographical precision than revelation gives.

---

The sea is opened by the command, and the Book describes the crossing in three other places with the same details: the waters parted as if they were great mounds, the path dried, and the pursuers drowned (20:77–78 — *“And We surely inspired Moses, ˹saying,˺ “Leave with My servants ˹at night˺ and strike a dry passage for them across the sea. Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned ˹of drowning˺.” Then Pharaoh pursued them with his soldiers—but how overwhelming were the waters that submerged them”*; 2:50 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes”*; 44:24 — *“And leave the sea parted, for they are certainly an army bound to drown.”*). The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf adds the splitting of the sea into twelve roads, one for each tribe, with the water like great mountains between them (7:143 context — *“When Moses came at the appointed time and his Lord spoke to him, he asked, “My Lord! Reveal Yourself to me so I may see You.” Allah answered, “You cannot see Me! But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me.” When his Lord appeared to the mountain, He levelled it to dust and Moses collapsed unconscious. When he recovered, he cried, “Glory be to You! I turn to You in repentance and I am the first of the believers.”*). This verse's image, that each part of the sea stood like a mountain, is the Book's way of saying that the way of escape was surrounded on both sides by the thing that would have drowned them if the command had not come.

## Verse 26:64

> We drew the pursuers to that place

**The Pursuers Are Brought Into the Scene of Deliverance**

The verse shifts attention from the opened sea to the Egyptian host. God draws the pursuers to that place. Pharaoh's army has followed with confidence, expecting to recover the Israelites. It now arrives at the location where the sea has become a path for the oppressed and a test for the oppressor.

The wording exposes a reversal in movement. The Egyptians believe they are acting freely and closing the distance by their own strength. The narrative reveals that their arrival is also within God's action. They are brought to the place of judgment, not because their pursuit is wise or legitimate, but because their own decision to chase has led them there. Divine sovereignty surrounds the choices of the pursuers without removing their responsibility for those choices.

**A Place of Rescue Can Also Be a Place of Judgment**

The same location has two meanings. For Moses and his companions, it is a route opened by God. For the Egyptians, it is the place toward which the consequences of tyranny are drawing them. A physical site does not carry one fixed moral meaning for everyone who enters it. The sea is a path for those obeying God and a danger for those pursuing oppression.

---

## Verse 26:65

> and delivered Moses and those with him all together

**The Prophet's Safety Is Joined to the People's Safety**

Moses is not delivered into a separate privilege. His fate is narrated alongside the fate of those with him. This reflects the nature of prophetic leadership. A messenger's success is not measured by personal survival while the people remain vulnerable. The prophet guides, warns, and moves with the community, and God's rescue confirms the message by protecting those who trust it.

**The Earlier Warning Becomes Visible Fulfillment**

God had told Moses that He would be with him and guide him. The deliverance is the visible fulfillment of that assurance. Moses' confidence at the shore was not an empty speech. The Lord who accompanied him in Pharaoh's court has now accompanied him through the sea. The people witness the continuity between promise and act.

A parallel account in 2:50 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes”* recalls the sea being parted, Israel being saved, and Pharaoh's people drowning before the witnesses. That cross-reference confirms the public and communal character of this deliverance. It is not a private vision known only to Moses. It is an event through which a people learns what God's protection means.

---

## Verse 26:66

> Then We drowned the others

**The Pursuers Meet the End of Their Own Pursuit**

The verse describes the fate of those who followed Pharaoh into the sea: they are drowned. The sentence is brief because the moral sequence has already been established. Pharaoh refused the command to release Israel, mobilized the cities, called the departing people a threat, and sent a ready host after them. The drowning is not an arbitrary disaster detached from those choices. It is the judgment that overtakes a force committed to recovering the oppressed.

The word others distinguishes the drowned host from Moses and those delivered with him. The separation is complete. The same sea that opened as a path for the Israelites does not become a possession of the pursuers. God's sign has a different consequence for each group according to the purpose with which each entered the scene.

**Drowning Is a Divine Judgment, Not a Victory of Human Force**

The Egyptians are not defeated by an Israelite army. No human host is credited with overpowering Pharaoh. God Himself is the agent of the drowning. That attribution matters because the story is about the limits of tyranny before divine sovereignty, not about one earthly power replacing another.

---

## Verse 26:67

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**The Sea Crossing Becomes Evidence for Every Witness**

The verse calls the entire episode a sign. The sign includes the command to leave, the opening of the sea, the rescue of Moses and his companions, and the drowning of the pursuing host. No single detail carries the whole argument by itself. Together the events reveal God's power, His care for the oppressed, and the danger of treating tyranny as permanent.

**Most Still Refuse the Meaning of What They See**

The statement that most would not believe follows a miracle of extraordinary public force. That sequence rejects the idea that every person believes once evidence becomes dramatic enough. The event can be clear while the response remains resistant. People may explain it away, refuse its demand, or admire it without surrendering their authority to God.

Most is not all. The wording leaves room for those who respond rightly, including the believers who followed Moses. It also warns against judging truth by majority. A large number of people can witness a sign and still choose the interpretation that protects habit or power. The sign's strength is not measured by the percentage of people willing to submit to it.

---

## Verse 26:68

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**The Refrain Reads the Sea Through Two Divine Names**

The account of Moses ends with two names: the Almighty and the Most Merciful. Al-ʿAzīz names power that cannot be overcome; al-Raḥīm names mercy that reaches servants with care. The pairing gives the correct interpretation of the sea. Israel was not rescued by chance, and Pharaoh was not drowned by uncontrolled force. The Lord acted with power and mercy together.

**Mercy Explains Why Warning Came Before Judgment**

The name Most Merciful recalls the long interval before the drowning. Moses was sent, signs were shown, Pharaoh was addressed, and the magicians were given an opportunity to recognize the truth. Judgment did not arrive without warning. Mercy had already appeared in the form of repeated invitations to stop the oppression and release Israel.

This prevents a distorted view of the final act. Mercy is not only the opening of the sea. It is also the time granted to Pharaoh and his people before the sea closed. A warning that exposes danger is a mercy because it makes repentance possible. When the tyrant uses the interval to deepen pursuit, the final judgment remains just rather than sudden cruelty.

---

## Verse 26:69

> Relate to them ˹O Prophet˺ the story of Abraham

**The Prophet Is Told to Make Abraham's Story Public**

The command to relate Abraham's story changes the scene from the sea to a family and a community debating worship. The Prophet is not asked to invent a new argument for monotheism. He is told to bring an earlier prophetic encounter before his audience. Abraham's history becomes a living proof that the call to worship God alone is not a recent demand tied to one nation or one messenger.

**Abraham Follows Moses as a Different Model of Confrontation**

Moses confronted a ruler who claimed divine authority and enslaved a people. Abraham will confront idolatry in his family and society. The settings differ, but the central question remains who deserves worship and who has the right to define truth. The transition from one story to the other shows that false lordship can appear as political tyranny or as inherited religious devotion.

This sequence broadens the lesson of the sea. Deliverance from a tyrant is not the only form of monotheistic struggle. A person may live without Pharaoh's army and still give ultimate loyalty to objects, ancestors, or social habits. Abraham's story brings the call into the intimate space of family and community, where obedience to custom can be as strong as fear of a ruler.

---

## Verse 26:70

> when he questioned his father and his people, “What is that you worship ˹besides Allah˺?”

**Abraham Begins With the Object of Worship**

Abraham's first recorded act in this story is a question. He asks his father and his people what they worship besides Allah. The question reaches both the household and the wider community. Idolatry is not treated as a private preference that no one may examine. Worship shapes a people's understanding of power, dependence, and moral duty, so Abraham addresses it openly.

**The Story Connects to Abraham's Earlier Debate**

A related account in 6:74 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said to his father, Ȃzar, “Do you take idols as gods? It is clear to me that you and your people are entirely misguided.”* records Abraham questioning his father about taking idols as gods. That parallel confirms that the family conversation is part of a consistent prophetic method. Abraham presses the question of what can be called a god before discussing signs in the heavens or the weakness of particular images.

The recurrence also shows that the Qur'an presents Abraham's monotheism as a deliberate break with inherited worship. He does not merely move away from idols in private. He calls the family and the community to account for the authority they have granted them. His questioning is therefore both intellectual and moral.

---

## Verse 26:71

> They replied, “We worship idols, to which we are fully devoted.”

**An Idol Is a Social Center Before It Is a Stone**

The word idols points to visible forms, but the practice includes the social order built around them. Shrines, rituals, caretakers, festivals, and family expectations can all support the object's authority. People may fear that abandoning the idol will mean abandoning their ancestors, neighbors, and place in society. Devotion is therefore reinforced by belonging.

**A Related Example Shows How Devotion Can Persist**

The Qur'an later describes the Israelites saying that they will remain devoted to a calf until Moses returns (20:91 — *“They replied, “We will not cease to worship it until Moses returns to us.”*). That parallel shows how quickly a community can attach intense loyalty to a created form, even after receiving signs of God's care. Devotion can persist through waiting, uncertainty, and social pressure because it gives the heart a visible object around which to gather.

Abraham's people stand at the same danger. Their devotion is not proof that the idols deserve it. It is the very reason the practice must be examined. The question is whether the heart's steadfastness is anchored in truth or merely in the strength of habit.

---

## Verse 26:72

> Abraham asked, “Can they hear you when you call upon them

**Abraham Tests Worship Through a Basic Human Relation**

Abraham asks whether the idols can hear when people call upon them. The question begins with a capacity that any meaningful caller expects from a responsive addressee. Hearing is not the whole of divinity, but its absence exposes a basic problem. The worshippers direct appeals toward objects that do not receive the appeals in the human sense.

The question is careful. Abraham does not begin with an obscure argument about metaphysics. He asks about what happens when the worshipper calls. Does the idol perceive the request, distinguish the voice, and respond to the person standing before it? If not, the ritual has the outward form of communication without an answering subject. The community must explain why it calls to something unable to hear.

**Hearing Is a Sign of Relation, Not the Whole Definition of God**

The question should not be mistaken for a claim that God is limited to a created faculty of hearing. Divine hearing is perfect and unlike the hearing of bodies. Abraham uses a humanly understandable capacity to expose the idols' helplessness. He is not defining God by comparison with an image. He is showing that the image lacks even the basic relation its worshippers assume.

---

## Verse 26:73

> Or can they benefit or harm you?”

**Abraham Tests the Idols' Power Over Consequence**

The second question moves from hearing to effect. Can the idols benefit or harm their worshippers? The issue is not whether an image can influence feelings or whether a shrine can organize social life. It is whether the object has power over the goods and dangers that shape human existence. If people fear an idol's anger or hope for its favor, the idol must have more than symbolic value in their theology.

Benefit and harm form a broad pair. They include protection and danger, provision and loss, healing and affliction, success and failure. Abraham asks whether the idols have any real agency in these matters. The question brings the community's religious claims into ordinary life, where people know that an inert object cannot independently alter the world around it.

**Worship Is Tied to Dependence on Power**

People do not usually worship an object only because it is beautiful. They worship because they believe it can secure something or avert something. Abraham's question identifies that dependence. If the idols cannot benefit or harm, then the fear and hope directed toward them lack a worthy object.

---

## Verse 26:74

> They replied, “No! But we found our forefathers doing the same.”

**The Idolaters Admit the Evidence Against Their Gods**

Abraham's people answer that the idols cannot hear, benefit, or harm. Their admission is decisive at the level of capacity. They do not defend the idols as active powers. Instead, they give a reason for continuing to worship them: their forefathers did the same. The community has conceded the weakness of its objects and shifted from evidence to ancestry.

**Custom Can Preserve a Contradiction**

The answer contains a contradiction. The people know the idols cannot benefit or harm, yet they remain devoted to them. The contradiction survives because custom supplies social meaning that the idols themselves lack. A ritual can feel authoritative when generations have repeated it, even if its object has never shown power.

Abraham's role is to make the contradiction visible without allowing it to hide behind reverence for ancestors. A practice does not become true by aging. If anything, a long-standing error can become harder to see because people confuse familiarity with evidence. The community's answer shows why reasoning must be renewed in every generation rather than outsourced to the dead.

**The Qur'an Repeats This Debate Across Communities**

The same defense appears in other Qur'anic discussions of inherited practice, including 5:104 — *“When it is said to them, “Come to Allah’s revelations and to the Messenger,” they reply, “What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us.” ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance”* and 31:21 — *“When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.” ˹Would they still do so˺ even if Satan is inviting them to the torment of the Blaze”*, where people appeal to what their forefathers followed. The repeated pattern shows that Abraham's people are not unique in using ancestry to avoid examination. The argument recurs whenever custom becomes more authoritative than revelation and reason.

---

## Verse 26:75

> Abraham responded, “Have you ˹really˺ considered what you have been worshipping—

**Abraham Demands Reflection Rather Than Repetition**

Abraham answers the appeal to forefathers with a new question: have you truly considered what you worship? The question targets the gap between performing a practice and examining its object. His people have admitted that the idols cannot benefit or harm, yet they continue to worship them because previous generations did so. Abraham asks whether they have ever stopped to think about what that devotion means.

**The Question Includes the Whole History of Their Worship**

The phrase what you have been worshipping gathers present practice and inherited practice into one object of examination. Abraham does not ask only about a single statue or one ceremony. He asks them to look at the entire system by which idols have received their devotion. The community must consider not just what it does today, but what it has been doing across generations.

That wider scope prevents a narrow defense. A person might admit that one image is powerless while preserving the general institution of idols. Abraham asks about the whole direction of worship. If every object shares the same inability to hear, help, or harm, then changing one image will not solve the problem. The community must reconsider the category itself.

---

## Verse 26:76

> you and your ancestors

**The Question Reaches Beyond One Generation**

The continuation of Abraham's question includes both the present worshippers and their ancestors. He does not allow the people to place all responsibility in the past. Their forefathers may have begun or preserved the practice, but the living generation has adopted it again and is now answerable for what it worships. The question therefore crosses time without confusing the responsibilities of different people.

**The Question Separates Explanation From Justification**

The ancestors can explain why the idols are present. They cannot justify why the idols deserve worship. This distinction is essential whenever custom is used as proof. History tells us how a belief arrived; it does not tell us whether the belief is sound. Abraham's question draws attention to that missing step.

A community can preserve an error for centuries through repetition, authority, and fear of change. The length of the process may make the error difficult to challenge, but it never turns repetition into revelation. Abraham asks the people to move from “this is what they did” to “this is why this object deserves worship.” If they cannot make that movement, their defense remains only imitation.

---

## Verse 26:77

> They are ˹all˺ enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds

**Abraham Draws a Boundary Around His Allegiance**

After questioning the idols and the inherited worship surrounding them, Abraham declares that they are all enemies to him except the Lord of all worlds. The sentence is a statement of allegiance. He will not remain spiritually neutral between the Creator and objects that compete with Him. Worship requires a clear direction, and Abraham names the Lord as the one relation he accepts.

The wording “all” gives the declaration breadth. Abraham does not preserve one favorite idol as a harmless exception, nor does he allow the community to divide worship among many objects. The false objects stand on one side, and the Lord of the worlds stands on the other. The boundary is not drawn by personal taste. It is drawn by the difference between the created and the Creator.

**Enmity Names the Moral Effect of Idolatry**

Calling the idols enemies may seem stronger than calling them powerless. Abraham is identifying what false worship does to a human being. An object that cannot hear, benefit, or harm becomes an enemy when people give it the devotion, fear, and obedience that belong to God. It turns the heart away from reality and trains the community to protect an empty authority.

---

## Verse 26:78

> ˹He is˺ the One Who created me, and He ˹alone˺ guides me

**Creation Leads to Guidance**

Abraham does not stop at “He created me.” A person may acknowledge a creator and still wonder how to live. The verse joins origin to direction. The One who created Abraham also guides him. Creation is not an act that leaves the creature abandoned in a meaningless world. It is followed by care, instruction, and a path by which the creature can fulfill the purpose of existence.

**Abraham Begins With Creation**

After rejecting false objects of worship, Abraham identifies the Lord through two acts: creation and guidance. The first is creation. God is the One who brought Abraham into existence, and that origin establishes a relation no idol can claim. An image may be carved by human hands, but it cannot create the person who stands before it. The Creator's claim precedes every custom, shrine, and inherited name.

Creation also means more than a beginning in the distant past. Abraham's existence remains dependent on the One who created him. He does not own himself in an absolute sense, and the idols do not own him at all. Worship becomes intelligible because the creature's life comes from the Creator. The Lord's right is grounded in reality, not in a social agreement.

---

## Verse 26:79

> ˹He is˺ the One Who provides me with food and drink

**Abraham Sees Provision as a Divine Act**

Abraham continues his description of the Lord by naming food and drink. These are ordinary necessities, but their ordinariness is precisely the point. Divine care is not limited to extraordinary signs. The Lord who created and guides also provides what sustains the body day after day. Abraham's worship grows from noticing the familiar gifts that habit can make invisible.

Food and drink come through many visible means: cultivation, labor, animals, water, trade, and the hands of other people. Abraham's statement does not deny those means. It identifies the ultimate Provider behind them. Created causes have a role, but they do not become independent lords. The idol cannot produce the life it claims to protect, while the Lord gives the conditions under which every meal and drink become possible.

**Provision Creates a Community of Gratitude**

No one eats completely alone in the moral sense. Food carries the labor of many people and the gifts of soil, water, animals, and time. Remembering God as Provider widens the heart's field of gratitude. It becomes harder to treat another person's hunger as irrelevant when one's own nourishment is understood as mercy rather than entitlement.

---

## Verse 26:80

> And He ˹alone˺ heals me when I am sick

**Abraham Names God as the Healer**

Abraham now speaks about sickness and healing. After mentioning creation, guidance, food, and drink, he identifies the Lord as the One who heals him when he is ill. The statement recognizes a need that can make human beings feel vulnerable and powerless. Illness reveals that the body is not self-enough, while healing reveals care that reaches the person in weakness.

**Abraham Attributes Healing to God With Deliberate Language**

He says “He heals me,” rather than assigning healing to an idol, to luck, or to his own control. The wording preserves divine agency without pretending that illness is unreal. Abraham expects sickness to occur and speaks of recovery within that reality. Faith is not the denial of the body's limits. It is trust that the body's Lord remains present within them.

The statement also carries personal tenderness. Abraham does not speak only of a general principle that God heals creatures. He says that God heals me. The Lord's care is experienced in the singular life of a servant. This personal recognition can sustain a person through illness without turning every delay in recovery into a verdict about God's absence.

---

## Verse 26:81

> And He ˹is the One Who˺ will cause me to die, and then bring me back to life

**Death Belongs to the Same Lord as Creation**

Abraham's account of God moves from creation and provision to death. The transition is deliberate. The Lord who gives life does not lose authority when life reaches its appointed end. Death is not an outside power that interrupts God's rule. It occurs under the command of the One who created the person and sustained him throughout his days.

This conviction changes the meaning of mortality. Abraham does not speak as though death is a rival deity or a meaningless accident. He places it within the relation between servant and Lord. The human being receives life for a time, then returns to the One who gave it. Idols cannot claim that authority because they neither began Abraham's life nor determine its true end.

**The Two Acts Announce a Complete Divine Care**

A parallel prayer in 14:39 — *“All praise is for Allah who has blessed me with Ishmael and Isaac in my old age. My Lord is indeed the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers”* praises God for granting Abraham children in old age. That account and this verse share the same outlook: human life is received at every stage, whether beginning, continuing, ending, or being restored. Abraham's worship is therefore grounded in the Lord's complete authority over the servant's journey.

---

## Verse 26:82

> And He is ˹the One˺ Who, I hope, will forgive my flaws on Judgment Day.”

**Abraham's Hope Faces the Day of Judgment**

Abraham's description of God ends with forgiveness and the Day of Judgment. The future day gives his hope a definite setting. He is not asking for a vague feeling that everything will be fine. He is looking toward the day when deeds are exposed and the servant stands before the Lord. Hope becomes serious when it is attached to accountability.

The wording keeps both truths together. Abraham trusts God's mercy, yet he does not treat forgiveness as something he can claim by right. He hopes for it. That posture is not weakness. It is the humility of a servant who knows the greatness of the Lord and the limits of his own record. Confidence belongs to God's promise and mercy, not to the servant's ability to declare himself safe.

**A Parallel Prayer Confirms the Pattern**

In the prayer recorded in 14:41 — *“Our Lord! Forgive me, my parents, and the believers on the Day when the judgment will come to pass.”*, Abraham asks God to forgive him, his parents, and the believers when judgment is established. That parallel shows that forgiveness is not a marginal concern in Abraham's devotion. It appears within his public hopes for his family and the believing community. Mercy is sought personally and shared with others.

The two prayers also show that hope can be specific without becoming presumptuous. Abraham knows the day of judgment will come, knows that forgiveness belongs to God, and still asks with humility. His certainty concerns God's authority and mercy; the servant's own standing remains a matter for prayer.

---

## Verse 26:83

> “My Lord! Grant me wisdom, and join me with the righteous

**Abraham Asks for Wisdom From His Lord**

Abraham's prayer turns from describing God's acts to asking for gifts. He begins with wisdom. The translation renders the key term as wisdom, while the interpretive tradition also connects it with sound judgment, understanding, and the capacity to decide rightly. The request is larger than information. Abraham wants knowledge governed by truth and action governed by that knowledge.

Calling God my Lord places wisdom inside a relationship of formation. Abraham is not asking for cleverness that will make him independent. He is asking the Lord who created and guided him to deepen that guidance. Wisdom is therefore a form of servanthood. It helps a person see what matters, distinguish truth from appearance, and choose the response that obedience requires.

**A Related Prayer Extends the Same Concern**

In 14:40 — *“My Lord! Make me and those ˹believers˺ of my descendants keep up prayer. Our Lord! Accept my prayers”*, Abraham asks that he and some of his descendants be made keepers of prayer. That parallel shows how his requests combine personal obedience with the formation of a faithful community. He does not seek wisdom as a private distinction. He seeks a life and a lineage ordered toward worship.

---

## Verse 26:84

> Bless me with honourable mention among later generations

**Abraham Asks for a True Legacy**

Abraham asks God for honourable mention among later generations. The request is not a demand for fame detached from truth. He asks for a legacy that remains worthy when the generation that knows him personally has passed away. Honourable mention means that his name will be carried with praise because his life pointed people toward God.

**Honourable Mention Is Not Vanity**

There is a difference between seeking honour for the self and asking God to make one's life a truthful benefit for others. Vanity wants admiration as proof of superiority. Abraham's request seeks a memory that keeps later people connected to faith. The desired honour is not private applause; it is the survival of a good word.

**A Verified Parallel Shows the Prayer's Fulfilment**

The parallel account in 37:108 — *“and blessed Abraham ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations”* states that Abraham was blessed with honourable mention among later generations. The wording confirms that the request in this verse is not self-display but a prayer whose fulfilment becomes part of the revealed account. Later communities remember Abraham as a model of devotion because God preserved his name together with his truth.

---

## Verse 26:85

> Make me one of those awarded the Garden of Bliss

**The Garden Is an Award and a Gift**

To be awarded the Garden does not mean that Abraham can purchase it through his own deeds. An award is received, and the language keeps God's generosity in view. Deeds matter because they express faith and obedience, but the servant remains dependent on the Lord's acceptance. The request is therefore a confession of need rather than a claim of entitlement.

**The Garden Is Described as Near, Not Merely Promised**

The following scene in the chapter will describe the Garden as brought near to the reverent, but this verse should be understood on its own as the prayer for admission to that blessed destination. Nearness in the Qur'anic account conveys welcome and accessibility for those whom God honours. Abraham's request is not for an abstract reward without presence. It is for entry into a real abode of peace.

The parallel description in 37:43 — *“in the Gardens of Bliss”* places the blessed in Gardens of Bliss. That cross-reference confirms that bliss is not a vague metaphor for moral satisfaction. It names the lasting home associated with the faithful. The believer may not see it now, but the prayer gives the future a concrete direction.

---

## Verse 26:86

> Forgive my father, for he is certainly one of the misguided

**A Parallel Prayer Broadens the Family Concern**

In 14:41 — *“Our Lord! Forgive me, my parents, and the believers on the Day when the judgment will come to pass.”*, Abraham asks forgiveness for himself, his parents, and the believers on the day judgment is established. That parallel shows that his concern is not limited to private emotion. He places family within a wider community of people who need God's mercy. The individual household is important, but it is not the whole horizon of prayer.

The parallel also confirms that asking God to forgive parents belongs within Abraham's devotional language. The request is made to the Lord who hears prayer, not to an ancestor or household power. Its proper end is mercy from God and a community oriented toward Him.

Abraham's concern for his father must not be used to defend false worship or to suggest that love makes every belief acceptable. The verse contains both the request and the diagnosis: forgive my father, for he is misguided. Mercy is sought precisely because the condition is wrong. A prayer that hides the wrong would not be Abraham's prayer.

A family member may therefore keep making sincere prayers and offering honest counsel while refusing to join the error. Abraham's example does not demand emotional distance; it demands that affection serve truth.

---

## Verse 26:87

> And do not disgrace me on the Day all will be resurrected—

**A Parallel Supplication Explains the Fear**

The believers' prayer in 3:192 — *“Our Lord! Indeed, those You commit to the Fire will be ˹completely˺ disgraced! And the wrongdoers will have no helpers”* also links being admitted to the Fire with disgrace and the absence of helpers. That parallel confirms that disgrace is a spiritual consequence of rejection, not simply an emotional embarrassment. Abraham's request is therefore a plea for God's protection from the final state in which no created support can repair the loss.

The connection also shows why his earlier prayer for forgiveness matters here. Mercy and honour belong together. The servant asks for pardon because he wants to meet the Day of resurrection as one accepted by God rather than as one whose public identity conceals a rejected heart.

The verse ends with a dash after naming the Day of resurrection. The pause holds the believer before the event rather than allowing the mind to move quickly past it. Abraham's prayer remains unfinished in the sense that every servant must carry it into his own life. The day is certain, but the personal outcome is not something to treat casually.

That concern can shape the present without creating panic. The believer corrects the heart now because the final day will reveal what the heart became.

---

## Verse 26:88

> the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit

**The Day Exposes False Security**

Abraham explains the Day of resurrection by naming two of the strongest forms of worldly security: wealth and children. Neither will be of any benefit then. The statement does not deny that money and family can be blessings in this life. It denies that either can purchase acceptance, carry a person through judgment, or replace a heart turned toward God.

**Wealth Cannot Be Taken Into the Final Account**

This does not make wealth evil. Abraham's words concern its inability to benefit on the Day of resurrection, not its lawful use in the world. Wealth can be a trust used for charity, family care, justice, and worship. Its limitation is precisely what makes responsible use important: it is a means for a short life, not a shield against the final meeting.

The parallel warning in 3:10 — *“Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire”* expressly joins wealth and children with the inability to benefit those who reject God. The repetition across the Qur'an makes the point a stable principle rather than a detail limited to Abraham's prayer. Worldly security becomes dangerous when it persuades a person that no higher account will be required.

---

## Verse 26:89

> Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”

**The Sound Heart Is the True Provision**

After removing wealth and children as final supports, Abraham identifies what saves: coming before Allah with a pure heart. The heart is not a passing mood or a private feeling separated from conduct. It is the center of intention, trust, worship, and moral direction. A sound heart arrives before God free from the loyalties that make created things rivals to Him.

**A Verified Parallel Places Abraham Beside the Sound Heart**

The parallel account in 37:84 — *“˹Remember˺ when he came to his Lord with a pure heart”* describes Abraham as coming to his Lord with a pure heart. That wording confirms that the quality in this verse is not an abstract virtue detached from Abraham's life. It describes the inner foundation of his rejection of idols and his single-minded worship.

The parallel also guards against reducing purity to outward calm. Abraham's heart was sound while he confronted his people and endured their opposition. A pure heart can be courageous and forceful against falsehood. Its gentleness is not surrender; its firmness is not hatred. Both are governed by devotion to God.

---

## Verse 26:90

> ˹On that Day˺ Paradise will be brought near to the God-fearing

**God-Fearing Means Living Under Taqwa**

The God-fearing are those whose awareness of God shapes choices. Taqwa is not panic that makes a person unable to act. It is a guarding of the self from what displeases God, joined to hope in His mercy. The people described here have treated accountability as real, so their conduct was not governed only by immediate appetite or public approval.

This quality is deeper than a reputation for caution. A person may appear restrained while hiding arrogance or false worship. Taqwa reaches the heart and the ordinary decision. It guides speech, wealth, family duty, private conduct, and the use of power. Paradise is brought near to people whose inner awareness has become a practical form of obedience.

**A Parallel Describes the Same Nearness**

Quran 50:31 — *“And Paradise will be brought near to the righteous, not far off”* describes Paradise as brought near to the righteous and not far away. The parallel confirms that nearness here expresses readiness and welcome rather than a human journey that forces open the Garden. The righteous find the promised home close because God has prepared the way to it.

---

## Verse 26:91

> and the Hellfire will be displayed to the deviant

**Seeing the Fire Is Part of the Judgment**

A person can fear an unseen consequence without allowing that fear to shape action. On the Day of resurrection, the unseen becomes visible. The deviant no longer has to be persuaded that judgment is real. The Fire itself stands before them as evidence, and the distance between warning and reality disappears.

**A Parallel Links the Fire With Confession**

The account in 67:11 — *“And so they will confess their sins. So away with the residents of the Blaze”* records the residents of the Blaze confessing their sins. That parallel shows the relation between seeing the punishment and recognizing the moral failure behind it. The confession does not make the display unnecessary. It confirms that the condemned understand what they refused to understand earlier.

The present verse keeps the emphasis on the first sight. Hellfire is displayed to the deviant before the later words of dispute and regret are heard. The visual encounter already judges the false story they told themselves. Their former objects of hope cannot make the Fire disappear, and their former confidence cannot turn its display into a warning for someone else.

---

## Verse 26:92

> And it will be said to them, “Where are those you used to worship

**Where Is a Worshipped Object When Judgment Arrives?**

The verse asks where the objects are, not where the people kept them or how they decorated them. Their physical location is not the real issue. The issue is their absence from authority. They are not present as protectors, advocates, or lords. The community's ceremonies could make them appear important, but ceremony cannot create a power that stands before judgment.

That absence reveals the difference between a symbol and a god. A symbol can remain as an object of memory, while a god must have actual authority over the worshipper's fate. The idols fail the second test. They were addressed in life, but they cannot answer the question placed before them now.

**A Parallel Exposes the Same Absence**

The Qur'an describes those invoked besides God as unable to create and as themselves created in 16:20–21 — *“But those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Allah cannot create anything—they themselves are created”*. That parallel gives the present question a clear religious background. An object that did not create life cannot be expected to control the final destiny of life. Its worshippers may grant it a name, but they cannot grant it creative or saving power.

---

## Verse 26:93

> besides Allah? Can they help you or even help themselves?”

**The Question Tests the Idols' Ability to Help**

The interrogation becomes specific. Can the objects worshipped besides Allah help their devotees, or can they even help themselves? The two parts expose the same failure from different angles. A claimed god must have power for the worshipper and stability for its own condition. These idols have neither.

The question is framed in help because worship is usually tied to need. People turn to a supposed power for rescue, protection, provision, or relief. Abraham's earlier question already tested whether the idols could hear and benefit. This verse places the test at the final crisis, where the difference between appearance and power cannot be hidden.

**The Lord Is Not Placed Beside the Idols as an Equal**

Quran 7:192 — *“which cannot help them, or even help themselves”* describes the idols as unable to help their worshippers or themselves. The parallel confirms that the doubled question is a recurring Qur'anic test, not a detail confined to Abraham's debate. A false god fails both outwardly and inwardly: it cannot aid its follower and cannot defend its own existence.

---

## Verse 26:94

> Then the idols will be hurled headlong into Hell, along with the deviant

**The Idols Are Thrown Into the Fire**

The verse describes the idols being hurled headlong into Hell together with the deviant. The scene reverses the honor given to them in worldly life. They were lifted, decorated, protected, and placed at the center of ritual. Now they are toppled and gathered into punishment. Their treatment reveals what they were worth before God all along.

The action is not the punishment of a true deity. The idols have no divine power and no independent life. Their presence in the Fire is part of the exposure of those who worshipped them. The objects become evidence against the false system that assigned them a sacred status. What people treated as protectors is shown as incapable of protecting even the role they were given.

**Headlong Captures a Violent Reversal**

The translation headlong evokes a fall without dignity or control. The Arabic verb kabkaba also carries the sense of tumbling and being gathered together. The word makes the image physical. The idols are not escorted as honored guests; they are overturned and thrown down. Their public elevation has become a humiliating collapse.

---

## Verse 26:95

> and the soldiers of Iblîs, all together

**The Judgment Includes the Hosts of Iblîs**

The verse adds the soldiers of Iblîs to the group gathered for punishment. The word soldiers suggests organized allegiance and service. Iblîs is not pictured as acting alone in the work of misguidance. He has followers who carry his aims into human and unseen life. Their power is derivative, but their choices make them responsible for the side on which they stood.

**A Soldier Is Defined by Service**

Calling them soldiers focuses on what they do. A person can serve Iblîs without using military language, simply by defending false worship, beautifying sin, or leading others away from God. The title names function rather than costume. It asks who benefits from the work a person performs and whose purposes that work advances.

**A Parallel Describes the Devil as a Companion**

Quran 43:36 — *“And whoever turns a blind eye to the Reminder of the Most Compassionate, We place at the disposal of each a devilish one as their close associate”* describes the one who turns blindly away from the Reminder as being assigned a devilish companion. That parallel helps explain how a soldier of Iblîs can be formed. Repeated refusal creates an opening for influence, and influence becomes familiar company. The relationship is not an innocent accident; it grows through turning away from remembrance.

---

## Verse 26:96

> There the deviant will cry while disputing with their idols

**Dispute Reveals the Collapse of Trust**

A person argues with someone from whom he expected an answer. The condemned speak to the idols as though the objects could bear responsibility, but the very act of disputing exposes the failure of that expectation. The idols have no reply and no power to change the scene. The worshippers are left speaking into the emptiness they once called sacred.

**The Idols Are Treated as Responsible Partners**

The deviant disputes with the idols as though the objects had participated in the wrong. This language may be rhetorical, because the idols are powerless, but it captures how worshippers experienced their own attachment. They gave the objects authority and relied on them as partners beside God. At judgment, they address the objects in the language of a broken alliance.

**A Parallel Shows Satan Rejecting the Blame**

In 14:22 — *“And Satan will say ˹to his followers˺ after the judgment has been passed, “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, nor can you save me. Indeed, I denounce your previous association of me with Allah ˹in loyalty˺. Surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment.”*, Satan speaks after judgment and denies possessing authority over his followers beyond calling them and receiving their response. That parallel illuminates the dispute here. Those who followed false powers may blame the objects or the tempters, but the existence of influence does not erase the choice to answer it.

---

## Verse 26:97

> “By Allah! We were clearly mistaken

**The Condemned Swear by the God They Once Denied**

The deviant begin their confession with an oath by Allah. The oath is striking because they had treated other objects as worthy of worship. At judgment, the name of the true Lord becomes unavoidable. They do not swear by their idols; they invoke Allah while admitting that their former allegiance was wrong.

**The Oath Reverses the Earlier Religious Order**

In worldly life, the idols received devotion while Allah's claim was ignored or divided. In the Fire, the speakers swear by Allah and name their error. The order has been reversed, but only as a confession. The true center appears when the false center has already failed.

Quran 67:11 — *“And so they will confess their sins. So away with the residents of the Blaze”* records the inhabitants of the Blaze confessing their sins. That parallel confirms that recognition of wrongdoing can appear in the final scene without becoming a path out of it. The confession is morally accurate, but it follows the loss of the worldly period in which repentance, repair, and renewed worship were possible.

---

## Verse 26:98

> when we made you equal to the Lord of all worlds

**The Error Corrupts Both Worship and Ethics**

Once a created object is made equal to God, moral obedience becomes unstable. The person begins to ask what the idol, leader, ancestor, or group wants rather than what God commands. A false sacred authority can then approve injustice, protect privilege, and punish anyone who questions it. The religious error spreads into public conduct.

This explains why Abraham did not treat idolatry as a harmless private preference. Worship establishes the highest authority in life. If that authority is false, the entire moral order can be bent around it. The confession in the Hereafter names the root from which many visible corruptions grew.

Quran 16:20–21 — *“But those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Allah cannot create anything—they themselves are created They are dead, not alive—not even knowing when their followers will be resurrected”* describes the objects invoked besides God as created and unable to create. That parallel supports the confession's logic. An object that is itself dependent cannot be placed beside the One on whom all depend. The comparison fails before any elaborate argument about ritual.

The same principle appeared in Abraham's questions about hearing, benefiting, and harming. The confession now gives the conclusion in the worshippers' own words. Their idols did not become rivals because they had rival power. They became rivals because people assigned them rival worship.

---

## Verse 26:99

> And none led us astray other than the wicked

**The Condemned Try to Identify Their Misleaders**

After admitting that they made idols equal to the Lord of all worlds, the speakers name the wicked as those who led them astray. The statement recognizes that false worship was not maintained by ideas alone. People transmitted it, beautified it, defended it, and directed others toward it. Misguidance can be organized through voices that present corruption as wisdom.

**The Sentence Raises the Question of Blame**

This tension is central to moral accountability. Human beings are shaped by parents, teachers, rulers, friends, and culture. Those influences can be powerful, especially when they make falsehood appear normal. Still, influence is not compulsion in every case. The final confession must include both the evil of the leaders and the choice of those who accepted their direction.

**A Parallel Makes the Follower's Responsibility Plain**

In 14:22 — *“And Satan will say ˹to his followers˺ after the judgment has been passed, “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, nor can you save me. Indeed, I denounce your previous association of me with Allah ˹in loyalty˺. Surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment.”*, Satan denies possessing authority over his followers beyond calling them and receiving their response. That passage clarifies the present confession. A misleader may invite a person toward error, but the invitation does not become an irresistible command. The follower's response is part of the account that judgment uncovers.

---

## Verse 26:100

> Now we have none to intercede for us

**The Verse Rejects False Intercession**

This verse should be read as a judgment on the intercessors claimed by the idolaters, not as a careless denial of every form of intercession in every context. The Qur'anic doctrine places intercession under God's ownership and permission. What is rejected here is the assumption that idols have an independent right to secure a person's forgiveness.

**God's Permission Is the Measure of Intercession**

The verified parallel in 2:255 — *“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.”* places any intercession under God's permission. That principle keeps the subject within monotheism. An intercessor does not act as a rival power or force God's decision. The intercessor's ability, speech, and acceptance are all granted by the Lord.

**Family and Status Cannot Become Intercessors by Themselves**

This does not make prayer for others meaningless. Abraham prays for his father and for believers, but he asks God to forgive; he does not claim power to forgive. The distinction is between supplication offered to God and worship offered to a supposed intermediary. The first acknowledges divine authority, while the second invents a rival refuge.

Quran 29:25 — *“He said ˹to his people˺, “You have taken idols ˹for worship˺ instead of Allah, only to keep ˹the bond of˺ harmony among yourselves in this worldly life. But on the Day of Judgment you will disown and curse one another. Your home will be the Fire, and you will have no helper!”* describes idolaters forming worldly bonds around their idols, followed by mutual disowning and cursing on the Day of Judgment. That parallel explains why the absence of intercessors is so painful. The alliances that seemed to secure belonging cannot survive the final truth. The object, the leader, and the follower cannot save one another.

---

## Verse 26:101

> nor a close friend

**The Final Scene Removes Intimate Support**

The line completes the confession that the condemned have no intercessors, no close friend, and no loyal friend. The emphasis is not on ordinary loneliness after a social disappointment. It is on standing before God without a person whose closeness can bring relief, plead successfully, or share the burden of judgment. The friend who once cared about every concern is absent as a source of rescue.

This final absence exposes the limits of human attachment. Friendship can be one of life's greatest gifts, but it cannot become a substitute for God. A friend may comfort, advise, and help within worldly limits. No friend can carry another person's worship, erase deliberate association, or command forgiveness on the Day of judgment.

**A Parallel Describes the Same Solitude**

Quran 40:18 — *“Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress. The wrongdoers will have neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard”* warns of the approaching Day when wrongdoers have neither a close friend nor an intercessor whose word is accepted. That parallel confirms that the absence in this verse is a feature of final accountability, not a general condemnation of friendship. Human closeness remains valuable in the world, but it cannot become an independent channel of salvation.

---

## Verse 26:102

> If only we could have a second chance, then we would be believers.”

**The Promise of Belief Appears Only After Proof**

They say they would be believers if returned. The statement may be sincere as an expression of regret, but it comes after the unseen has become visible and the result can no longer be denied. Belief that arises from unavoidable exposure is different from faith that trusts God's warning while the choice remains open.

**Regret Asks for a Return That Cannot Be Granted**

The condemned wish for another turn. They imagine returning to the world and living differently, believing after seeing the consequence that they earlier denied. The request reveals that their problem was not a lack of desire for safety. They wanted safety, but they postponed the faith and obedience that would have shaped their safety before judgment.

Quran 23:99–100 — *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, “My Lord! Let me go back so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected”* describes a person asking to be sent back so that he may do good with what he left behind, followed by the statement that the appeal is only a word and that a barrier stands until resurrection. That parallel confirms the moral point of this verse: the request to return is recognized as regret, not granted as a new earthly life.

---

## Verse 26:103

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**The Whole Abraham Narrative Is a Sign**

The verse gathers the account of Abraham and calls it a sign. The sign is not limited to one sentence or one debate. It includes the exposure of powerless idols, the appeal to inherited custom, the declaration of exclusive allegiance, the prayer for wisdom and forgiveness, and the final picture of false worship collapsing before judgment. The parts form one proof about whom human beings should worship.

A sign points beyond the story to the Lord who sent it. Abraham's reasoning is clear, but the account is more than a lesson in debate. It reveals a pattern in which truth confronts custom, false supports fail, and sincere reliance is directed to God. The narrative becomes evidence for anyone willing to receive its meaning.

**Clarity Does Not Guarantee Belief**

Most of them would not believe even after the sign. This statement separates the strength of evidence from the response of the audience. A proof can be clear while a person refuses its demand. The obstacle may lie in pride, loyalty to ancestors, fear of social loss, or unwillingness to surrender control. None of these makes the sign unclear.

---

## Verse 26:104

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**Abraham's Prayer Shows Mercy Received Personally**

The preceding prayer names creation, guidance, provision, healing, forgiveness, wisdom, righteous company, honourable remembrance, and the Garden. Those requests are not evidence that Abraham doubts God's power. They are the language of a servant receiving mercy at every stage. The Almighty can give, and the Merciful chooses to give in ways that form the servant.

The account of Noah in 7:59–64 — *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous Day.”* displays the same balance. Noah calls his people to worship God, warns them of a tremendous day, and the story ends with God saving him and those with him while the rejecters are drowned. The parallel confirms that divine might and mercy accompany the prophetic call across different communities.

Success is therefore measured by faithful delivery and sound allegiance, not by immediate numbers. The believer can grieve over refusal without imagining that God's plan has failed. The Almighty is not weakened by a majority's denial, and the Merciful does not stop inviting people because some turn away.

---

## Verse 26:105

> The people of Noah rejected the messengers

**Noah's Story Opens a New Prophetic Example**

The chapter moves from Abraham's confrontation with idolatry to the people of Noah. The first statement is blunt: they rejected the messengers. The immediate story concerns Noah, yet the plural messengers gives their refusal a wider meaning. Denying a true messenger is not an isolated disagreement with one person. It is rejection of the prophetic call as a class.

**A Verified Parallel Introduces Noah's Core Call**

The parallel account in 7:59 — *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous Day.”* records Noah calling his people to worship Allah because they have no other god. That cross-reference confirms that the central issue in this story is monotheism, not a private dispute about Noah's personality. The people reject the messenger because his call threatens the authority of their inherited gods and the order built around them.

This also links Noah to Abraham. Both messengers return the community to the Lord alone, but the forms of resistance differ. Abraham questions the idols' capacity; Noah addresses a whole people over time. The prophetic call remains one even when the setting and audience change.

---

## Verse 26:106

> when their brother Noah said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺

**Brotherhood Does Not Mean Approval**

Noah's relation to the people is warm in address but firm in truth. Calling them brothers does not mean he accepts their worship or treats their conduct as harmless. Prophetic compassion does not erase moral disagreement. He speaks as someone who wants his people rescued from a danger, not as someone seeking to flatter them into temporary agreement.

The phrase will you not fear Allah invokes taqwa. Taqwa is not mere panic before punishment. It is careful awareness of God that guards a person from disobedience. Noah asks whether the people will develop this awareness before their habits and idols carry them further away from truth.

The parallel account in 7:59 — *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous Day.”* records Noah calling his people to worship Allah because they have no other god. The present question gives the moral entry point to that call. Fear of God means recognizing that worship belongs to Him alone and that no rival has a rightful claim.

Another parallel in 7:63 — *“Do you find it astonishing that a reminder should come to you from your Lord through one of your own, warning you, so you may beware and perhaps be shown mercy?”* describes the warning as coming through one of the people so that they may beware and receive mercy. That wording confirms that Noah's nearness is part of the mercy of the message. He is one of them, yet he brings a warning they could not safely ignore.

---

The call of their brother Noah in this sūrah is given in the same form as the appeals that follow the other messengers in the passage: fear God, obey me, ask no reward. The fuller account is preserved in the sūrah of Hūd, with the people's answer and the dialogue about those who followed him (11:27–31 — *“O my people! I do not ask you for a payment for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from Allah. And I will never dismiss the believers, for they will surely meet their Lord. But I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly”*), and in the sūrah of Nūḥ, where the messenger is told to warn his people before a painful punishment comes (71:1–4 — *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people ˹saying to him˺, “Warn your people before a painful punishment comes to them.”*). The word "brother" is the sūrah's standard address, which is why the messages in this chapter read as family business rather than foreign demands.

## Verse 26:107

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you

**Noah Grounds His Mission in Trustworthiness**

Noah describes himself as a trustworthy messenger. The two parts belong together. He is not presenting private wisdom as though it were revelation, and he is not claiming a message while being unworthy of trust. His reliability concerns both the person who carries the message and the source from which the message comes.

**Trust Is the Bridge to Obedience**

People obey advice when they trust both its content and the person delivering it. Noah names his trustworthiness before asking the people to follow his guidance. The order is wise. A community that thinks the messenger is deceptive will treat every command as manipulation. A community that recognizes his trust must confront the content honestly.

Quran 71:2 — *“Noah proclaimed, “O my people! I am truly sent to you with a clear warning”* describes Noah as sent with a clear warning. That parallel adds an useful distinction to trustworthiness. Noah is not offering a vague spiritual mood; he is delivering a defined warning whose urgency comes from God. His trust lies in conveying that warning faithfully, not in promising the people an easy message.

---

## Verse 26:108

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**The Two Commands Form One Response**

Fear Allah and obey me are not unrelated instructions placed side by side. The first supplies the reason for the second. If the people fear God, they will not dismiss the messenger He has sent. If they obey Noah sincerely, their obedience will show that they have accepted God's claim over their lives.

The command could be misunderstood if separated from its first half. “Obey me” by itself might sound like a leader's demand for control. Joined to fear Allah and spoken by a trustworthy messenger, it means accept the guidance God has entrusted to me. Noah is not building a kingdom of personal loyalty.

The account in 71:2–3 — *“Noah proclaimed, “O my people! I am truly sent to you with a clear warning worship Allah ˹alone˺, fear Him, and obey me”* presents Noah's warning, the command to worship Allah, and the call to fear and obey. The parallel confirms that reverence and obedience are the basic structure of his mission. He is not offering a new social identity first; he is returning the people to their Lord.

---

## Verse 26:109
> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds

**Freedom From Payment Protects the Message**

If Noah demanded payment from each hearer, the people could treat the call as a private bargain. Those who paid might imagine they had purchased favor, while those who could not pay might be excluded. By refusing a reward, Noah shows that guidance is not a privilege reserved for the wealthy or powerful.
In 11:29 — *“O my people! I do not ask you for a payment for this ˹message˺.”*, Noah likewise declares that he does not ask the people for payment and that he will not dismiss the believers. The parallel shows that financial independence and social fairness belong together. A messenger who seeks no fee has no reason to reserve God's message for a wealthy class or to remove poor believers for the comfort of elites.
Noah's refusal concerns the message he conveys. It does not create a rule that every worker must serve without lawful compensation or that communities may exploit teachers and caregivers. Work has value, and justice requires that people be paid for labor when payment is due. Noah's point is that prophetic guidance is not a commodity whose price grants spiritual access.

**The Refusal of Payment**

The sentence is part of the speech of Noah, and it is repeated by messenger after messenger in this sūrah: Hud (26:127 — *“I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds”*), Ṣāliḥ (26:145 — *“I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds”*), Lot (26:164 — *“I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds”*) and Shuʿayb (26:180 — *“I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds”*) say the same words, and the repetition is the sūrah's design. Each one refused to be paid for the message, and each one said that his wage is with the Lord of the worlds. The phrase answers an accusation the messengers faced in every age: that the new teaching was a way of getting money or position out of people.

The Prophet is given the same instruction elsewhere, and the Book states the reward he is allowed to ask for: nothing but the love of the relatives (42:23 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I do not ask you for a reward for this ˹message˺—only honour for ˹our˺ kinship.”*). The message is free at the point of delivery, and the Book keeps saying so in the mouth of every man sent with it.

---
## Verse 26:110

> So fear Allah, and obey me.”

**The Repetition Counters Social Resistance**

A community can turn a simple call into a debate about status, numbers, and influence. Repetition returns the issue to its center. Fear Allah and obey me. Noah does not allow the audience to hide the spiritual question behind a dispute about his popularity or the social position of his followers.

The account of Noah in 71:2–3 — *“Noah proclaimed, “O my people! I am truly sent to you with a clear warning worship Allah ˹alone˺, fear Him, and obey me”* joins clear warning with worship of Allah, reverence, and obedience. The parallel confirms that this verse summarizes the structure of his mission. He warns because he wants the people to return to God, not because he enjoys announcing their danger.

The combination also guards against an one-sided religious message. Warning without worship can produce fear without direction. Obedience without reverence can produce empty conformity. Noah joins both to the Lord, so the people know whom they fear and why they should follow the messenger.

Mercy does not mean that Noah will conceal consequences. It means he continues to call before judgment arrives. The repeated command keeps the door open for a person who was distracted by custom, pride, or fear and now becomes ready to listen.

---

## Verse 26:111

> They argued, “How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?”

**The Chiefs Judge Truth by the Followers**

Noah's opponents ask how they can believe in him when the people following him are, in their view, the lowest of the low. Their objection does not examine the message first. It examines the social position of the believers and treats that position as evidence against Noah. A claim becomes unacceptable to them because humble people have accepted it.

**Faith Is Not Validated by Elite Approval**

This principle does not romanticize poverty or condemn wealth. A wealthy person can believe sincerely, and a poor person can be arrogant. The error lies in turning social position into a gatekeeper for faith. Noah's opponents do that because status is the one form of evidence that protects their own place.

Quran 11:27 — *“The disbelieving chiefs of his people said, “We see you only as a human being like ourselves, and we see that no one follows you except the lowliest among us, who do so ˹hastily˺ without thinking. We do not see anything that makes ˹all of˺ you any better than us. In fact, we think you are liars.”* records the chiefs of Noah's people saying that only the lowliest follow him and that they see no advantage in the believers. The parallel confirms that the insult is a repeated feature of their rejection, not an accidental phrase. They treat social difference as proof that Noah's message has no divine value.

---

## Verse 26:112

> He responded, “And what knowledge do I have of what they do

**God Knows What Noah Does Not**

Noah's answer points toward God's knowledge without using ignorance as an insult. He does not say that the believers' actions are unimportant. He says that he does not have the knowledge the chiefs pretend to have. God alone sees the complete record: former conduct, present repentance, hidden intention, and future persistence.

This arrangement gives the community a sound division of responsibility. Noah teaches and warns. Believers strive to live faithfully. The Lord judges the unseen and the final account. When human beings exchange these roles, they either become arrogant judges or careless people who refuse all accountability. Noah's measured reply avoids both errors.

Quran 6:52 — *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you. So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers”* instructs the Prophet not to dismiss poor believers who invoke their Lord morning and evening. That parallel confirms the principle at stake in Noah's response. The worth of a believer is not determined by the demand of chiefs who want religious space reserved for themselves.

Evidence concerns what happened and what can be responsibly known. Prejudice begins when a group is assigned motives because of its class, appearance, or reputation. The chiefs offer prejudice in the language of evaluation. Noah answers by asking whether they can actually know the claim they are making.

---

## Verse 26:113

> Their judgment is with my Lord, if you had any sense

**Ultimate Judgment Belongs to Noah's Lord**

Noah assigns the judgment of his followers to his Lord. He does not deny that actions matter or that a community may correct visible wrongdoing. He denies that the chiefs can demand a final verdict based on social contempt and that Noah should pretend to have God's complete account. Ultimate reckoning belongs to the Lord.

**Divine Judgment Includes the Hidden and the Public**

Human courts can assess public actions according to evidence. God's judgment includes the inner intention behind those actions and the full history of a servant's response to guidance. That wider knowledge makes the Lord's judgment more complete than any social verdict. It also makes human humility necessary.

Quran 6:52 — *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you. So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers”* presents the Prophet being told not to dismiss poor believers and makes clear that their account is not placed on the messenger in the way it is placed on God. This parallel confirms Noah's principle. Prophets protect the believing community from elite contempt, while God remains the final judge of every person's deeds.

---

## Verse 26:114

> I am not going to expel the believers

**The Messenger Protects the Community's Moral Center**

A community's boundary reveals what it values. Noah's refusal says that faith and truth matter more than elite comfort. The community will not be organized around the pride of those who want to enter only after the humble have been removed. Its center remains God's call, not the social preference of its strongest members.

Quran 11:29 — *“O my people! I do not ask you for a payment for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from Allah. And I will never dismiss the believers, for they will surely meet their Lord. But I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly”* records Noah saying that he will not dismiss those who believe. The parallel confirms that this is a settled principle in his mission, not a momentary emotional reaction. He refuses the chiefs' request because the believers are accountable to God and because the message is not a property of the elite.

The same concern appears in 6:52 — *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you. So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers”*, where poor believers are protected from being dismissed. Across these passages, revelation establishes a clear boundary: social influence cannot purchase the right to exclude sincere worshippers. The messenger's task is to guide, not to curate a congregation for the comfort of status.

Noah's refusal rejects that precedent. Evidence must concern belief, conduct, and truth, not the accident of birth or possession. A person can be rich or poor by circumstances beyond his control. What God asks is how the person responds to Him, not whether the person can impress the chiefs.

---

## Verse 26:115

> I am only sent with a clear warning.”

**Noah Defines the Limit of His Mission**

Noah says that he is only sent with a clear warning. The word only marks a boundary around his task. He is not claiming control over the people's hearts, possession of God's treasures, or authority to manufacture the final judgment. He has been sent to make the danger plain and to call the people toward a responsible response.

**Clear Warning Includes Consequence and Mercy**

A warning is not only a threat. It identifies a danger so that people can avoid it. Noah's call carries fear of God's judgment, but it also opens a path to safety through worship, taqwa, and obedience. The purpose of clarity is prevention. People are told what the road leads to while they can still turn.

The parallel account in 7:63 — *“Do you find it astonishing that a reminder should come to you from your Lord through one of your own, warning you, so you may beware and perhaps be shown mercy?”* describes a warning delivered through one of the people so that they may beware and receive mercy. That wording shows why the warning is itself mercy. A messenger exposes the danger before it becomes unavoidable. Rejecting the warning does not make the message harsh; it makes the rejection tragic.

---

## Verse 26:116

> They threatened, “If you do not desist, O Noah, you will surely be stoned ˹to death˺.”

**Noah's Belonging Does Not Protect Him From His People**

They call him O Noah, acknowledging the person they know even while threatening him. Shared kinship and history have not softened their response. Familiarity can make a messenger's words more painful because he is correcting people who assumed that he belonged to their old order.

**Rejection Turns Into a Threat**

The people answer Noah's warning with a threat of stoning. They do not address the truth of his call. They try to silence the messenger by making the cost of continued speech physical and final. A community that cannot defeat an argument may try to frighten the person who presents it.

Quran 19:46 — *“He threatened, “How dare you reject my idols, O Abraham! If you do not desist, I will certainly stone you ˹to death˺. So be gone from me for a long time!”* records Abraham's father threatening to stone him if he does not abandon his rejection of idols. The parallel shows that threats of stoning appear when a messenger or reformer challenges inherited worship. The threat can come from a whole people or from within a family, but its purpose remains the same: preserve the false object by silencing the one who questions it.

---

The threat is recorded in the sūrah of the Poets, and the answer that follows is Noah's prayer: "My Lord, save me from what they deny" (26:117–118). The sūrah of Hūd supplies the other half of the story — that no one else would believe and the ark was to be built (11:36–38 — *“And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions, and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.”*) — and the sūrah of Nūḥ records his complaint that he called his people night and day and they only fled from him (71:5–7 — *“He cried, “My Lord! I have surely called my people day and night”*). The pattern the sūrah establishes is that the messenger's safety does not depend on his standing in the town; his protection is from God, and the town's threats are part of the record kept against it.

## Verse 26:117

> Noah prayed, “My Lord! My people have truly rejected me

**The Word Rejected Includes Accusing Noah of Falsehood**

The source tradition explains rejection as declaring Noah a liar. The people do not simply decline his invitation. They judge his claim to be false and treat his warning as unworthy of belief. Their refusal therefore attacks both the message and the messenger who carries it.

Noah's prayer does not respond by inventing a counter-insult. He reports what happened to God. That restraint gives the complaint dignity. A person can state that he has been wronged without allowing the wrong to dictate his character. Noah's sorrow remains directed toward the Lord rather than toward the satisfaction of humiliating his opponents.

**The Prayer Admits the Pain of Prophetic Work**

A messenger is not emotionally untouched by rejection. Noah has lived among these people and warned them over time. Their refusal is not an abstract statistic. It concerns people whose safety he wants and who answer his concern with denial and threat.

The prayer makes room for sadness without treating sadness as spiritual failure. Faith does not require pretending that rejection is pleasant. It requires carrying the pain to God and remaining within the limits of justice. Noah's example shows that a servant can grieve over a people's refusal while still refusing to become vindictive.

---

## Verse 26:118

> So judge between me and them decisively, and save me and the believers with me.”

**Noah Asks God to Decide Between Truth and Rejection**

Noah asks his Lord to judge between him and his people decisively. The request does not arise from a personal contest for honor. It asks God to reveal the truth of the dispute and to settle the conflict between the messenger's warning and the community's rejection. Noah has delivered the message; the people have denied it; the matter now belongs before God's judgment.

**Decisive Judgment Does Not Mean Human Impatience**

The decisive judgment Noah seeks belongs to a situation in which the community has rejected the message and threatened the messenger. Even then, he asks God rather than taking the decision into his own hands. Patience and firmness can coexist. The servant waits for God's wisdom and still asks for protection when the danger becomes clear.

Quran 7:89 — *“We would surely be fabricating a lie against Allah if we were to return to your faith after Allah has saved us from it. It does not befit us to return to it unless it is the Will of Allah, our Lord. Our Lord has encompassed everything in ˹His˺ knowledge. In Allah we trust. Our Lord! Judge between us and our people with truth. You are the best of those who judge.”* records Shuʿayb asking God to judge between him and his people in truth. The parallel shows that prophets may bring a dispute to God after the message has been delivered and the community has persisted in opposition. The prayer is not a personal demand for status; it is a request for truth to be established by the Lord.

---

## Verse 26:119

> So We saved him and those with him in the fully loaded Ark

**God Answers Noah's Prayer With Rescue**

The verse announces that God saved Noah and those with him in the fully loaded Ark. The subject changes from Noah's request to God's action. Rescue is not credited to Noah's engineering, the believers' strength, or the Ark as an independent object. God saves, and the Ark is the means through which His command becomes visible.

**Noah Is Saved With Those Who Trusted Him**

Quran 11:37 — *“And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions, and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.”* records God commanding Noah to build the Ark under divine direction and protection. That parallel explains why the vessel in this verse should not be treated as a human invention that happened to succeed. Noah acts, builds, and prepares, but the work unfolds under God's command.

The connection also shows that rescue begins before the visible crisis. Obedience to the building command may have looked strange while the flood had not yet arrived. The believers had to trust God's instruction before the Ark could appear as the means of their survival. Faith often obeys a command whose wisdom becomes clear only later.

---

## Verse 26:120

> Then afterwards We drowned the rest

**The Flood Separates the Saved From the Rejecters**

The verse states that God drowned the rest after saving Noah and those with him. The word rest marks a complete division between the people who followed the warning and those who rejected it. The judgment is not presented as an accident that overtook everyone without distinction. It follows a long call, a clear warning, and a settled refusal.

**The Word Rest Exposes the Cost of Exclusion**

The people who demanded that Noah expel the believers are now outside the Ark themselves. Their social ranking has not protected them. Those whom they considered lowly are saved, while the chiefs' judgment has become part of the evidence against them. The word rest reverses their hierarchy: the powerful are not automatically included, and the humble are not automatically left behind.

Quran 7:64 — *“But they rejected him, so We saved him and those with him in the Ark, and drowned those who rejected Our signs. They were certainly a blind people”* describes God saving Noah and those with him in the Ark and drowning those who rejected the signs. The parallel confirms that rescue and drowning form one connected judgment. The Ark is not only a survival device; it is the means by which God's mercy distinguishes the believing community from the rejecting people.

---

## Verse 26:121

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**The Story of Noah Becomes a Sign**

The account of Noah is called a sign. Its sign is not only the flood or the Ark. The entire sequence points beyond itself: a messenger warns his people, the powerful dismiss the believers, the community refuses correction, and God saves those who trust Him while judging persistent rejection. History becomes evidence when its pattern reveals the Lord's rule.

The sign also answers the class objection raised against Noah. The people whom the chiefs considered lowly were the people God saved. Social prestige did not determine truth, and numerical strength did not determine safety. The story exposes a world in which divine judgment reverses the rankings that pride treats as permanent.

**A Parallel Account Shows the Same Reversal**

The parallel account in 7:64 — *“But they rejected him, so We saved him and those with him in the Ark, and drowned those who rejected Our signs. They were certainly a blind people”* describes the rejecters as blind to the signs after God saved Noah and those with him in the Ark. That detail confirms the moral character of the present verse. Blindness does not mean that no evidence appeared. It means that the people refused to let the evidence govern their judgment.

---

## Verse 26:122

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**Noah's Story Ends With Power and Mercy**

The refrain names God as the Almighty and the Most Merciful after the flood. The two names interpret the rescue and the drowning together. Divine power explains why the Ark was protected and why the rejecting people could not escape the judgment. Divine mercy explains why Noah was sent, why the warning continued, and why a path of safety was made available.

Neither name should be isolated. Power without mercy would be misunderstood as arbitrary domination, while mercy without power would offer no assurance that God can protect the faithful or end oppression. The refrain holds both truths in one sentence. The Lord is strong enough to judge and gentle enough to save.

**A Parallel Account Shows the Two Names in Action**

The account in 46:21–26 — *“And remember the brother of ’Ȃd, when he warned his people, who inhabited the sand-hills—there were certainly warners before and after him—˹saying,˺ “Worship none but Allah. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.”* describes Hūd warning ʿĀd, their rejection, and the destructive wind that followed. That parallel shows that the refrain after Noah is part of a wider pattern. God sends warning as mercy and exercises power when a people persists in denial.

---

## Verse 26:123

> The people of ’Âd rejected the messengers

**The Community's Strength Does Not Validate Its Faith**

ʿĀd is associated in other Qur'anic passages with great stature and power. Yet this verse introduces them through rejection, not through praise of their achievements. Strength can make a people confident that its culture is self-enough. The prophetic call exposes the weakness beneath that confidence.

Quran 7:65 — *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. Will you not then fear Him?”* describes Hūd as the brother sent to ʿĀd and records his call to worship Allah and fear Him. The parallel confirms that the people were not confronted by an outsider with no connection to their lives. Their rejection was a refusal of a familiar messenger who understood their world and addressed their own society.

Quran 46:21 — *“And remember the brother of ’Ȃd, when he warned his people, who inhabited the sand-hills—there were certainly warners before and after him—˹saying,˺ “Worship none but Allah. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.”* likewise calls him the brother of ʿĀd and describes his warning among the sand-hills. These passages establish that the plural messengers in the present verse includes Hūd as part of a known prophetic mission, not as a symbolic figure detached from history.

**Rejection Can Hide Behind Cultural Confidence**

The plural also carries a moral warning for selective belief. A person may claim respect for earlier messengers while rejecting the one whose message challenges present interests. The Qur'anic presentation treats that division as incoherent. Truth does not change its status because the messenger appears in a different generation.

Hūd's people are not allowed to say that his call is too local to matter. His message belongs to the same line of guidance that confronted earlier peoples. The people who reject him reject the authority of that line, even if they have never encountered every messenger personally.

---

## Verse 26:124

> when their brother Hûd said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺

**Hūd Addresses ʿĀd as Their Brother**

Hūd is called their brother before his question is heard. The relation identifies him as one of the people of ʿĀd, not a distant critic with no knowledge of their world. He shares their human community and understands the habits, ambitions, and fears that shape them. His warning comes from within their society.

The word brotherly does not soften the truth into approval. Hūd asks whether they will fear Allah because he wants them rescued from a direction that will harm them. The question is severe because the danger is severe, but its source is concern rather than hatred.

**A Verified Parallel Gives Hūd's Exact Setting**

Quran 7:65 — *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. Will you not then fear Him?”* records the same address to ʿĀd: Hūd calls them to worship Allah alone and asks whether they will not fear Him. The parallel confirms that the present question is the core of his mission, not a passing comment within a larger political dispute.

Quran 46:21 — *“And remember the brother of ’Ȃd, when he warned his people, who inhabited the sand-hills—there were certainly warners before and after him—˹saying,˺ “Worship none but Allah. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.”* places his warning among the sand-hills and notes that warners had come before and after him. Hūd's brotherhood therefore joins local knowledge with universal guidance. He is one of them, yet he speaks in continuity with the messengers.

People sometimes assume that a message from a stranger is easier to reject because the stranger lacks credibility. Hūd's people show the opposite difficulty. They know him, yet they still refuse the question. Familiarity can become a reason to dismiss a person as ordinary rather than to consider the truth he brings.

---

## Verse 26:125

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you

**Hūd Grounds His Call in Trustworthiness**

Hūd describes himself as a trustworthy messenger. The statement identifies both the quality of the bearer and the authority of the mission. He is not presenting a personal theory about ʿĀd or seeking influence through a new religious program. He has been entrusted with a message from God and is committed to conveying it faithfully.

**The Message Requires Moral Courage From the Bearer**

A trustworthy messenger cannot edit the warning to protect his reputation. Hūd must tell ʿĀd that their power, construction, and violence are subject to God's judgment. Such speech risks the anger of chiefs who benefit from the existing order. His trustworthiness includes the courage to say what the people need rather than what will preserve his comfort.

Quran 11:51 — *“O my people! I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the One Who created me. Will you not then understand”* records Hūd telling ʿĀd that he asks no reward for the message and that his reward is with the One who created him. That parallel adds a concrete sign of his trustworthiness. He is not seeking money from the people whose beliefs he challenges.

---

## Verse 26:126

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**The Command Follows the Messenger's Claim**

Hūd has identified himself as a trustworthy messenger. This verse now states the response that trustworthiness requires: reverence God and obey the messenger. The command is not an invitation to admire Hūd's character while leaving life unchanged. His reliability matters because his guidance must be followed.

To fear Allah is not to imagine Him as an unpredictable force waiting to harm the people. Reverence combines awareness of His greatness with caution about disobedience. It makes the heart conscious that power, wealth, and public honor remain under His judgment. The fear Hūd calls for is therefore a protection from arrogance.

**A Repeated Prophetic Pattern**

The same pairing appears in the surrounding narratives. Noah tells his people to fear God and obey him, and Hūd now receives the identical duty. The repetition shows continuity in the prophetic mission. Hūd is not asking ʿĀd to accept a novel religious demand; he is renewing the summons that earlier peoples heard.

Quran 7:65 — *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. Will you not then fear Him?”* records Hūd calling ʿĀd to worship Allah and asking whether they will not fear Him. The parallel confirms that the present command joins worship, reverence, and obedience. The three belong together: worship identifies the true Lord, reverence guards the heart, and obedience proves the claim in life.

---

## Verse 26:127

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds

**The Lord of All Worlds Is the Proper Recipient of Hope**

This title also prevents tribal bargaining. Hūd is not saying, “I ask no reward from you because another tribe will pay me.” His hope is in the Lord of every people. The call is therefore universal in its moral scope, even while he speaks to one particular society.

**The Messenger Removes a Suspicion**

Hūd has asked a powerful people to change its worship and conduct. Such a demand can provoke the accusation that the messenger is pursuing a private benefit. This verse answers that suspicion directly. He does not seek payment from ʿĀd for conveying the warning. His service is not a commercial arrangement in which acceptance purchases his approval.

**A Verified Parallel Establishes the Same Principle**

Quran 11:51 — *“O my people! I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the One Who created me. Will you not then understand”* records Hūd telling ʿĀd that he asks no reward and that his reward is with the One who created him. The parallel confirms that the absence of payment is part of his public claim, not a detail invented by later readers. His mission is grounded in dependence on the Creator rather than on the community.

---

## Verse 26:128

> ˹Why˺ do you build a landmark on every high place in vanity

**A Question About Visible Construction**

Hūd turns from the inner question of worship to the public face of ʿĀd's society. He asks why they place a landmark on every high place in vanity. Their construction is not presented as a neutral achievement. It has become a statement intended to be seen, admired, and associated with the power of its builders.

**A Parallel Recalls ʿĀd's Stature**

Quran 89:6–8 — *“Did you not see how your Lord dealt with ’Ȃd— ˹the people˺ of Iram—with ˹their˺ great stature unmatched in any other land”* recalls how God dealt with ʿĀd and associates them with Iram and great stature. That passage confirms the broader memory of a people known for impressive presence. It does not require later readers to identify every ancient ruin or assign a modern site to each landmark. The revealed point is the contrast between their imposing reputation and their eventual accountability.

Hūd's challenge is therefore relevant to every organized society. Construction is moral when it answers real needs and acknowledges limits. A landmark becomes vanity when its primary function is to make others submit to the builders' image of themselves.

---

## Verse 26:129

> and construct castles, as if you are going to live forever

**A Later Passage Describes the End of Their Security**

Quran 46:24–25 — *“Then when they saw the torment as a ˹dense˺ cloud approaching their valleys, they said ˹happily˺, “This is a cloud bringing us rain.” ˹But Hûd replied,˺ “No, it is what you sought to hasten: a ˹fierce˺ wind carrying a painful punishment!” It destroyed everything by the command of its Lord, leaving nothing visible except their ruins. This is how We reward the wicked people”* describes the punishment approaching ʿĀd's valleys and says that the destructive wind left only their dwellings visible. This parallel gives a sober answer to the imagined permanence in the present verse. Structures may remain as traces, but the people who assumed their own endurance do not remain within them.

A fortified environment may insulate a people from the suffering it causes. Those inside see comfort and strength; those outside experience exclusion. When the walls prevent self-examination, the castle becomes more than a building. It becomes a system that keeps correction away from the powerful.

A structure's age does not prove the righteousness of its builders. A famous monument may survive while its society's injustice is forgotten by its admirers. God's judgment evaluates what human admiration cannot see: intention, oppression, gratitude, and obedience.

This distinction frees the servant from envy of impressive power. Another person's palace may look permanent, but appearance is not security. The true question is whether the builder has prepared to meet the Lord. A modest shelter raised with gratitude can be more valuable than a vast fortress raised in denial.

---

## Verse 26:130

> and act so viciously when you attack ˹others˺

**Strength Becomes Corrupt When It Has No Accountability**

The earlier verses show a people with resources, construction, and provision. None of those gifts automatically produces cruelty. The problem arises when the people interpret prosperity as permission. If they believe that their success proves their superiority, they may treat every weaker group as an object to be controlled.

Fear of Allah would interrupt that logic. The powerful person remembers that the same Lord who grants strength can judge its use. Every blow, order, seizure, and threat is known. Accountability turns power from a private possession into a trust whose exercise must be justified.

Quran 41:15 — *“As for ’Ȃd, they acted arrogantly throughout the land with no right, boasting, “Who is superior to us in might?” Did they not see that Allah ˹Himself˺, Who created them, was far superior to them in might? Still they persisted in denying Our signs”* records ʿĀd boasting about their greater strength and asks who could be mightier than they. That parallel helps explain the violence challenged here. A people convinced that it is unsurpassed can mistake victory for moral right and resistance for an insult that deserves punishment.

Violence committed by individuals is serious, but Hūd addresses a culture that normalizes it. When a society praises the conqueror, excuses the strong, and calls victims weak, aggression becomes part of its identity. The question then concerns laws, institutions, stories, and leaders, not only private tempers.

---

## Verse 26:131

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**The Call Returns to Its First Demand**

After exposing ʿĀd's vanity, imagined permanence, and violent attacks, Hūd returns to the central command: fear Allah and obey the messenger. The repetition is not a failure to advance the argument. It shows that every social problem he has named grows from the same root, a heart that no longer lives under God's authority.

**The Messenger's Role Remains Servanthood**

Hūd repeats the command without making himself the object of the people's devotion. They are to fear Allah first. His authority comes from carrying God's message, and obedience to him is valid because it leads the people back to the Lord rather than toward Hūd's personal glorification.

The same summons has appeared in Noah's story and appears in the accounts of other messengers in this sūrah. Prophetic guidance is not a collection of competing demands. Each messenger calls his people to the One God, to reverence, and to obedience. The differences in their circumstances do not change the center of the message. Quran 7:65 — *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. Will you not then fear Him?”* presents Hūd's call to ʿĀd in the same connection between worship, reverence, and obedience, confirming that this repeated command is the mission's center rather than a passing refrain.

---

## Verse 26:132

> Fear the One Who has provided you with ˹the good˺ things you know

**Their Blessings Were Common Knowledge**

The next verses name cattle, children, gardens, and springs. The present verse prepares the people to look at those blessings as a coherent provision from one Lord. Hūd is not flattering ʿĀd with vague praise; he is identifying concrete realities they cannot reasonably deny.

The list also prevents the chiefs from claiming that only their elite experience God's favor. Animals, family, cultivation, and water support the whole community in different ways. The Provider's gifts are not proof that one class is inherently superior. They are trusts distributed through a society that must answer for how it shares them. Quran 16:5–6 — *“And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits They are also pleasing to you when you bring them home and when you take them out to graze”* likewise describes cattle through the benefits and beauty people receive from them, joining usefulness to a reminder of the Giver.

Entitlement is dangerous because it turns a gift into a claim against both God and people. The entitled person expects provision to continue while refusing the duties attached to it. Fear of Allah restores proportion: enjoy what is lawful, acknowledge its source, and do not use it as an excuse to disregard the Giver or harm His creation.

---

## Verse 26:133

> He provided you with cattle, and children

**Provision Becomes Specific**

Hūd moves from the general reminder of provision to two familiar forms: cattle and children. The examples bring theology into daily life. The people do not have to search for a hidden miracle before recognizing dependence on God. Their food, labor, wealth, family life, and future are all tied to gifts they did not create from nothing. Quran 16:5 — *“And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits”* links cattle with warmth, food, and other benefits, while 16:72 — *“And Allah has made for you spouses of your own kind, and given you through your spouses children and grandchildren. And He has granted you good, lawful provisions. Are they then faithful to falsehood and ungrateful for Allah’s favours”* names children among God's good provisions; those parallels illuminate why Hūd chooses these ordinary signs.

The verse places animals and children together without reducing either to property. Both are blessings that involve responsibility. Cattle may support food, work, transport, or livelihood; children carry the hope of continuity and require protection, formation, and care. The gifts enlarge a people's capacity, but they also enlarge its accountability.

A society can count animals as units of prestige and economic power. Hūd asks the people to see beyond the count to the Provider. Each benefit drawn from an animal—food, clothing, labor, or means of exchange—reminds the community that its prosperity depends on life it did not manufacture.

**Children Are a Gift and a Trust**

Cattle and children connect private life to public life. Animals may support households and markets; children depend on parents, relatives, teachers, and neighbors. God's provision therefore creates networks of obligation rather than permission for isolated accumulation.

---

## Verse 26:134

> and gardens, and springs

**The Landscape Itself Is Named as Provision**

Hūd completes his immediate list with gardens and springs. The gifts are not confined to objects owned by a household. They include a living landscape: cultivated growth and the water that makes growth possible. ʿĀd's prosperity is therefore surrounded by signs of sustaining care.

Gardens display the fruit of time, soil, labor, and water. Springs suggest a source that continues to feed life beyond a single moment. By naming them, Hūd asks the people to look at the environment that supports their comfort and to recognize that its order is not an achievement of their pride alone. Quran 16:10–11 — *“He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect”* similarly connects rain with drinking water, grazing, crops, and fruit, presenting the chain of provision as a sign for reflection.

**Springs Point to Dependence**

When gardens remain green and springs continue to flow, a generation may assume that prosperity is automatic. The more stable the blessing appears, the easier it is to forget the One who sustains it. Hūd's list restores the origin of what the people see every day.

---

## Verse 26:135

> I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.”

**Hūd's Warning Comes From Fear for Them**

After recalling the gifts given to ʿĀd, Hūd explains why he has urged reverence and obedience. He fears for them because their rejection places them before a tremendous day. The sentence reveals the emotional source of the warning: not envy of their prosperity, but concern about what their choices will bring.

The word tremendous gives the coming day a scale that exceeds ordinary human disputes. ʿĀd may think only in rivals, buildings, and present security. Hūd places their lives before a judgment larger than any authority they recognize on earth. Their wealth cannot make that day small.

**A Verified Parallel Shows the Same Warning**

Quran 46:21 — *“And remember the brother of ’Ȃd, when he warned his people, who inhabited the sand-hills—there were certainly warners before and after him—˹saying,˺ “Worship none but Allah. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.”* describes Hūd warning ʿĀd at the sand-hills and saying that he fears for them a tremendous day's punishment. The parallel confirms that this fear belongs to the heart of his mission. It is not an exaggerated phrase added only after the people's destruction; it is spoken before the outcome is visible.

ʿĀd's horizon has been narrowed to what it can build, defend, and command. Hūd opens it again. The tremendous day makes present decisions transparent. A monument, a castle, or a victorious attack cannot be considered apart from the account that follows life.

---

## Verse 26:136

> They responded, “It is all the same to us whether you warn ˹us˺ or not

**Warnings Are Meant to Preserve Choice**

Hūd warns before the punishment becomes visible. That timing is a mercy because it leaves the people room to change. They treat the warning as irrelevant, but the very fact that it has arrived shows that their choice still matters. A person who can repent does not stand in the same position as one who has already met the consequence.

The answer of ʿĀd tries to reverse this mercy. By saying that warning and no warning are equal, they make opportunity sound pointless. The Qur'anic account shows the opposite: the warning is evidence that God has not abandoned them to their chosen path. Quran 2:6 — *“As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe”* uses a closely related formulation for people who persist in disbelief, showing that the problem lies in the hearer's refusal, not in any weakness of the warning.

Humility listens even when a message is unwelcome. Arrogance decides in advance that no correction deserves attention. The people of ʿĀd have made their own judgment the measure of every warning, so they can dismiss Hūd without responding to his reasons.

---

## Verse 26:137

> This is simply the tradition of our predecessors

**The People Appeal to Precedent**

ʿĀd explains its indifference by appealing to the tradition of its predecessors. The statement shifts responsibility from the present generation to those who came before. What they do feels safe because it is inherited. Yet age and familiarity do not turn a practice into truth, and an ancestor's decision cannot answer for a descendant's judgment.

The Arabic expression can carry the sense of an inherited way, custom, religion, or claim repeated from earlier people. The ambiguity itself is instructive. Quran 25:5 — *“And they say, “˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening.”* records a related dismissal in which opponents reduce revelation to tales of the past, showing how an appeal to antiquity can be used to evade the truth being heard now. ʿĀd may be defending its worship, its conduct, or the stories that make its conduct appear respectable. In each case, the appeal to the past is being used as a shield against correction.

**Ancestors Are Not the Final Measure**

This error appears whenever a society says, “We have always done this,” as though history itself were a revelation. The statement avoids the real questions: Does the practice honor God? Does it protect justice? Does it withstand truthful examination? Hūd's message brings those questions back into view.

---

## Verse 26:138

> And we will never be punished.”

**The Denial Reaches Its Extreme**

ʿĀd's appeal to the past ends with a confident denial of punishment. They do not merely say that they have no evidence of consequence. They say that punishment will never come. The word never reveals a certainty that exceeds their knowledge and turns a hope of escape into a creed.

The claim can coexist with acknowledgment of a creator while denying that people will be held to account after death. Whether expressed through that distinction or through complete rejection, the practical result is similar: God's claim over conduct is emptied of consequence. Quran 46:26 — *“Indeed, We had established them in a way We have not established you ˹Meccans˺. And We gave them hearing, sight, and intellect. But neither their hearing, sight, nor intellect were of any benefit to them whatsoever, since they persisted in denying Allah’s signs. And ˹so˺ they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule”* makes the parallel point that hearing, sight, and understanding do not benefit a people who persist in denying God's signs.

**“Never” Converts a Claim Into a Barrier**

Humility leaves room for correction. It can say, “I do not yet understand,” or, “I need to examine this.” Arrogance says, “This cannot be true,” even when the speaker lacks authority to make that judgment. Hūd's people choose the second posture and make their certainty part of their guilt.

## Verse 26:139

> So they rejected him, and ˹so˺ We destroyed them. Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**Rejection Becomes a Verdict**

The account now moves from speech to consequence. ʿĀd rejected Hūd, and God destroyed them. The connection is not a claim that every worldly difficulty is a direct punishment that humans can confidently identify. Here the Qur'an itself explains the judgment within a prophetic account: the people had received warning, persisted in rejection, and then met the consequence announced to them.

Quran 46:24–25 — *“Then when they saw the torment as a ˹dense˺ cloud approaching their valleys, they said ˹happily˺, “This is a cloud bringing us rain.” ˹But Hûd replied,˺ “No, it is what you sought to hasten: a ˹fierce˺ wind carrying a painful punishment!” It destroyed everything by the command of its Lord, leaving nothing visible except their ruins. This is how We reward the wicked people”* describes ʿĀd seeing a dense cloud approach their valleys, mistaking it for rain, and then facing a fierce wind whose command destroyed what God willed. It also says that the remains visible were their dwellings. This parallel confirms the seriousness of the present verdict without requiring speculation about every historical ruin.

**The Saved Lesson Is Not a License for Arrogance**

History can humble the believer or feed another form of pride. The humble reader says, “Let me not repeat their certainty.” He does not assume that belonging to a later community or possessing different technology makes him safe from the pattern exposed here.

## Verse 26:140

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**The Refrain Interprets the Destruction**

After stating that ʿĀd were destroyed, the verse closes with the names of the Lord: the Almighty, Most Merciful. The judgment is therefore not a chaotic disaster outside divine wisdom. It occurs under the power of the One who sent warning and made repentance possible. The names tell the reader how to understand both the severity and the care in the account.

Power explains why ʿĀd's castles, attacks, and confidence could not protect them. Mercy explains why Hūd came to them as a brother, why he asked for no payment, and why he continued to warn them before the final consequence. Quran 46:24–25 — *“Then when they saw the torment as a ˹dense˺ cloud approaching their valleys, they said ˹happily˺, “This is a cloud bringing us rain.” ˹But Hûd replied,˺ “No, it is what you sought to hasten: a ˹fierce˺ wind carrying a painful punishment!” It destroyed everything by the command of its Lord, leaving nothing visible except their ruins. This is how We reward the wicked people”* likewise places the approaching punishment after Hūd's warning and describes the remains of ʿĀd's dwellings, reinforcing the contrast between divine power and human security. The refrain holds these meanings together.

ʿĀd measured greatness through landmarks, fortifications, physical strength, and the ability to strike others. The title Almighty dismantles that measure. No created power can compete with the Lord who gives existence to people, land, water, and time.

## Verse 26:141

> The people of Thamûd rejected the messengers

**Thamûd Is Introduced by Its Response**

The account of Thamûd begins with a verdict about how the people received revelation: they rejected the messengers. The verse does not first praise their settlements, skill, or resources. It puts their moral response before their material description. A people may be remembered for what it builds, but the Qur'an asks first whether it accepted the truth sent to it.

This opening also connects Thamûd to the communities already mentioned. Noah's people and ʿĀd had heard warning and refused it. Thamûd now enters the same sequence, not because every historical detail is identical, but because human pride keeps returning to the same decision. The names change; the test of submission remains.

**Rejection Comes Before the Famous Sign**

Quran 7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment”* likewise presents Ṣāliḥ as Thamûd's brother and describes the she-camel as a sign from their Lord. That parallel shows that the miracle was placed within a prior call to worship Allah. The community's central problem was not a shortage of marvels but a refusal to let evidence govern its allegiance.

## Verse 26:142

> when their brother Ṣâliḥ said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺

**The Messenger Is Close Enough to Be Known**

A local messenger carries a special kind of evidence: the people can compare his claim with the character they have observed. Ṣāliḥ is not asking them to trust an unknown adventurer. They know his family and his conduct. If they reject his warning, they must explain why the message is false rather than simply inventing a reason to distrust his motives.

Quran 7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment”* also presents him as Thamûd's brother and describes his call to worship Allah alone. The parallel confirms that the family language is part of the mission's meaning. God sends guidance through a human being who can speak plainly to a human community, not through a voice detached from ordinary life.

Some people hear religious fear as a demand for constant anxiety. Ṣāliḥ's question has a different purpose. Fear of Allah restrains the hand before it harms, the ruler before he abuses authority, and the wealthy person before he turns provision into domination. It is a moral guard placed before the deed, not only a reaction afterward.

---

## Verse 26:143

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you

**Trustworthiness Supports a Difficult Claim**

Ṣāliḥ identifies himself as a trustworthy messenger because the people are being asked to accept a message that will unsettle their worship and confidence. Trust is not decorative praise. It addresses the question of whether the speaker is delivering God's command faithfully or using religion to pursue influence, wealth, or revenge.

**The People Are Not Asked for Blind Admiration**

Trustworthy speech deserves a fair hearing, not a personality cult. Ṣāliḥ does not ask Thamûd to abandon understanding. His claim can be considered alongside the clarity of his call, the coherence of his worship, and the signs God provides. The messenger's character opens the door to listening; it does not replace the truth of what he conveys.

Quran 18:110 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.”* states the same basic relation for the Prophet Muhammad: the messenger is a human being who receives revelation and calls to one God. Humanity is not a defect in an envoy. It is the condition that makes imitation, instruction, and moral accountability possible for those who hear him.

---

## Verse 26:144

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**Trust Must Become Obedience**

Ṣāliḥ has said that he is a trustworthy messenger. This verse states what the people should do with that claim: fear Allah and obey the messenger. The command does not ask Thamûd merely to respect Ṣāliḥ's honesty. It asks the community to let his guidance govern its choices.

Fear of Allah gives obedience its right motive. A person may comply with a leader because of force, custom, or the promise of reward. Taqwa is different. It makes the servant aware that God sees the hidden intention and the public action. Obedience then grows from reverence rather than from fear of social consequences alone.

**A Repeated Prophetic Formula Carries Continuity**

Quran 7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment”* presents Ṣāliḥ calling Thamûd to worship Allah alone and identifying a clear proof from their Lord. That parallel shows the command is attached to a call to divine oneness, not to blind loyalty. Obedience to Ṣāliḥ is meaningful because it leads the people away from idols and back to God.

---

## Verse 26:145

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds

**A Parallel From Hūd Clarifies the Pattern**

Quran 11:51 — *“O my people! I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the One Who created me. Will you not then understand”* records Hūd telling ʿĀd that he asks no reward and that his reward is with the One who created him. Ṣāliḥ's statement follows the same prophetic ethic. The repetition establishes that the messengers do not come to create personal empires. They direct all reward and worship back to God.

The parallel also gives Thamûd a comparison it should understand. Another powerful people heard the same refusal of payment and still rejected its messenger. Ṣāliḥ's mission will test whether Thamûd learns from that earlier failure or repeats it under a different name.

The absence of a demand also protects the poor from a second humiliation. They need not prove their worth by funding the messenger, and the wealthy cannot claim that their contribution gives them control over the message. Divine guidance is not a monument financed by patrons. It is a trust announced to every person under the same Lord.

---

## Verse 26:146

> Do you think you will be ˹forever˺ left secure in what you have here

**A Question Challenges Their Security**

Ṣāliḥ turns from the people's identity as a prosperous community to the assumption beneath their prosperity. Do they think they will be left secure in what they have here? The question does not deny that Thamûd has real comforts. It challenges the leap from possession to permanence, from present ease to an expectation that no judgment or loss can reach them.

Security is a blessing when it produces gratitude and responsibility. It becomes dangerous when it persuades a people that its present arrangement is guaranteed. Ṣāliḥ asks Thamûd to examine the belief hidden inside its confidence: not simply “We have been given much,” but “What we have means we cannot be disturbed.”

**Security Does Not Cancel Accountability**

Quran 7:74 — *“Remember when He made you successors after ’Âd and established you in the land—˹and˺ you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains. So remember Allah’s favours, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”* recalls Thamûd as a people established in the land who built palaces on its plains and carved homes in the mountains. The parallel confirms the connection between their material security and their construction. Their settlement was real, but its reality did not make them independent of the Lord who granted it.

---

## Verse 26:147

> amid gardens and springs

**The Scene of Security Is Abundant**

The phrase completes the setting of Ṣāliḥ's question. Thamûd imagines security amid gardens and springs. Its environment is not described as empty or desperate. Green growth and flowing water create the conditions in which a people can feel settled, nourished, and confident about tomorrow.

Quran 16:10–11 — *“He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect”* connects rain with water for drinking, grazing, crops, and fruit. That parallel shows how the Qur'an treats cultivated abundance as a sign that invites reflection. Gardens are not only scenery. They reveal dependence and ask whether the people will respond with thanks or with a stronger claim of self-sufficiency.

**Springs Make Provision Feel Permanent**

A garden can be cultivated by honest work or maintained through exploitation. The appearance of abundance does not reveal the whole moral account. Who labored? Who received the harvest? Who was excluded from the water? The setting of security must be examined through justice as well as admiration.

Gardens and springs support families, workers, travelers, and animals. Their benefit naturally extends beyond the person who first claims them. A prosperous community honors the Giver by making provision serve life broadly rather than concentrating it only where power already exists.

---

## Verse 26:148

> and ˹various˺ crops, and palm trees ˹loaded˺ with tender fruit

**Cultivation Shows the Work of Time**

Ṣāliḥ continues describing the abundance around Thamûd through crops and palms carrying tender fruit. The image is agricultural, but its meaning reaches beyond food. A harvest depends on a sequence that cannot be rushed completely: planting, growth, care, ripening, and collection. The people benefit from time ordered by God, not only from their own effort.

Quran 16:11 — *“With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect”* includes date palms among the growth produced through rain, alongside crops and other fruits. The parallel confirms that agriculture is presented as a sign for reflection. The question is not whether the people may enjoy these goods, but whether enjoyment leads them to remember the One who makes them possible.

**Abundance Can Become a Distraction**

The right interpretation is neither “we made this alone” nor “wealth must be rejected.” It is “God has provided this, and we will answer for its use.” That answer would turn cultivation into gratitude. Without it, the heaviest harvest can become another reason for the people to imagine themselves beyond correction.

---

## Verse 26:149

> to carve homes in the mountains with great skill

**Mountain Homes Display Mastery**

Ṣāliḥ's question reaches the most striking expression of Thamûd's skill: homes cut into mountains. The rock itself becomes a building material, and the settlement appears protected by the permanence of the landscape. Such work requires planning, labor, and technical knowledge. The verse recognizes ability while questioning the pride that can grow around it.

Skill is honorable when it serves life and is joined to gratitude. It becomes spiritually dangerous when the maker begins to love the evidence of his own power more than the Lord who gave him hands, minds, materials, and time. The issue is not whether Thamûd can build, but what its building teaches the people to believe about themselves.

**The Mountain Does Not Guarantee Permanence**

Quran 89:9 — *“and Thamûd who carved ˹their homes into˺ the rocks in the ˹Stone˺ Valley”* recalls Thamûd as a people who carved rocks in a valley. That parallel confirms that their mountain work was part of their remembered identity. It also places the achievement in a warning about the fate of powerful peoples. A famous technique can survive in memory while its boastful society does not.

---

## Verse 26:150

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**Obedience Is the Practical Form of Reverence**

The command has two parts because inner awareness must produce outward fidelity. Fear of Allah that never changes behavior remains a claim without proof. Obeying Ṣāliḥ means accepting the instruction he brings even when it limits a profitable habit or challenges a custom protected by the powerful.

Quran 7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment”* presents Ṣāliḥ's call to worship Allah alone alongside the sign granted to Thamûd. That parallel shows why the command returns: the miracle and the material blessings are both meant to direct the people to God. Repetition is patient instruction before it becomes testimony against them.

**The Command Includes a Limit on Pride**

Thamûd may think obedience is a humiliation after the people have showed such skill. Ṣāliḥ reverses that thought. The humiliating condition is to be ruled by appetite, fear the loss of status, and remain unable to admit dependence. Obedience to the Creator is dignity because it frees the servant from those lesser masters.

Taqwa lets a person say, “I was wrong,” even when the admission costs honor. It lets a society change course before its habits become a public disaster. The command is strong because pride is strong, but its purpose is mercy.

---

## Verse 26:151

> And do not follow the command of the transgressors

**The Arabic Sense Includes Excess**

The word used for the transgressors is associated with crossing bounds and, in the source discussion, with prodigality or wasteful excess. That range is important. Corruption is not limited to a single violent act. It can include a pattern of wanting more than justice permits and encouraging others to pursue worldly advantage without restraint.

Leadership is not condemned simply because it directs others. A just leader can organize work, protect people, settle disputes, and distribute responsibility. The danger arises when authority is separated from accountability. The transgressors want people to follow the command while ignoring the consequences borne by others.

**Moderation Protects the Community**

Quran 7:31 — *“O Children of Adam! Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat and drink, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful”* commands people to eat and drink without waste and says that God does not love the wasteful. That parallel clarifies one dimension of Ṣāliḥ's warning. Excess is not harmless when it consumes resources, teaches vanity, and leaves others to bear scarcity. Moderation is a form of obedience, not a refusal of lawful enjoyment.

---

## Verse 26:152

> who spread corruption throughout the land, never setting things right.”

**Corrupt Leaders Can Call Themselves Reformers**

Those who cause harm rarely describe themselves as corrupt. They may claim to be defending the people, preserving tradition, or bringing progress. Quran 2:11–12 — *“When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!” Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it”* describes a related posture in which people deny that they spread corruption and present themselves as reformers. The parallel shows why self-description cannot be the measure of a leader's work.

Results and methods must be examined. Does the policy protect life or consume it? Does it make truth easier to speak or punish correction? Does it distribute benefit fairly or enrich a narrow circle? A title such as reformer has no value when the actual effect is damage.

The land includes the physical environment, but the phrase also embraces the human order established upon it. Corruption can wound rivers and fields, and it can wound trust, family life, law, and worship. Ṣāliḥ's warning is broad because transgression rarely stays in one area. Disorder in power spreads through the whole community.

Quran 7:56 — *“Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. And call upon Him with hope and fear. Indeed, Allah’s mercy is always close to the good-doers”* likewise forbids spreading corruption after the land has been set right and joins the warning to hope and fear of Allah. That parallel connects environmental and social responsibility with worship. A people does not honor God by praying while using its authority to ruin the world around it.

A just community must therefore measure leaders by what they restore, not only by what they promise. Apologies without restitution leave the injured carrying the cost. New buildings without honest law leave the underlying corruption intact. Iṣlāḥ joins moral intention to concrete correction.

## Verse 26:153

> They replied, “You are simply bewitched

**The Accusation Cannot Erase Ṣāliḥ's Brotherly Relation**

A believer protects the heart from this form of certainty by remaining willing to examine. Do not make the messenger's humanity, unfamiliarity, or social position a substitute for testing the message. Thamûd's word “bewitched” is short, but it displays a long refusal to let truth judge the people who hear it.

**The Accusation Can Carry Two Senses**

The Arabic wording allows a discussion about what Thamûd means by the charge. It may refer to a person affected by sorcery, or it may carry a sense connected with a human creature who eats and lives ordinary bodily life. The first reading attacks Ṣāliḥ's reliability; the second treats his humanity as evidence that he cannot be a messenger.

Thamûd may think that a messenger should be unlike the people he addresses. The Qur'an repeatedly corrects that expectation. Quran 25:7 — *“And they say ˹mockingly˺, “What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner”* records critics objecting that a messenger eats food and walks through marketplaces, while Quran 18:110 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.”* presents the messenger as human while distinguishing him through revelation.

The charge also reveals how quickly a community can make an insult sound like a conclusion. Repeated often enough, the label may become part of public memory and obscure the messenger's actual words. Truthful judgment requires returning to the claim itself rather than accepting the crowd's description of the person who made it.

The people speak with confidence, but confidence is not the same as knowledge. Their accusation may make the crowd feel secure, yet it leaves the central question unanswered. What does God require of them, and is Ṣāliḥ conveying it faithfully?

## Verse 26:154

> You are only a human being like us, so bring forth a sign if what you say is true.”

**Humanity Becomes Their Objection**

Thamûd expands its accusation by saying that Ṣāliḥ is a human being like them. The phrase sounds reasonable until its implication is examined. They are not only observing that the messenger shares human needs. They are using shared humanity to deny that God can choose a human envoy.

The Qur'an presents the objection as a recurring one. Quran 25:7 — *“And they say ˹mockingly˺, “What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner”* records critics objecting to a messenger's ordinary eating and movement among people, while Quran 18:110 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.”* affirms human likeness together with revelation and divine oneness. The pattern shows that humanity is not the contradiction Thamûd imagines. The contradiction lies in expecting guidance while refusing the form God gives it.

If Ṣāliḥ were an angel, Thamûd could claim that obedience was impossible for ordinary people. A human messenger removes that excuse. He lives within the same limits of bodily life, yet he shows what it means to submit to God. His humanity makes the path concrete rather than remote.

**The Demand for a Sign Is Conditional**

A sign cannot replace willingness to obey. Someone determined to resist may demand another sign after receiving one, not because evidence is absent but because submission is unwelcome. Ṣāliḥ's earlier call already has moral clarity; the requested sign will test whether the people recognize God's authority or treat a miracle as a spectacle they command.

A prophet does not manufacture signs to prove personal strength. They are acts of God that support the truth of the messenger's call. This distinction prevents the messenger from becoming a magician who controls extraordinary events for applause. Ṣāliḥ's role is to convey and explain, while God decides what sign will appear.

Quran 7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment”* presents the she-camel as a clear proof from the Lord and calls it a sign for Thamûd. That parallel answers the demand in the present verse. The sign is not a performance by Ṣāliḥ; it is a divine test placed before the people.

## Verse 26:155

> Ṣâliḥ said, “Here is a camel. She will have her turn to drink as you have yours, each on an appointed day

**The Sign Arrives as a Living Trust**

Ṣāliḥ answers Thamûd's demand for proof by identifying a she-camel as a sign from God. The sign is not only an object to admire. It is a living creature placed within the people's daily economy and given a protected share of water. The miracle therefore enters the ordinary world where the community's habits and interests operate.

Quran 7:73 — *“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment”* also calls the she-camel a clear proof from the Lord and instructs Thamûd to leave her to graze. The parallel makes the source of the sign clear. Ṣāliḥ presents it, but he does not claim to have created or controlled it. The camel points beyond the messenger to the Lord whose authority is being tested.

This interpretive range does not weaken the command. Both readings make the resource a trust rather than a free-for-all. Thamûd must accept a limit that its appetite did not design. The divine sign turns water use into an act of obedience and exposes the community's capacity for fairness.

---

## Verse 26:156

> And do not ever touch her with harm, or you will be overtaken by the torment of a tremendous day.”

**Collective Knowledge Increases Responsibility**

The sign becomes a lesson when it changes the hand before the hand strikes. Fear the tremendous day by respecting the small boundary today. Ṣāliḥ's warning joins compassion, justice, and worship: leave what God has protected, and do not let appetite persuade you that the command is negotiable.

**The Camel's Protection Is a Test of Obedience**

The animal is not protected because it can argue its case or defend itself. It is protected because God has commanded the people to respect it. That fact exposes the difference between justice and power. If Thamûd honors only the rights of those who can demand them, its system is not just.

Quran 11:64 — *“And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s earth and do her no harm, or a swift punishment will overtake you!”* similarly describes the she-camel as a sign and warns the people not to harm her. The parallel confirms that the prohibition belongs to the central meaning of the event. The miracle tests whether the community will accept a divine allocation even when its own desire would prefer unrestricted access.

---

## Verse 26:157

> But they killed her, becoming regretful

**The People Cross the Boundary**

Thamûd does what it was warned not to do: it kills the she-camel. The act is brief in the verse, but it carries the weight of the earlier call, the public sign, and the assigned share of water. The people are not acting without guidance. They cross a boundary that Ṣāliḥ has explained and connected to a tremendous consequence.

**Remorse Arrives After the Choice**

There is a difference between remorse that leads to honest repentance and regret that merely mourns the loss of comfort. Thamûd may fear what its act will bring, but fear of consequence is not yet the same as submission to God. True repentance names the wrong, abandons its cause, and seeks to repair what can be repaired.

Quran 91:13–14 — *“But the messenger of Allah warned them, “˹Do not disturb˺ Allah’s camel and her ˹turn to˺ drink!” Still they defied him and slaughtered her. So their Lord crushed them for their crime, levelling all to the ground”* links the warning about the camel's turn to drink with the people's later slaughter of her and the punishment that followed. The parallel confirms the sequence: command, violation, and consequence. It also shows why the remorse cannot be separated from the earlier defiance.

## Verse 26:158

> So the punishment overtook them. Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**The Consequence Arrives After the Warning**

The punishment overtakes Thamûd after the people have rejected Ṣāliḥ's call and killed the she-camel. The wording presents the judgment as an arrival that catches the community, not because God lacked knowledge of their conduct, but because the people had lived as though consequence could never reach them.

Quran 7:77–78 — *“Then they killed the she-camel—defying their Lord’s command—and challenged ˹Ṣâliḥ˺, “Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are ˹truly˺ one of the messengers.” Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them, and they fell lifeless in their homes”* describes the people killing the camel, defying their Lord's command, and then being struck by an overwhelming earthquake. The parallel gives the punishment a concrete form while preserving the moral center: the event is tied to rebellion, not to an arbitrary loss of fortune.

**The Punishment Overtakes False Security**

Thamûd had imagined itself secure among gardens, crops, palms, and mountain homes. The verb overtook cuts through that confidence. A community can plan for decades and still be unable to schedule the moment when its security ends. Created arrangements remain subject to the command of the Creator.

Thamûd's strength could carve mountains and manage resources, but it could not protect the people from the consequence of defying God. The contrast between their skill and their end is part of the sign. Ability is real, yet it is never ultimate.

## Verse 26:159

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**The Two Names Reject Two Distortions**

One distortion sees only power and imagines God as a force whose judgment leaves no hope. Another sees only mercy and assumes that no wrongdoing can bring a final consequence. The refrain rejects both. God is Mighty enough to judge and Merciful enough to warn, delay, and accept a sincere return.

A believer needs both truths. Fear of the Almighty prevents pride, while hope in the Merciful prevents despair. Thamûd's story especially corrects presumption, the idea that abundance and delay prove safety. The mercy they received was an invitation to repent, not permission to continue violating the command.

This also disciplines human judgment. People can resist oppression and seek lawful justice, but they should not claim to know every hidden reason behind a calamity. God's power is complete because His knowledge is complete. Human beings must act with evidence, restraint, and humility.

Quran 7:77–78 — *“Then they killed the she-camel—defying their Lord’s command—and challenged ˹Ṣâliḥ˺, “Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are ˹truly˺ one of the messengers.” Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them, and they fell lifeless in their homes”* records the killing of the camel followed by an overwhelming earthquake that left the people lifeless in their homes. That parallel confirms the severity of the judgment described here. It also preserves the order that makes the judgment intelligible: a clear sign and command came before the community's defiance.

---

## Verse 26:160

> The people of Lot rejected the messengers

**Lot's Message Is Not a Private Innovation**

The plural messengers places Lot within continuity. His call must be heard alongside the prophetic summons already described in this sūrah: worship God, fear Him, obey the messenger, and do not let appetite rule the community. The setting changes, but divine oneness and moral accountability remain central.

Quran 7:80 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Lot scolded ˹the men of˺ his people, ˹saying,˺ “Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before”* introduces Lot's rebuke to his people within a broader sequence of prophetic warnings. That parallel confirms that the narrative is not inviting curiosity about an isolated scandal. It is exposing what happens when a society is warned about wrongdoing and chooses to defend the wrongdoing instead.

**Collective Identity Cannot Carry a Person to Safety**

The phrase “the people of Lot” can tempt later readers to look at another society with distance. The Qur'an places the verse here to prevent that escape. Every community has desires it protects and boundaries it resents. The question is whether it will accept correction when revelation confronts those desires.

The safest response is humility. Hear the messenger, test claims by God's guidance, and do not treat a familiar social order as an authority above Him. The people of Lot rejected the messengers; the account is a warning to anyone who believes that a community's insistence can make its chosen way immune to judgment.

---

## Verse 26:161

> when their brother Lot said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺

**A Local Messenger Removes an Excuse**

The people cannot claim that Lot knows nothing of their circumstances. He belongs to them and can speak their language. The closeness makes their response more responsible: they are hearing a warning from someone who has shared their environment and understands the social pressure around the conduct he addresses.

Quran 7:80 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Lot scolded ˹the men of˺ his people, ˹saying,˺ “Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before”* also introduces Lot's rebuke to his people in the context of a shameful practice. The parallel confirms that the question is a moral summons, not a private dispute between a prophet and a few individuals. Lot is calling the community to examine its public direction before harm becomes normalized.

Sexual desire, social custom, and public approval can reinforce one another. A person may participate because he wants the act, because others praise it, or because he fears being excluded if he objects. Lot's question places every one of those pressures beneath God. The crowd is not allowed to turn agreement into moral truth.

A society that fears Allah must protect the ability to refuse wrongdoing. It cannot force every person to celebrate what he believes is forbidden, nor can it make public approval a substitute for conscience. Taqwa creates space for truthful correction even when the dominant mood is hostile.

---

## Verse 26:162

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you

**A Messenger From Among the People Can Be Known**

Lot's closeness to the community is part of the moral situation. The people have seen his conduct, family life, and manner of dealing with others. They cannot say that the messenger appeared without a history. If they disagree, they must address the message rather than pretend that Lot's character is unknowable.

Familiarity, however, can be used in the opposite way. People may say that they know Lot too well to accept a special calling from him. They mistake ordinary human familiarity for evidence against revelation. A messenger's shared humanity makes his example accessible; it does not cancel the trust he carries.

**The Messenger Does Not Own the Message**

Quran 18:110 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.”* presents the same principle for the Prophet Muhammad: human nature and revelation are not opposites. A messenger's humanity shows dependence on God, while the revelation he receives gives him an authority no personal invention could supply. Lot's people must distinguish the messenger's human form from the divine source of his call.

---

## Verse 26:163

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**The Messenger's Obedience Is the Pattern**

Lot does not stand outside the command he gives. He fears Allah and obeys the Lord by delivering a difficult warning. His authority is not based on claiming that he is above human moral responsibility. He is the first servant of the message he brings.

The same wording has appeared in the calls of Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ. The repetition shows that the moral center of prophecy does not change when the social problem changes. Worship God, fear Him, and obey the messenger. A community cannot declare that its custom is exempt because its temptation has a different form.

Quran 29:36 — *“And to the people of Midian ˹We sent˺ their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah, and hope for ˹the reward of˺ the Last Day. And do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”* records Shuʿayb calling Midian to worship Allah, hope for the Last Day, and avoid corruption. The parallel shows how reverence for God becomes a public ethic. Fear is not an escape from society; it is what keeps society from turning desire and power into corruption.

**Obedience Does Not Mean Human Domination**

The distinction is important for every community. Religious obedience must be measured by the revealed call, not by the unchecked preference of a leader. Lot's mission curbs human domination; it does not establish a new form of it.

---

## Verse 26:164

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds

**Lot Denies a Financial Motive**

Lot has asked his people to fear Allah and obey him, a request that challenges a settled public practice. They could accuse him of seeking money, influence, or a position above the community. He answers that he asks no reward from them. His warning is not a transaction in which moral approval can be bought.

Lot names God as the Lord of all worlds rather than as a tribal patron. The authority and recompense behind his mission extend beyond the people in front of him. Their approval is limited, their wealth is temporary, and their power cannot determine the value of faithful service.

This title also means that Lot's message is not a try to impose one family's private code on a rival group. He speaks for the Lord of every people. The community is being asked to answer to an universal Creator, not to submit to Lot because he wants to win a local dispute.

---

The pattern of the appeal is the same as Noah's and Hūd's, and the Book applies it to every messenger in this sūrah in the same words: no reward asked, the reward with the Lord of all the worlds (26:109, 127, 145, 164, 180 — *“I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds”*). The sūrah of the Cattle puts the same disavowal in the Prophet's own words after the people refuse him (6:90 — *“These ˹prophets˺ were ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah, so follow their guidance. Say, “I ask no reward of you for this ˹Quran˺—it is a reminder to the whole world.”*), and the sūrah of the Story of the Cave in his reply to the townspeople (18:6 — *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message”*). The repetition is deliberate: the message is separated from the messenger's gain so that no one can say the religion is a business.

## Verse 26:165

> Why do you ˹men˺ lust after fellow men

**Desire Does Not Become Right Through Intensity**

The force of an appetite can make it feel authoritative. A person may reason that because a desire is strong, fulfilling it must be necessary or good. Lot's question exposes the gap between intensity and moral truth. Feelings can be real without becoming commands that God has authorized.

Quran 29:28–30 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Lot rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people: “You certainly commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before you Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.” Lot prayed, “My Lord! Help me against the people of corruption.”* records Lot rebuking his people for male-male desire and also mentions their abuse of travelers and misconduct in public gatherings. That parallel shows that the present question belongs to a wider moral disorder. The problem is not presented as an isolated private feeling but as part of a community's public conduct.

Lot speaks to men as a group because the conduct has become a defining part of the society he addresses. A private struggle can be met with counsel and repentance; a public order that pressures people to accept wrongdoing requires a public warning. The prophet challenges the standard the community is building around desire.

---

## Verse 26:166

> leaving the wives that your Lord has created for you? In fact, you are a transgressing people.”

**The People Are Called Transgressors**

Lot does not describe the conduct as an innocent variation that becomes right through social approval. He calls the people transgressors, meaning that they have crossed a boundary. The term places their behavior within a larger pattern of refusing God's limits, not merely within a debate over taste.

Transgression also includes the social harms described in the parallel account. Quran 29:29 — *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.”* associates the people with abuse of travelers and public immorality alongside their sexual wrongdoing. That context shows why the community's condition is not a private matter detached from justice. A people that celebrates desire while violating others has crossed several boundaries at once.

This discipline is especially important when a society is polarized. Moral truth should not be weakened to avoid offense, but the delivery of truth must not become an excuse for cruelty. Lot's sentence is severe because the matter is severe, yet its purpose is to stop the community and return it to God.

---

The word used here, that they are a people transgressing, is the same judgment stated twice elsewhere in the Book: in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where the conduct is described as an outrage and an excess with no precedent in the worlds (7:80–81 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Lot scolded ˹the men of˺ his people, ˹saying,˺ “Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before You lust after men instead of women! You are certainly transgressors.”*), and in the sūrah of the Spider, where it is called what no one in creation had done before them (29:28–29 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Lot rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people: “You certainly commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before you”*). The verse's mention of "the wives your Lord has created for you" makes the argument from creation rather than convention: the offence is against the arrangement God made, not merely against the town's standards.

## Verse 26:167

> They threatened, “If you do not desist, O Lot, you will surely be expelled.”

**The Messenger's Place Is Not Granted by the Crowd**

Lot belongs to the community by human relation, but his mission comes from God. The people may control access to their town, yet they cannot decide whether God's command is true by voting on it. Their threat can endanger Lot's worldly residence, but it cannot cancel his status as a messenger.

This distinction gives courage to anyone who loses social approval for obeying God. Belonging to a place is valuable, and expulsion is painful, but no community is the final owner of a servant's conscience. The believer seeks to remain just and peaceful, yet he cannot purchase membership by surrendering truth.

The people behave as if Lot's warning is the shameful act and their practice is the norm that must be protected. Their response reverses the moral positions. Instead of examining themselves, they label the corrector intolerable. This is a common feature of entrenched wrongdoing: correction is experienced as aggression because it threatens comfort.

Quran 7:82 — *“But his people’s only response was to say, “Expel them from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”* records Lot's people responding to his warning by saying that they should be expelled from the town. That parallel confirms that expulsion was not an isolated metaphor but a stated social response to Lot's moral opposition.

---

The expulsion threatened here is answered in the next two verses with the prayer of Lūṭ and the rescue of his household: "My Lord, save me and my family from what they do" (26:168–170). The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf gives the same scene with the additional detail that the town's answer was to drive the family out, since they professed to be purified (7:82 — *“But his people’s only response was to say, “Expel them from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”*), and the sūrah of the Spider records the warning the visitors brought before the towns were overturned (29:31–35 — *“He said, “But Lot is there!” They responded, “We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.”*). The Book's point in each account is that the messenger had no stake in the town beyond his charge, and the loss of the town was the loss of the people who expelled him.

## Verse 26:168

> Lot responded, “I am truly one of those who despise your ˹shameful˺ practice

**The Messenger Will Not Bargain Away Truth**

The people have offered Lot a choice between silence and expulsion. His reply shows that the threat has not made him uncertain about the command. He will not call shameful what God has forbidden merely to preserve social comfort.

Quran 29:28–30 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Lot rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people: “You certainly commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before you Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.” Lot prayed, “My Lord! Help me against the people of corruption.”* describes Lot's rebuke of the men and also mentions their abuse of travelers and misconduct in their gatherings. That parallel helps explain why Lot's detestation reaches beyond a private preference. He is confronting a public culture in which sexual wrongdoing, aggression, and communal celebration of immorality reinforce one another.

Some people assume that compassion requires avoiding clear words. Lot shows otherwise. He speaks plainly because ambiguity would strengthen the community's self-deception. Compassion tells the truth about danger so that people can leave it; it does not rename the danger as harmless.

The people threaten to expel him as if his objection were the offense. Lot refuses that inversion. He will not accept the idea that the person who warns is more blameworthy than the practice being warned against. His reply restores the proper order: wrongdoing is wrong even when a powerful group protects it.

---

## Verse 26:169

> My Lord! Save me and my family from ˹the consequences of˺ what they do.”

**Saving Means Rescue From the Consequences**

The translation makes clear that Lot seeks rescue from what the people do. Their practice is not simply a private disagreement that leaves everyone untouched. It has consequences for the community, for outsiders, and for anyone who refuses to participate. Lot asks God to separate him and his household from the harm generated by that order.

Quran 29:30 — *“Lot prayed, “My Lord! Help me against the people of corruption.”* records Lot asking God for help against the people of corruption. The parallel confirms that his response to the community is supplication, not personal revenge. He names the corruption and seeks divine aid because only God can deliver him from a people determined to persist.

The prayer also keeps the messenger from imagining that moral clarity makes him self-enough. Lot knows the truth, but he still needs God's protection. Correct belief does not remove vulnerability; it teaches the servant where to take vulnerability.

Lot's concern for his family is natural and honorable, but it is not a request that God overlook wrongdoing for the sake of blood ties. The Qur'anic stories repeatedly separate biological connection from faithfulness. A family is protected by obedience, not by lineage alone.

---

## Verse 26:170

> So We saved him and all of his family

**Deliverance Creates Separation From Corruption**

Lot's family is saved by being removed from the environment that has become hostile to God's guidance. Separation here is not an expression of superiority. It is protection from participation in a public evil and from the judgment that will overtake the people who persist in it.

A believer may need distance from a corrupt institution, gathering, or social pressure to preserve obedience. Such distance should be taken with wisdom and without contempt. Lot does not leave because his family is inherently better; he leaves because the community has chosen a path that cannot be shared by those who fear God.

At the same time, the household is not a substitute for individual faith. Each member must respond to God. The rescue of the family is a mercy to people who stand with Lot, not a declaration that every relative is safe regardless of conduct.

**A Parallel Confirms the Pattern**

Quran 7:83 — *“So We saved him and his family except his wife, who was one of the doomed”* likewise states that God saved Lot and his family except his wife, who was among the doomed. The parallel confirms that the present verse stresses a real act of deliverance while the next verse will qualify the household's membership in that deliverance.

---

## Verse 26:171

> except an old woman, who was one of the doomed

**Family Relation Does Not Guarantee Salvation**

The previous verse said that Lot and his family were saved; this verse identifies an exception. An old woman from his household is counted among those doomed. The distinction is essential. Divine rescue is not distributed by bloodline alone. A person may live in the home of a prophet and still choose the side of the community that rejects God.

The verse keeps family affection from becoming a false assurance. Lot loved his household and prayed for its protection, but God's judgment remained based on faithfulness and response. The saved group is defined by allegiance to truth, not by a name shared with the messenger.

The woman's old age is mentioned without turning age into an excuse. Human beings deserve care when they are weak or elderly, but age does not make deliberate opposition to God righteous. Vulnerability calls for compassion in human dealings; it does not erase the moral account before God.

This balance prevents two errors. A society must not neglect the elderly because they are weak, and a family must not assume that an elder's position makes every choice beyond question. God knows the circumstances, intentions, and degree of responsibility of each person more fully than any human observer.

**A Household Can Contain Different Allegiances**

Quran 7:83 — *“So We saved him and his family except his wife, who was one of the doomed”* repeats that God saved Lot and his family except his wife, who was among the doomed. Quran 11:81 — *“So travel with your family in the dark of night, and do not let any of you look back, except your wife.”* likewise says that the wife would suffer the fate of the others. These parallels confirm the moral point without requiring later stories to supply details absent from this verse.

---

## Verse 26:172

> Then We utterly destroyed the rest

**The Remaining Community Faces Judgment**

After Lot and the faithful household are delivered, the verse turns to the rest of the people. They are utterly destroyed. The contrast is deliberate: the community that threatened the messenger is not allowed to define the outcome, while those who followed God are preserved by His command.

The destruction is not presented as a private victory for Lot or as the result of his personal strength. It belongs to God. Lot warned, prayed, and remained separate from the wrongdoing, but the Lord alone carried out the judgment. The verse keeps divine authority at the center.

**Utter Destruction Is Divine Judgment, Not Human License**

This distinction is a safeguard against cruelty. The believer learns fear of God from the destruction, not pleasure in another people's ruin. The event should make him cautious with power and eager to prevent wrongdoing before judgment reaches its end.

Quran 29:32 — *“He said, “But Lot is there!” They responded, “We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.”* records that the angels knew who was in Lot's city and would save him and his family while the wife was among the doomed. That parallel shows that the division is deliberate and known to God. It is not a confused disaster that happens to catch some people and miss others.

---

## Verse 26:173

> pouring upon them a rain ˹of brimstone˺. How evil was the rain of those who had been warned

**Mercy and Punishment Use the Same Creation**

Rain is a reminder that creation is not autonomous. Water can nourish crops, refresh the land, or, by God's command, become part of a destructive event. People cannot infer divine approval from the ordinary continuation of nature. They must read blessings and warnings through revelation and moral accountability.

Quran 25:40 — *“They have certainly passed by the city ˹of Sodom˺, which had been showered with a dreadful rain ˹of stones˺. Have they not seen its ruins? But they do not expect to be resurrected”* recalls a city passed by and showered with a dreadful rain, asking whether its ruins were not seen, while noting that the people did not expect resurrection. That parallel connects physical remains with a failure of moral imagination. The ruins were visible, but the lesson could still be ignored.

The present verse calls the rain evil because it was the consequence of a warned community's refusal. Later observers should not turn the ruins into a tourist curiosity detached from accountability. The remnant is a sign only when it leads the heart to fear God and reject the assumptions that produced the judgment.

---

## Verse 26:174

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**The Sign Joins Rescue to Judgment**

A reader who focuses only on destruction misses half the sign. Lot and his family are delivered, showing that God's power is not only the power to end. It is also the power to protect a faithful household when a larger society has become hostile.

Quran 54:34 — *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot’s family, We delivered them before dawn”* describes a stone-bearing wind and the deliverance of Lot's family before dawn. The parallel confirms that the event is remembered as both punishment and grace. God does not leave the reader with destruction alone; He points to the rescued people as evidence that obedience has a future.

Quran 25:40 — *“They have certainly passed by the city ˹of Sodom˺, which had been showered with a dreadful rain ˹of stones˺. Have they not seen its ruins? But they do not expect to be resurrected”* also connects the ruined city with denial of resurrection. The ruins are therefore not only an archaeological trace. They challenge the assumption that death ends accountability. A sign is understood when it expands the horizon beyond the visible remains.

Most people may refuse to believe because a society teaches them how to interpret every sign. If leaders call warning hatred and corruption freedom, individuals may repeat those categories until they seem obvious. Social agreement can make denial feel like common sense.

**The Refusal Is Moral, Not Merely Intellectual**

The phrase “would not believe” indicates resistance, not only lack of information. A person can understand that an account is serious and still reject the surrender it demands. The sign threatens self-rule, so the heart searches for distance from it.

---

## Verse 26:175

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**The Two Names Close Lot's Account**

After the rescue of Lot's household and the destruction of the rest, the verse names the Lord as the Almighty and Most Merciful. The pairing interprets the entire account. God's power explains why the corrupt community could not protect itself, while His mercy explains why Lot was warned, supported, and delivered.

This title also corrects Lot's own vulnerability. The messenger has no need to pretend that he can manage the outcome by himself. He speaks faithfully and turns to the Lord whose power exceeds the crowd's. Courage grows from dependence on the Almighty, not from an illusion of personal invincibility.

**A Parallel Shows Rescue as Grace**

Quran 54:34 — *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot’s family, We delivered them before dawn”* describes the punishment of Lot's people and the delivery of his family before dawn as an act of grace. That parallel makes the two names visible in the narrative. Judgment displays divine power, while deliverance displays mercy granted to those who remained with Lot.

This continuity also teaches that no age can claim exemption. Lot's people had its own customs and desires, just as earlier nations had theirs. The forms of resistance change, but the Almighty remains above every society and the Most Merciful remains near to those who return.

---

## Verse 26:176

> The residents of the Forest rejected the messengers

**A New Community Is Named by Its Landscape**

The story now turns to the residents of the Forest, or thicket. Unlike the earlier accounts named for Noah, ʿĀd, Thamûd, and Lot, this people is introduced through the place it inhabits. The setting is part of the community's identity, but the opening verdict remains the same: its response to the messengers is rejection.

The source tradition preserves a question about whether the residents of the Forest were the same people as Midian or a distinct community to whom Shuʿayb was sent. The Qur'an identifies Shuʿayb as a messenger to Midian in 7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib.”* and as their brother in 29:36 — *“And to the people of Midian ˹We sent˺ their brother Shu’aib.”*, while other passages name the Forest people separately. The text here does not require a forced resolution.

The cautious conclusion is that the Qur'an places Shuʿayb's warning in a remembered setting associated with a forest or thicket, while leaving the exact relation between that group and Midian open in this verse. The uncertainty does not weaken the message. It directs attention to what the verse makes certain: a community received warning and rejected it.

**Rejection Is the First Fact of the Story**

Shuʿayb's mission is not a random speech addressed to an unknown crowd. Quran 7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers”* presents him addressing Midian as their brother and calling them to worship Allah, deal justly, and avoid corruption. That parallel shows the prophetic method: guidance meets a real community in its own setting and addresses the wrongs that shape its common life.

Even if the Forest people and Midian are distinguished in some readings, the parallel reveals the character of Shuʿayb's mission. He is not seeking abstract debate. He calls a community to recognize God and reform the way it uses the gifts placed in its land.

---

## Verse 26:177

> when Shu’aib said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺

**Shuʿayb Begins With Reverence**

Shuʿayb addresses the residents of the Forest with the same essential question heard by the earlier communities: will they fear Allah? The question places worship and accountability before the particular social problems that will be discussed. A society cannot repair its dealings while treating God as irrelevant to those dealings.

Taqwa is an awareness that guards the heart from crossing limits. It joins fear of judgment to hope in God's mercy and makes a person careful with wealth, promises, measurement, and power. Shuʿayb's question is therefore the root of the practical reform associated with his mission.

Quran 7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers”* presents Shuʿayb speaking to Midian as their brother and calling them to worship Allah, use just measure and weight, and avoid corruption. That parallel shows how reverence becomes social conduct. Fear of God cannot remain in the prayer space while transactions and public decisions ignore His limits.

**Fear of Allah Is Not Fear of People**

This frees a person from the pressure to imitate wrongdoing. If everyone around him cheats, the fear of being called naive can be strong. Taqwa gives him a greater reason to remain honest. He can accept a worldly loss rather than purchase temporary approval through disobedience.

---

## Verse 26:178

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you

**The Messenger Is Not the Owner of the Message**

The title messenger limits as much as it authorizes. Shuʿayb brings guidance; he does not invent a religion and then demand that people submit to his personality. His trustworthiness is shown by his willingness to remain a servant of the Lord rather than a rival source of worship.

Quran 7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers”* presents Shuʿayb as sent to Midian and records his call to worship Allah, deal justly, and avoid corruption. That parallel gives concrete shape to the trust he claims. He is not asking the people to abandon ordinary life; he is asking them to conduct ordinary life under divine limits.

Quran 29:36 — *“And to the people of Midian ˹We sent˺ their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah, and hope for ˹the reward of˺ the Last Day. And do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”* likewise describes Shuʿayb calling Midian to worship Allah, hope for the Last Day, and avoid corruption in the land. The repeated connection between faith and social repair confirms that his trustworthiness includes the courage to address public wrongdoing rather than retreat into private spirituality.

The next verse will state that Shuʿayb asks no reward, but even here trustworthiness implies independence from purchase. A messenger who can be paid to approve dishonest trade is not a trustworthy guide. Shuʿayb's authority cannot be bought by the people whose interests his message unsettles.

**Trustworthy Speech Can Threaten Comfortable Arrangements**

The people may have built their prosperity around habits that no longer withstand moral examination. Shuʿayb's trustworthiness requires him to name the problem even if doing so costs him popularity, safety, or social standing. Silence would protect the arrangement but betray the trust placed in him.

---

## Verse 26:179

> So fear Allah, and obey me

**Obedience Is More Than Formal Compliance**

A person may follow a rule outwardly while searching for a loophole that preserves the same injustice. Shuʿayb's command requires more than technical compliance. It asks the people to obey the moral purpose of God's guidance: honesty, fairness, and protection from exploitation.

This is why obedience begins with fear of Allah. Without reverence, a person may treat rules as obstacles to outsmart. With taqwa, he asks what justice requires even where a narrow reading might allow him to take advantage. The spirit and form of obedience belong together.

**A Parallel Links Worship With Social Repair**

Quran 29:36 — *“And to the people of Midian ˹We sent˺ their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah, and hope for ˹the reward of˺ the Last Day. And do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”* records Shuʿayb calling Midian to worship Allah, hope for the Last Day, and avoid corruption in the land. The parallel shows that reverence and obedience are not separate from public reform. Belief in the Last Day gives present conduct lasting significance.

The community cannot claim to fear God while treating the marketplace as a zone without accountability. Nor can it claim to repair society while ignoring worship. Shuʿayb's command joins both: recognize the Lord, then obey the guidance that protects people from one another's greed.

---

## Verse 26:180

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds

**Shuʿayb Removes the Suspicion of Profit**

Shuʿayb has challenged the economic conduct of the residents of the Forest. They could claim that he is seeking money or influence through the warning. He answers that he asks no reward from them. His message is not a business arrangement in which they pay for moral advice or purchase a favorable judgment.

The declaration is especially fitting for a messenger addressing a people whose life is connected to exchange. Shuʿayb does not enter their world as another trader seeking a better bargain. He stands outside the transaction and calls them to a Lord whose command cannot be bought or adjusted according to wealth.

Shuʿayb locates his reward with the Lord of all worlds. His hope therefore does not depend on the residents accepting him, praising him, or financing his work. God knows the labor of warning, the pain of rejection, and the sincerity hidden beneath every word.

This independence allows the messenger to speak against practices that benefit powerful people. If his reward came from them, he might soften the call whenever the market, chiefs, or wealthy families objected. By expecting reward from God, he can remain truthful without turning his opposition into personal resentment.

---

The appeal closes the last of the sūrah's narratives, and the next verses set out the offence that made it necessary: the full measure, the short weight, and the withholding of what is due to people (26:181–183 — *“Give full measure, and cause no loss ˹to others˺”*). The same charge is made in the sūrah of Hūd, where the call is to give full measure and just weight and not to defraud (11:84–85 — *“And do not give short measure and weight.”*), and in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf with the same wording (7:85 — *“So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order.”*). The messenger's refusal of a wage is what makes the economic reform credible: a prophet who took a fee for the call could be suspected of wanting the traffic himself.

## Verse 26:181

> Give full measure, and cause no loss ˹to others˺

**Worship Is Tested by What the Hand Delivers**

The Qur'an returns to this same pattern in 83:1–3 — *“Woe to the defrauders Those who take full measure ˹when they buy˺ from people but give less when they measure or weigh for buyers”*, where those who demand full measure for themselves but reduce what they give others are condemned. The evil is not ignorance of the proper quantity. They know what they want when they are receiving, then choose a different standard when they are giving.

That double standard exposes the heart of the matter. A person may manipulate a mark, a contract, or a calculation, but he cannot hide the moral direction of the choice from Allah. Full measure trains the soul to give others the standard it wants for itself. It turns belief into a habit of exact fairness, one ordinary exchange at a time.

This is why honest measurement matters even when the buyer never discovers the shortage. Justice is not created by the victim's ability to complain. The owner of a shop may know that a customer will not return, and an employer may know that a worker cannot challenge a record. Full measure remains due because Allah sees the party who has been made vulnerable. The command gives moral protection where human bargaining power cannot.

---

## Verse 26:182

> Weigh with an even balance

**An Even Balance Refuses a Tilted Standard**

The command moves from the quantity given to the instrument and process by which quantity is judged. An even balance does not favor the person who owns it, the customer who is present, or the trader who knows how to adjust it. Its purpose is to make the same standard govern both sides of the exchange.

**Fairness Must Survive Both Directions of Exchange**

An even balance applies when one buys and when one sells. People often demand precision when their own money is leaving and accept approximation when another person's money is entering. The verse rejects that split. The standard must remain stable when the role changes.

The same lesson appears in 17:35 — *“Give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is fairest and best in the end”*, which joins full measurement with an even balance and describes that practice as fairest and best in the end. The phrase about the end gives the command a horizon beyond immediate profit. A dishonest adjustment may produce cash today, while a fair standard produces trust, a clean account, and safety before Allah.

---

## Verse 26:183

> and do not defraud people of their property. Nor go about spreading corruption in the land

**Other People's Property Is Not Yours to Reduce**

The command names the victim as well as the act. People have property, and the person who deals with it must not quietly turn part of it into his own gain. Defrauding is more than a mistake in arithmetic. It is a knowing reduction of another person's right through deceit, pressure, or misuse of trust.

Quran 2:188 — *“Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly, nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing that it is a sin”* applies the same principle to wealth taken through false means and even mentions using authorities to consume part of another person's property. The reference shows why Shuʿayb's warning cannot be reduced to marketplace weights. A false claim, a corrupt ruling, a hidden fee, and a short measure can share one moral structure: taking what belongs to someone else while pretending that the taking is legitimate.

**Corruption Travels Beyond One Transaction**

Corruption grows when a gain made by one person becomes a fear carried by many. A trader who cheats one buyer harms that buyer; a group that intimidates travelers changes how an entire region lives. The verse joins property and land because private greed can become public disorder.

This is why 7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers”* pairs the prohibition of defrauding property with the prohibition of spreading corruption after the land has been set right. The command does not leave justice to private goodwill. It requires a public order in which trade, authority, and security do not cooperate in the removal of people's rights.

---

## Verse 26:184

> And fear the One Who created you and ˹all˺ earlier peoples.”

**Fear of the Creator Grounds Every Other Duty**

Shuʿayb now gives the command behind the commands about measure, weight, and property: fear the One who created you. Taqwa is not panic that makes a person flee from God. It is alertness before the Creator that prevents desire, habit, and power from becoming false gods. A person who remembers where his life came from cannot treat another created person as material for his profit.

The warning returns the community from social rules to worship. Fair dealing is not a clever arrangement invented by traders. It is obedience to the One who made both parties and knows the right of each. The people may hide a reduction from a customer, but they cannot hide it from the One who gave them hands, goods, judgment, and time.

Quran 7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. This is for your own good, if you are ˹truly˺ believers”* places Shuʿayb's call to worship Allah beside his demand for just measure and weight. The order is significant: recognizing the Creator gives the community a reason to respect created persons. No customer is outside God's care, and no prosperous trader is outside God's judgment.

---

## Verse 26:185

> They replied, “You are simply bewitched

**The Reply Attacks the Messenger Instead of the Claim**

Shuʿayb has spoken about God, property, and public corruption. The people's first recorded reply does not examine any of those points. It assigns a label to the speaker: bewitched. The accusation tries to make his words seem unworthy of serious consideration before the community has to answer them.

**Bewitchment Is Used as a Social Shortcut**

Quran 26:153 — *“They replied, “You are simply bewitched”* records the same accusation against Shuʿayb in this very narrative pattern, and 17:47 — *“We know best how they listen to your recitation and what they say privately—when the wrongdoers say, “You would only be following a bewitched man.”* preserves a similar charge against the Prophet Muhammad when people heard the Quran. The repetition reveals a recurring defense of denial: make revelation look like confusion, and the hearer will feel permitted to ignore its demands.

Even if a person were ill, distressed, or unusual, that would not make a false measure just. The truth of the command must be tested by its evidence and content, not by an insult directed at the messenger. A community that cares about justice should be especially careful not to use mental or spiritual language as a weapon against a difficult critic.

The accusation also reveals the people's priorities. They are more concerned to discredit Shuʿayb than to deny that they owe others their property. When a society spends its energy policing the reformer's reputation while refusing to inspect its own conduct, the response becomes evidence of moral resistance.

---

## Verse 26:186

> Also, you are only a human being like us, and we think you are indeed a liar

**Humanity Is Treated as a Reason for Rejection**

The people now present two claims about Shuʿayb. He is human like them, and they regard him as a liar. The first claim is true in one sense: a messenger eats, speaks, and lives among human beings. The error is turning that shared humanity into proof that God could not choose him to carry guidance.

Quran 17:94 — *“And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: “Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?”* records the same objection in direct form: some people found it strange that a human messenger should be sent. The objection misunderstands the purpose of a messenger. If guidance came through a being who never shared human weakness, people could dismiss obedience as impossible to imitate. A human messenger gives the command a visible form within ordinary life.

Because Shuʿayb belongs to their world, the people know his voice, habits, and social position. Familiarity should allow them to test his character, but they use it to reduce him. They see the person they have known and refuse to see the responsibility God may have placed upon him.

---

## Verse 26:187

> So cause ˹deadly˺ pieces of the sky to fall upon us, if what you say is true.”

**Truth Does Not Need to Perform on Demand**

Shuʿayb's message concerns the rights of people and the authority of Allah. Its truth is not established only by a falling sky. Honest measure, the protection of property, and the rejection of corruption are already intelligible duties, and revelation gives them final authority.

The sky imagery reaches beyond the immediate market dispute. People who cannot control a balance may still imagine that wealth gives them control over their destiny. The requested fragments answer that fantasy at the level of the heavens. Human beings are dependent on a creation they did not make, yet arrogance makes them speak as if divine power must satisfy their challenge.

Quran 17:92 — *“or cause the sky to fall upon us in pieces, as you have claimed, or bring Allah and the angels before us, face to face”* shows that this style of demand was not unique to Shuʿayb's people. The Qur'an also presents falling pieces of the sky in 34:9 — *“If We willed, We could cause the earth to swallow them up, or cause ˹deadly˺ pieces of the sky to fall upon them.”* as an event within Allah's power and a sign for servants who turn to Him. The same image can be received as a warning by a servant or used as mockery by a denier. The difference lies in the heart's purpose.

The challenge is also a warning about conditional repentance. Some people say that they will change if a crisis arrives, yet the crisis may leave no time or willingness for change. Shuʿayb's people demand the most extreme confirmation while neglecting the plain command already placed before them. A person should not wait for punishment to make a duty feel real.

---

## Verse 26:188

> Shu’aib responded, “My Lord knows best whatever you do.”

**Divine Knowledge Makes Hidden Conduct Public Before God**

Quran 28:84 — *“Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded with what is better. And whoever comes with an evil deed, then the evildoers will only be rewarded for what they used to do”* states that good and evil deeds receive their fitting recompense. Shuʿayb's brief reply rests on the same moral order. The Lord knows what each person does, so no injustice becomes harmless merely because the victim lacks power to expose it. Knowledge precedes judgment and makes judgment exact.

**Restraint Is Not Weakness**

A speaker under attack may feel compelled to prove strength by predicting a punishment or cursing his opponents. Shuʿayb chooses another kind of strength. He does not surrender the truth, but he declines to make his anger the measure of justice.

**The Messenger Does Not Need to Own the Outcome**

Shuʿayb's duty is faithfulness, not control over the audience. He has explained the command, named the corruption, and warned of accountability. Whether the people repent, persist, or mock does not alter the truth he was sent to convey.

The phrase whatever you do includes the good that a person may hide as well as the wrong he may hide. Divine knowledge is not only a threat. It is also the guarantee that a honest measure, a returned possession, and a private refusal to join corruption are not wasted. Human observers may overlook the deed, but the Lord does not.

---

## Verse 26:189

> So they rejected him, and ˹so˺ were overtaken by the torment of the day of the ˹deadly˺ cloud. That was really a torment of a tremendous day

**Rejection Is Followed by a Consequence**

The verse joins the people's rejection to the torment that overtakes them. The order is moral, not a claim that every hardship in the world can be read by observers as a direct punishment. Here revelation identifies a specific community that persisted after warning and then records the consequence appointed for it.

**Other Accounts Confirm Punishment Without Flattening Its Form**

Quran 7:91 — *“Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them and they fell lifeless in their homes”* describes Shuʿayb's people being seized by an earthquake, and 29:37 — *“But they rejected him, so an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them and they fell lifeless in their homes”* also connects their rejection with an overwhelming earthquake. Quran 15:78 — *“And the residents of the Forest were truly wrongdoers”* calls the residents of the Forest wrongdoers. These passages use different descriptions of the punishment while agreeing about the community's guilt and destruction.

**The Warning Still Works Before the Day Arrives**

The people in the story could no longer use the warning once the torment had overtaken them. Later generations, however, receive the account while choice remains open. The cloud is not preserved to satisfy curiosity about catastrophe. It warns every society that property, public safety, and truth cannot be sacrificed to profit without consequence.

A tremendous day can expose the weakness of every false refuge. Wealth cannot buy shade when Allah commands the cloud to become torment, and social agreement cannot make rejection safe. The people who once controlled the terms of trade discover that they cannot negotiate with the Lord of the worlds. Their story is a warning against confusing temporary influence with real security.

The living can answer differently by making justice ordinary before crisis comes. Give the full measure, protect property, refuse public corruption, and fear the Creator. These are not separate repairs added after the story. They are the very duties whose rejection made the community's apparent prosperity unstable.

---

## Verse 26:190

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe

**The Event Calls for a Response Before Judgment**

The sign is merciful while it can still teach. Later readers are given the account before sharing the destroyed people's final condition. They can inspect their own dealings, return property, oppose corruption, and seek forgiveness rather than waiting for a public collapse to prove that wrongdoing was dangerous.

A sign should therefore make the believer more humble and more active. Humble, because guidance is a gift rather than a certificate of personal superiority. Active, because recognizing the warning creates duties toward people who may be harmed by the same greed. The verse honors the truth of judgment without turning the believer into a spectator of other people's ruin.

Nor should the minority surrender its judgment to social pressure. If dishonest trade is treated as normal, the believer cannot make normality the measure of right. Quran 83:1–3 — *“Woe to the defrauders Those who take full measure ˹when they buy˺ from people but give less when they measure or weigh for buyers”* shows that a common commercial habit can still be condemned when it contains a deliberate double standard. Public agreement may explain why a wrong persists, but it cannot transform the wrong into justice.

The phrase most of them also protects the believer from a crude story in which evidence has no value because some people refuse it. Signs are real even when reception is poor. The task is to make the heart ready to recognize what the evidence demands and to avoid the pride that treats refusal as sophistication.

Quran 26:8–9 — *“Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful”* uses the same sign-and-belief refrain earlier in the sūrah, making the pattern clear: evidence can be present while many still refuse it.

---

## Verse 26:191

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**Power and Mercy Belong Together**

The account closes with two names that must be held together. Allah is the Almighty, so the people cannot escape the consequence of defiance or protect a corrupt order by force. He is the Most Merciful, so the destruction is not an arbitrary display of power. Warning, time, and the opportunity to change belong to mercy before judgment arrives.

Separating the names produces two distortions. If power is remembered without mercy, fear becomes despair or harshness. If mercy is remembered without power, delay is mistaken for approval and forgiveness is treated as permission to continue harming people. The verse refuses both errors.

The people may have thought their land, wealth, and social influence could define the limits of what was possible. The name Almighty breaks that illusion. Allah is not one authority among others, and no local custom can make a command impossible to enforce.

**Mercy Appeared Before the Punishment**

Quran 26:9 — *“And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful”* repeats these two divine names at the close of the earlier signs in the sūrah. The refrain teaches that every account of judgment carries a prior history of care. The Lord is powerful enough to end corruption and merciful enough to send guidance before doing so.

---

## Verse 26:192

> This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds

**Revelation Has a Lord**

The pronoun this points to the message being delivered through the Prophet, not to a human speech act that belongs to its speaker. The verse identifies the Quran as revelation and then identifies its source: the Lord of all worlds. Its authority does not come from the eloquence of the messenger, the approval of a tribe, or the ability of an audience to explain every part of it. It comes from Allah, who owns and governs every realm.

**Revelation Is Received, Not Invented**

The verse answers the suspicion that a prophet might be composing a moral program for personal influence. A messenger can be the human recipient and reciter without being the author of what he conveys. Quran 17:106 — *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace. And We have sent it down in successive revelations”* describes the Quran as revealed in stages, which fits a message delivered through time, circumstance, warning, and response rather than a work produced at one sitting by a human planner.

Staged revelation does not make the message less divine. It shows wisdom in the manner of delivery. Guidance meets people as their questions arise and as their conduct is exposed. The Lord of all worlds is not limited by the pace of a human audience; He teaches in the measure that serves His purpose.

---

## Verse 26:193

> which the trustworthy spirit ˹Gabriel˺ brought down

**The Trustworthy Spirit Is Gabriel**

The bracketed name Gabriel identifies the trustworthy spirit as the angel who conveys revelation. The title trustworthy is central. Gabriel is not presented as a source who edits the message, chooses which commands to soften, or mixes divine speech with personal opinion. He is a faithful bearer of what Allah sends.

**Gabriel's Trust Is Confirmed Elsewhere**

Quran 2:97 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”* also identifies Gabriel with bringing the Quran to the Prophet's heart by Allah's permission and describes the revelation as confirming what came before it. That parallel supplies an important check on the title trustworthy. Gabriel's trust is seen in faithful delivery and in the message's place within the line of revelation, not in a vague claim about an unknown spiritual force.

The designation spirit should not be turned into speculation that the angel has divine status. Gabriel is a created servant who carries what Allah commands. The title honors his function and purity of service. It does not blur the boundary between the Lord and the messenger of the Lord.

---

## Verse 26:194

> into your heart ˹O Prophet˺—so that you may be one of the warners—

**The Heart Makes Warning Personal Without Making It Private**

A warning can become cold information if it never reaches the moral center of the person who speaks. The Prophet's heart receives the message, so his warning is marked by certainty, concern, and patience. He can confront denial without treating the audience as a problem to be discarded. He warns because their response matters before Allah.

Quran 2:97 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”* likewise connects Gabriel's revelation with bringing the Quran to the Prophet's heart. The repeated image shows that the heart is not an ornamental word for emotion alone. It is the place of understanding and orientation. Once revelation reaches it, the messenger stands ready to warn, and the audience is faced with a communication that asks for more than passing attention.

The heart also holds the relation between knowledge and remembrance. A person may encounter a fact once and forget it when desire becomes strong. Revelation returns the truth to the center of awareness, where it can govern a decision made under pressure. The Prophet's reception of the Quran makes him a living reminder, not merely a carrier of written information.

---

## Verse 26:195

> in a clear Arabic tongue

**Clarity Is Part of the Revelation's Mercy**

The Quran is delivered in a clear Arabic tongue. The description concerns more than national identity. A clear tongue makes the message available for hearing, reflection, questioning, and obedience. The first audience is not confronted with a code that cannot be understood in its own language. Allah sends guidance in a form that allows the people to recognize its claims and answer them.

Quran 14:4 — *“We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them. Then Allah leaves whoever He wills to stray and guides whoever He wills. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise”* states the general principle that messengers are sent in the language of their people so that the message can be made clear to them. Arabic in this verse therefore serves communication. It does not make one ethnicity closer to Allah by birth, nor does it turn the language into an object of worship apart from the revelation carried in it.

**Clarity Does Not Coerce Belief**

A clear statement can be rejected for reasons that are not linguistic. Quran 41:44 — *“Had We revealed it as a non-Arabic Quran, they would have certainly argued, “If only its verses were made clear ˹in our language˺.”* presents the deniers objecting even in a hypothetical situation involving a non-Arabic Quran, showing that they could have changed the complaint while keeping the refusal. The issue is therefore not only whether words can be decoded. It is whether the heart will submit to what the words establish.

The final test remains moral. A person can speak Arabic beautifully and still resist the Lord, while another can approach the Quran through translation and obey with sincerity. The verse honors the clear vehicle Allah chose without making pronunciation the measure of righteousness. Clarity opens the door; faith walks through it.

---

## Verse 26:196

> And it has indeed been ˹foretold˺ in the Scriptures of those before

**The Revelation Stands in a Line of Earlier Guidance**

The verse places the Quran within the history of revelation. It is not presented as a message with no relation to the scriptures given before it. The bracketed word foretold captures an interpretive sense: the earlier scriptures contained a description, anticipation, or testimony connected with what has now been revealed.

Continuity does not mean that every community preserved or interpreted its scripture in the same way. It means that Allah's guidance has a recognizable direction. The call to worship Him, reject corruption, and prepare for judgment is not an invention of one generation. The Quran confirms that prophetic truth has a history before its final Arabic expression.

**Two Readings Explain What Was Foretold**

Exegetes have read the pronoun in two main ways. Some understand it to refer to the Quran, meaning that its truth or description was present in earlier scriptures. Others understand the reference to concern the Prophet, whose coming and qualities were known through earlier revelation. The first reading follows the immediate subject of the verse, the revealed message. The second gains support from passages such as 7:157 — *“˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel.”*, which speaks of the Messenger's description in the Torah and the Gospel.

Neither reading turns the earlier scriptures into a replacement for the Quran. The stronger conclusion is that the verse affirms continuity at the level of divine guidance while allowing both the book and its messenger to be part of what earlier revelation anticipated. The Quran's own claim in 2:97 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”* that it confirms what came before supports this connected reading.

---

## Verse 26:197

> Was it not sufficient proof for the deniers that it has been recognized by the knowledgeable among the Children of Israel

**Recognition by Others Does Not Replace Personal Faith**

The knowledgeable among the Children of Israel had a particular position because they knew the language of scripture and the history of prophetic promise. Their recognition could expose the deniers' claim that the Quran had no relation to revelation. The evidence was especially relevant to people who already appealed to the People of the Book when it suited them, then ignored their testimony when it challenged their desires.

**Knowledge Is Valuable Because It Can Recognize Truth**

The verse honors knowledge that leads to recognition rather than knowledge used for status. A scholar may know languages, texts, and histories, yet still resist what that knowledge demands. The people named here are valuable as witnesses because their learning gives their testimony weight, not because their group identity makes every opinion correct.

Quran 46:10 — *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Consider if this ˹Quran˺ is ˹truly˺ from Allah and you deny it, and a witness from the Children of Israel attests to it and then believes, whereas you act arrogantly. Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.”* presents a witness from the Children of Israel as recognizing the truth of the Quran and believing while others act arrogantly. The two passages together show that inherited access to scripture can become either a path to recognition or a reason for pride. Knowledge is a responsibility: it should make a person more honest before evidence, not more skilled at avoiding it.

---

## Verse 26:198

> Had We revealed it to a non-Arab

**The Message Is Not the Property of an Ethnic Group**

Because the Quran is in Arabic, it first addresses an Arab audience in a language it can understand. Quran 14:4 — *“We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them. Then Allah leaves whoever He wills to stray and guides whoever He wills. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise”* gives the broader rule that messengers speak in the language of their people for clarity. That rule serves communication, not ethnic ownership. A non-Arab community can receive the message through translation and teaching without the revelation becoming a tribal possession.

The deniers' imagined alternative therefore collapses. A non-Arab messenger would not have removed their resistance; it would only have supplied a new surface for it. The verse exposes a heart that wants conditions under which it can never be held accountable. Honest faith does the opposite: it seeks understanding, then accepts the claim of the Lord even when the form challenges pride.

The imagined non-Arab reciter would still be a servant carrying a message from the Lord of all worlds. His foreign origin would not lower the truth, just as Shuʿayb's belonging to his people did not make the command about justice optional. Guidance can enter a community from outside its preferred identity, and the honest response is to examine it rather than reject it for being unfamiliar.

---

## Verse 26:199

> who would then recite it to the deniers ˹in fluent Arabic˺, still they would not have believed in it

**Even Fluent Recitation Would Not Satisfy Them**

The hypothetical reaches its conclusion: a non-Arab messenger could recite the Quran in fluent Arabic, and the deniers would still refuse to believe. This removes the idea that their problem is simply a foreign accent or an inaccessible language. They are imagined hearing the same clear recitation they claim to need, yet their response remains denial.

**The Excuse Moves Because the Heart Has Settled**

Quran 41:44 — *“Had We revealed it as a non-Arabic Quran, they would have certainly argued, “If only its verses were made clear ˹in our language˺. What! A non-Arabic revelation for an Arab audience!” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “It is a guide and a healing to the believers. As for those who disbelieve, there is deafness in their ears and blindness to it ˹in their hearts˺. It is as if they are being called from a faraway place.”* describes the same kind of objection from another angle: a non-Arabic revelation would be criticized by an Arab audience for being in the wrong language. The two passages expose a double bind. Arabic is rejected because it is familiar and foreign speech would be rejected because it is foreign. The standard is designed to protect disbelief, not to discover truth.

**Understanding and Believing Are Different Acts**

A person can understand the words of a warning and still reject the duty that follows. Language gives access to meaning; it does not force the will to accept the meaning. This distinction explains why the deniers' fluent-recitation scenario ends with disbelief. Their imagined difficulty has been removed, but their unwillingness remains.

---

The hypothetical belongs to the sūrah's argument about the denial of the revelation: the Book imagines the Qur'an revealed to a non-Arab speaker and recited to the Arab audience in their own tongue, and says they would still not believe (26:198–199 — *“Had We revealed it to a non-Arab”*). The Book states the same fact twice elsewhere — if the angels descended, or the dead spoke, or everything were gathered before them, they would not believe unless God willed it (6:111 — *“Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign ˹they demanded˺, they still would not have believed—unless Allah so willed.”*) — and the sūrah of Jonah puts the reason plainly: no one believes except by God's permission, and the refusal is a refusal of the will. The verse strips the last excuse from the charge that the message was foreign, since the language is not the obstacle.

## Verse 26:200

> This is how We allow denial ˹to steep˺ into the hearts of the wicked

**Denial Becomes a Condition of the Heart**

The verse describes denial as something that can steep into hearts. The image suggests gradual absorption. Rejection begins as a response to one command or one messenger, then becomes a settled way of seeing. After enough refusals, the person no longer meets each proof freshly. He receives it through the habits created by earlier denial.

**The Pronoun Carries a Real Interpretive Question**

Exegetes have differed over what is being allowed to steep into the hearts. Some understand it as denial itself, meaning that Allah leaves the guilty to absorb disbelief through their repeated refusal. Others understand the reference to be the Quran, which enters their hearts as a proof that they then reject. A further contextual reading sees their reception of Arabic without belief as like hearing a language without accepting its meaning.

**Divine Allowance Is Not an Excuse for First Rejection**

The verse should not be read as saying that people are condemned without a prior response. Quran 6:110 — *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance”* describes hearts and eyes being turned away after people refused to believe at first. That parallel places hardening in a moral sequence. Repeated choices matter, and divine allowance of a condition does not make the original arrogance innocent.

---

## Verse 26:201

> They will not believe in it until they see the painful punishment

**Belief at the Edge of Punishment Is Not the Belief They Refused**

The verse describes a belief delayed until painful punishment is visible. This is not a calm recognition of truth followed by willing obedience. It is the surrender of a person whose alternatives have been removed by the arrival of what he spent his life denying. When the consequence stands before the eyes, the heart may admit what the will refused while choice still mattered.

**Crisis Does Not Turn Refusal Into Innocence**

Quran 10:90–91 — *“We brought the Children of Israel across the sea. Then Pharaoh and his soldiers pursued them unjustly and oppressively. But as Pharaoh was drowning, he cried out, “I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am ˹now˺ one of those who submit.” ˹He was told,˺ “Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors”* records Pharaoh declaring belief while drowning, then rejects the idea that such a last instant can erase the earlier life of arrogance. Quran 6:158 — *“On the Day your Lord’s signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith.”* likewise warns that belief arriving only with the decisive signs will not benefit those who did not believe earlier. These parallels explain the force of until: the timing is part of the moral problem.

Allah is not unjust to a person who had no warning and no fair chance to understand. The crisis comes after a history of rejection, not as the first information the people receive. Their late belief is therefore an admission under pressure, not the repentance of someone who freely turned when the truth was presented.

**The Painful Punishment Exposes What the Heart Protected**

A person may say that he needs more proof, while what he really needs is permission to keep his advantage. Painful punishment removes the protective story. Wealth cannot explain it away, leaders cannot negotiate it away, and companions cannot carry the person through it. What remains is the truth he had placed beneath his desires.

## Verse 26:202

> which will take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺

**The Consequence Arrives Outside Their Schedule**

The punishment does not arrive according to the timetable of the people who rejected it. It takes them by surprise when they least expect it. This is the opposite of their earlier demand that punishment be brought quickly and visibly. They treated delay as proof that the warning was empty, then discovered that delay had never meant cancellation.

Surprise here does not mean that Allah's judgment is random. The time is known to Allah even when it is hidden from the people. The shock belongs to those who built their plans on the assumption that tomorrow would resemble today. Their ignorance of the hour makes their false security collapse, not the divine decree become uncertain.

**Unawareness Is a Moral Condition**

Quran 7:187 — *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, “When will it be?” Say, “That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.” They ask you as if you had full knowledge of it. Say, “That knowledge is only with Allah, but most people do not know.”* describes the Hour as something that will take people by surprise, and 21:40 — *“In fact, the Hour will take them by surprise, leaving them stunned. So they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them”* likewise presents it as arriving suddenly. These passages place the emphasis on human unpreparedness. Allah has warned, but people have mistaken the absence of immediate consequences for evidence that no consequence exists.

**A Hidden Deadline Exposes False Control**

The hidden deadline also limits arrogance. A ruler may believe that institutions will protect him, a trader may believe that wealth will keep him secure, and a denier may believe that he can ask for proof later. Surprise strips away those imagined guarantees. It does not take away what was truly done; it takes away the fantasy that the account can be postponed by human preference.

## Verse 26:203

> Then they will cry, “Can we be allowed more time?”

**The Request for Time Comes After Time Was Given**

The people cry for more time only after the punishment has become real. Their request reveals what they did not value while opportunity remained. They had time to hear Shuʿayb, time to stop defrauding people, and time to abandon the demand for a spectacle. When the consequence arrives, they discover that time was the very mercy they had treated as worthless.

A request for respite can sound humble because it admits weakness, but the verse places it after the decisive moment. The question is no longer whether they understand the warning. They understand enough to want the punishment delayed. What is missing is not information; it is the ability to return to the world and use time differently.

**Late Regret Cannot Reopen the Earlier Choice**

Quran 23:99–100 — *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, “My Lord! Let me go back so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected”* describes people asking to return when death approaches so that they can do good with what they left behind, then presents the request as an appeal that will not be granted. Quran 39:58 — *“Or say, upon seeing the torment, ‘If only I had a second chance, I would have been one of the good-doers.’”* also pictures a person wishing for a second chance after seeing the torment. These parallels show that regret may be sincere as an emotion while still arriving too late to count as the repentance that should have shaped life.

**Time Is Valuable Because It Can Produce Change**

A year is not automatically a blessing, and a long life is not automatically a good. Time has value when it gives the servant room to recognize wrong, repair damage, and build obedience. If a person uses it only to strengthen denial, additional days become evidence of persistence rather than proof of safety.

---

## Verse 26:204

> Do they ˹really˺ seek to hasten Our torment

**The Question Exposes a Reversal**

The verse answers the people's earlier posture with a question. Do they really seek to hasten the torment of Allah? They had treated punishment as a challenge, asking for a sign that would fall from the sky. When the reality of judgment arrives, the same people want more time. Their two requests expose the emptiness of the first one.

**Hastening Judgment Is a Form of Denial**

People sometimes ask when a promised consequence will arrive, not because they want truth but because they want to use the waiting period against the warning. If nothing happens immediately, they call the message false. If the consequence does come, they ask for it to be postponed. Both reactions avoid the intended response: repentance before judgment.

Quran 21:40 — *“In fact, the Hour will take them by surprise, leaving them stunned. So they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them”* describes the Hour as taking people by surprise, while 10:50–51 — *“Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, “Imagine if His torment were to overcome you by night or day—do the wicked realize what they are ˹really˺ asking Him to hasten Will you believe in it only after it has overtaken you? Now? But you always wanted to hasten it!”* connects questions about punishment with the failure to believe before it is seen. The pattern is consistent. Allah's delay gives room for reflection; the denier tries to convert that room into evidence against Allah's promise.

**A Threat Is Not a Toy for the Proud**

A believer must avoid a similar misuse of warnings. Judgment should not be invoked to display superiority or to satisfy anger toward an opponent. The proper purpose of a warning is protection: it tells people what to leave before the consequence arrives. To enjoy the thought of punishment while neglecting one's own repentance is to misunderstand the warning.

---

## Verse 26:205

> Imagine ˹O Prophet˺ if We allowed them enjoyment for years

**Long Enjoyment Does Not Mean Divine Approval**

The verse asks the Prophet to consider a long period of enjoyment granted to the deniers. Years can make a society feel established. Children grow, businesses expand, homes are repaired, and the next generation inherits the same confidence. Yet the length of enjoyment does not change the moral status of what the people are doing.

This is a necessary correction to a common human judgment. People often read comfort as approval and hardship as rejection. The Quran refuses that simple calculation. Allah may allow a corrupt people prosperity for a time, not because their corruption is safe, but because the period serves a wisdom that may include warning, testing, and the completion of their chosen course.

**Enjoyment Can Hide the Cost of Delay**

A comfortable life can make repentance feel unnecessary. Each uneventful year confirms the story that no reckoning is coming. The person who reduced a measure yesterday can reduce it again today, then teach the practice to his children. Time does not merely preserve the wrong; it can make the wrong look like an inheritance.

Quran 3:178 — *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment”* warns that longer life can give those who disbelieve more time to increase in sin. Quran 6:44 — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair”* describes people becoming proud when they receive what they desire before being seized by surprise. These passages explain why enjoyment must not be used as a measure of spiritual health. A gift can become a test when it feeds forgetfulness.

---

## Verse 26:206

> then there came to them what they had been threatened with

**The Threatened Consequence Eventually Arrives**

The verse moves from imagined delay to arrival. Even if the people enjoy years, what they were warned about comes to them. The promised consequence is not weakened by the passage of time. A warning may seem distant while daily life continues, but distance is not disappearance.

**A Warning Is Kind Because It Comes Before Arrival**

Shuʿayb's people treated the warning as something to challenge. The verse allows the later reader to treat it as something to receive. The consequence comes to those who continued, but the message reaches us while a different response remains possible. Let the delay produce repentance rather than the arrogance that mistakes delay for exemption.

A warning can be fulfilled in a way that is known only to Allah before it is seen by people. The believer therefore holds two duties together: trust the promise and avoid inventing details about its timing or form. The Forest people's account is clear because revelation identifies it. Other events require humility. Certainty about Allah's truth does not give human beings unlimited certainty about every application.

---

## Verse 26:207

> would that enjoyment be of any benefit to them ˹at all˺

**The Verse Questions the Value of Enjoyment Without Faith**

The question is not whether the people enjoyed their years. They did. It asks whether that enjoyment will benefit them when the promised consequence arrives. Benefit is measured at the point of final accountability, not only by the comfort felt while life is easy.

The answer implied by the question is no. Wealth, food, influence, and long life cannot pay a debt of deliberate injustice. Enjoyment may have provided opportunities for gratitude and repentance, but if the people used it to deepen denial, the same years become part of the evidence against them. What looked like advantage did not become lasting safety.

**A Gift Can Become Evidence Against Its Owner**

Provision carries a responsibility. A person who receives wealth can use it to honor agreements, feed dependents, and support what is right. If he uses it to increase fraud or silence correction, the gift exposes the direction of his heart. The problem is not the existence of enjoyment but the refusal to acknowledge the Giver and the rights of other people.

Quran 3:178 — *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment”* warns that extra time can increase the sins of those who disbelieve, and 6:44 — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair”* describes abundance followed by sudden seizure after people become proud. These parallels explain why the question is so sharp. Enjoyment is not judged by how much pleasure it supplied but by whether it moved the person toward gratitude or further away from truth.

---

## Verse 26:208

> We have never destroyed a society without warners

**Destruction Is Never Separated From Warning**

The verse states a rule of divine justice: Allah does not destroy a society without warners. Judgment is not an arbitrary strike against people who were given no explanation. Before destruction, guidance comes through messengers and reminders that make the moral choice known.

This principle gives the stories of the prophets their proper place. Shuʿayb did not arrive after the cloud as an explanation of an already completed punishment. He came before it, named the corruption, and invited the community to change. The warning establishes responsibility, while the later consequence confirms that the opportunity was not imaginary.

**Warners Make Accountability Fair**

A person cannot be held to an obligation that never reached him in a meaningful way. Quran 17:15 — *“Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺”* says that a messenger is sent before punishment, and 28:59 — *“Your Lord would never destroy a society until He had sent to its capital a messenger, reciting Our revelations to them.”* likewise connects destruction with the sending of a messenger who recites Allah's revelations. These passages show that divine judgment includes a prior act of communication.

**A Society Is Addressed as a Moral Body**

The word society points beyond isolated individuals. Public corruption can become an order protected by leaders, habits, and shared excuses. A community may therefore receive a warning about conduct that affects everyone: property, trade, worship, violence, or the safety of the land. The warning calls the society to reform its common life, not merely to add private words of regret.

---

## Verse 26:209

> to remind ˹them˺, for We would never wrong ˹anyone˺

**The Warners Carry a Reminder**

The verse explains the function of the warners: they remind. A reminder brings a truth back into attention; it does not invent a new moral reality. People already have signs of dependence, mortality, and the harm caused by injustice, but desire can make familiar truths feel absent. The messenger restores them to the center of thought.

A reminder is therefore both gentle and serious. It assumes that the hearer can recognize what has been neglected, yet it does not soften the consequence of continuing to neglect it. Shuʿayb reminded his people that worship and fair dealing belong together. Their problem was not that no one had ever spoken about right and wrong. It was that profit and custom had pushed the truth aside.

**No Wrong Is Done by Divine Judgment**

Quran 17:15 — *“Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺”* states that punishment does not come before a messenger has warned, while 28:59 — *“Your Lord would never destroy a society until He had sent to its capital a messenger, reciting Our revelations to them. Nor would We ever destroy a society unless its people persisted in wrongdoing”* describes the same principle in relation to destroyed societies. These passages do not portray Allah as seeking excuses to punish. They portray a Lord whose judgment is preceded by evidence and whose knowledge includes the circumstances of the judged.

**Reminder Is Different From Coercion**

A reminder makes the truth clear, but it does not force the heart to love it. People can hear Shuʿayb and still choose denial. That freedom is part of why their response matters. If belief were imposed by irresistible pressure, warning would become unnecessary and moral responsibility would disappear.

---

## Verse 26:210

> It was not the devils who brought this ˹Quran˺ down

**The Quran's Source Is Not Demonic**

After establishing that the Quran is revelation from the Lord of all worlds and that the trustworthy spirit brought it down, the verse rejects a competing explanation. The devils did not bring the Quran. Its guidance, moral clarity, and call to worship Allah do not come from the powers that mislead human beings.

**The Message Opposes the Work of Devils**

Devils invite people toward confusion, pride, and disobedience. The Quran calls people toward recognition of Allah, truthful speech, justice, repentance, and restraint. The contrast is not based on a vague impression. It appears in the direction the message gives to the heart and the social order it demands.

**Revelation Has a Trustworthy Chain**

Quran 2:97 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”* likewise connects Gabriel with bringing the Quran to the Prophet's heart. The two passages support one another: the revelation has a named, trustworthy carrier and a divine source. The denial of satanic origin is therefore not a bare assertion. It belongs to a wider account of how revelation reaches the human messenger.

---

## Verse 26:211

> it is not for them ˹to do so˺, nor can they

**Moral Opposition Shows Their Lack of Suitability**

The Quran commands worship of the One God and calls people to justice. Devils seek to divide worship, inflame pride, and make wrong attractive. A message that repeatedly exposes those practices cannot be explained as their work merely because it speaks about the unseen.

Suitability here is therefore moral as well as metaphysical. The Quran's purpose is guidance, while the devil's purpose is misguidance. One calls the servant back to accountability; the other urges the servant to forget accountability. The two directions cannot be made identical by saying that both involve invisible beings or unusual speech.

**Power Belongs to Allah, Not to Hidden Rivals**

This matters when people become fascinated by claims of hidden knowledge. The unseen is not a free marketplace in which any spirit can provide divine instruction. A believer does not accept a message because it sounds mysterious or predicts something impressive. Revelation is recognized by its source, its trustworthy delivery, and its call to truth under Allah's authority.

The same rule applies to a private voice that tells a person to break a clear obligation. Mystery cannot make disobedience sacred, and a prediction cannot make a false command divine. The protected Quran remains the measure by which spiritual claims are tested, not the other way around.

---

## Verse 26:212

> for they are strictly barred from ˹even˺ overhearing ˹it˺

**The Revelation Is Guarded From Eavesdropping**

The verse adds a specific protection: the devils are barred from overhearing the revelation. The image answers the suspicion that they might listen to a heavenly message, steal part of it, and mix truth with falsehood before it reached the Prophet. The Quran is not an unguarded announcement open to every hidden listener.

**The Heavenly Boundary Has a Purpose**

Quran 72:8–9 — *“˹Earlier˺ we tried to reach heaven ˹for news˺, only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping, but whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for them”* describes the jinn finding heaven filled with guards and shooting stars after they had tried to take positions for eavesdropping. Quran 15:17–18 — *“And We protected it from every accursed devil except the one eavesdropping, who is then pursued by a visible flare”* likewise presents the heaven as protected, with an exception for an eavesdropper who is pursued. These passages give a wider picture of the barrier named here.

**Hearing Is Not the Same as Understanding or Obedience**

The source tradition preserves a further interpretive possibility: being barred from hearing can include being unable to understand the Quran or refusing to obey what is heard. The physical image of eavesdropping remains primary in the verse's context, but the wider moral point is useful. A person can hear sounds while remaining closed to their meaning.

This does not erase the distinction between human unbelief and satanic exclusion. People are addressed with warning and can choose to respond, while the devils are described as barred from the protected hearing of revelation. The comparison simply shows that access to sound alone is not the same as receiving guidance into the heart.

---

## Verse 26:213

> So do not ever call upon any other god besides Allah, or you will be one of the punished

**Exclusive Invocation Is the Foundation**

The command returns the Prophet and the audience to the first duty: call upon Allah alone. The wording is direct because the issue is not a small error within worship. Invoking another god gives a created thing a place that belongs only to the Creator. It divides dependence, fear, hope, and prayer when all of them should return to Allah.

The command comes after the Quran's source and protection have been established. The message is not delivered by devils, and the Prophet is not permitted to turn the audience toward any rival power. Revelation has a single Lord, so worship must have a single Lord as well. The unity of the source demands the unity of the call.

**The Address to the Prophet Teaches the Community**

A command addressed to the Prophet also reaches the community through him. If the messenger is warned against invoking another god, no follower can claim that religious rank makes a person safe from idolatry. The warning is not an accusation against the Prophet; it is a clear statement about the boundary of servanthood. Worship is never transferred to the messenger who teaches it.

**Punishment Follows the False Direction of Worship**

Quran 6:19 — *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the best witness?” Say, “Allah is! He is a Witness between me and you. And this Quran has been revealed to me so that, with it, I may warn you and whoever it reaches. Do you ˹pagans˺ testify that there are other gods besides Allah?” ˹Then˺ say, “I will never testify ˹to this˺!” ˹And˺ say, “There is only One God. And I totally reject whatever ˹idols˺ you associate with Him.”* presents Allah as the witness between the Prophet and his people and the Quran as a warning that reaches whoever it reaches. Quran 10:41 — *“If they deny you, then say, “My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do!”* gives the Prophet a way to distinguish his deeds from those of people who reject him. These passages reinforce the same order: Allah alone bears ultimate authority, while human beings remain answerable for how they respond.

---

## Verse 26:214

> And warn ˹all, starting with˺ your closest relatives

**Warning Is Not Control of the Family's Outcome**

The Prophet can warn his relatives, explain the truth, and show them mercy. He cannot force their hearts or carry their sins. That limit protects the nature of guidance. Family love should produce honest counsel, not a claim that the messenger owns the final destiny of those he loves.

**Family Nearness Does Not Transfer Faith**

Kinship can create love, duty, and practical care, but it cannot perform belief on another person's behalf. Quran 66:6 — *“O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* commands believers to protect themselves and their families, showing that family responsibility includes teaching and warning. It does not say that one person's obedience erases another person's choice.

Family concern can become distorted in two opposite ways. Some people speak gently to relatives because they want to preserve peace, even when silence leaves a serious wrong untouched. Others use religious correction to dominate every family conversation and make obedience to themselves the measure of faith. The verse avoids both. Warn about Allah, not about personal control, and let the message remain higher than the speaker's ego.

The home is also where a warning is tested by consistency. A person who calls outsiders to justice while exploiting relatives turns the message into a public costume. A person who teaches the family to pray, repair harm, and protect one another gives the warning a credible shape. Quran 20:132 — *“Bid your people to pray, and be diligent in ˹observing˺ it. We do not ask you to provide. It is We Who provide for you. And the ultimate outcome is ˹only˺ for ˹the people of˺ righteousness”* links family instruction with persistence in prayer, showing that household guidance is patient work rather than a single dramatic speech.

---

## Verse 26:215

> and be gracious to the believers who follow you

**Mercy Does Not Remove Moral Direction**

Gentleness is not the abandonment of command. The Prophet warns against worshiping other gods, but he also treats believers with graciousness. These duties belong together. A person can be clear about the boundary of faith while speaking to those inside it with patience and respect.

Quran 3:159 — *“It is out of Allah’s mercy that you ˹O Prophet˺ have been lenient with them. Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you. So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him”* describes the Prophet's leniency as a mercy from Allah and links it with pardon, prayer for the believers, consultation, and trust in Allah. That passage confirms that softness is not a weakness added to leadership after the real work is done. It is one of the means by which a community remains attached to guidance rather than fleeing from harshness.

**A Believing Community Needs Tender Strength**

People who follow guidance may still be learning, grieving, or struggling against old habits. Gracious leadership creates room for growth without calling sin acceptable. It corrects clearly, forgives where forgiveness is due, and helps the believer return after failure. Hardness may win a moment of silence, but it can drive the weak away from the very guidance they need.

Quran 3:159 — *“It is out of Allah’s mercy that you ˹O Prophet˺ have been lenient with them. Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you. So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him”* connects leniency with pardon, seeking forgiveness for the community, consultation, and trust after decision. That combination shows that gentleness is not indecision. The leader can consult, decide, and act firmly while keeping the hearts of believers close. A hard heart may call itself strong, but the Quran describes hardness as a cause of people scattering.

## Verse 26:216

> But if they disobey you, say, “I am certainly free of what you do.”

**Disavowal Concerns the Deed, Not a License for Cruelty**

To be free of what they do means to reject the wrongdoing and decline responsibility for it. It does not require the Prophet to act with personal hatred or to deny the humanity of those who disobey. Quran 10:41 — *“If they deny you, then say, “My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do!”* gives a parallel distinction between each side's deeds and each side's responsibility.

This distinction matters in moral correction. A person can say that fraud, idolatry, or oppression is unacceptable without claiming that he knows the full hidden account of every wrongdoer. He can protect victims, oppose the act, and still hope that the one who committed it will repent. Separation from wrongdoing is not the same as delight in another person's ruin.

**Freedom From Wrong Protects the Community**

Quran 10:41 — *“If they deny you, then say, “My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do!”* gives a parallel form of this boundary by distinguishing the Prophet's deeds from the deeds of those who reject him. The principle protects both sides. The messenger cannot be blamed for a refusal he opposed, and the opponent cannot borrow the messenger's obedience as a substitute for his own. Every person meets Allah with a record that belongs to that person.

When this boundary is maintained, warning remains honest. The Prophet can be gracious to believers and firm toward disobedience without confusing the two. He can invite the wrongdoer back while refusing to carry the wrong as his own. Allah knows the difference between a servant who warned and a person who chose to disobey, and His justice assigns each responsibility correctly.

---

## Verse 26:217

> Put your trust in the Almighty, Most Merciful

**The Most Merciful Keeps Trust From Becoming Hardness**

The second name protects trust from turning into severity. The Prophet does not rely on Allah's power so that he can enjoy the downfall of those who oppose him. He relies on the Almighty and Most Merciful, hoping for guidance and leaving room for repentance while warning remains possible.

Quran 3:159 — *“It is out of Allah’s mercy that you ˹O Prophet˺ have been lenient with them. Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you. So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him”* joins gentleness with trust in Allah after a decision has been made. Quran 14:11–12 — *“And in Allah let the believers put their trust”* presents messengers and believers placing trust in Allah while facing opposition. These parallels show that tawakkul contains courage and patience together. The servant is brave before people because Allah is powerful, and gentle toward people because Allah is merciful. He does not need to turn an opponent into a permanent enemy to remain firm, because the outcome belongs to the Lord.

**Tawakkul Holds Means and Outcome Together**

The pairing of Almighty and Most Merciful also corrects the emotional extremes of public work. A caller who remembers only power may become harsh and eager to defeat opponents. A caller who remembers only mercy may soften a command until no one knows what is being asked. Trust in both names produces firmness without cruelty and compassion without surrender.

The pairing of the names is especially valuable when a caller faces relatives who oppose him. Power can feel threatening inside a family because rejection touches affection and history. Mercy keeps the messenger from answering pain with revenge. He trusts the Almighty enough to speak clearly and the Most Merciful enough to leave a door open for those who may still return.

## Verse 26:218

> Who sees you when you rise ˹for prayer at night˺

**Night Worship Nourishes Public Warning**

One interpretation connects the rising with night vigil prayer. Quran 73:20 — *“So recite ˹in prayer˺ whatever you can from the Quran.”* confirms that the Prophet and some believers stood in prayer at night, while Allah knew their limits and made the practice manageable. The connection places private worship behind public warning. The person who calls people to Allah must return to Allah when no audience is present.

Night prayer is not valuable because darkness itself gives a person spiritual status. Its value lies in the unobserved turning of the servant toward his Lord. It trains sincerity, patience, and dependence. A messenger who rises before people rise receives strength from the One who sees him before the community hears him.

**Prayer Anchors the Messenger's Authority**

The Prophet does not rise as an independent reformer who draws authority from his own resolve. He rises before the Almighty, Most Merciful, and receives the mission as a servant. Prayer keeps warning connected to worship and protects leadership from becoming a search for control over people.

If the verse refers to night prayer, the hidden hour gives special meaning to the Prophet's public endurance. The community sees a warning delivered in daylight, but Allah sees the servant who stood when sleep and comfort invited him to remain in bed. Quran 73:20 — *“So recite ˹in prayer˺ whatever you can from the Quran.”* confirms the reality of the Prophet's night standing and Allah's knowledge of the differing capacities of the worshippers. Private worship is not a separate life from public mission; it is its source of steadiness.

---

## Verse 26:219

> as well as your movements ˹in prayer˺ along with ˹fellow˺ worshippers

**Allah Sees Prayer in Its Shared Movement**

The verse extends divine sight from the Prophet's rising to his movements among worshippers. Prayer is not an abstract intention detached from the body. Standing, bowing, prostrating, and moving with the congregation all occur under Allah's knowledge. The physical pattern becomes an act of shared obedience rather than a private performance.

The wording has received several interpretations. Some connect the movement with the Prophet's night worship and his movement among those who prayed with him. Others understand it as the movements of a prayer leader alongside the worshippers who follow him. Another reading gives movement a broader sense, including the Prophet's attention moving across the congregation. The common point is Allah's complete sight of worship and worshippers.

**Congregation Makes Humility Visible**

When worshippers move together, rank and wealth are temporarily placed under one order. The leader is followed, but he remains a worshipper; the follower obeys the prayer's order, but he stands before Allah directly. The verse's shared setting shows that leadership in worship is not ownership of the people who follow.

This matters for the Prophet's public role. He warns relatives and separates from disobedience, yet he also stands among believers in worship. His authority does not remove him from servanthood. The congregation sees a messenger leading, while Allah sees every heart and every movement within that leadership.

---

## Verse 26:220

> He ˹alone˺ is indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**Hearing and Knowing Complete the Reassurance**

The passage closes with two divine attributes. Allah is All-Hearing and All-Knowing. He hears the Prophet's recitation, prayer, warning, and private appeal, and He knows the intention, effort, and response connected with each act. Nothing is lost because it was spoken softly, performed at night, or ignored by the audience.

The word alone in the translation reinforces exclusivity. The Prophet trusts the One who hears and knows, not a rival power and not the approval of people. This completes the command to trust the Almighty, Most Merciful. His power is joined to perfect perception, so His care is never blind and His judgment is never based on incomplete information.

**Allah Knows the Movement Behind the Act**

Quran 6:19 — *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the best witness?” Say, “Allah is! He is a Witness between me and you. And this Quran has been revealed to me so that, with it, I may warn you and whoever it reaches. Do you ˹pagans˺ testify that there are other gods besides Allah?” ˹Then˺ say, “I will never testify ˹to this˺!” ˹And˺ say, “There is only One God. And I totally reject whatever ˹idols˺ you associate with Him.”* presents Allah as the witness between the Prophet and his people and the Quran as a warning that reaches whoever it reaches. The same principle appears here at the close of prayer and mission. Allah knows who conveyed faithfully, who listened honestly, and who turned the reminder into an excuse for denial.

---

## Verse 26:221

> Shall I inform you of whom the devils ˹actually˺ descend upon

**The Guarded Revelation Has a Different Descent**

Quran 72:8–9 — *“˹Earlier˺ we tried to reach heaven ˹for news˺, only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping, but whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for them”* describes the jinn finding heaven guarded and their former tries at eavesdropping blocked. That parallel supports the contrast in this verse. The devils do not have free access to the heavenly source of revelation. What reaches the Prophet is brought by permission through a trustworthy channel, not gathered by hostile listeners.

Influence can be understood as a relation of desire. The devil does not make a truthful heart into a liar by force. He finds a person who wants permission to lie, then offers a story that makes the lie easier to accept. The person's readiness does not excuse the influence; it explains why the influence gains room. Repentance begins by closing that room through truthfulness and obedience.

The question also warns against judging a message by the unusual nature of its delivery alone. The unseen is not a sign of evil, and ordinary speech is not automatically a sign of truth. A claim must be measured by its source, its chain, its content, and its fruits. The Quran gives these measures openly, while the satanic channel is associated with hidden fragments and habitual falsehood.

---

## Verse 26:222

> They descend upon every sinful liar

**The Description Separates Prophecy From Soothsaying**

The description every sinful liar also exposes a false kind of charisma. A speaker may be quick, confident, and entertaining while carrying no concern for truth. The crowd may mistake certainty of tone for certainty of knowledge. The Quran refuses that measure. Character and conduct matter before the drama of delivery.

**Truthful Speech Is a Protection**

The Quran gives a practical contrast to this type of receiver. A servant who values truth admits ignorance, corrects an error, and refuses to turn revelation into a tool for personal gain. Such a servant may still be tempted, but he does not build a home for falsehood in the heart.

Quran 49:6 — *“O believers, if an evildoer brings you any news, verify ˹it˺ so you do not harm people unknowingly, becoming regretful for what you have done”* commands verification when news comes from an evildoer, because unexamined information can lead to harm and regret. The instruction assumes that character affects how reports should be received. It does not license suspicion of everyone; it teaches careful truth-seeking rather than surrender to the sinful liar's version of events.

The verse also teaches how to respond when a report comes from a morally compromised person. Do not spread it because it contains one detail that fits what you already believe. Verify it, seek another account, and consider who would be harmed if it were false. Quran 49:6 — *“O believers, if an evildoer brings you any news, verify ˹it˺ so you do not harm people unknowingly, becoming regretful for what you have done”* makes this discipline explicit. Careful verification is an act of justice, not a failure of trust.

---

## Verse 26:223

> who gives an ˹attentive˺ ear ˹to half-truths˺, mostly passing on sheer lies

**Truth Needs Humility and Restraint**

The right response to uncertain information is not to decorate it with confidence. Say what is known, mark what is uncertain, and stop the report when passing it on would harm people. The person who does this resists the path described in the verse, even when rumor would bring attention or advantage.

The contrast is decisive. The satanic channel thrives on fragments and lies; the believer seeks complete truth and accountability before Allah. Listening becomes worship when it leads to verified obedience. It becomes corruption when it turns half-heard claims into a market of false certainty.

The same discipline applies to public speech. Do not repeat a claim about a person because it sounds spiritually serious, and do not attribute secret knowledge to a speaker merely because he predicts something that later occurs. Verify what can be verified, admit what cannot, and protect people's honor from reports that have not earned confidence. Quran 49:6 — *“O believers, if an evildoer brings you any news, verify ˹it˺ so you do not harm people unknowingly, becoming regretful for what you have done”* gives this principle a direct social form.

---

## Verse 26:224

> As for poets, they are followed ˹merely˺ by deviants

**The Verse Leaves Room for Beneficial Speech**

Later passages distinguish between harmful poets and those who believe, do good, remember Allah, and defend the wronged. That exception shows how to read the present verse. The condemned pattern is not the mere use of rhythm, metaphor, or emotional force. It is the alliance of speech with deviation, falsehood, and conduct that contradicts truth.

The verse should therefore be read against the specific accusation facing the Prophet. His opponents did not use poet as a neutral description. They used it to deny that the Quran was revelation and to place the Prophet among people whose words were attributed to inspiration, imagination, or unseen companions. Quran 52:29–30 — *“So ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺. For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman Or do they say, “˹He is˺ a poet, for whom we ˹eagerly˺ await an ill-fate!”* records the same kind of pressure, contrasting the prophetic reminder with the labels of fortune-teller, had person, and poet.

The presence of imagery does not erase this difference. Human poetry can use metaphor for truth or falsehood, just as ordinary prose can deceive. The category is about source, purpose, and moral role rather than about whether a sentence is vivid. Quran 36:69 — *“We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him. This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran”* calls the Book a reminder and clear Quran, making its function explicit: it brings people to awareness and warning rather than to admiration of a human poet.

Poetry is therefore not saved by a label or condemned by one. It is judged by the Lord who hears every word. The verse protects revelation from a false classification and teaches the community to treat verbal beauty as a trust rather than an authority independent of Allah.

**What the Earliest Readers Said the Verse Was About**

Ibn Kathir preserves several distinct readings, and they are more concrete than the verse alone. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that *"the astray ones follow them"* means the disbelievers follow the misguided among mankind and the jinn — the same view as Mujāhid and ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān ibn Zayd ibn Aslam. ʿIkrimah gave the social setting: two poets would trade ridicule in verse, one group of people backing one and another group backing the other, and it was on that that this verse came down.

On the following line — *do you not see that they wander in every valley* — ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah again reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that it means they indulge in every kind of nonsense, and aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk reports Ibn ʿAbbās to the same effect.

Ibn Kathir then records the exchange that shows how the first Muslims handled being described this way. Imām Aḥmad records that Kaʿb ibn Mālik said to the Prophet ﷺ, "Allah has revealed what He has revealed about the poets" — that is, a poet asking how a verse apparently condemning poets applied to him. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ answered, *"The believer wages jihād with his sword and with his tongue. By the One in Whose Hand is my soul, it is as if you are attacking them with arrows."*

That answer is the key to the passage. The verse is not a verdict on verse-making; it is a description of speech untethered from conduct, and the man who asked was himself a poet whose words were being used in the community's defence. He is told that his tongue is a weapon in the same cause as the sword. Which is exactly the distinction the verses that follow draw — excepting those who believe, do righteous deeds, remember Allah much, and answer back after being wronged. The exemption is not for poets who are pious in private; it is for speech that has somewhere it is going.

---

## Verse 26:225

> Do you not see how they rant in every field

**A Wide Range of Subjects Can Hide a Narrow Motive**

The speaker may appear versatile because he can move through politics, love, tribal memory, religion, and satire. Yet the motive can remain the same: gain attention, protect status, or defeat an enemy. Variety of subject does not prove depth of thought. It can be a way of keeping the audience from testing the speaker's consistency.

Quran 18:103–104 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds ˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good!”* describes people whose efforts go astray while they think they are doing good. That parallel warns that confidence and productivity do not guarantee a sound direction. A person can produce much speech, win admiration, and still be moving away from truth if his work is detached from obedience and honest self-examination.

**A Moral Center Gives Speech Direction**

A truthful communicator can change his judgment when new evidence appears. That is not the wandering condemned here. Correction is a sign of humility when it follows truth. The problem is changing positions to follow appetite while pretending that each new position was always held. Honest revision has reasons; verbal wandering has only momentum.

---

## Verse 26:226

> only saying what they never do

**Words Can Become a Substitute for Obedience**

A powerful description of good can make a person feel close to goodness without doing it. The audience applauds the speech, the speaker enjoys the applause, and the moral work is postponed. In that way language becomes a substitute for action rather than a guide toward action.

Quran 61:2–3 — *“O believers! Why do you say what you do not do How despicable it is in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do”* directly condemns saying what one does not do, and Quran 2:44 — *“Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand”* rebukes those who preach righteousness while forgetting themselves. These parallels show that the problem is not limited to poets. Poetry provides a vivid case because its emotional power can make a false life appear noble, but the same contradiction can affect scholars, leaders, parents, and ordinary believers. Sacred vocabulary does not cancel the obligation to embody the values being proclaimed.

**Practice Gives Language Its Moral Weight**

The Quran does not separate speech from action. A warning should lead to repentance, praise should lead to gratitude, and a call to justice should lead to fair dealing. The person who says what he does not do has broken that connection and made language serve vanity.

The cure is self-application: every command spoken to others must first be heard as a command to the speaker.

Action gives speech a history. A promise kept over time, a wrong admitted, and a sacrifice made when no one applauds can give simple words great weight. Conversely, repeated contradiction can make even a true phrase sound empty in the speaker's mouth. Allah knows the difference between a servant who fails and returns and a performer who has no intention of returning.

---

## Verse 26:227

> Except those who believe, do good, remember Allah often, and ˹poetically˺ avenge ˹the believers˺ after being wrongfully slandered. The wrongdoers will come to know what ˹evil˺ end they will meet

**Remembrance Keeps Creative Speech Accountable**

Remembering Allah often does not mean repeating words mechanically while the heart remains absent. It means keeping Allah's presence, commands, mercy, and judgment active in the choices made by the speaker. Quran 33:41 — *“O believers! Always remember Allah often”* commands believers to remember Allah often, so remembrance is a broad discipline that shapes speech, action, and attention.

**Defending the Wronged Is Not the Same as Revenge**

Quran 42:39–40 — *“and who enforce justice when wronged The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah. He certainly does not like the wrongdoers”* describes believers who enforce justice when wronged and also praises pardon and reconciliation when they are appropriate. That balance clarifies this exception. A response to slander may be necessary, but it must remain proportionate and free of fresh injustice. The believer does not become righteous by defeating one lie with ten lies.

**The Final Warning Returns to the Wrongdoers**

The ending also keeps the righteous from pride. Defending believers does not make a person immune from judgment. He must continue to believe, do good, and remember Allah while responding to wrong. The best speech is therefore joined to a good life and a sober awareness of the homecoming awaiting both the slanderer and the defender.

To avenge the believers after being wronged also requires truth about what happened. A person cannot call every criticism oppression and then use religious language to silence correction. Quran 42:39–40 — *“and who enforce justice when wronged The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah. He certainly does not like the wrongdoers”* balances resistance to aggression with pardon and reconciliation where they are better. A just response protects people without making the defender a new wrongdoer.

This makes the exception a standard for all creative and public speech. Believe, do good, remember Allah, and use talent to repair rather than corrupt. The form may be a poem, a speech, a post, or a story; the moral test remains the same. Language becomes honorable when it stands with truth and when the speaker is willing to live under the truth he proclaims.

---

The exception the verse carves out is the rule the community's poets worked under afterwards: faith, good deeds, the frequent remembrance of God, and the defence of the believers after they have been wronged. The Prophet ﷺ encouraged the same use of verse — the report in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections describes him telling one of the poets to answer the enemy in verse and asking God to support him with the spirit of holiness — and the Book elsewhere condemns the poets' way of saying what they do not do (26:226 — *“only saying what they never do”*). The verse's standard has been the chapter's method throughout: it identifies a practice, names its corruption, and then marks the measure that distinguishes the two, which in this case is the protection of the wronged rather than the pursuit of fame.
