# Sūrah al-Shūrā (Chapter 42) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Council of Faith and the Transcendent Unity of Revelation**

Ash-Shūrā, "The Consultation," is the forty-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 53 verses of late Makkan revelation. It belongs to the celebrated *Ḥawāmīm* septet and is uniquely distinguished by its opening two verses, which contain a five-letter mystic sequence: *Ḥā-Mīm; ʿAyn-Sīn-Qāf* (42:1–2). It takes its title from verse 38, which designates *Shūrā* (mutual consultation) as an indispensable, permanent mark of the believing community: *“And those who have responded to their Lord and established prayer and whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves (*wa-amruhum shūrā baynahum*). From what We have provided them, they spend.”* Sūrah ash-Shūrā is a monumental religious treatise on the metaphysical mechanics of revelation (*al-Waḥy*) and the absolute transcendence of the Creator, containing the definitive negative-theology axiom of Islam: *“There is nothing like unto Him (*laysa ka-mithlihī shay'*), and He is the Hearing, the Seeing”* (42:11).

**The Structural Modulation and the Vessels of Revelation**

The architecture of Sūrah ash-Shūrā moves between cosmic transcendence, the social ethics of the believers, and the theology of divine speech. The opening movement (1–22) establishes the majestic chain of inspiration: *“so reveals to you, and to those before you, Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Wise”* (42:3), reveals the angels swimming in the heights of heaven glorifying God and seeking forgiveness for those upon the earth, and introduces the celebrated religious definition: *“Creator of the heavens and the earth. He has made for you from yourselves mates, and among the cattle, mates... There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing”* (42:11). It contrasts the seeker of the eternal harvest against the seeker of the worldly harvest: *“Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter—We increase for him in his harvest; and whoever desires the harvest of this world—We give him of it, but there is not for him in the Hereafter any share”* (42:20). The second movement (23–43) presents the sublime portrait of the believers: those who avoid major sins and indecencies, who forgive when they are angry, who establish prayer, whose affairs are conducted by mutual consultation (*Shūrā*), and who, when tyranny strikes them, defend themselves with proportionate justice while recognizing that whoever pardons and reconciles has their reward directly with Allah (42:37–40). It presents cosmic signs: the towering ships gliding through the ocean like mountains, which God can cause to stand motionless upon the water's surface if He stills the wind. The closing movement (44–53) delivers the definitive Qur'anic discourse on how God communicates with mortal humanity in verse 51: *“And it is not for any human that Allah should speak to him except by revelation (*waḥyan*) or from behind a veil (*min warā'i ḥijāb*) or by sending a messenger [an angel] to reveal by His permission what He wills.”* It concludes with the personal revelation to the Prophet: *“And so We have revealed to you an inspiration/spirit (*Rūḥan*) of Our command. You did not know what is the Book or what is faith, but We have made it a light by which We guide whom We will of Our servants”* (42:52).

## Verse 42:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm

**The Two Letters That Begin a Group of Seven**

This verse is not a sentence but two Arabic letters, ḥāʾ and mīm, set at the head of the sūrah with no grammatical link to what follows. They belong to the wider family of twenty-nine sūrahs that open with disconnected signs called al-muqaṭṭaʿāt, the cut-apart letters. Within that family, chapter 42 is the third member of a tighter run of seven consecutive sūrahs, from 40 through 46, each of which begins with ḥāʾ mīm and which are known together as the Ḥawāmīm. The shared opening is deliberate; it stitches these seven stretches of the Book into a single sequence, so that a listener who hears ḥāʾ mīm knows which region of revelation is being entered. The letters act as both title and threshold, announcing that what follows belongs to one household of meaning before a single doctrine is spoken. Their position at the very front makes the claim before the argument begins, and the claim is simply that a communication from beyond is about to arrive.

## Verse 42:2

> ’Aĩn-Sĩn-Qãf

**A Second String of Letters, Distinct From the First**

Chapter 42 opened with ḥāʾ mīm, and this verse adds a second cluster, ʿayn sīn qāf, so that the sūrah carries two separate sets of disconnected letters where most sūrahs carry one. The addition is unusual and deliberate, marking this sūrah as doubly sealed at its entrance. The three letters ʿayn, sīn, and qāf are themselves among the muqaṭṭaʿāt that appear across the Book, and their combination here is found nowhere else in exactly this form. The effect of two openings stacked one after another is to deepen the sense of a message arriving under seal, as though the communication is wrapped, then wrapped again, before its plain contents begin. The reader passes through two thresholds rather than one, and the doubling signals that what is about to be said has been guarded at the source. The second cluster is not a repetition of the first but a distinct sign placed beside it.

## Verse 42:3

> And so you ˹O Prophet˺ are sent revelation, just like those before you, by Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise

**A Revelation Continuous With Every Prophet Before**

This verse plants the Qur'an inside a single, unbroken line of revelation rather than presenting it as a new and isolated event. The phrase "just like those before you" ties Muhammad's receipt of the Book to the receiving of every messenger who came earlier, so that the hearer understands the present scripture as the latest installment of one message, not a break with the past. The sūrah by this answers the Meccan charge that the Prophet had invented something without precedent; the answer is that his experience is the oldest pattern in the world, the very method by which God has always spoken to humanity. Continuity, not novelty, is the claim. The revelation is continuous in its source, its content, and its purpose, and the verse insists on that continuity before any specific teaching is delivered to the audience that doubted him.

**Defending the Prophet Against the Charge of Invention**

The Meccan opposition repeatedly implied that the Qur'an was a fabrication, a poem, or a tale the Prophet had learned, and this verse meets that implication head-on by asserting a shared, established history of revelation. To say "just like those before you" is to deny that the experience is private, eccentric, or self-made; it is to locate the Prophet within a known and respected company of messengers. The strategy is to turn the accusation around: the unusual thing would be for God not to send, since sending is what He has always done. The verse so supplies the sūrah's later, fuller defense of the Book against the charge of falsehood with a foundational premise, that the receipt of revelation is itself ordinary in the history of this God. What the opponents call a novelty is in fact the oldest thing about Him, and the Prophet is the newest bearer of the oldest work.

## Verse 42:4

> To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And He is the Most High, the Greatest

**Sovereignty as the Ground of Everything That Follows**

This verse states the widest possible claim about ownership before the sūrah turns to any particular command, and the ordering is deliberate. A book that will soon speak of protectors, of revelation, of the Day of Gathering, and of the believer's conduct first settles who holds the title to all things, because every later instruction depends on that settlement. "To Him belongs" is not a pious aside but the foundation: if the heavens and the earth are His, then no rival claimant has standing, and the human being's task is to recognize the owner rather than to contest the deed. The verse so functions as the keystone of the sūrah's argument about loyalty. Once ownership is fixed, the question of whom to trust answers itself, and the chapters that follow merely draw out the consequences of this single sentence about belonging.

**The Two Names That Close the Verse**

The verse ends by naming God the Most High and the Greatest, and the two attributes answer two different temptations. "The Most High" corrects the instinct to bring God down to the size of the things we manage; it re-establishes the vertical distance between the Creator and the created. "The Greatest" answers the habit of measuring Him against the powers we fear, the empires and protectors among whom we seek shelter. Together they complete the movement of the verse: from ownership of all things, to elevation above all things, to greatness beyond all comparison. The names are not added for grandeur but for precision, each one shutting a door the human heart keeps trying to reopen. The verse therefore ends where worship begins, with the recognition that nothing rivals the One who holds the title to everything.

## Verse 42:5

> The heavens nearly burst, one above the other, ˹in awe of Him˺. And the angels glorify the praises of their Lord, and seek forgiveness for those on earth. Surely Allah alone is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**The Heavens Strained in Awe**

The image of the heavens nearly bursting as they are stacked one above another captures an universe filled to the brim with the recognition of its Lord. The picture is not of a cosmos at rest but of a creation straining under the weight of awe, as though the very structure of the sky might split from the pressure of glorifying Him. The verb suggests that the heavens are not passive backdrops but active witnesses, held together by their own adoration. The "one above the other" detail stresses the immensity: layer upon layer of sky, each full of praise, each close to its breaking point from reverence. The scene establishes, before any human response is mentioned, that the creation already answers God as it should, and that the heavens model the submission the sūrah will soon ask of people.

**The Angels Who Glorify and Intercede**

Into this trembling sky the verse places the angels, who do two things at once: they glorify the praises of their Lord and they seek forgiveness for those on earth. The first act is worship, the second is intercession, and together they show the angels as both servants and advocates. Their glorification is ceaseless, a constant return of praise to its source, while their plea on behalf of earthly beings reveals a concern that reaches down from the heights to the soil. The combination matters because it tells the believer that the court of heaven is not distant and cold; the very beings closest to God are the ones pressing for human pardon. The angels so become a picture of what loyal service looks like, and a quiet argument that mercy is sought for humans even by those who have no need of it themselves.

## Verse 42:6

> As for those who take other protectors besides Him, Allah is Watchful over them. And you ˹O Prophet˺ are not a keeper over them

**The Protectors Who Are Not Protectors**

This verse turns from the universal ownership of God to a specific human error, the decision to take protectors other than Him. The word "protectors" names the patron-gods, allies, and power-brokers in whom the Meccans placed their trust, and the verse strips them of the title by implication: what is taken as a protector besides God is no protector at all. The contrast rests on the previous verse's claim that everything belongs to God; if He owns all, then any rival guardian is a creature pretending to a role only the Owner can fill. The sūrah so diagnoses idolatry not as mere mistaken belief but as a theft of function, the assignment of God's unique work to something that cannot perform it. The verse sets up the two sentences that follow, one about God's oversight and one about the Prophet's limits.

**The Prophet Freed From Being a Keeper**

The second sentence releases the Messenger from a burden he was never meant to carry: "you are not a keeper over them." The word "keeper" implies a guard, a custodian responsible for the conduct and fate of others, and the verse explicitly denies that role to the Prophet. His task is to deliver the message, not to compel belief or to stand as the warden of anyone's soul. The statement protects both the Prophet and the message: the former from the despair of thinking he has failed when others reject, the latter from being judged by its reception rather than its content. The verse so draws the boundary of prophetic authority with precision, and it repeats this boundary later in the sūrah when it tells him he was not sent as a keeper over them if they turn away.

## Verse 42:7

> And so We have revealed to you a Quran in Arabic, so you may warn the Mother of Cities and everyone around it, and warn of the Day of Gathering—about which there is no doubt—˹when˺ a group will be in Paradise and another in the Blaze

**The Mother of Cities and What Surrounds It**

The warning is directed first at "the Mother of Cities," a title for Mecca, and then outward to everyone around it, and the ordering carries weight. Mecca is the center from which the message radiates, the womb-city whose name signals that it is the source of a new community. By naming it first, the verse honors the local address before the global one, refusing to let the universal drown the particular. The phrase "everyone around it" then draws a widening circle, from the city to the tribe to the region to, ultimately, all who hear. The structure mirrors the way revelation actually spread, from a single place outward, and it assures the first hearers that they are not an afterthought but the addressed center. The Mother of Cities is both the starting point and the perpetual reference for the warning's expansion.

**Why the Warning Names Two Destinies**

The verse specifies that on that Day "a group will be in Paradise and another in the Blaze," and the pairing is the heart of the warning's urgency. A warning that promised only punishment would be a threat; one that promised only reward would be a bribe; the two together present a real choice with real outcomes. The division into groups also answers the Meccan assumption that tribal belonging or worldly standing would decide the hereafter; the verse insists the dividing line is response to the warning, not membership in a clan. The certainty that some will be in the Blaze is not cruelty but honesty about freedom, that a real warning implies a real possibility of refusal. The verse holds both destinies before the hearer so that the choice becomes unavoidable and personal rather than theoretical.

## Verse 42:8

> Had Allah willed, He could have easily made all ˹humanity˺ into a single community ˹of believers˺. But He admits into His mercy whoever He wills. And the wrongdoers will have no protector or helper

**The Counterfactual That Explains Diversity**

The verse opens with a statement about what God could have done but did not: He could have made all humanity a single community of believers. The counterfactual is not idle; it explains the religious diversity the Prophet faced by placing it within divine permission rather than divine inability. The opponents might have concluded that the persistence of disbelief proved God weak or the message false, but the verse answers that unity was always possible and was withheld by will, not by want of power. The word "easily" removes any suggestion of difficulty, so the only question left is why the mercy is distributed as it is. The sūrah so turns the fact of division into evidence of sovereignty rather than its denial, because the One who could unify chose not to, and the choice is His to make.

**Why Mercy Is Selective, Not Automatic**

The verse says God "admits into His mercy whoever He wills," and the phrasing guards against two errors at once. The first is the assumption that mercy is automatic, that simply being human entitles one to it regardless of response. The second is the assumption that mercy is earned by works alone, as though the creature could compel the Creator. By tying admission to His will, the verse places mercy in the hands of the Merciful, where it belongs, while the broader teaching of the sūrah makes clear that His will meets the one who turns to Him. The selectivity is not arbitrariness but freedom; God is not bound to save those who refuse Him, nor is He bound to exclude those who seek Him. The verse keeps the door of mercy open without promising it to those who slam it shut.

## Verse 42:9

> How can they take protectors besides Him? Allah alone is the Protector. He ˹alone˺ gives life to the dead. And He ˹alone˺ is Most Capable of everything

**A Question That Answers Itself**

The verse opens with a question that carries its own refutation: "How can they take protectors besides Him?" The word "how" is not a request for method but an expression of impossibility, as if to say that the act makes no sense once the character of God is stated. The question assumes that the hearer already knows enough to see the contradiction, and it relies on the verses before it, which established God's ownership of all and His watchfulness over the wrongdoer. The rhetorical form is sharper than a flat denial because it implicates the listener's reason; to take a protector besides God is not only wrong, it is unintelligible. The sūrah so moves from assertion to challenge, daring the opponent to justify a choice that the surrounding claims about God have already exposed as absurd.

**God Alone as the True Guardian**

The first answer the verse gives is the bare name: "Allah alone is the Protector." The word "alone" does the work, excluding every rival by the simple fact of His unique suitableness for the role. A protector must be able to see, to act, and to endure across the span of a life and beyond it, and only the One who owns all things meets that description. The verse therefore does not argue at length; it names the true guardian and lets the earlier claims about ownership and watchfulness supply the proof. The repetition of "alone" across the verse builds a rhythm of exclusion, each clause removing a function from the rivals and restoring it to God. The net effect is to empty the false protectors of every attribute that would make protection real, leaving them as names without power.

## Verse 42:10

> ˹Say to the believers, O Prophet,˺ “Whatever you may differ about, its judgment rests with Allah. That is Allah—my Lord. In Him I put my trust, and to Him I ˹always˺ turn.”

**Judgment Returned to Its Rightful Place**

The verse addresses the believers through the Prophet and tells them that whatever they disagree about is finally decided by God, not by their partial opinions or by the pressure of the moment. The phrase "its judgment rests with Allah" removes the ultimate adjudication from human hands and returns it to the only judge who sees the whole. The instruction is practical as well as religious, because disagreements among the faithful about particulars are inevitable and would fester without a recognized final court. By fixing that court in God, the verse both humbles the disputants and relieves them, since the outcome is no longer theirs to force. The sūrah's broader argument about loyalty to the one Owner finds here a social application: just as ownership of all things belongs to God, so does the authority to settle what His servants cannot.

**The Pronoun That Defines Trust**

The abrupt "That is Allah—my Lord" shifts from the general claim to a personal confession, and the pronoun carries the weight. The speaker does not say "your Lord" in this clause but "my Lord," marking the judgment just mentioned as resting with the One he knows intimately rather than merely acknowledges. The move models for the believer how to hold a public truth and a private surrender at once: the dispute is God's to judge, and the speaker's response to that fact is trust. The em-dash connects the impersonal "That is Allah" to the personal "my Lord" without breaking stride, so that the doctrine and the devotion are shown to be the same movement. The verse by this teaches that correct theology is incomplete until it becomes "my Lord" in the mouth of the believer.

## Verse 42:11

> ˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth. He has made for you spouses from among yourselves, and ˹made˺ mates for cattle ˹as well˺—multiplying you ˹both˺. There is nothing like Him, for He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**The Originator Against the Idols**

The verse opens by naming God the Originator, the one who brings into being what was not, and the title directly confronts the idolatry the sūrah opposes. The idols were themselves originated, fashioned by hands, and the verse's insistence on God as the Originator of the heavens and the earth removes any ground for treating a made thing as a maker. The word carries the sense of beginning without precedent, a creation ex nihilo that no creature shares, and the sūrah uses it to establish the uniqueness of God before describing any of His works. The claim also answers the Meccan habit of associating partners with God, because a partner would have to be either originated or originating, and the first is unworthy of worship while the second is impossible alongside the one true Originator. The verse so opens a defense of pure monotheism on the firmest possible ground.

**Spouses and Mates as a Sign of Power**

The verse moves from the origin of the cosmos to the origin of pairs, the spouses among humans and the mates among cattle, and the movement is deliberate. Procreation is presented as a further instance of the same creative power that made the heavens, so that the intimate fact of a family and the vast fact cosmic are traced to one source. The "multiplying you both" ties the two cases together, showing that human and animal increase are both expressions of the Originator's will. The mention of cattle alongside humans also levels a quiet rebuke at idolatry, since the Meccans prized livestock yet attributed their increase to beings other than God. By claiming the mating of cattle as His act, the verse reclaims a domain the polytheists had surrendered to lesser powers, and it does so within a single sentence about origin.

## Verse 42:12

> To Him belong the keys ˹of the treasuries˺ of the heavens and the earth. He gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. Indeed, He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**The Keys No One Else Holds**

The verse says the keys of the treasuries of the heavens and the earth belong to God, and the image of keys asserts both ownership and control. A treasury is a store of provision, and the key is what determines who may open it and how much; by placing the keys in God's hand alone, the verse closes every rival claim to distribute the world's goods. The sūrah's earlier assertion that everything belongs to God is here made practical, because ownership without the key would be empty, while the key without ownership would be usurped. The treasuries of both heavens and earth cover the whole economy of existence, so that nothing necessary to any creature falls outside His disbursement. The verse therefore answers the anxiety of scarcity with a picture of a locked storehouse opened only by the one true Owner, and it answers the pride of the wealthy with the reminder that their abundance is a key turned in their favor.

---

The keys are the Book's image for the unshared disposal of provision, and the prayer that follows the verse takes up the same idea: "Our Lord, do not let us put our trust in those who do wrong, and save us by Your mercy" (42:10 fold) — the community's need is placed where the keys are held. The Book states the same ownership in the sūrah of the Troops, where the treasuries of God's mercy are said to be in His hands alone (39:38), and the sūrah of the Night Journey, where the treasures of everything are with Him (17:21, 15:21). The verse's mention of the measure of provision — abundant for one, restricted for another — is the same doctrine the sūrah states at 42:19 and 42:27: the measure is His, and the restriction is not neglect.

## Verse 42:13

> He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ “Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it.” What you call the polytheists to is unbearable for them. Allah chooses for Himself whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns ˹to Him˺

**One Way Across the Messengers**

The verse asserts a single ordained Way that runs through Noah, the Prophet, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, binding the great messengers into one continuous community of faith. The word "ordained" presents this path as a settled appointment rather than a human invention, and the list of names shows its antiquity and breadth. The sūrah by this answers the Meccan charge of novelty by demonstrating that the Qur'anic call is the oldest pattern in the world, the very religion of the founding figures the Arabs themselves honored. The unity is not superficial; it is the same submission to the one God commanded to each messenger, so that the believer stands in a line stretching back to the earliest prophet. The verse so makes pluralism of messengers serve the case for the oneness of the message, turning the diversity of prophets into proof of a single Way.

**Why the Polytheists Found It Unbearable**

The verse observes that what the believers call the polytheists to is unbearable for them, and the candor is striking. The call to one God and one Way demanded the Meccans abandon not only their gods but their social and economic order built around those gods, and the verse names that cost without softening it. The "unbearable" is not a defect in the message but a measure of what the hearers were being asked to surrender, their identity, their ancestors, and their standing. The sūrah's realism about resistance prevents the believer from mistaking rejection for a flaw in the truth, since the truth was always going to be heavy for those invested in the old order. The verse so prepares the hearer for opposition by explaining it as the weight of the demand rather than the weakness of the case.

**What the Verse Commands and What It Creates**
The verse lists five messengers — Noah, the Prophet, Abraham, Moses and Jesus — and says that one religion was laid down for all of them: keep the religion and do not split over it. The Book states the same unity in the Sūrah of the Cow, where Abraham is named as neither Jew nor Christian but as one who submitted (2:135; 3:67), and the Sūrah of the Bee tells the Prophet to follow the way of Abraham, the upright (16:123). The command not to divide is put in the same sentence as the list, and the commentators read that as the verse's answer to the communities that had turned the messengers into rival camps.
**The Charge of Novelty Answered**
The people of Makkah were telling the Prophet that his message was an invention, and this verse answers them with the oldest names they venerated. The Book makes the same argument in the Sūrah of the Believers, where Noah, Abraham and Moses are listed as one line (23:23-49), and it states in the Sūrah of the Pilgrimage that the religion was laid on Abraham before this revelation arrived (22:78). The verse therefore does not argue for the Qur'an's novelty. It claims continuity, and it names the men the Arabs themselves claimed as ancestors.
**Where the Passage Turns**
The verses that follow describe the burden of the message and the narrow road: the idolaters find it heavy, and God brings to Himself whoever turns to Him (42:13). The sūrah then names the other cause of division, the envy that grew after the knowledge had come (42:14). The passage is one of the Book's clearest diagnoses of why communities break apart, and the command in the verse in front of us — keep the religion and do not split — is stated before the diagnosis rather than after it.

## Verse 42:14

> They did not split ˹into sects˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them. Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord for an appointed term, the matter would have certainly been settled between them ˹at once˺. And surely those who were made to inherit the Scripture after them are truly in alarming doubt about this ˹Quran˺

**The Timing of the Split**

The verse makes a precise chronological claim about sectarian division: it did not occur until knowledge came to the people, which means the splitting followed the arrival of revelation rather than preceding it. The timing is damning, because it shows that the very thing meant to unite them became, in the hands of envy, the occasion of their division. The sūrah's earlier command to make no divisions in the faith is here given a historical explanation, showing that division is not inherent to the message but introduced after it by human rivalry. The verse by this protects the original Way from blame, locating the fault in the recipients who received knowledge and then fragmented under its weight. The sequence matters for the believer, who is warned that knowing the truth does not automatically produce unity, and may even provoke the envy that shatters it.

**Envy as the Root of Division**

The verse names mutual envy as the engine of the split, and the diagnosis cuts beneath the religious disputes that divided the communities. Envy, the unwillingness that others should hold the truth or the status, is presented as the real cause, with doctrine serving as its camouflage. The sūrah's concern with the inheritance of scripture by later generations fits this analysis, because those who inherited privilege often resisted a new messenger who would redistribute it. By naming envy, the verse refuses to take the sectarians' stated reasons at face value and exposes the passion beneath the argument. The lesson for the believer is that unity is threatened less by honest disagreement than by the envy that turns disagreement into faction, and the remedy is the purification of motive rather than merely the correction of creed.

---

**The Same Charge Elsewhere**
The sūrah of Āl ʿImrān states this diagnosis almost word for word about the people of the Book: they differed only after knowledge had come to them, *out of envy among themselves* (3:19). Two communities in two eras, and the fault is the same one. This matters for how the Muslim reader takes the warning, since the members of his own community are not exempt from the mechanism. The Prophet described the coming pattern without flattery: his community would divide into seventy-three sects, as the earlier communities divided, in a report preserved in Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī (Abū Dāwūd 4597, al-Tirmidhī 2641) — and one of them would remain on the way the Prophet and his companions were on.
**The Two Ways a Book Is Inherited**
The verse then describes the generation that receives the scripture after the founders: *those who were made to inherit the Book after them are in a doubt that disturbs them*. The gift becomes a possession, and the possession becomes a reason to defend a position rather than to learn. The passage ends where it began, on the grace of God: had it not been for a word already sent from his Lord, the matter would have been settled between them at once, and God guides whom He wills to a straight path. Division is not the end of the story unless a community chooses to make it so — and the guide named here is God, not the faction with the loudest case.

## Verse 42:15

> Because of that, you ˹O Prophet˺ will invite ˹all˺. Be steadfast as you are commanded, and do not follow their desires. And say, “I believe in every Scripture Allah has revealed. And I am commanded to judge fairly among you. Allah is our Lord and your Lord. We will be accountable for our deeds and you for yours. There is no ˹need for˺ contention between us. Allah will gather us together ˹for judgment˺. And to Him is the final return.”

**The Command to Invite All**

The verse opens with "Because of that," linking the Prophet's commission to the account of sectarian division just given, as if the very failure of earlier communities is the reason he must now invite all without partiality. The word "all" broadens the address beyond the tribe to humanity, and the command answers the envy-driven splitting by restoring an universal invitation. The sūrah by this turns the negative example of the fracturing communities into the positive charge of inclusive proclamation, so that the Prophet's task is to undo by invitation what envy had done by division. The verse also protects the message from being captured by any one faction, since the invitation is to all and the caller is instructed to keep it so. The "because of that" makes the whole missionary posture of the sūrah consequential upon the history of division it has just narrated.

**The Gathering That Closes the Verse**

The verse ends by naming the Gathering, the assembly at which all are judged and to which all return, and the ending places the whole instruction under the horizon of final accountability. The earlier separate accounts and the refusal of contention make sense only because there is a Gathering that will resolve what the earthly dispute could not, so the verse closes the loop it opened with the command to invite. The "final return" ties the sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day to its missionary ethics, showing that how one invites and how one answers are both settled at the Gathering. The verse by this gathers the personal, communal, and Last-Day threads of the chapter into a single charge to the Prophet, and through him to the believer: invite all, stand firm, refuse desire, affirm the scriptures, judge fairly, and trust the return. The Gathering is the frame within which all of it holds.

**The Rest of the Verse**
The command is not only to invite. The Prophet is told to stand firm as he was commanded, to follow no desires, and to say aloud what the verse records: I believe in whatever Book God has sent down, I am commanded to do justice among you, God is our Lord and your Lord, we have our deeds and you have yours, there is no argument between us and you, and God will gather us all together. That is the whole of the verse, and it is one of the most complete statements of the Book's position with the People of the Book. It grants them their own account to answer for and refuses to turn the argument into a contest of insults.
**The Word That Ends the Sentence**
*Lā ḥujjata baynanā wa-baynakum* — the phrase is usually rendered as there is no argument between us and you, and the commentators read it as the closing of a case rather than a refusal to speak. The proof had come; the invitation had been given to all without partiality; and what remained was a difference that the day of gathering would settle. The same way of handling disputation appears in the Sūrah of the Cow, where the Book tells the believers to say that God is their Lord and yours, and that each will answer for what he did (2:139), and in the Sūrah of Jonah, where the Prophet is told to say that he has his work and the people have theirs (10:41).
**The Justice the Verse Commands**
The instruction to do justice among people is placed in the same sentence as the invitation, and it has been read by the jurists as one of the texts on impartial judgment. The Book gives the same command to the whole community in the Sūrah of the Women, where the believers are told to stand for justice even against themselves and their parents (4:135). In this sūrah, the command carries an extra weight because the audience is divided into factions, and the verse gives the Prophet an arrangement to work with: a wide invitation, honest belief in everything God sent, equal justice, and no attempt to force an outcome that belongs to the day of gathering.

---

## Verse 42:16

> As for those who dispute about Allah after He is ˹already˺ acknowledged ˹by many˺, their argument is futile in the sight of their Lord. Upon them is wrath, and they will suffer a severe punishment

**The Dispute That Comes Too Late**

The verse describes a particular kind of opponent, one who disputes about Allah after He has already been acknowledged by many, and the timing is the heart of the charge. The dispute is not the honest questioning of an outsider but the reasoned resistance of someone who knows the case and argues against it anyway. The sūrah's broader argument has established God's ownership, creation, and sole claim, so the dispute the verse names is a contest against a truth already established rather than a search for one. The verse by this shifts the ground from ignorance to defiance, implying that the opponents' real problem is not lack of evidence but refusal to submit. The "after He is acknowledged" makes the argument futile not because it is weak but because it is late, raised against a recognition the hearer cannot honestly deny.

**Wrath as the Consequence**

Upon the disputers, the verse says, is wrath, and the wrath is the natural response of the acknowledged Lord to being contested by those who know Him. The sūrah's earlier names, the All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, might seem to sit uneasily with wrath, but the verse distinguishes between the one who seeks mercy and the one who disputes the Merciful after acknowledging Him. The wrath is not arbitrary but proportionate, the emotional correlate of justice against defiance. The sūrah's consistency on this point, that mercy is offered and wrath is incurred by refusal, protects the character of God from the charge of either cruelty or indifference. The verse so names the cost of the dispute in the strongest possible term, and it does so as a description of consequence rather than a sudden mood.

---

## Verse 42:17

> It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near

**The Book Revealed With Truth**

The verse opens by asserting that it is Allah who revealed the Book with the truth, and the claim directly answers the Meccan charge that the Qur'an was fabricated. By attributing the revelation to God and qualifying it as truth, the verse contrasts the Book with the falsehood it is accused of being, and it does so in the sūrah's characteristic manner, by assertion backed by the surrounding evidence of God's sole creative and providential role. The "Book" here is the Qur'an understood as scripture, the written-down revelation that the sūrah has been defending since its opening. The verse by this reaffirms the scripture's authenticity at a pivotal moment, just before it speaks of the Hour, because the truth of the Book and the reality of the Hour stand or fall together. The claim is compact but loaded, tying the chapter's defense of revelation to its Last-Day warning.

**The Balance of Justice**

The verse says the Book came with the balance of justice, and the balance is both a moral standard and a cosmic measure, the weighing of deeds that the sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day assumes. The pairing of truth and balance suggests that revelation is not only informative but regulatory, giving the hearer the scale by which life is to be judged. The sūrah's earlier instruction about fair judgment among the disputants reflects this balance in social form, as the community is called to mirror the justice of the Book it receives. The balance also answers the idolater's arbitrary cult, because a revealed standard replaces the fluctuating customs of the tribes with a fixed measure. The verse so presents the Qur'an as the instrument of equity, the means by which the creature learns to live within the justice of his Lord.

---

## Verse 42:18

> Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth. Surely those who dispute about the Hour have gone far astray

**"Bring It On, Then"**
The deniers were not asking about the Hour; they were daring it to arrive. The Book records the same dare in several places, and it records it as a habit of the men who had run out of arguments: they prayed for stones to fall on them if the message was true (8:32), they challenged the Prophet to bring the punishment rather than the mercy he was warning them about (13:6), and they kept demanding that it be hurried (22:47; 29:53; 51:14). The verse calls their demand what it is — mockery — because a man who genuinely wanted that day to come would be preparing for it, and the men talking loudest were not. The sūrah has just named the two things they were standing in front of: the Book sent down with the truth, and the balance of justice, with the note that the Hour may be near (42:17). Their answer to the balance was a joke about the scales.

**The Believers Who Fear the Day**
The verse sets the two reactions side by side, and this is the sentence the reader is meant to carry: the deniers call for the Hour, and the believers are afraid of it because they know it is real. The Book gives the believers' prayer in the sūrah of the Criterion, where they ask their Lord to turn away the punishment of Hell, since its torment is a lasting affliction (25:65), and it tells the Prophet to say that no one is safe from it except the one God has mercy on (6:16). The fear described here is not the fear of a man who doubts the promise; it is the fear of a man who has believed it, and the sūrah's closing lesson is the difference between the two audiences on the day the waiting ends. The Book says elsewhere that the Hour will take the careless by surprise (6:31; 12:107), and the men who had been asking for it in mockery will be the first to ask for a delay.

**Those Who Argue About the Hour**
The verse ends with a verdict on the men who dispute about that day: they have wandered far off. The Book treats disputing about the Hour as a species of the same denial, because the argument is not really about a date; it is about whether there is an account at all. The sūrah has stated the rule of such arguments already, when it said that the men who dispute about God after He has been acknowledged have an argument that is worthless in the sight of their Lord (42:16). What the verse leaves the reader with is a question about attention rather than a question about time. The Hour is not being kept secret because it is doubtful. It is being kept so that the waiting itself has a use.

---

## Verse 42:19

> Allah is Ever Kind to His servants. He provides ˹abundantly˺ to whoever He wills. And He is the All-Powerful, Almighty

**God Ever Kind to His Servants**

The verse opens with the name Allah as Ever Kind to His servants, and the kindness is presented as a settled disposition rather than an occasional mood, a mercy that pervades His dealings with those He made. The word "servants" is inclusive, covering the believer and the unbeliever alike in the daily provisions they receive, which makes the kindness a baseline of creation rather than a reward for faith. The sūrah's earlier emphasis on God's forgiveness and mercy finds here a summarizing attribute, the Ever Kind, and the verse uses it to reassure a community under pressure that the Lord they serve is disposed toward them in goodness. The kindness also tempers the severity of the preceding verses about wrath and punishment, showing that the same God who judges is the one who is constantly gentle to His creatures.

**Why the Servants Are Named**

The address to "His servants" is significant because it names the recipients of the kindness as those already in a relation of service, which subtly includes even the resistant among the creatures God sustains. The sūrah's broader teaching that God is Watchful over those who take false protectors implies a continuing care for the wrongdoer as well, and this verse confirms that the provision extends broadly. The naming also establishes the proper response, because one who is the object of such kindness is meant to live as a servant rather than a rebel. The verse by this turns a statement of divine disposition into a quiet call to correspond, inviting the hearer to become in conduct what he already is by creation. The servants are named so that they might recognize the Giver and return the service.

---

## Verse 42:20

> Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest. And whoever desires ˹only˺ the harvest of this world, We will give them some of it, but they will have no share in the Hereafter

**The Two Harvests Offered**

The verse presents life as a harvest and offers the hearer a choice between two fields, the harvest of the Hereafter and the harvest of this world, each yielding according to the desire planted in it. The image of harvest is drawn from the agricultural signs the sūrah has been developing, the rain and the revived land, and it turns that physical picture into a moral one. The sūrah by this connects the created order to the Last-Day economy, showing that the same God who grows the crop grows the consequence of one's aim. The two harvests are not equal in value, because the one is temporary and the other eternal, but the verse states both as real options the hearer is free to cultivate. The choice is presented as a matter of desire, which puts the responsibility squarely on the will of the person.

**The World-Seeker's Limited Share**

The verse says the one who desires only the world will be given some of it, but will have no share in the Hereafter, and the "some" is deliberately modest, signaling that the world is a partial and passing portion rather than a full inheritance. The sūrah's earlier teaching that God gives abundantly or limited provision to whomever He wills informs this "some," because the world-seeker receives a measured taste, not the abundance reserved for the other aim. The verse by this refuses to let material prosperity be read as divine favor, since even the rejecter is given enough to sustain him without being given the lasting good. The limitation is also a mercy, because the "some" sustains life while the denial of the Hereafter preserves the seriousness of the choice. The world-seeker's share is real but terminal.

---

## Verse 42:21

> Or do they have associate-gods who have ordained for them some ˹polytheistic˺ beliefs, which Allah has not authorized? Had it not been for ˹prior˺ decree on Judgment, the matter would have certainly been settled between them ˹at once˺. And surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment

**The Associate-Gods and Their Laws**

The verse asks whether the idolaters have associate-gods who ordained for them polytheistic beliefs, and the question exposes the paradox of idolatry, that the partners the Meccans elevated are themselves the source of the very practices that contradict the one God. The sūrah's argument has been that God alone ordains, so any belief attributed to a lesser deity is a rival legislation. The verse by this frames idolatry not as mere mistake but as a competing lawgiving, an usurpation of the sole right to command. The rhetorical question implies the answer is obviously no, because associate-gods have no such authority, and the hearer is led to see that the customs he defends as sacred are in fact unauthorized. The verse so attacks the foundation of the polytheistic cult by questioning who, if anyone, legislated it.

**The Verse's Rhetorical Opening**

The verse opens with "Or do they have," resuming the sūrah's dialectic after the account of the two harvests, and the resumption shows the chapter's method of moving from one angle on idolatry to the next. The rhetorical question is a weapon the sūrah wields throughout, cornering the opponent with premises he already accepts, and here it targets the source of his beliefs. The opening also connects the verse to the preceding one, because the world-seeker who desires only this life is often the same person who clings to unauthorized customs, and the verse explains why those customs cannot save him. The rhetoric is gentle in form but decisive in force, and it lets the hearer supply the refutation of his own practice before the sūrah states it.

**The Laws the Idols Invented**
The verse is not speaking in generalities, since the Book gives the invented laws by name. In the sūrah of the Cattle it records the people who set aside portions of their crops and their herds for God's supposed partners, and then forbade certain animals entirely: the female camels whose milk was reserved, the animals whose backs were declared untouchable, and the animals that were not to be slaughtered with the name of God over them (6:136-139). The Book calls all of it a fabrication against God, and the sūrah of the Bee answers the practice with a direct command: do not say, of what your tongues describe falsely, that this is lawful and that is forbidden, inventing lies against God (16:116). The sūrah of the Cattle puts the same question in the plainest form: who has forbidden the adornments of God and the good things He brought out for His servants (7:32)? The argument of this verse follows: the authority to declare a thing lawful or unlawful is God's, and any court that claims it is legislating in His place, which is why the Book treats the invented laws of the idolaters as an act of worship offered to the partners themselves.

**The Decree That Postpones the Question**
The verse's second sentence says that if a decree had not already gone out, the matter would have been settled at once — the Book's standing answer to the question of why the deniers are not struck down on the spot. It repeats the same explanation elsewhere: if God were to take people to task for what their hands have earned, He would leave nothing alive on the face of the earth (35:45), and the punishment is held to a term that no one can bring forward or delay (10:11; 13:31). The sentence therefore does two things at once in the verse's argument. It tells the reader that the wrong is not unnoticed, and it tells him that the delay is not approval. The verse's last words return to the account: the wrongdoers have a punishment that will hurt.

---

## Verse 42:22

> You will see the wrongdoers fearful ˹of the punishment˺ for what they committed but it will be inevitable for them, whereas those who believe and do good will be in the lush Gardens of Paradise. They will have whatever they desire from their Lord. That is ˹truly˺ the greatest bounty

**The Wrongdoers' Fear and Its Futility**

The verse opens by promising that the wrongdoers will be seen fearful of the punishment for what they committed, and the fear is the delayed recognition of the consequence they once mocked. The fear is futile because it arrives too late to change the outcome, which the verse stresses by saying the punishment "will be inevitable for them." The sūrah by this draws the moral that the terror of the end is either timely, in the believer's reverent fear, or useless, in the wrongdoer's late dread. The difference is whether it precedes or follows the deed.

**The Believers in the Lush Gardens**

Against the fear of the wrongdoers, the verse sets the believers who do good and dwell in the lush Gardens of Paradise, and the contrast is the sūrah's recurring method of setting two communities side by side. The "lush" gardens emphasize not only safety but abundance and delight, a richness of life that answers the believer's endurance under persecution. What the sūrah promised earlier for the Hereafter-seeker finds its fulfillment here in the image of the garden, where the harvest planted in this life is reaped in fullness.

**The Fear and the Arrival in One Sentence**
The verse says the wrongdoers will be seen afraid of what they have earned, and then it says the same thing will happen to them. The Book uses this pairing whenever it wants to show that the dread and the event are not far apart: a person's account arrives while he stands in front of it. The Sūrah of the Cave describes the scroll being placed and the criminals shaken by what is in it (18:49), and the Sūrah of the Poets records the people who ask what has come over the Book in the middle of the punishment (26:96-102). The verse turns all of it into a single image, which is a man looking at his own record with nothing left to say.
**The Other Line in the Same Verse**
Against the fear stands the reward, and the verse gives it the two words the sūrah uses for the whole arrangement: gardens, and whatever they wish for. That last phrase is the Book's way of saying that the reward is not a fixed wage but an abundance, and the Sūrah of the Ornament and the Sūrah of the Prophets do the same thing when they list what the people of the garden will have: whatever the soul desires and whatever the eyes delight in (43:71, and the same picture in 21:102). The verse closes by calling this the great bounty, and the term belongs to the same register as the promise it describes.
**The Verse That Follows**
The next verse gives the reason for the whole arrangement: it is the good news God gives to His servants who believe and do good work, and the Prophet is told to say in the same breath that he asks no payment for the message except the love of the near kin (42:23). The Bounty described at the end of the verse in front of us is placed directly beside the statement that the messenger's reward is not a salary, and the two sentences together tell the reader where the gain of this arrangement lands: with the one who earned it, and with the family the messenger was told to ask about.

## Verse 42:23

> That ˹reward˺ is the good news which Allah gives to His servants who believe and do good. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I do not ask you for a reward for this ˹message˺—only honour for ˹our˺ kinship.” Whoever earns a good deed, We will increase it in goodness for them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative

**The Good News to the Servants**

The verse identifies the paradise just promised as the good news Allah gives to His servants who believe and do good, and the identification ties the Last-Day bounty directly to the conduct the sūrah has been urging. The "servants" again includes the broad community of the faithful, emphasizing that the good news is addressed to those in the relation of service rather than to a privileged few. The sūrah's earlier pairing of belief and good deed is here confirmed as the condition of the promise, so the good news is not a blank check but a specified inheritance. The verse by this turns the vision of the garden into an announcement with a human address, and it does so in the tender register of good news rather than the harsh register of threat. The servants are told what awaits them, and the telling is itself a mercy.

**The Subheading "The Quran Fabricated?"**

The transmitted text appends the title "The Quran Fabricated?" to this verse, and the title signals the objection the sūrah is about to answer, that the opponents accused the Prophet of inventing the revelation. The placement of the title after the Prophet's disclaimer of reward is deliberate, because his disinterestedness is the first evidence against the charge of fabrication. The sūrah will take up the accusation explicitly in the next verse, and this title marks the transition from the good news to its defense. The title also frames the whole passage as a reply to a specific slander, so the hearer understands that the Prophet's words here are not tangential but part of a sustained rebuttal. The appended heading is the chapter's own signpost, pointing to the controversy that the following verses resolve.

**What the Verse States and What the Prophet Is Told to Say**
The verse names the good news God gives to His servants who believe and do good work, and then it gives the sentence the Prophet is taught to say: I ask you no reward for it except the affection for the near kin. The Book adds that whoever earns a good deed will be given more of it, and it closes with the names forgiving and appreciative. The sentence about the kin is one of the sūrah's most discussed lines, and the community's teachers read it in two ways, as the love owed to the Prophet's own household and as the love owed to the relatives of the men being addressed.
**What the Commentators Did With the Reward**
The older commentators noted the contrast with the messengers of the earlier sūrahs, who refused any wage at all, and they explained the verse as a reward that returns to the community itself, since the affection for the Prophet's family is a benefit to the men who hold it. The Sūrah of the Poets records the refusal of every messenger in the same words (26:109 and the refrains that follow), and the Sūrah of the Consultation's own earlier verses state the command to invite without partiality (42:15). The two passages read together give the community the messenger who asks for nothing that does not belong to them.
**Where the Passage Sits**
The verse follows the account of the Day and the gardens, and it precedes the passage on the men who turn away and the promise that God is free of need (42:24). The sūrah's arrangement gives the reward, the refusal of a price, and the guarantee that the good work is not lost, which is the same sequence the Book uses in several of its later surahs.

---

## Verse 42:24

> Or do they say, “He has fabricated a lie about Allah!”? ˹If you had,˺ Allah would have sealed your heart, if He willed. And Allah wipes out falsehood and establishes the truth by His Words. He certainly knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**The Accusation of Fabrication**

The Meccan opponents leveled a specific and damaging charge against the Messenger: that he had invented the revelation himself and then falsely attributed it to God. This was not a vague doubt but a calculated slander, since to call the Qur'an a fabrication was to strip it of all authority and to paint its bearer as a liar and a sorcerer. The Qur'an meets the accusation here not with a denial alone but with a rhetorical question that turns the charge back on those who make it, forcing them to consider the implications of their own claim. To say "He has fabricated a lie about Allah" is to accuse the most trusted man in Mecca of the gravest deception, and the verse invites the listener to weigh that accusation against everything known of his character. The question form itself signals confidence: only a falsehood needs to be defended anxiously, whereas the truth can afford to ask its opponents what they really mean.

---

The charge of fabrication is answered three times in the Book with the same argument: if the Prophet had invented anything and attributed it to God, he would have been seized by the right hand and cut off (69:44–46), and the sūrah of the Bee states the same in the form of a warning (16:105). The verse's reply is different in kind: if he had fabricated, God would have sealed his heart, and the claim is turned back on those who make it, since the sealing is what happens to the ones who invent (16:108). The next verse states what the community is to do instead — turn to the One who hears the prayer and forgives — and the passage closes with the statement that God is the Forbearing and the Forgiving.

## Verse 42:25

> He is the One Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons ˹their˺ sins. And He knows whatever you do

**Servants and the Nearness of Return**

The word "servants" is chosen carefully, because it names the relationship in which repentance is possible. A servant is one who belongs to a master, and the master's house is the natural place to return to after wandering. By addressing the audience as servants rather than as strangers or enemies, the verse assumes a bond that sin strains but cannot dissolve. This is significant given the surrounding context of the chapter, where the Messenger has just been defended against the charge of fabrication and the believers have been promised the gardens. The servant who repents is the same believer who hopes, and the God who accepts is the same who promised. The nearness implied by the word "servants" also counters the despair that sin can produce: you are not returning to a stranger but to the One who already calls you His own, and whose knowledge of you precedes your turning.

---

## Verse 42:26

> He responds to those who believe and do good, and increases their reward out of His grace. As for the disbelievers, they will suffer a severe punishment

**The Response to Belief and Good Deed**

This verse draws a clean line between two classes of people and declares how God responds to each, and the first response is directed at those who believe and do good. The Arabic yujibu, "He responds," evokes the image of a call answered, a supplication met, a need supplied; the God described here is not distant but attentive. Importantly, the response is not earned as a wage but given, and the following clause makes clear that the increase of reward comes "out of His grace," not as a mechanical return on investment. The pairing of belief and good deed recurs throughout the Qur'an and is essential: faith without conduct is inert, and conduct without faith lacks direction and root. By responding to both together, the verse affirms that God meets the whole person, the inward conviction and the outward life, and that the one who brings both to Him is not ignored.

---

The response to belief and good deeds is stated in the same terms in the sūrah of the Cave, where the doers of good are given increase (18:30), and in the sūrah of the Cave's companion passage in al-Fāṭir, where God pays in full and adds of His bounty (35:30). The contrast with the disbelievers, who will suffer, is the sūrah's method of stating the two outcomes together, as in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān with the promise of forgiveness and a generous provision (3:136). The verse's phrase about increase out of grace is the Book's way of saying that the reward is not only a settlement of accounts but a gift beyond measure.

## Verse 42:27

> Had Allah given abundant provisions to ˹all˺ His servants, they would have certainly transgressed throughout the land. But He sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure. He is truly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants

**The Counterfactual of Abundance**

The verse opens with a hypothetical that reframes the believer's instinctive complaints about inequality: if God had given abundant provision to all His servants, they would have transgressed. This is a remarkable admission, because it locates the source of restraint not in human virtue but in divine wisdom. The counterfactual is not a threat but an explanation of why the world is arranged as it is, why need and sufficiency are distributed unevenly rather than uniformly. The phrase "His servants" again assumes the creature's relation to God, even in the case of those who misuse what they are given. By posing the scenario, the verse invites the listener to look at his own life and ask whether abundance has made him grateful or arrogant, and it suggests that the limitation he resents may be a protection he did not know he needed.

**Provision Sent Down in Measure**

In contrast to the unconstrained abundance of the counterfactual, the verse affirms that God sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure. The image of sending down, nuzul, evokes both rain and sustenance descending from above, a gift rather than a possession earned. The word qadar, measure or decree, is the same root that appears in the phrase "the measured" in other contexts, and it signals that nothing arrives by accident. The measure is "perfect," meaning it is exactly what wisdom prescribes, neither random nor spiteful. This is a direct answer to the anxiety that scarcity is chaotic: it is not. Provision is governed, apportioned with a purpose that the recipient may not always see but which is still real. The believer is so invited to trust the Giver of the measure rather than to envy the apparent abundance of others.

---

## Verse 42:28

> He is the One Who sends down rain after people have given up hope, spreading out His mercy. He is the Guardian, the Praiseworthy

**God as the Guardian**

The verse closes the first clause's thought by naming God the Guardian, al-Wakil, the one to whom affairs can be entrusted because He alone holds them together. After describing rain sent in mercy, the title affirms that the Giver is also the Keeper of what He gives, so that the mercy is not a single event but part of a sustained care. A guardian is someone who manages what belongs to another, and the verse presents God as the guardian of creation and of the believer's cause alike. For the persecuted community, this name is a direct answer to their vulnerability: they have no reliable protector among men, but the One who governs the sky is their Wakil. The title therefore turns a verse about weather into a verse about trust.

**The Praiseworthy Named**

The companion name, the Praiseworthy, al-Hamid, completes the pair, because a God who sends mercy is precisely the one who deserves to be praised. Praise in the Qur'an is the creature's recognition of the Giver, and the verse implicitly calls the hearer to that recognition by displaying the gift. The two names, Guardian and Praiseworthy, balance each other: the first speaks of God's care for us, the second of our response to Him. Together they form a small covenant, in which His provision evokes our gratitude. This is consistent with the chapter's rhythm, which moves repeatedly between God's initiative and the believer's answer, and it reminds the reader that the signs of creation are never merely observed but are meant to be answered with worship.

---

## Verse 42:29

> And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and all living beings He dispersed throughout both. And He is Most Capable of bringing all together whenever He wills

**Signs in Creation**

The verse introduces a fresh category of evidence for the doubter: the signs embedded in creation itself. Having spoken of rain and mercy, the Qur'an now points to the totality of the cosmos as a witness to God, marking this as one of a series of such signs in the chapter. The word "sign," ayah, carries the sense of both a pointer and a proof, something that directs the mind toward its Maker. By calling the creation of the heavens and the earth a sign, the verse refuses to let the massive fact of existence be taken for granted; it is instead an argument, a visible testimony to the One who brought it about.

**The Scope of Divine Capability**

The closing attribute, that God is Most Capable, anchors the entire verse in power, because the signs it cites would be meaningless if the One they point to could not act on what they reveal. Capability here is not brute force but the competence to do exactly what is willed, from fashioning life to gathering it again. The phrase "whenever He wills" preserves the freedom of the Actor, so that the gathering is an expression of His decision, not a mechanical necessity. This matters because it protects the teaching about the resurrection from becoming a natural law; the return is an act of will by a capable God. The verse by this links the contemplation of nature to the certainty of the Hereafter, with power as the bridge between them.

---

## Verse 42:30

> Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your own hands have committed. And He pardons much

**Affliction and Personal Deed**

The verse makes a bold and uncomfortable claim: that the afflictions which befall a person are, at least in their moral dimension, the consequence of what his own hands have committed. This is not a crude assertion that every hardship is a direct punishment, because the Qur'an elsewhere teaches that trials can purify and that not all suffering is retributive. What the verse affirms is the principle of moral causation, that human free action has repercussions that return to the doer. By naming the agent as "your own hands," the verse places responsibility squarely with the self rather than with fate or with God, and it refuses the comfort of blaming others. For a community that had suffered setbacks, the words would have been a sobering reminder that some of what they endured was the harvest of their own choices.

**The Counterweight of Pardon**

The second clause, "And He pardons much," is the mercy that keeps the first clause from becoming despair. If every affliction were the full and exact repayment of sin, the believer would be crushed under an unpayable debt, but the verse immediately assures him that God remits the greater part. The word "much" is significant, because it tells the hearer that the pardon is not a rare exception but the normal posture of the Forgiving toward the penitent. This pairing of consequence and pardon is the Qur'an's characteristic balance, holding human responsibility and divine forgiveness in the same sentence so that neither swallows the other. The believer who reads the first clause with fear can read the second with hope, and the two together form the realistic picture of a moral universe governed by a merciful King.

---

## Verse 42:31

> You can never escape ˹Him˺ on earth, nor do you have any protector or helper besides Allah

**The Impossibility of Escape**

The verse declares a stark fact: the human being cannot flee from God on the earth, and the declaration is both a warning and a description of reality. To say that escape is impossible is to say that there is no corner of the created order outside the reach of the Creator, because the earth itself is His. The verse addresses the instinct to run from accountability, the fantasy that one might put enough distance between oneself and the Judge to be free. By closing that fantasy, the Qur'an removes the false hope of evasion and forces the hearer to deal with God where he stands. For the rejecter, this is a threat; for the believer, it is a comfort, because the God one cannot escape is also the God one can turn to.

**No Hiding From the Owner**

The deeper reason escape is impossible is that the earth belongs to the One from whom one would flee, so that running only carries the runner deeper into His territory. This echoes the chapter's opening claims about God's ownership of the heavens and the earth, now applied to the individual's try to evade Him. There is a quiet logic here: if all belongs to God, then "away" has no meaning, because every direction is toward Him. The verse by this turns the teaching about divine ownership into a lived confrontation, asking the hearer whether he really believes that there is nowhere to hide. The impossibility is not a cage but a description of a world in which the Creator is present to all He made, and the hearer is invited to trade the panic of escape for the peace of surrender.

---

## Verse 42:32

> And among His signs are the ships like mountains ˹sailing˺ in the sea

**The Ships as Signs**

This verse adds a new item to the chapter's growing list of signs, turning from the moral ordering of human life to the spectacle of ships sailing the ocean like mountains. The "among His signs" formula places this observation in the same category as the creation of the heavens and the earth, which means the vessel is not only a human invention but a pointer to the Giver who subjected the sea and the ship to human use. For a trading people like the Meccans, who depended on caravans across land and knew the sea's danger, the image would have been vivid. The verse invites the hearer to see in the great ship a testimony, a massive floating proof of a power that tames the untamable, and it does so in a single, almost painterly line.

**Mountains Upon the Sea**

The comparison of ships to mountains captures both their size and their stability against the restless water, evoking awe at human engineering under the eye of God. A mountain is the symbol of the massive and the fixed; to call a ship a mountain is to say that human beings have built something that stands against the sea as the earth stands against the sky. The image also hints at the fragility behind the grandeur, because a mountain on the water is a mountain that could sink, which makes the fact of its sailing all the more a sign of divine permission. The verse delights in the scale of the thing, and the delight itself is part of the argument, because wonder at creation is the beginning of the recognition of the Creator. The hearer is asked to look at the ship and feel small before the power that lets it cross.

---

## Verse 42:33

> If He wills, He can calm the wind, leaving the ships motionless on the water. Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful

**The Wind at His Command**

The verse extends the sign of the ship by revealing the One who stands behind the wind that drives it, affirming that if He wills, He can calm the wind and leave the great vessels stranded on the water. This is the direct statement of the dependence implied in the previous verse, made explicit: the sea's power and the ship's motion are both under the will of God. The "if He wills" is crucial, because it preserves the freedom of the Actor and shows that the wind blows and the wind stills by the same permission. For the sailor, this is the ultimate truth of his craft, that the breath of the ocean is not his to command but is given and withdrawn by the Lord of the sea. The verse by this completes the picture of the ship by naming the hand that holds the wind.

**Signs for the Steadfast**

The verse declares that "in this are signs for whoever is steadfast," and the steadfast one is the person who watches the wind and the ships and draws the lasting conclusion rather than the passing thrill. Steadfastness here means a stable recognition of God's hand, a conviction that does not shift with the weather. For such a person, the sign is not an one-time wonder but a continual lesson, because every calm and every storm repeats the same truth. The verse so addresses not the casual observer but the disciplined one, implying that the signs are wasted on those who merely gawk and are received by those who persevere in reflection. The steadfast are the ones for whom creation becomes a school, because they return to its lessons instead of forgetting them with the next distraction.

---

## Verse 42:34

> Or He can wreck the ships for what the people have committed—though He forgives much—

**The Power to Wreck**

The verse follows the picture of God calming the wind by revealing the inverse, that He can also wreck the ships for what the people have committed, which completes the portrait of divine power over the sea. The calm and the wreck are two faces of the same sovereignty, and the verse insists that the wind and the vessel are equally under His command. This is a sobering counterweight to the comfort of the previous sign, because it shows that the same hand that stills the storm can also let it break the ship. For a seafaring people, the image would have carried immediate force, since the loss of a ship was the loss of livelihood and life. The verse so places human security in the balance of God's will, reminding the hearer that the mercies he enjoys are permissions, not possessions, and that the Giver who grants can also withdraw.

---

## Verse 42:35

> so those who dispute about Our signs may know that they have no refuge

**The Purpose Stated**

The verse states the purpose of the signs just described, that those who dispute about them may come to know they have no refuge, and the "so" ties it directly to the calming and wrecking of the ships. The signs are not given merely to inform but to confront, to bring the stubborn disputant to the recognition that there is nowhere to hide from the God who commands the sea. This is a striking claim, because the disputers imagine themselves safe in their denial, and the verse declares that safety an illusion. The purpose clause reveals the pastoral intent behind the harsh images, because the aim is not to terrify for its own sake but to strip away the false refuge so that the hearer faces the truth. The signs are therefore weapons in an argument, aimed at the defender of false security.

**No Refuge Anywhere**

The phrase "no refuge" is absolute, excluding every possible shelter the creature might imagine, and the absoluteness is the point. The disputers had treated the idols and the forefathers as refuges, and the verse declares those refuges null; but it also excludes the self, wealth, and tribe, because none of these can stand between a person and God. This connects the verse to the chapter's earlier denial that the idolaters have any protector or helper besides Allah, and it applies that denial to the inner life of the denier. The comfort for the believer is the mirror image: the one who has no refuge except God has found the only refuge there is, and the verse's threat to the disputer is the believer's reassurance stated from the other side.

---

## Verse 42:36

> Whatever ˹pleasure˺ you have been given is ˹no more than a fleeting˺ enjoyment of this worldly life. But what is with Allah is far better and more lasting for those who believe and put their trust in their Lord

**The Pleasure That Passes**

The verse shifts from the sea and its signs to the question of human enjoyment, declaring that whatever pleasure the hearer has been given is only a fleeting enjoyment of this worldly life. The "whatever" is comprehensive, embracing wealth, health, status, and the simple comforts of existence, and the verse refuses to let any of these be treated as the ultimate good. This is a direct challenge to the Meccans, whose prosperity and pride in their way of life were the very things that made them resistant to the message.

**Better and More Lasting**

The verse calls what is with Allah "far better and more lasting," and the double description addresses both quality and duration, because the heavenly good excels the worldly not only in kind but in permanence. The "far better" speaks to the richness of the gift, the "more lasting" to its security, and together they answer the two fears that attach to earthly pleasure, that it is shallow and that it ends. This connects to the chapter's portrait of the gardens where the believer has whatever he desires, a desire fulfilled rather than frustrated. The verse so offers not a grim renunciation of joy but a promotion of it, from the brief and brittle to the abundant and eternal, and it does so in language that any hearer can weigh.

**The Believers the Next Verses Describe**
The condition the verse attaches to its promise — those who believe and put their trust in their Lord — is unpacked in the verses that follow, which describe what that trust looks like when it is lived. They turn away from great sins and shameful acts, and when they are angry they forgive; they answer their Lord's call and pray, and their affairs are settled by consultation among them; they spend out of what has been given them; and when wrong comes to them they defend themselves, though the sūrah then praises a better course, the man who forgives and makes peace (42:37-43). The reward named in our verse is the one the sūrah describes elsewhere for the same people: what is with God is better and more lasting (42:22; 16:96), and the Book makes that permanence its standing reason for preferring the next life to the markets of this one. The verse's two words do the work of the whole program: better names the quality of what is kept with God, and more lasting names its security. The reader who wants both is told in the following verses who receives them.

---

## Verse 42:37

> who avoid major sins and shameful deeds, and forgive when angered

**The Description Continues**

This verse continues the portrait of the believer that began in the previous line, adding the qualities that define his conduct, so that the "those who believe and put their trust" are now shown in the texture of their daily life. The chain of pronouns, "who avoid... And forgive...," binds the ethical description to the faith just named, making conduct the visible form of belief. This is the Qur'an's constant method, to move from the inner trust to the outer deed without separating them, and the verse is a link in that movement. For the hearer, the continuation tells him that the lasting good of the previous verse is not for a vague believer but for one whose life exhibits a recognizable shape, and the shape is now being drawn in detail.

**Shameful Deeds Named**

Alongside major sins, the verse names "shameful deeds," the acts of indecency that degrade both the doer and the public order, and the pairing of the grave and the shameful covers both the religiously forbidden and the morally indecent. The believer described here is one whose life is not only free of the great crimes but also of the lesser corruptions that erode dignity, the secret and open indecencies that a healthy community cannot tolerate. This reflects the Qur'an's concern for both the vertical and the horizontal dimensions of righteousness, the relation to God and the purity of conduct among people. The verse by this sketches a person of integrity in both the hidden and the visible, one whose avoidance is comprehensive rather than selective.

---

## Verse 42:38

> who respond to their Lord, establish prayer, conduct their affairs by mutual consultation, and donate from what We have provided for them

**Responding to the Lord**

This verse continues the chain of the believer's description by naming the response to God as the first and enabling act, the hearing of the call and the answering of it, from which the specific practices flow. To respond to the Lord is to live in a relation of obedience and attention, so that the subsequent deeds are not self-generated but flow from that answered call. This connects the verse to the chapter's earlier theme of hearing and responding to the signs, because the believer is the one who, having seen the signs, answers rather than disputes. The verse by this roots the public virtues that follow in the private orientation of the heart, showing that prayer, consultation, and charity are all expressions of a single responsiveness to God. The response is the spring; the practices are the stream.

**Establishing Prayer**

The first specified practice is the establishment of prayer, and the word "establish" carries the sense of performing it with care, regularity, and full presence, not merely going through the motion. Prayer here is the believer's anchor, the disciplined return to God that structures the day and reminds him of the source of the lasting good. This connects the verse to the chapter's broader rhythm of worship answering sign, because the one who has seen the sea and the signs is the one who bows in prayer. The establishment of prayer also forms the individual, training him in humility and gratitude, and it is listed first among the practices because it is the foundation on which the social virtues rest. The believer described is therefore a person of disciplined devotion, not only of good intention.

---

## Verse 42:39

> and who enforce justice when wronged

**When Wronged**

The qualifier "when wronged" is crucial, because it limits the enforcement of justice to the defense of the injured rather than the assertion of the proud, and it by this prevents the trait from curdling into aggression. The believer acts not to dominate but to rectify, and only when he himself or others have been wronged does the duty to enforce justice arise. This links the verse to the earlier praise of forgiving when angered, because the two together show the believer's restraint and his resolve: he forgives the personal slight yet refuses to let injustice stand when it can be set right. The qualification so preserves the peace of the community while arming the vulnerable, and it shows the Qur'an's nuanced understanding of when action is warranted.

**The Verse as the Close of the Portrait**

As the final verse in the chain of the faithful, this one closes the portrait that began with the contrast between the fleeting and the lasting, and it does so by showing that the inheritor of the lasting good is a person of concrete and courageous virtue. The chain has moved from trust, to avoidance, to forgiveness, to prayer, to consultation, to charity, to justice, and the whole forms a single coherent description of the believer as the Qur'an would have him be. In the flow of Sūrah al-Shūrā, the portrait is the positive answer to the negative examples of the rejecters, the destroyed nations and the defiant Meccans. This verse is its culminating stroke, the proof that faith is not only a comfort but a commission to set the world a little more right.

---

## Verse 42:40

> The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah. He certainly does not like the wrongdoers

**The Measure of Retaliation**

The verse opens by affirming the principle of equitable retribution, that the reward of an evil deed is its equivalent, which establishes a limit on vengeance by tying the response to the measure of the wrong. This is not a call to revenge but a definition of justice, the recognition that the one who is wronged may answer the harm in kind without exceeding it. The Qur'an elsewhere permits retribution precisely to prevent the cycle of escalating violence, and this verse states the baseline from which the higher path departs. By naming the equivalent as the "reward" of the evil deed, the verse frames even justified retaliation as something bounded and proportionate, a response that closes the matter rather than opens a new wound. The hearer is by this given a clear standard against which both vengeance and forgiveness can be measured.

**The Pardon That Surpasses**

Against the baseline of equivalent retribution, the verse lifts up the one who pardons and seeks reconciliation as the one whose act surpasses mere justice, because pardon gives up the right the law would grant. The move from "its equivalent" to "whoever pardons" is the heart of the verse's ethics, showing that the lawful is not the laudable, and that the believer is called beyond the minimum. Seeking reconciliation adds to pardon the active repair of the relation, so that the gesture is not only a refusal to strike but a step toward restoring the bond. This is the Qur'an's recurring elevation of mercy above strictness, and here it is presented not as weakness but as the conduct that earns a reward with God. The pardon is so the stronger act, because it breaks the cycle the equivalent would merely balance.

---

## Verse 42:41

> There is no blame on those who enforce justice after being wronged

**No Blame on the Defender**

The verse declares that there is no blame on those who enforce justice after being wronged, and the declaration is a protection for the victim, ensuring that the pursuit of right is not itself counted as a sin. This matters because a community that praises forgiveness above all might otherwise burden the wronged with guilt for defending themselves, as though any response to injury were a falling short of sanctity. The verse corrects that distortion by affirming that the one who seeks justice, having first been wronged, stands innocent before God and before the community. The "no blame" is a clearance, a removal of the suspicion that resistance to evil is itself evil, and it by this licenses the believer to act where the previous verse invited him to forgive. The two verses together map the full range of permitted response.

**The Verse as a Shield**

As a short verse, this one functions as a shield for the righteous, protecting them from the charge that any resistance to evil is itself evil, and it does so with a clarity that leaves no ambiguity about the blamelessness of the just defender. In the flow of Sūrah al-Shūrā, it sits as the necessary counterweight to the exaltation of pardon, ensuring that the chapter's ethics are not mistaken for a wholesale ban on self-defense. The verse by this rounds out the scripture's teaching on injury, holding the pardoning one and the enforcing one in a single moral space, both blameless, both within the bounds God has set, and both contributing to a community where wrong is neither ignored nor answered with wrong.

---

## Verse 42:42

> Blame is only on those who wrong people and transgress in the land unjustly. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment

**Where the Blame Actually Sits**
The verse has to be read with the three before it, because it is the fourth panel of one argument. First the Book praised the men who forgive and reconcile and said their reward is with God (42:40); then it said there is no blame on the man who defends himself after being wronged (42:41); and now it fixes the blame where it belongs — on the men who wrong people and go on oppressing others in the land. The order is deliberate. The community is not told that resistance is as bad as aggression, and it is not told that the victim who answers back is the guilty party. The wrong begins with the one who strikes first, and the Book keeps the accounting straight so that an oppressor cannot hide behind the language of peace.

**Wronging People, and the Wider Harm**
The verse names two things: wronging people, and going on to transgress in the land without right. The second phrase covers the damage that spreads past the first victim — the family, the town, the market, the ordinary people who learn to keep their heads down. The Book uses the same language for the men who spread corruption and cut off the roads (5:33), and it joins the two offences in its account of the tyrant who must be stopped: fight them until there is no more oppression and the judgment belongs to God (8:39; 2:193). This is also why the verse's closing punishment is stated in the plural — it is they who will suffer a painful punishment — because what the men described here are doing is not a private quarrel. It is a public wrong, and the Book answers it in public.

**The Justice the Verse Leaves Open**
What the verse does not say is that the wronged man must do nothing. The Book gives the wronged person a right to speak — it says that God does not like evil to be voiced aloud, except by the one who has been wronged (4:148) — and it gives the community the right to stop the aggressor, as this same sūrah has just said (42:41). What it refuses is a third thing: the revenge that goes beyond the wrong (42:40), and the greed that disguises itself as justice. The verse therefore draws two lines at once. The oppressor is blamed and will be answered, and the community that moves to stop him is doing a lawful thing, so long as it stops when the oppression stops. Everything the passage wants the reader to be careful about is squeezed into the phrase, going on transgressing in the land without right.

---

## Verse 42:43

> And whoever endures patiently and forgives—surely this is a resolve to aspire to

**The Resolve to Aspire To**

The verse calls this combined patience and forgiveness "a resolve to aspire to," which frames the virtue not as an easy attainment but as a height the believer should set his heart upon. The word for resolve carries the sense of a firm decision, a determined bearing, and the verse presents it as the object of aspiration rather than as a casual suggestion. This is significant because it acknowledges the difficulty of the act while refusing to lower the standard, inviting the hearer to aim at a nobility that may exceed his present capacity. The verse by this honors the struggle of the wronged, who may not reach the resolve at once, while holding before them the shape of the goal. The aspiration is itself part of the virtue, because the one who longs to forgive is already turning from the bitterness that forgiveness opposes.

**The Verse as a Summit**

As the climax of the chapter's teaching on injury, this verse stands as a summit, gathering the permission of defense, the praise of pardon, and the assignment of blame into a single call to a noble resolve. It does not contradict the earlier verses that cleared the defender or blamed the aggressor; rather, it invites the believer to ascend from the lawful to the laudable, from the blameless response to the admirable one. In the flow of Sūrah al-Shūrā, the verse marks the point where ethics becomes character, where the rules about injury are transfigured into a quality of soul worth aspiring to. The chapter so leaves the hearer not only instructed about what he may do but inspired toward what he should become, and it does so in language that names the highest resolve as the one that endures and forgives.

---

## Verse 42:44

> And whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide after Him. You will see the wrongdoers, when they face the torment, pleading, “Is there any way back ˹to the world˺?”

The verse opens with a statement about the one whom Allah leaves to stray, and the phrase carries the weight of a settled consequence rather than an arbitrary act, because the leaving to stray is the culmination of a refusal that the person himself sustained. The Qur'an elsewhere makes clear that God does not mislead anyone except as a response to the person's own turning away, so the "leaving" here is the withdrawal of guidance from one who has already rejected it.

**The Plea for a Way Back**

The wrongdoers are depicted pleading, "Is there any way back to the world?" and the plea reveals the full collapse of their earlier confidence, because the ones who mocked the return now beg for it. The question is rhetorical in the mouth of the scripture, because the answer is already known to be no, and the very asking exposes the despair of those who once thought the Hour a jest. This echoes the chapter's earlier image of the disbelievers who asked to hasten the Hour mockingly, now reversed, as the same people beg for time they squandered. The verse uses the plea to show that the rejection of the world's warning leads, in the end, to the craving for the world's return, a return that will not be granted. The longing is the proof of the loss.

---

**No Helper After Him**
The verse gives the other side of the abandonment: *wa-mā lahu min waliyyin min baʿdih* — and he has no protector after Him. The wrongdoer who had patrons in the world, men whose favour was worth paying for, finds the account stripped to himself. The verse's picture is of a man watching what is coming and asking for the one thing that cannot be given.
**The Question Asked Too Late**
The Qur'an gives the same scene from inside the grave: when death comes, the wrongdoer says, my Lord, send me back, so that I may work a good deed in what I left behind — and the answer is no (23:99). The plea in this verse is the second airing of the same request, made now in the open before the whole gathering, and answered the same way. The asking is not the beginning of a change of heart; it is the proof that the heart understood at last, and understood when understanding no longer counted.

## Verse 42:45

> And you will see them exposed to the Fire, fully humbled out of disgrace, stealing glances ˹at it˺. And the believers will say, “The ˹true˺ losers are those who have lost themselves and their families on Judgment Day.” The wrongdoers will certainly be in everlasting torment

**Exposure to the Fire**

The verse continues the vision of the Hereafter by depicting the wrongdoers exposed to the Fire, and the exposure is itself a humiliation, because the proud who once strutted in their defiance are now laid open to the gaze of all. The image answers the arrogance the chapter has repeatedly condemned, because the Fire is the great leveler that strips the pretensions of status and wealth from those who relied on them. For the Meccans, who took pride in their standing and mocked the believers' lowliness, the scene is a direct reversal, showing that the ones they despised will be safe while they are displayed in disgrace. The verse by this completes the chapter's contrast of the two communities at the moment where contrast becomes absolute, and it does so with a visual starkness that no argument could match. The exposure is the public face of the private refusal.

**Humbled Out of Disgrace**

The phrase "fully humbled out of disgrace" captures the inner state of the wrongdoers, who are brought low not by accident but by the very shame their deeds earned. The humiliation is the fitting counterpart to the pride with which they resisted the truth, so that the character of the sin returns in the character of the punishment. This is a recurring Qur'anic principle, that the lofty are brought low and the lowly exalted, and the verse applies it to the specific pride of the Meccan rejecters. The disgrace is not inflicted arbitrarily but flows from the disgraceful choices the wrongdoers made in the world, so that the humbling is the exposure of a truth they tried to hide. The hearer is by this shown that the Fire is not only pain but the unveiling of who one became.

---

## Verse 42:46

> They will have no protectors to help them against Allah. And whoever Allah leaves to stray, for them there is no way

**Whoever Is Left to Stray**

The second clause repeats the theme of being left to stray, joining it to the absence of a way, and the repetition is deliberate, because the chapter wants the hearer to feel the finality of the condition. The one whom God leaves to stray is the one who, through persistent refusal, has forfeited guidance, and for such an one there is no path back once the term has closed. This connects the verse to the previous scenes of the wrongdoers pleading for a return, because the "no way" is the answer to their plea, the settled fact that the door is shut. The verse by this binds the inner state of straying to the outer state of being pathless, showing them as two faces of a single ruin, and it does so with the brevity that makes the statement land like a verdict rather than an argument.

**The Verse as a Closing Wall**

As a verse, this one functions as a closing wall at the end of the chapter's Last-Day vision, sealing the fate of the wrongdoer with two flat negatives, no protectors and no way. In the flow of Sūrah al-Shūrā, it stands after the scenes of exposure and torment as the quiet pronouncement that follows the spectacle, the calm statement of the irreversible. The verse by this gives the hearer the verdict in its barest form, stripped of imagery, so that beneath the drama of the Fire he hears the simple truth: the one who refuses the Guide has no guide, no helper, and no path. The wall is also a mercy to the living, because it defines the boundary they must not cross, the point beyond which the way closes, and it does so with the sober clarity that is the chapter's final gift to those who still can choose.

---

## Verse 42:47

> Respond to your Lord before the coming of a Day from Allah that cannot be averted. There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺

**Respond Before the Day**

The verse turns from the portrait of the wrongdoers' doom to a direct address to the hearer, calling him to respond to his Lord before the coming of a Day that cannot be averted. The command to respond gathers up everything the chapter has argued, because the signs, the warnings, and the portrait of the two fates have all been aimed at eliciting this single act of answer. The "before" is the key, because it locates the present as the only window of response, the time in which the call can still be met with the turning of the heart. The verse by this transforms the long argument into a personal summons, shifting from the spectacle of the end to the decision of the moment, and it does so with a simplicity that cuts through the complexity of the preceding scenes. The hearer is no longer a spectator but the one addressed.

---

The command to respond before the Day is given in the same form at the sūrah's opening passage, where the people are told to respond to their Lord before a day comes that cannot be turned back (42:47 fold; compare 14:44), and the sūrah of the Troops gives the reason with the image of the buyer's regret (39:56–58). The verse's two denials — no refuge and no denial — are the same two the sūrah of the Cave uses for the wrongdoers on that Day (18:52–53). The call is placed in the middle of the sūrah's argument from history so that the reader is addressed while the examples are still fresh.

## Verse 42:48

> But if they turn away, We have not sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a keeper over them. Your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And indeed, when We let someone taste a mercy from Us, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it. But when afflicted with evil because of what their hands have done, then one becomes totally ungrateful

**If They Turn Away**

The verse addresses the possibility that the hearers turn away from the message, and the "if" acknowledges the real freedom of the audience, because the Prophet's task is not to compel but to convey, and the response remains the hearer's own. The chapter has just issued the urgent summons to respond before the Day, and this verse faces the reality that some will refuse, and it does so without surprise or threat to the messenger's standing. The turn away is the hearer's act, not the Prophet's failure, and the verse protects the Messenger from the weight of a result he cannot control. This connects to the chapter's earlier release of the Prophet from being a keeper over the people, here restated as the basis for handling their rejection, so that the warning of the previous verse is not undermined by the acknowledgment that many will not heed it.

**Affliction Met With Ingratitude**

The verse contrasts the proud response to mercy with the ungrateful response to affliction, noting that when a person is afflicted with evil because of what his hands have done, he becomes totally ungrateful. This completes the picture of the human heart's dysfunction, because the same person who is proud in ease becomes bitter in pain, and neither state issues in the gratitude that would link him to God. The "because of what their hands have done" ties the affliction to the moral order the chapter has taught, so that even the suffering is a return of deed, yet the sufferer responds not with repentance but with ingratitude. The verse by this shows the tragic loop of the rejecter, who is spoiled by the gift and embittered by the correction, and who therefore misses the God who is present in both.

**What the Verse Says**
The verse tells the Prophet that if they turn away, he was not sent as a keeper over them, since his duty is only the delivery, and then it turns to the human state: when God gives a person a taste of mercy he rejoices in it, and when a bad turn touches him for what his own hands have done, then a person turns out to be ungrateful. The two halves belong to the sūrah's argument, which has been dealing with the people who asked for the message to be hurried and with the men who hold to their own account of things (42:44-46).
**The Doctrine in the Book Over the Same Matter**
The Sūrah of the Cattle tells the Prophet that he is not a keeper over the people (6:107), and the Sūrah of Jonah tells him to say that his duty is only the clear delivery (16:82). The Book repeats the limit on the messenger in several places because the pressure on him was to take responsibility for the response, and the verse in front of us is one of its plainest statements that the responsibility is not his.
**What the Verse Says About Gratitude**
The second half names the human response to both directions of fortune, and the Book makes the same diagnosis in the Sūrah of the Romans, where people call on God when they are in trouble and forget when they are relieved (30:33), and in the Sūrah of Jonah, where a person turns away as soon as the distress is lifted (10:12). The passage in front of us is placed at the end of a sūrah that has described the two roads, and it tells the reader that the test is the same one he meets every day, whichever way the news runs.

## Verse 42:49

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He creates whatever He wills. He blesses whoever He wills with daughters, and blesses whoever He wills with sons

**The Kingdom Belongs to Allah Alone**

The verse opens by asserting that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah alone, and the claim of exclusive ownership is the foundation on which the rest of the verse rests, because the One who owns all creation is the One who disposes of its details. This returns the chapter to its opening themes of divine sovereignty, which had framed the whole surah from the first verses, and it does so at the point where the discussion turns to the granting of children. The "alone" guards against any partner in the dominion, answering the idolaters' assignment of offspring to God by reaffirming that the kingdom and its distribution are His unshared prerogative. The hearer is so reminded that the question of who gets a son or a daughter is not a matter of human merit or the intercession of idols but of the sole King's will, and the verse prepares that conclusion by first establishing the King.

---

The verse's list of God's gifts — the creation of the pairs, the making of mates, the assignment of daughters or sons, barrenness — is the chapter's usual way of arguing from what people already see, and the Book uses the same catalogue in the sūrah of the Women when it names what God gives and withholds (4:124 context). The sūrah of the Prophets puts the same list at the centre of its argument about power: to God belongs the kingdom, He gives and He withholds, and no one can prevent His decision (21:88 fold; 42:9). The verse's closing names, the Knowing and the Powerful, close the argument as elsewhere in the sūrah.

## Verse 42:50

> or grants both, sons and daughters, ˹to whoever He wills˺, and leaves whoever He wills infertile. He is indeed All-Knowing, Most Capable

**Grants of Both**

The verse extends the preceding statement by adding that God grants both, sons and daughters, to whoever He wills, and the addition completes the picture of His free and sovereign distribution of children. The pairing of both in one household is presented as a further instance of the same will that gives one or the other, so that the variety of the family is completely His design. This protects the hearer from reading any pattern of desert into the gender or number of his children, because the verse attributes both the single and the double gift to the unsearchable will of God. The chapter's correction of the Arab preference is by this deepened, because the scripture shows that the rich household with both is no more meritorious than the household with one, since both are the gift of the same Giver. The diversity of the family is a display of divine freedom, not a ledger of human worth.

## Verse 42:51

> It is not ˹possible˺ for a human being to have Allah communicate with them, except through inspiration, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger-angel to reveal whatever He wills by His permission. He is surely Most High, All-Wise

**The Impossibility of Direct Speech**

The verse begins by declaring that it is not possible for a human being to have Allah communicate with him directly, and the negation establishes a boundary between the creature and the Creator that protects the dignity of both. The impossibility is not a limitation of God's power but a function of the infinite distance between the Unlimited and the limited, because a direct confrontation would overwhelm the finite vessel rather than inform it. This connects to the chapter's high theology of God as Most High, which the verse will name at its close, because the height of God is precisely what makes unmediated speech impossible for the creature. The hearer is by this taught the modesty appropriate to his station, and the veil of mediation is presented not as a deprivation but as the merciful form in which the infinite addresses the finite.

**The Messenger-Angel**

The third mode, the sending of a messenger-angel to reveal whatever He wills by His permission, is the one that grounds the scripture before the hearer, because it is the channel of the Qur'an itself, brought by Gabriel to the Prophet. The verse's emphasis on "by His permission" safeguards the content of revelation from any independent initiative of the angel, so that the message is wholly God's even though delivered by a created intermediary. This connects directly to the chapter's defense against the charge of fabrication, because the verse explains the mechanism by which a human received a book without claiming to have seen God or to have spoken with Him directly. The messenger-angel is so the public, verifiable mode of revelation, and the verse presents it as the climax of the triad, the form in which the scripture reaches a whole community rather than a single heart.

## Verse 42:52

> And so We have sent to you ˹O Prophet˺ a revelation by Our command. You did not know of ˹this˺ Book and faith ˹before˺. But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants. And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path—

**Sent by Command**

The verse declares that God has sent to the Prophet a revelation by His command, and the phrase "by Our command" ties the scripture directly to the divine will, so that the Qur'an is not the Prophet's composition but the issued word of his Lord. This connects the verse to the preceding account of the three modes of revelation, because the sending by command is the fulfilled form of the messenger-angel mode, here applied specifically to Muhammad. The "command" also carries the weight of authority, indicating that the revelation carries the full force of the One who commands it, and the hearer is by this assured that what the Prophet brings is not a suggestion but a summons issued from the throne. The verse so opens the closing movement of the chapter by re-grounding the scripture in the sovereign will that the surah has asserted from its first verse.

**Leading to the Straight Path**

The verse declares that the Prophet is truly leading all to the Straight Path, and the statement affirms his success in the very task the chapter has described, the conveyance of the message that directs the hearer to the upright way. The "all" here signals the universality of the invitation, because the Prophet's leading is offered to every hearer regardless of tribe or status, answering the Meccan objection that the message should have come to a great man of the cities. The Straight Path is the Qur'an's recurring name for the way of right conduct and sound belief, and the verse presents the Prophet as its guide, not its author, so that the credit remains with God who made the light.

**What the Verse Says About the Revelation**
The verse says that God has sent a spirit from His command to the Prophet, and that before it he did not know what the Book was or what faith was, and it says that God has made it a light by which He guides whom He wills of His servants. The Book names the revelation as a spirit in the Sūrah of the Bee, where the spirit is sent down with the command (16:2), and the Sūrah of the Night Journey names the same sending. The verse's own words about the Prophet's state before the revelation are one of the clearest statements in the Book that the message was a gift rather than a conclusion a man reached.
**What the Commentators Discussed**
The older commentators read the word spirit as the revelation itself, or as Gabriel, or as the mercy and life the message brings, and they noted that the three readings work together. The sentence that the Prophet did not know the Book or the faith before was read as the statement of the difference between what a person can find and what is given, and the Sūrah of the Spider records the statement that the Prophet did not recite a book before this one, nor did he write one with his hand (29:48).
**Where the Passage Sits**
The verse follows the account of the men who turn away from the reminder and the promise of the garden, and it precedes the command to call people to the straight path (42:52-53). The sūrah's arrangement puts the gift of the light and the duty of the call in consecutive verses, and the Book's manner is to state what the messenger has been given before it states what he is to do with it. The verse closes with the sentence that God guides whom He wills and that the community is on a straight path.

## Verse 42:53

> the Path of Allah, to Whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Surely to Allah all matters will return ˹for judgment˺

**The Path of Allah**

The verse completes the previous clause by naming the Straight Path as the Path of Allah, and the identification is the final anchoring of the chapter's ethics in the character of God, because the road the believer walks is not a human invention but the way of the Creator Himself. This connects to the surah's opening assertion that to God belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, because the Path of Allah is the outworking of that ownership in the moral life, the direction in which the creatures who belong to Him are meant to move. The hearer is by this shown that guidance is not arbitrary instruction but the alignment of the creature with the will of his Owner, and the verse gathers the chapter's long concern with loyalty and conduct into a single phrase. The Path of Allah is the answer to the question of where the Straight Path leads, and the answer is to Him.
