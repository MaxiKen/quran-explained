# Sūrah al-Shūrā (Chapter 42) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Council of Faith and the Transcendent Unity of Revelation**

Ash-Shūrā, "The Consultation," is the forty-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 53 verses of late Makkan revelation. It belongs to the celebrated *Ḥawāmīm* septet and is uniquely distinguished by its opening two verses, which contain a five-letter mystic sequence: *Ḥā-Mīm; ʿAyn-Sīn-Qāf* (42:1–2). It takes its title from verse 38, which designates *Shūrā* (mutual consultation) as an indispensable, permanent mark of the believing community: *“And those who have responded to their Lord and established prayer and whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves (*wa-amruhum shūrā baynahum*). From what We have provided them, they spend.”* Sūrah ash-Shūrā is a monumental religious treatise on the metaphysical mechanics of revelation (*al-Waḥy*) and the absolute transcendence of the Creator, containing the definitive negative-theology axiom of Islam: *“There is nothing like unto Him (*laysa ka-mithlihī shay'*), and He is the Hearing, the Seeing”* (42:11).

**The Structural Modulation and the Vessels of Revelation**

The architecture of Sūrah ash-Shūrā moves between cosmic transcendence, the social ethics of the believers, and the theology of divine speech. The opening movement (1–22) establishes the majestic chain of inspiration: *“so reveals to you, and to those before you, Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Wise”* (42:3), reveals the angels swimming in the heights of heaven glorifying God and seeking forgiveness for those upon the earth, and introduces the celebrated religious definition: *“Creator of the heavens and the earth. He has made for you from yourselves mates, and among the cattle, mates... There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing”* (42:11). It contrasts the seeker of the eternal harvest against the seeker of the worldly harvest: *“Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter—We increase for him in his harvest; and whoever desires the harvest of this world—We give him of it, but there is not for him in the Hereafter any share”* (42:20). The second movement (23–43) presents the sublime portrait of the believers: those who avoid major sins and indecencies, who forgive when they are angry, who establish prayer, whose affairs are conducted by mutual consultation (*Shūrā*), and who, when tyranny strikes them, defend themselves with proportionate justice while recognizing that whoever pardons and reconciles has their reward directly with Allah (42:37–40). It presents cosmic signs: the towering ships gliding through the ocean like mountains, which God can cause to stand motionless upon the water's surface if He stills the wind. The closing movement (44–53) delivers the definitive Qur'anic discourse on how God communicates with mortal humanity in verse 51: *“And it is not for any human that Allah should speak to him except by revelation (*waḥyan*) or from behind a veil (*min warā'i ḥijāb*) or by sending a messenger [an angel] to reveal by His permission what He wills.”* It concludes with the personal revelation to the Prophet: *“And so We have revealed to you an inspiration/spirit (*Rūḥan*) of Our command. You did not know what is the Book or what is faith, but We have made it a light by which We guide whom We will of Our servants”* (42:52).

## Verse 42:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm

**The Letters That Begin the Sūrah**
The sūrah opens with two sets of separated letters, ḥā mīm and ʿayn sīn qāf, and they are not an accident of this chapter. Ḥā mīm opens six other sūrahs of this part of the Book — the Sūrah of the Forgiver, the Fussilat, the Ornaments of Gold, the Smoke, the Kneeling and the Sand-Dunes (40:1; 41:1; 43:1; 44:1; 45:1; 46:1) — and the group is known among the scholars as the ḥawāmīm, the sūrahs that a reader of the Qur'an meets as a family. The men who first heard these letters were the masters of the language the revelation was being sent in, and the letters are drawn from the same alphabet they used every day: the Book takes the raw material of their speech and then says the thing they cannot match. The pattern of the Book is that the letters are followed by a statement about itself, which is exactly what happens two verses down, where the same sūrah says that God, the Almighty, the All-Wise, sends revelation to the Prophet and sent it to those before him (42:3).

**What the Scholars Said About Them**
The early teachers took two positions that have stayed side by side. One reads the letters as something whose meaning is left with God, and the scholars who hold this place them in the class of verses the Book itself calls unclear in meaning, whose interpretation is reserved for God (3:7). The other reads them as a device of attention and challenge: the letters announce that the Book is made of the letters, and then the Book goes on to say what no one has produced from them. Al-Ṭabarī collected the reports on both, and his own position was to say that the letters have a meaning, that God knows it, and that the community should not guess its way into it. The reader is left with what the Book intended to leave him: a text that begins with the alphabet and then asks to be understood, not one that begins with a puzzle and then asks to be decoded. The two names that close the passage, the Almighty and the All-Wise, give the sūrah its tone. Power is in the revelation, and wisdom is in what it says.

## Verse 42:2

> ’Aĩn-Sĩn-Qãf

**A Second Set of Letters**
The second verse adds ʿayn sīn qāf to the ḥā mīm before it, and this doubled opening is unique to this sūrah: no other chapter of the Book is prefaced by two separate groups of separated letters. The early scholars offered several readings of these three. Some reports take them as abbreviations of divine names — the All-Knowing, the All-Hearing, the All-Powerful — and others take the whole set as a name of the sūrah or as an opening that draws the hearer's attention to what follows. Al-Qurṭubī gathered these readings and noted what they have in common: the letters work on the ear before they deliver a content, which is why the Arabic tongue cannot reproduce them in translation without keeping the sounds bare. What follows immediately in the next verse is the sentence the letters prepare for: revelation comes down from the Almighty, the All-Wise. Read that way, the second group is doing what the first one did — it makes the audience listen before it teaches them.

**The Letters and the Charge of Dressing Up the Book**
The letters also answer a complaint the sūrah will handle in more detail later, when the Book reports the men who said that a Qur'an other than this one should have been brought, or that it should have been changed (10:15; 42:52 names the revelation as a light). A Book that opens with the alphabet of its own people is not a foreign revelation parachuted into a strange language. It is the language of the audience, handled in a way the audience had never heard, and the practice of the Book elsewhere supports this: the sūrah that follows this family, the Ornaments of Gold, opens the same way and then says that its Book is preserved in the Mother of the Book (43:1-4). The reader who meets the letters twice in two verses is being told that the message and the tongue it came in are not strangers to each other, and that the distance between the two is the distance between what people say and what God says about them.

## Verse 42:3

> And so you ˹O Prophet˺ are sent revelation, just like those before you, by Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise

**Revelation as the Same Event Every Prophet Knew**
The first complete sentence of the sūrah tells the Prophet that the revelation coming to him is the same work God was doing through the messengers before him: thus God, the Almighty, the All-Wise, sends revelation to you and to those before you. The Book states the continuity of revelation wherever the charge is raised that the Prophet has invented a private book: it says that God revealed to him as He revealed to Noah and the prophets after him, and it lists the line — Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the tribes, Jesus — and says that the community believes in what was sent to them all, making no distinction (4:163-165; 2:136). Against the makkans' claim that they were hearing something new, the answer is that they were hearing the last chapter of something old, and that the God who was sending it was the same God who sent the earlier Books.

**The Two Names That Carry the Whole Passage**
The verse closes with the names the letters had opened with: the Almighty and the All-Wise. The pair is chosen with the sūrah's topic in mind. The power answers the makkans' confidence that the message would be stopped, because a revelation that comes from the All-Mighty cannot be defeated by the men who hear it; and the wisdom answers the objection that the Book's content is strange or uneven, because a message from the All-Wise will not be pointless. The Book uses the same pairing at the head of the other ḥawāmīm, where it names the Book's own sending as the product of might and wisdom (40:2; 45:2; 46:2), so that a reader arriving at this sūrah from the chapters before it hears a familiar seal. What the verse adds is the personal address: the revelation is sent to you, and it was sent to those before you. The Prophet is not being told that he is the first to carry a message, only that he is the latest in a line that has run through human history.

## Verse 42:4

> To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And He is the Most High, the Greatest

**What the Ownership of the Heavens Means**
The verse states the fact the sūrah builds on: everything in the heavens and everything on the earth belongs to God, and He is the Most High, the Greatest. The Book repeats the ownership in its longest statement of God's authority, where the seat of His rule covers the heavens and the earth and no one intercedes with Him except by His permission, and it closes that passage with the same two names used here: He is the Most High, the Most Great (2:255). Read together, the two verses say the same thing in different registers. The Book's description of God's throne, of His knowledge of the past and the future, and of the power that keeps the world standing, is all one statement of ownership, and the ownership means that no creature holds anything that was not given to it.

**The Two Names at the End**
Most High, Most Great — the Arabic words describe height and size, and the Book uses them for God as a way of stating that He is above the categories the creation lives in. The sūrah of the Throne pairs the two names with the sentence that neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him (2:255), which is the practical edge of the doctrine: the One who owns everything does not lose attention on His property, and the One who is above everything is not surprised by what happens in it. The sūrah will use the same ownership to answer the makkans' trust in their own strength (42:9-10), and it uses it here to place the reader, before any argument begins, in front of the One whose world he is in. The proper response the Book attaches to the statement is stated elsewhere: it is the worship that does not treat God as one of the things He made (17:111).

## Verse 42:5

> The heavens nearly burst, one above the other, ˹in awe of Him˺. And the angels glorify the praises of their Lord, and seek forgiveness for those on earth. Surely Allah alone is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**The Heavens That Nearly Split**
The verse describes the heavens as on the point of splitting, one above the other, out of awe before the One they belong to, and it follows that with the work of the angels: they praise their Lord and ask forgiveness for those on the earth. The Book uses the same picture of the sky almost coming apart in another sūrah, where it is said that the heavens nearly break and the earth split and the mountains fall when men attribute a son to the Most Merciful (19:88-91). The two passages together show the size of the thing the makkans were doing in their talk about God: the Book reads their theology as a weight the creation itself strains under, and it counters the strain with the glorification that the angels keep up without tiring (41:38).

**The Angels Who Ask Forgiveness for the People of the Earth**
The second half of the verse is one of the Book's clearest statements about the angels' care for the human beings. The sūrah of the Forgiver pictures the bearers of the Throne and the angels around it praising their Lord, believing in Him and asking forgiveness for the believers (40:7), and the same sūrah records their prayer in full — the prayer for those who repent, for their protection from the Fire, and for the fulfilment of the promise made to them (40:7-9). The sūrah of the Assemblies describes the angels' praise as unbroken and free of weariness (41:38), and the Sūrah of the Inevitable places them around the sky on the day the account is taken (69:17). The Book's picture is that the human being stands in a universe where the voices around him are not hostile. The angels are interceding for the people of the earth while the people of the earth are busy with their business, and the verse puts the reason at the end: God is Forgiving, Merciful, and what the angels ask for matches what His mercy already intends.

**Between the Splitting Sky and the Forgiving Name**
The verse joins two things that a reader might not expect in one sentence: the terror of the creation before its Maker, and the mercy that reaches down to the earth. The Book does the same in the sūrah of the Prophets, where the mountains and the sky tremble at the claim of a son and then the verse states that everything in the heavens and earth belongs to the Most Merciful as a servant (19:93). The terror belongs to the greatness of God, and the mercy belongs to the same greatness: the One before whom the skies almost burst is the One whose angels are asking Him to forgive the people walking on the earth below them. The reader is being shown the room he occupies in the universe before he is asked to change anything about his life.

## Verse 42:6

> As for those who take other protectors besides Him, Allah is Watchful over them. And you ˹O Prophet˺ are not a keeper over them

**The Men Who Take Other Protectors**
The verse turns to the people who put their trust in protectors other than God, and it gives the Prophet two facts about them: God is watching closely over them, and the Prophet is not their keeper. The Book uses the same pair again and again in the makkan sūrahs, because the pressure on the Prophet was the pressure of a man who wanted people to be saved and watched them refuse. The sūrah of the Heights closes with the instruction that he is not a keeper over them but a reminder (88:21-22), the Sūrah of the Cattle tells him that he is not responsible for their watch and that they are not his to keep (6:107), and the Sūrah of the Bee tells him that the duty is to convey, and that the reckoning is God's (16:82; 24:54; 13:40). The verse is a comfort with an edge to it. The Prophet is released from a burden he was never given, and the men he was worrying about are told that they have not escaped the attention of the One who did give them their lives.

**Who the Protectors Are**
The Book's phrase for what the makkans were holding to covers everything they trusted instead of God: their idols, their wealth, their ancestry, their standing at the Sanctuary. The same sūrah will say in a few verses that these men have taken protectors from beside God while God is the only Protector (42:9), and it names the moment the arrangement collapses, when the men who were followed disown the men who followed them and the ties between them are cut (2:166). The verse leaves the reader with the sentence that names the opening of the door: it is not that the protectors are weak and need replacing; it is that the position they were given belongs to someone else.

**Watching Without Forcing**
The Book never treats the fact that God sees everything as a threat that will do the work of persuasion. The verses around this one leave the hearer his choice, and the sūrah's own statement of the Prophet's office comes later in the same chapter, where he is told that his duty is to warn and that the return of all of it is with God (42:48; 13:40). The pattern runs through the Book: the men who were being watched were also being given time, and the messenger was told that his job was to speak a clear word and not to be the keeper of anybody's soul. The verse's two sentences belong together: the One who is watching is able to settle every account, and the messenger is the man sent to make sure nobody can say he was not told.

## Verse 42:7

> And so We have revealed to you a Quran in Arabic, so you may warn the Mother of Cities and everyone around it, and warn of the Day of Gathering—about which there is no doubt—˹when˺ a group will be in Paradise and another in the Blaze

**A Qur'an in Arabic, for the Mother of the Cities**
The verse names what the Prophet has been given and who the message is for. The Book was sent in Arabic so that the warner could warn the Mother of the Cities — Makkah, the place the Book elsewhere calls the mother of settlements because the House stands there and the city around it is the root of the communities of the peninsula — and everyone around it, which is the rest of the Arabian tribes and, by extension, everyone the Book reaches (6:92; 28:59). The Book states this address in the same terms when it calls the message a warning to the people of Umm al-Qurā and those around them, and it does not let the local form of the first address shrink the range of the message: the same verses that name the Arabic tongue also call the message a warning to all people, and describe the Prophet as sent to humanity as a whole (34:28; 7:158). The Arabic of the Book is not a boundary around its audience; it is the door its first audience could hear through.

**The Day of Gathering**
The verse warns of the Day of Gathering, about which there is no doubt, and then it draws the line that runs down the whole sūrah: one group in Paradise and another in the Blaze. The Book calls that day the Day of Gathering in one other place, where it says that God will gather the people for the Day whose coming is certain (64:9), and it names the day by several other names in the same family: the Day of Decision, the Day of Reckoning, the Day that will not be delayed (77:13; 40:27; 42:7's own doubt-free note). What the verse does with the familiar material is important. It does not describe the fire or the garden; it states the fact that the audience will be split into two and leaves the hearer to ask which group he is in. The sūrah's method all the way through is to state the division plainly and then spend its length on the character of the people on each side, and it closes with the account of how God speaks to the men He chooses and guides them to a straight path (42:51-53).

## Verse 42:8

> Had Allah willed, He could have easily made all ˹humanity˺ into a single community ˹of believers˺. But He admits into His mercy whoever He wills. And the wrongdoers will have no protector or helper

**The One Community That Was Not Made**
The verse states a possibility God did not take: had He willed, He could have made all people a single community of believers. The Book puts the same sentence in three other places, and reading them together shows what the verse is doing. In the Sūrah of the Bee it introduces the reason the message was sent at all: had God willed He would have made the whole of humanity one community, and the disagreement that exists is the space in which the messengers speak (16:93). In the Sūrah of Hūd it is followed by the sentence that people will keep differing except those on whom their Lord has mercy, and that this is what they were created for (11:118-119). And in the Sūrah of the Table it is joined to the test: if God had willed He could have made the community one, but He is testing it in what He has given it, so compete in good works (5:48). The sūrah's point is not that God could not produce a world of automatic believers; it is that this world was made for a different arrangement, one in which a person's faith is his own act.

**Admitted to Mercy, and the Wrongdoers Left Without Help**
The verse then names what happens in such a world: God admits into His mercy whom He wills, and the wrongdoers have no protector and no helper. The Book always joins the mercy to the choice, because the choice is the door the mercy comes through, and it does not read human difference as a failure of the message. The ending is the sentence the sūrah repeats for the men who refused: their protectors will not be there for them when they are asked to account (42:44-46; 2:166). The reader is left with two facts he can hold at once. God could have made the whole of humanity one nation, and He did not, and the mercy He gives is given to the people who come for it.

## Verse 42:9

> How can they take protectors besides Him? Allah alone is the Protector. He ˹alone˺ gives life to the dead. And He ˹alone˺ is Most Capable of everything

**The Question the Sūrah Cannot Leave Alone**
The verse asks how these men can take protectors other than God, and the question is one of astonishment at a contradiction. The sūrah has just said that everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God (42:4), so the partners being trusted alongside Him are His own property: a created thing, held by its Owner, called on as if it were the Owner. The same astonishment is stated in the Book's longest verse, where the men are asked who could intercede with God without His permission, in the same breath as the statement that everything in the heavens and the earth is His (2:255). The Book's argument about the idols never depends on denying that the idols exist; it depends on where the ownership sits.

**The Two Facts Attached to the Question**
The verse answers its own question with two statements about God: He is the Protector, and He alone gives life to the dead, and He is able to do all things. The first answers the makkans' search for protection, and the second answers their contempt for the talk of resurrection, which was the doctrine they found most difficult to imagine. The sūrah of the Roman's account of the dead earth and the Sūrah of the Bee's account of the creation give the same answer: the One who brings a land back to life after its death can bring bodies back after theirs (30:50; 16:65-67), and the Book says that the raising of the dead is no more difficult for Him than the first creation, which the men had already watched happen in their own mothers (22:5; 75:3-4). The verse therefore does not argue with the makkans on their own ground of power. It points out that the power they were comparing themselves to is the power that started them.

## Verse 42:10

> ˹Say to the believers, O Prophet,˺ “Whatever you may differ about, its judgment rests with Allah. That is Allah—my Lord. In Him I put my trust, and to Him I ˹always˺ turn.”

**The Judgment That Belongs to God Alone**
The verse gives the Prophet and the community a rule for every disagreement: whatever you differ about, its judgment rests with God. The Book repeats the rule because it is the answer to the disputes that had been running between the communities of the Book, and the sūrah of the Cow names the cause of those disputes: the people of the earlier Books differed only after the clear proofs had come to them, out of envy and rivalry (2:213; 45:17). The sūrah of the Bee says that God sent down the Book to make clear to people what they differ about (16:64), and the verse in front of us turns that statement into a discipline for the reader: the answer to a disagreement inside the community is not the loudest voice but the text, and the final ruling on any question is with God, whether it arrives in this world as a law or is left to the day of the account.

**The Formulas at the End of the Verse**
The verse closes with the words of the Prophet himself: my Lord is that God, in Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn. The Book gives its messenger the same pair of verbs in other places, and they describe the two halves of a believer's life: the trust that carries the man into his work, and the turning that brings him back when he finds he was wrong or weak (11:88; 40:44; 39:54). The practical side of the verse for a community is the combination it recommends. Nothing about a dispute gives a person the right to hold his view as though the account had already been settled; the door of the turning is kept open on purpose. The sūrah will say later that the One who has the judgment also has the power to let the people who differ have a look at what they were arguing about (42:18; 42:22).

## Verse 42:11

> ˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth. He has made for you spouses from among yourselves, and ˹made˺ mates for cattle ˹as well˺—multiplying you ˹both˺. There is nothing like Him, for He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**The Originator and the Pairs**
The verse names God the Originator of the heavens and the earth — the One who brought them into being without a model — and then points to the pairs He has made: spouses from among yourselves, and mates among the cattle, multiplying the creation by pairing it. The Book treats pairing as a universal signature of the creation, naming it as a sign in the formed world, in the plants, and in what people do not yet know (36:36), and the sūrah of the Romans gives the human pair its fullest statement, with the comfort, the compassion and the mercy placed between the spouses as signs of God (30:21). The second half of the verse extends the same arrangement to the animals men lived with and ate, which the Sūrah of the Bee lists with their benefits to the households that kept them (16:5-8). The reader is being told that his family life and his farm life are not the ordinary parts of his day that religion ignores.

**"There Is Nothing Like Him"**
The verse's central sentence is the Book's clearest statement about the difference between God and everything else: there is nothing like Him. It is the sentence the scholars of the community leaned on whenever they spoke about God's names and attributes, and they set it beside the closing verse of the sūrah of Purity, which states that nothing is comparable to Him (112:4), and beside the verse in the Cattle that says no vision can grasp Him while He grasps all vision (6:103). What the sentence does is cut off both of the errors the makkans were caught between: the men who made their idols into images of God, and the men who denied that God had any attributes at all. He is not like anything He made, and He is not absent from what He made either.

**The Hearing and the Seeing**
The verse does not end with the denial of likeness; it ends with two names that speak about relation: He is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing. The pairing is the answer to the temptation to read the first sentence as distance: the One who is like nothing hears the man who speaks to Him, and sees what that man does in the dark. The Book closes its command about judging between people with the same pair (4:58), and it attaches the seeing to the promise that nothing a person sends ahead of himself is lost (2:110), while the answering of prayer is stated in the verse the Book gives to the servants who ask about their Lord (2:186). The two halves of the verse therefore complete each other: the God who cannot be pictured is the God who is listening, and the man who takes that seriously finds a life in which nothing he does is unseen.

## Verse 42:12

> To Him belong the keys ˹of the treasuries˺ of the heavens and the earth. He gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. Indeed, He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**The Keys No One Else Holds**

The verse says the keys of the treasuries of the heavens and the earth belong to God, and the image of keys asserts both ownership and control. A treasury is a store of provision, and the key is what determines who may open it and how much; by placing the keys in God's hand alone, the verse closes every rival claim to distribute the world's goods. The sūrah's earlier assertion that everything belongs to God is here made practical, because ownership without the key would be empty, while the key without ownership would be usurped. The treasuries of both heavens and earth cover the whole economy of existence, so that nothing necessary to any creature falls outside His disbursement. The verse therefore answers the anxiety of scarcity with a picture of a locked storehouse opened only by the one true Owner, and it answers the pride of the wealthy with the reminder that their abundance is a key turned in their favor.

---

The keys are the Book's image for the unshared disposal of provision, and the prayer that follows the verse takes up the same idea: "Our Lord, do not let us put our trust in those who do wrong, and save us by Your mercy" (42:10 fold) — the community's need is placed where the keys are held. The Book states the same ownership in the sūrah of the Troops, where the treasuries of God's mercy are said to be in His hands alone (39:38), and the sūrah of the Night Journey, where the treasures of everything are with Him (17:21, 15:21). The verse's mention of the measure of provision — abundant for one, restricted for another — is the same doctrine the sūrah states at 42:19 and 42:27: the measure is His, and the restriction is not neglect.

## Verse 42:13

> He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ “Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it.” What you call the polytheists to is unbearable for them. Allah chooses for Himself whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns ˹to Him˺

**One Religion Laid Down for All the Messengers**
The verse names five messengers and says that one religion was ordained for the whole line: Noah, the Prophet himself, Abraham, Moses and Jesus, with the command that runs through all of them — keep the religion and do not split over it. The Book states the same unity in several places the first readers of this sūrah knew well. It tells the Prophet to follow the faith of Abraham the upright, who was not one of the polytheists (16:123), it names Abraham as neither Jew nor Christian but as one who submitted (2:135; 3:67), and it calls the religion the way of their forefather Abraham, laid on the community without hardship (22:78). The line of the messengers is drawn as one family in the Sūrah of the Believers too, where Noah and Moses are named in the same breath (23:23, 23:49). The Prophet described the same fact in one sentence: the prophets are brothers of one father, their mothers are different and their religion is one, in a report preserved in the two Ṣaḥīḥ collections. The verse is therefore not asking the men of Makkah to accept a stranger. It is telling them that the call they found new was the oldest thing they had ever honoured.

**Why the Idolaters Found It Heavy**
The verse says plainly that what the Prophet was calling the idolaters to was heavy on them, and the word describes a weight they could feel. The Book is honest about the cost of the change: the men of Makkah were being asked to give up gods their fathers had served, a standing that came from their custody of the House, and the income that flowed to them from the pilgrimage of the tribes. The Book gives the same honesty to the makkans' own words in the sūrah that follows this one, where they answer the call with the sentence that they found their fathers on a way and are walking in their footsteps (43:22-23). The heaviness is not a flaw in the message. It is the size of what was being lifted off the shoulders of a people who had carried it for generations.

**The Two Names at the End**
The verse closes with the sentence that decides who arrives: God chooses for Himself whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns to Him. The choice is God's and the turning is the man's, and the Book keeps the two together everywhere it speaks about guidance: it tells the Prophet that he cannot guide whom he loves, but that God guides whom He wills (28:56), and it tells him to say that God leaves to stray whom He wills and guides to Himself the man who turns back (13:27). The verse therefore ends the passage with a door left open. The religion that the idolaters found too heavy to lift was the religion of men who had turned, and the turning was something every one of them could do that same day: the command to keep the faith and make no divisions stands at the head of the passage (42:13), and the divisions that followed the knowledge are diagnosed in the verse that comes next (42:14).

---

## Verse 42:14

> They did not split ˹into sects˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them. Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord for an appointed term, the matter would have certainly been settled between them ˹at once˺. And surely those who were made to inherit the Scripture after them are truly in alarming doubt about this ˹Quran˺

**The Split Came After the Knowledge**
The verse makes a claim about timing that shames the men it describes: the communities did not break into sects until the knowledge had reached them, and they broke out of envy between themselves. The sūrah of Āl ʿImrān states the same diagnosis almost word for word about the people of the Book: they differed only after knowledge had come to them, out of envy among themselves (3:19). The point of the sequence is that nobody can blame the revelation for the division. The Book came to unite people on one way, and the men who split it up did so with the text in their hands, for reasons the verse names plainly enough: who would hold the authority, who would keep the privileges, whose reading would be the official one.

**The Envy Behind the Argument**
Envy is the engine the verse names, and the diagnosis cuts the ground from under the stated reasons. A disagreement about a doctrine can be honest; a faction formed out of rivalry cannot, because the object is not the truth but the position the truth would give to somebody else. The Book warns the community of the Prophet with the same diagnosis rather than flattering it: it tells the believers not to be like the people who split and fell into disputes after the clear proofs had reached them (3:105), and the Prophet described the coming pattern in a report preserved in Abū Dāwūd (4597) and al-Tirmidhī (2641) — that the community would divide into seventy-three groups as the communities before it had divided, with one of them remaining on what he and his companions were on. The warning was given because the mechanism was known, and it is a warning the Muslim reader has to take personally, since the community of the Prophet is not exempt from the thing that broke the communities before it.

**The Decree, the Delay, and the Inheritors**
If the decree of God had not already gone out for a fixed term, the verse says, the matter would have been settled between them at once; and the men who inherited the Scripture after them are in a doubt that keeps them unsettled. The Book regularly explains the delay in the settlement of human disputes by the term that God has appointed (42:14), and the sūrah of the Cow names the same cause when it says that the people who received the Scripture disputed it only after the clear proofs had come to them, out of envy (2:213), and it names the doubt of the later generations as a special condition: a people who received a book they did not write, respected it without studying it, and ended up defending an inheritance rather than a truth. What the verse leaves the reader with is the grace that runs through the whole passage: God guides whom He wills to a straight path, and the path is still open to the man who asks for it (42:13).

---

## Verse 42:15

> Because of that, you ˹O Prophet˺ will invite ˹all˺. Be steadfast as you are commanded, and do not follow their desires. And say, “I believe in every Scripture Allah has revealed. And I am commanded to judge fairly among you. Allah is our Lord and your Lord. We will be accountable for our deeds and you for yours. There is no ˹need for˺ contention between us. Allah will gather us together ˹for judgment˺. And to Him is the final return.”

**Invite Everyone and Stand Firm**
The verse follows the account of the communities that split after they had been given knowledge, and it tells the Prophet to invite all of them, to stand firm where he has been commanded to stand, and not to follow their desires. The invitation is placed in its widest form: the Prophet is not asked to win over one faction or one tribe, and the Book elsewhere says that he was sent to all of humanity as a bringer of good news and a warner (34:28 states it, and 7:158 gives the same description). Read after the account of the division, the instruction tells him how not to repeat the history of the people who broke into parties: keep the door open to everybody and refuse to lean the way any of the parties wants him to lean.

**The Words He Was Told to Say**
The verse then gives the Prophet the sentence he was to use with the people of the earlier Books, and it is one of the fullest statements of how the community should speak to them: I believe in every Book God has sent down; I have been commanded to judge fairly among you; God is our Lord and your Lord; we have our own deeds and you have your own; there is no arguing between us; God will gather us all, and to Him is the return. The Book gives the same ground for handling the argument in the sūrah of the Cow, where the believers are told to say that God is their Lord and the Lord of the others and that each will answer for his own deeds (2:139 states it), and in the sūrah of Jonah, where the Prophet is told to answer denial without hostility: my deeds are mine and yours are yours (10:41 states it). The invitation stays open and the account is left with God.

**Justice in the Middle of the Sentence**
The command to judge fairly is placed inside the declaration, and that placement is deliberate. The sūrah has been describing people who wanted the Prophet to take their side against their rivals, and the verse answers them by making the standard public: one scripture accepted in full, equal justice between the groups, and no attempt to force an outcome that belongs to the day of gathering. The sūrah of the women gives the community the same standard in its sharpest form when it tells the believers to stand for justice even when the case cuts against themselves, their parents or their relatives (4:135 states it). In this sūrah the command carries extra weight because the audience is split into parties that watch the judge to see whose side he takes.

**Where the Argument Is Left**
The verse ends by saying that God will gather everyone and that the return is to Him. That sentence is the answer to every dispute the passage has been discussing, and the sūrah uses the same ending for its other arguments. The Book says in the same sūrah that God is the one who answers the wronged when they call on Him, and it says that the men who argue about God after He has been acknowledged will find their argument worthless with their Lord. The Prophet is told in this verse to leave the outcome in God's hands, and the sūrah then turns to the men who dispute about the message and tells them where their argument ends.

---

## Verse 42:16

> As for those who dispute about Allah after He is ˹already˺ acknowledged ˹by many˺, their argument is futile in the sight of their Lord. Upon them is wrath, and they will suffer a severe punishment

**The Argument That Comes After the Case Is Decided**
The verse describes the men who dispute about God after He has already been acknowledged, and the Book's use of that situation is precise. What the argument is worth is stated in the same place in the Sūrah of the Forgiver: those who dispute about God's signs without any authority given to them have nothing in their hearts but a greed for dominance that they will never reach (40:56). The Book states the same about the men who quarrel over the truth in order to discredit it, when it says that the messengers came only with good news and warnings and the disbelievers argued with falsehood to cancel the truth with it (18:56). The verse in front of us names the same method with fewer words, and it adds the fact that matters: the discussion was no longer an inquiry. Something had already been recognized, and the arguing was going on anyway.

**The Wrath Named in One Word**
The end of the verse says that their argument is worthless with their Lord, that wrath is on them, and that theirs is a severe punishment. The Book joins wrath to dispute in another place with a detail that fits the makkans: the thunder and the angels glorify God in awe of Him while the men keep disputing about Him, and the verse ends by stating that He is tremendous in might (13:13). The punishment is not announced as a sudden mood on God's part; the Book always attaches it to the treatment the message received at the hands of the men who heard it, and it names the argument itself as worthless rather than dangerous. What the verse measures is the distance the audience had traveled: they had moved from hearing a claim to contesting the One who made the claim, and the Book says that this is a place where an argument cannot win anything.

---

## Verse 42:17

> It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near

**The Book and the Balance Sent Down Together**
The verse says that the Book was revealed with the truth and with the balance, and the pairing has a long history in the Book. The Sūrah of Iron states it in full: God sent the messengers with clear proofs, sent down with them the Scripture and the balance so that people might deal justly, and sent down iron, in which there is might and benefit for people (57:25). Read together, the two verses say that revelation carries two things: a statement about what is true, and a scale by which human dealings are measured. The Book does not treat worship and justice as two separate departments of religion, and it does not present the law as a heavy addition to the message; the scale and the book arrived in the same hand.

**"Perhaps the Hour Is Near"**
The verse ends with the sentence the Book repeats whenever it wants the reader to stop treating the last day as an inconvenience far off in time: you never know, perhaps the Hour is near. The same words come back in the Sūrah of the Confederates, where people ask the Prophet about the Hour and he is told to answer that its knowledge is with God alone, and that perhaps it is near (33:63), and the Sūrah of the Prophets opens with the statement that the reckoning of the people has drawn close while they turn away heedless (21:1). The verse's two halves belong together in the argument of the sūrah. The Book came with truth and a scale, so the scale will be used; and the Hour that will use it is nearer than the men reading the scale are ready to admit.

---

## Verse 42:18

> Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth. Surely those who dispute about the Hour have gone far astray

**"Bring It On, Then"**
The deniers were not asking about the Hour; they were daring it to arrive. The Book records the same dare in several places, and it records it as a habit of the men who had run out of arguments: they prayed for stones to fall on them if the message was true (8:32), they challenged the Prophet to bring the punishment rather than the mercy he was warning them about (13:6), and they kept demanding that it be hurried (22:47; 29:53; 51:14). The verse calls their demand what it is — mockery — because a man who genuinely wanted that day to come would be preparing for it, and the men talking loudest were not. The sūrah has just named the two things they were standing in front of: the Book sent down with the truth, and the balance of justice, with the note that the Hour may be near (42:17). Their answer to the balance was a joke about the scales.

**The Believers Who Fear the Day**
The verse sets the two reactions side by side, and this is the sentence the reader is meant to carry: the deniers call for the Hour, and the believers are afraid of it because they know it is real. The Book gives the believers' prayer in the sūrah of the Criterion, where they ask their Lord to turn away the punishment of Hell, since its torment is a lasting affliction (25:65), and it tells the Prophet to say that no one is safe from it except the one God has mercy on (6:16). The fear described here is not the fear of a man who doubts the promise; it is the fear of a man who has believed it, and the sūrah's closing lesson is the difference between the two audiences on the day the waiting ends. The Book says elsewhere that the Hour will take the careless by surprise (6:31; 12:107), and the men who had been asking for it in mockery will be the first to ask for a delay.

**Those Who Argue About the Hour**
The verse ends with a verdict on the men who dispute about that day: they have wandered far off. The Book treats disputing about the Hour as a species of the same denial, because the argument is not really about a date; it is about whether there is an account at all. The sūrah has stated the rule of such arguments already, when it said that the men who dispute about God after He has been acknowledged have an argument that is worthless in the sight of their Lord (42:16). What the verse leaves the reader with is a question about attention rather than a question about time. The Hour is not being kept secret because it is doubtful. It is being kept so that the waiting itself has a use.

---

## Verse 42:19

> Allah is Ever Kind to His servants. He provides ˹abundantly˺ to whoever He wills. And He is the All-Powerful, Almighty

**The Kindness That Reaches Every Creature**
The verse calls God Ever Kind to His servants and says that He provides abundantly for whom He wills, and the Book explains what that kindness covers. The Sūrah of Hūd states the rule at the widest possible range: there is no creature on the earth for whom God has not guaranteed provision, and He knows where it lives and where it will die (11:6). The Sūrah of the Originator states the other half of the same fact: what God opens of mercy, nobody can withhold, and what He withholds, nobody can release (35:2). The Book's argument with the idolaters is built on this point: if the idols men trust cannot withhold or release anything, provision is one more proof that the Owner is God, and the Sūrah of the Sovereignty puts the question plainly — who will provide for you if He withholds His provision (67:21)? The kindness named here is therefore not a mood but the arrangement the world runs on.

**The Names at the End and the Men Who Contest Them**
The verse closes with the All-Powerful and the Almighty, and the two names answer the worry the Quran anticipates: a man may be warned that God's provision is measured and still wonder whether anyone can press Him to change His mind. The Book's answer comes from the other side of the same doctrine: the power that provides is absolute, and the might that withholds is the might that could also give. The next verse turns the doctrine into a decision for the reader: whoever aims at the harvest of the Hereafter is given more, and whoever aims only at this world is given something of it and nothing beyond (42:20). The kindness is universal; the harvest is not.

---

## Verse 42:20

> Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest. And whoever desires ˹only˺ the harvest of this world, We will give them some of it, but they will have no share in the Hereafter

**Two Harvests in Plain Words**
The verse takes the farming picture the sūrah has been using and turns it into a decision about the shape of a life. Whoever wants the harvest of the Hereafter has his harvest increased, and whoever wants only this world's harvest is given part of it and has no share in the Hereafter. The Book gives the same division in the Sūrah of the Night Journey with a detail this verse leaves implicit: the men who desire only this passing life are given what God pleases to give them quickly, and the men who desire the Hereafter and strive for it as believers have their effort appreciated, and God provides for both groups out of His bounty, which can never be withheld (17:18-20). The two passages make the same point in the same order: the desire, then the striving, then the giving, and the difference between the two groups is not the amount of provision but what the provision was for.

**Why the World-Seeker Gets Only a Part**
The Book's word for the world-seeker's share is deliberately small: he is given some of it. The Book elsewhere describes the man who wanted his garden's wealth for himself and had nothing left of it (18:42-44), and it names the desire for the world as a trap that shortens the horizon: they are satisfied with the life of this world, and it is a small thing compared with the Hereafter (9:38). What the verse refuses is the picture of a God who punishes the man who works honestly for his family. The Book praises the man who seeks God's bounty and trades fairly, and it names the provision that comes with a good life as one of the gifts the believer asks for (2:201; 28:77). The point is what the man is aiming at: a hand that takes the world as the whole of the prize has already spent its harvest, while a hand that takes the world as a field can keep planting.

---

## Verse 42:21

> Or do they have associate-gods who have ordained for them some ˹polytheistic˺ beliefs, which Allah has not authorized? Had it not been for ˹prior˺ decree on Judgment, the matter would have certainly been settled between them ˹at once˺. And surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment

**The Rules Nobody Authorized**
The verse puts a question to the men of Makkah: are there partners of God who have laid down for them a religion that God never authorized? The Book names the invented rules elsewhere in detail. The sūrah of the Cattle records the men who set aside a portion of their crops and herds for God's supposed partners, who declared certain animals' backs untouchable, who reserved the milk of some females and would not slaughter others with God's name over them (6:136-139). The sūrah of the Bee answers the whole practice in one command: do not say, of what your tongues describe falsely, that this is lawful and that is forbidden, inventing lies against God (16:116). And the sūrah of the Heights puts the question that closes the argument: who has forbidden the adornments of God and the good things He brought out for His servants (7:32)? The answer is nobody. Behind the taboos of Makkah stood no revelation, only custom and the interest of the men who profited from the pilgrimage trade.

**Who Owns the Right to Legislate**
The verse's phrase for the authorization is the sending down of warrant, and the Book uses it wherever men take the law into their own hands: an idol has never sent anything down. That is why the scholars of the community treated the making of religious law as an act of worship, and why they held that a man who declares a thing lawful or forbidden without warrant has joined himself to the authority that belongs to God alone. The chapter states the same principle in the verse that closes the passage, where the ruling on any dispute is said to rest with God (42:10), and the sūrah of the Cattle ends the argument for the reader when it says that the idolaters' own invented categories were nothing but a fabrication against Him (6:138-140).

**Why the Reckoning Is Held Back**
The second half of the verse answers the question every mocked messenger heard: if this is false, why is nothing happening to us? Had a decree not already gone out, the verse says, the matter would have been settled between them at once. The Book gives the same explanation wherever it handles the delay: if God were to take people to task for what their hands have earned, He would leave nothing alive on the face of the earth (35:45), and the punishment is held to a term that no one can bring forward or put back (10:11, 13:31). The sentence tells the reader two things at once, and both matter. The wrong is not going unnoticed, and the waiting is not approval. The verse's last words return to the account: the wrongdoers have a punishment that will hurt.

---

## Verse 42:22

> You will see the wrongdoers fearful ˹of the punishment˺ for what they committed but it will be inevitable for them, whereas those who believe and do good will be in the lush Gardens of Paradise. They will have whatever they desire from their Lord. That is ˹truly˺ the greatest bounty

**The Dread of the Men Who Earned It**
The verse shows the wrongdoers on the day when they see what they have earned, and the picture is exact: they are afraid, and the punishment is already on its way to them. The Book uses the same pairing whenever it wants to show that the dread and the event arrive together. The sūrah of the Cave places the record in front of the men and says that the criminals are terrified of what is written in it (18:49), and the sūrah of the Poets records the dispute in the middle of the punishment, when the leaders and the followers turn on one another with nothing left to say (26:96-102). What the verse adds is the measure of the loss: the fear comes to men who cannot change a word of the page in front of them.

**The Garden for the Men Who Did Good**
Against that picture the verse sets the people who believed and did good work, and it names their reward in the two words it uses for the whole arrangement: gardens full of green, and whatever they wish for. The second phrase is the Book's way of saying that the reward is not a wage measured out but a house where the asking itself is answered. The sūrah of the Ornaments of Gold says that the people of the garden will have whatever the soul desires and the eyes delight in, and that they will stay there (43:71), and the sūrah of the Prophets says the same about the garden they were promised (21:102). The verse closes by calling this the greatest bounty, which is the Book's highest word for what waits at the end of a good life.

**The Sentence the Verse Leaves Out**
The verse does not describe the punishment of the first group in any detail; it says only that what they earned is coming, and it spends its length on the garden. That is a pattern the Book uses when the warning has already been given: the men being addressed knew what they had done, and the verse leaves them to work out the rest. The next verse takes the reader from the garden to the messenger and the question of what he asks in return for bringing the news (42:23), which is the turn the sūrah needs before it answers the charge of invention.

---

## Verse 42:23

> That ˹reward˺ is the good news which Allah gives to His servants who believe and do good. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I do not ask you for a reward for this ˹message˺—only honour for ˹our˺ kinship.” Whoever earns a good deed, We will increase it in goodness for them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative

**The Good News and the Fruit of Belief**
The verse names the garden as the good news God gives to His servants who believe and do good work, and it keeps the two conditions together in one sentence. The Book never separates them, because the good work is the evidence that the belief has reached the hands. The same pairing runs through the sūrah of the Cave's account of the men who believed and did right, with the promise that God will settle their reward in the best form (18:30), and through the Sūrah of the Bee, where the good life is promised to the men who do right (16:97). The Book's word for the news is glad tidings, and the verse uses it here rather than a warning, because the men reading it had already heard the warnings.

**"I Ask You for No Reward"**
The verse then gives the Prophet the sentence to say, and it is one of the most discussed lines in the sūrah: I do not ask you for any reward for this, only the affection owed to the near kin. The Book records every messenger making the same disclaimer — Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, all of them say that their wage is with the Lord of the worlds (26:109, 26:127, 26:145, 26:180) — and the sūrah has already commanded the Prophet to invite all without partiality (42:15). What makes this verse different is the one thing he does ask for, and the teachers read it in two ways that do not exclude each other: the love owed to the Prophet's own household, and the kindness owed to the relatives of the men he was addressing, who were his own kin in the tribe. On either reading the request costs the audience nothing but their affection, and it returns to them the benefit of loving the house that carried the message.

**The Good Deed Increased**
The verse ends with the promise that whoever earns a good deed will be given more goodness, and with the two names Forgiving and Appreciative. The Book makes the multiplication of the good deed a standing rule: whoever brings a good deed will have ten like it (6:160), and the men who spend and strive are given much more (2:261; 57:18). The name that closes the verse, the Appreciative, is the Book's word for the God who counts a small good deed as a large one, and the sūrah uses it here at the exact point where the community is being told that the messenger's price is nothing and their own smallest work is not lost. The next verse turns from that generosity to the accusation the leaders were spreading, that the Qur'an was something the Prophet had made up (42:24).

---

## Verse 42:24

> Or do they say, “He has fabricated a lie about Allah!”? ˹If you had,˺ Allah would have sealed your heart, if He willed. And Allah wipes out falsehood and establishes the truth by His Words. He certainly knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**The Accusation of Fabrication**

The Meccan opponents leveled a specific and damaging charge against the Messenger: that he had invented the revelation himself and then falsely attributed it to God. This was not a vague doubt but a calculated slander, since to call the Qur'an a fabrication was to strip it of all authority and to paint its bearer as a liar and a sorcerer. The Qur'an meets the accusation here not with a denial alone but with a rhetorical question that turns the charge back on those who make it, forcing them to consider the implications of their own claim. To say "He has fabricated a lie about Allah" is to accuse the most trusted man in Mecca of the gravest deception, and the verse invites the listener to weigh that accusation against everything known of his character. The question form itself signals confidence: only a falsehood needs to be defended anxiously, whereas the truth can afford to ask its opponents what they really mean.

---

The charge of fabrication is answered three times in the Book with the same argument: if the Prophet had invented anything and attributed it to God, he would have been seized by the right hand and cut off (69:44–46), and the sūrah of the Bee states the same in the form of a warning (16:105). The verse's reply is different in kind: if he had fabricated, God would have sealed his heart, and the claim is turned back on those who make it, since the sealing is what happens to the ones who invent (16:108). The next verse states what the community is to do instead — turn to the One who hears the prayer and forgives — and the passage closes with the statement that God is the Forbearing and the Forgiving.

## Verse 42:25

> He is the One Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons ˹their˺ sins. And He knows whatever you do

**The Door of Repentance Kept Open**
The verse says that God accepts the repentance of His servants and pardons the bad deeds, and it ends with the sentence that makes the promise credible: He knows what you do. The immediate setting is a sūrah addressed to men who had heard the warnings and were being invited back, and the Book's treatment of repentance is consistent through it: it tells the men who have gone too far to despair of God's mercy and to come back, since He forgives all sins (39:53), it says that God loves the men who turn to Him (2:222), and it tells the Prophet that God accepts the repentance of His servants and takes the charity from their hands (9:104). The Book also sets a limit, so that the door is not treated as a place to stand: repentance belongs to the men who do wrong in ignorance and turn quickly, while the men who put it off until death finds them have only words left (4:17-18).

**Where the Verse Sits in the Sūrah**
The verse follows the account of the garden and the messenger who asks nothing for the message. That order matters for the reader, because it tells him what kind of God is behind the warnings that fill the sūrah: one who has not closed the file while the man is still alive, and one whose pardon covers the deeds that a person turns from. The Book ends the same thought elsewhere with the promise that the men who repent and believe and do right will have their bad deeds replaced by good ones (25:70), and the Prophet described God's joy at the return of a servant as greater than a man's joy at finding what he had lost in the desert (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The closing words of the verse, that He knows what you do, are the reason the offer is real: the One making the promise is the One who is already watching, and nothing about the man's condition is hidden from the invitation.

---

## Verse 42:26

> He responds to those who believe and do good, and increases their reward out of His grace. As for the disbelievers, they will suffer a severe punishment

**The Response to Belief and Good Deed**

This verse draws a clean line between two classes of people and declares how God responds to each, and the first response is directed at those who believe and do good. The Arabic yujibu, "He responds," evokes the image of a call answered, a supplication met, a need supplied; the God described here is not distant but attentive. Importantly, the response is not earned as a wage but given, and the following clause makes clear that the increase of reward comes "out of His grace," not as a mechanical return on investment. The pairing of belief and good deed recurs throughout the Qur'an and is essential: faith without conduct is inert, and conduct without faith lacks direction and root. By responding to both together, the verse affirms that God meets the whole person, the inward conviction and the outward life, and that the one who brings both to Him is not ignored.

---

The response to belief and good deeds is stated in the same terms in the sūrah of the Cave, where the doers of good are given increase (18:30), and in the sūrah of the Cave's companion passage in al-Fāṭir, where God pays in full and adds of His bounty (35:30). The contrast with the disbelievers, who will suffer, is the sūrah's method of stating the two outcomes together, as in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān with the promise of forgiveness and a generous provision (3:136). The verse's phrase about increase out of grace is the Book's way of saying that the reward is not only a settlement of accounts but a gift beyond measure.

## Verse 42:27

> Had Allah given abundant provisions to ˹all˺ His servants, they would have certainly transgressed throughout the land. But He sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure. He is truly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants

**Why Provision Is Measured Out**
The verse gives the reader the reason behind something he sees every day: God did not give the whole of humanity an unlimited supply. Had He given abundant provision to all His servants, they would have gone on to spread corruption in the land. That is a hard sentence, and the Book states it as a fact about people, not as an insult to them. A world of unmeasured wealth would be a world of unmeasured appetite, and the Book makes the same observation in other places where it explains the distribution of provision: God gives abundantly or sparingly to whom He wills, and most people do not know why (34:36; 13:26). Set against the sūrah of the Night Journey's warning not to strain the eyes at what others have been given (20:131), the verse tells the poor man that the size of another man's share is not a verdict on his own standing.

**The Measure That Is Not a Punishment**
The verse then says what God does instead: He sends down in the exact measure He wills, and He is All-Aware and All-Seeing of His servants. The two names answer the fear the first sentence could raise. If the amount of a man's provision were fixed by a blind rule, the poor would have reason to resent it; the verse says the One who measures sees the man he is measuring for, and knows what he can carry. The Book states the same doctrine in the sūrah of the Spider, where it says that God gives abundantly or sparingly to whoever He wills of His servants, and that He has full knowledge of everything (29:62). What the verse leaves its reader is a difficult but steady position: the measure is not a sign of God's displeasure with the hungry, and the abundance of another man is not a sign of his favour.

---

## Verse 42:28

> He is the One Who sends down rain after people have given up hope, spreading out His mercy. He is the Guardian, the Praiseworthy

**Rain After the Sky Has Failed Them**
The verse names one of God's mercies that the men of the desert understood better than any city could: He sends down rain after people have given up hope, and spreads His mercy over the land. The Book uses the revival of dead ground as its standing picture of resurrection, and it does so in verses the first hearers could confirm with their own eyes. The sūrah of the Distinguisher says that God sends the winds ahead of His mercy and sends down pure water from the sky to give life to a lifeless land and to water the animals and people He created (25:48-49). The sūrah of the Romans follows the wind to the cloud and the cloud to the rain and then draws the conclusion the verse is leading to: look at the traces of God's mercy, how He brings the earth to life after its death; surely He is the one who gives life to the dead (30:50). The sūrah of Qāf describes the blessed rain and the gardens and harvests it brings out (50:9), and the sūrah of Fussilat pictures the earth standing barren until the water comes down and it stirs and swells (41:39).

**The Guardian and the Praiseworthy**
The verse closes with two names: the Guardian, and the Praiseworthy. The Guardian is the Book's word for the one to whom a matter can be handed over and left in safe keeping, and the sūrah has already used it for God against the idolaters' protectors (42:9). The Praiseworthy is the name that answers what the rain should produce in the man who receives it, since a gift that comes after despair has no room left for the fiction that the receiver arranged it. The community learned to read the sky this way from the Prophet, whose prayer for rain when the land dried up is preserved in the two Ṣaḥīḥ collections, and who taught his people to ask God for water and to thank Him when it came. The verse's two names, taken together, tell the reader what the weather report is for: the God who holds the clouds deserves the praise of the man who had stopped expecting rain.

---

## Verse 42:29

> And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and all living beings He dispersed throughout both. And He is Most Capable of bringing all together whenever He wills

**Creatures Spread Across the Heavens and the Earth**
The verse adds another of God's signs to the list the sūrah is building: the creation of the heavens and the earth, and every living creature He has spread through them. The Book points at the same evidence in the sūrah of the Kneeling, where the creation of the reader himself and the creatures scattered around him are named together as signs for people of sure faith (45:4), and it says in the Sūrah of Light that God created every living creature out of water, some creeping, some walking on two legs and some on four (24:45). The Book's use of living things as proof is practical: the man who keeps animals or watches a flock knows how little he arranged of the arrangement in front of him. The verse does not ask the reader to perform an argument; it asks him to look at what he has already been living among.

**The Power to Gather Them Again**
The verse ends with the promise that follows from the sign: God is fully able to gather them whenever He wills. The Book attaches that ability to the same display of creation wherever it wants to close the door on the objection to resurrection — it says that the One who created the heavens and the earth is able to create their like (46:33), that the man who doubts is asked whether the One who made the heavens and earth is not able to make others like them (17:99), and it draws the conclusion for the reader in the sūrah of the Resurrection: does man think We cannot gather his bones? Yes indeed, We are able to put even his fingertips back together (75:3-4). The bridge between the first creation and the second is the power named in the verse: the God who has spread living creatures across two worlds is not short of the means to bring them back for an account.

---

## Verse 42:30

> Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your own hands have committed. And He pardons much

**What the Hands Have Earned**
The verse makes a claim a modern reader has to hold carefully: whatever affliction comes to a person comes from what his own hands have committed. The Book does not teach that every hardship is a punishment — it speaks of trials that purify, of prophets who suffered without sin, and of a man's illness that becomes the removal of his sins — but it does teach that the moral disasters of a community are the harvest of its deeds. The sūrah of the Women states the two sides in one verse: whatever good comes to you is from God, and whatever evil comes to you is from yourself (4:79), and the sūrah of the Romans describes the corruption that has spread on land and sea as the work of what people's hands have done, so that God may let them taste the consequences of some of their deeds and turn them back (30:41). The Book names what the world would be if it took the full account: if God held people to what they have earned, nothing on the surface of the earth would be left alive (35:45).

**The Pardon That Covers Most of It**
The verse's second half is the sentence that keeps the first from crushing the reader: and He pardons much. The word is deliberately broad. The Book says that God does not wrong people in the least (4:40), that His mercy is written down as encompassing all things (7:156), and that the men who turn back find the pardon waiting (39:53). What the verse does is hold human responsibility and divine pardon in one sentence, so that the wrongdoer cannot use mercy as an excuse to keep going and the repentant man cannot be driven to despair by what he has done. The sūrah returns to the same pair of facts in the passage that follows, where the men who are left to stray find no guide and the men who turn find the road (42:44-45). The verse is therefore the sūrah's quiet statement of the whole religion in one breath: the trouble is man-made, and the cure is not.

---

## Verse 42:31

> You can never escape ˹Him˺ on earth, nor do you have any protector or helper besides Allah

**Nowhere to Run On God's Earth**
The verse states a fact that takes away the last hope of the careful wrongdoer: you cannot escape God on the earth, and you have no protector or helper besides Him. The Book uses the same words about escape in the sūrah of the Spider, where the sentence closes an address to the deniers and adds that there is no protector or helper besides God (29:22), and it repeats the thought where men build their confidence on the size of their forces: they cannot escape, and no fortress will hold them (72:12; 46:32). The logic is simple once it is laid out. Everything the man is standing on, the ground under him, the air around him, the strength in his arm, is given to him by the One he is trying to avoid, so the distance he is putting between them is not a distance at all.

**The Comfort in the Same Sentence**
The verse is a threat to the man who is running and a comfort to the man who is not, and the second reading is the one the sūrah wants its readers to take. The God whom nobody can escape is the God whom nobody has to escape. The Book reads the turning to God in the same terms elsewhere: it tells the community that God is nearer to a man than his own jugular vein (50:16), and that when the servants ask about Him, He is near and answers the call of the man who calls on Him (2:186). The Messenger whom this sūrah addresses lived with the doctrine as a working fact: he was told that God was watching and hearing, and the Book says of him that his heart was never in doubt (53:11). The verse therefore closes the door of flight and leaves the door of turning open, and it leaves nothing in between.

---

## Verse 42:32

> And among His signs are the ships like mountains ˹sailing˺ in the sea

**Ships Like Mountains**
The verse points at something the men of the Hijaz had seen with their own eyes and had no explanation for: great ships moving across the sea like mountains. The Book names the same spectacle as evidence in several places, and the repetition shows how much weight it carries. The sūrah of the Most Merciful says that the ships raised like mountains on the sea belong to God (55:24), the sūrah of Luqmān says the ships sail by the grace of God so that He may show the reader some of His signs, and it draws the same conclusion the verse in front of us draws — that they are signs for everyone who is patient and grateful (31:31) — and the sūrah of the Pilgrimage lists the ships among the things God has subjected to people, along with the sky He holds back from falling on them (22:65). The sūrah of the Bee adds the provision the sea carries: the food and the ornaments taken from it, and the ships ploughing through the water so that people may seek God's bounty (16:14).

**The Sign That Costs Nothing to See**
The Book's habit of using ships is worth naming, because a ship is a case of a thing that is exactly as heavy as it looks. The sea does not hold water; the hull holds air; and between the two, the enormous thing moves with a cargo of people who did nothing to arrange the balance. The Book makes the same point about the sky two verses later in the Pilgrimage's list, where God is said to hold it back from falling except by His permission (22:65), and the sūrah of the Throne describes the One whose care never tires of preserving the heavens and the earth (2:255). The verse does not ask the reader to reason his way to God from the ship. It asks him to notice that the thing he is standing on does not hold itself up, and to remember who is holding it.

---

## Verse 42:33

> If He wills, He can calm the wind, leaving the ships motionless on the water. Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful

**The Wind That Can Simply Stop**
The verse continues the ship-sign by naming what is keeping the ship in motion: if God wills, He calms the wind, and the great vessels stand still on the face of the water. The Book makes the same point with the same picture in the sūrah of Luqmān, where the ships sailing through the sea by God's grace are named as the display of His signs, and the reader is told what kind of person the display is for: the one who is patient and grateful (31:31). A second verse of the sūrah of the Bee describes the ordering of wind, cloud and rain in one account and turns it into the same invitation to look (16:10-16). What the Book is doing each time is the same. It takes a mechanism people had learned to treat as a natural fact and puts a will back inside it: the wind that fills the sail is a decision.

**Signs for the Steadfast and the Grateful**
The verse names the two qualities that read the sign correctly: patience and gratitude. The pairing is not accidental. Patience covers the man in a becalmed ship with no wind and no land in sight, and gratitude covers the man whose sails are full and whose cargo arrives; the same pair of qualities is named in the sūrah of Luqmān's ship passage for exactly this reason (31:31). The Book names the pair again in the sūrah of Abraham, where the signs are called a reminder for everyone who is patient and grateful (14:5), and it closes the Family of ʿImrān by telling the believers to endure and to persevere (3:200). The verse therefore leaves the reader a way to use the weather he has: a wind that fails is an occasion for patience, and a wind that fills the sail is an occasion for thanks, and both of them are being handed to the man who notices who sends them.

---

## Verse 42:34

> Or He can wreck the ships for what the people have committed—though He forgives much—

**The Wreck That Is Also Measured**
The verse says that God can also destroy the ships for what their crews have earned, and then it adds the words that follow the sentence everywhere in the Book: though He forgives much. The pairing is the same one the verse three lines earlier used about affliction, and the sūrah returns to it because the sea was the place where the men of the peninsula felt most strongly that something other than themselves was in charge. The Book tells the story of the men who call on God in a storm with sincere devotion and promise to be grateful if they are saved, and then go back to associating others with Him once they reach land (10:22-23; 29:65; 31:32). The wreck the verse describes is the end of that cycle. The Book does not treat a sinking as a random accident of weather; it treats the sea as one more place where the reckoning can reach a man, and it makes the same point in the sūrah of the Children of Israel, where God saves the travellers at sea and then tells them that He can send them back out to drown them (17:66-67).

**Forgiveness as the Thing That Keeps It From Being Final**
The sentence that the verse attaches to the destruction is what saves the passage from despair: and He forgives much. The Book's account of the punishment of a community follows the same pattern, naming the offence and then naming the mercy that held back its full weight (30:41; 35:45). For the reader of the sūrah, the two clauses are a description of the two worlds he lives between: a God who can end him at any moment and a God who is not looking for the moment. The verse asks nothing of him beyond what the sūrah's signs have been asking all along — the patience and the gratitude that the previous verse named — and it leaves him with the knowledge that the sea he crosses is not neutral, and that its Owner is not hostile.

---

## Verse 42:35

> so those who dispute about Our signs may know that they have no refuge

**The Men Who Fight the Signs and the Refuge They Do Not Have**
The verse closes the ship-passage by naming who is meant by the whole display: the men who dispute about God's signs, and the sentence that they will have no place to hide. The Book keeps the two ideas tied together wherever it describes the obstinate argument, and it uses the same word for refuge that it used a few verses earlier: the men who argue will find out on the day that the arguing was done in front of the only court that could not be argued around (29:22; 41:48). What makes the verse land is the contrast with the sailors. A crew in a storm has somewhere to turn, and the Book records their cry; the man who has spent his life disputing the signs is the one person on the ship who has nothing to call on.

**Why the Signs Are Worth Noticing**
The passage has moved through rain, living creatures, ships and wind, and each of them was called a sign for the same two kinds of reader: the patient and the grateful (42:33; 31:31). The verse in front of us supplies the negative case. The man who has trained himself to argue with evidence will find no refuge from it, and the Book's final judgment on the argument of the makkans is stated in the same form in the sūrah of the Cave, where the deeds of the men who argued are described as scattered dust (18:103-105). The verse therefore does not end the passage on a threat for its own sake. It ends it by naming the price of a lifetime spent looking at rain, ships and animals and refusing to see the One behind them.

---

## Verse 42:36

> Whatever ˹pleasure˺ you have been given is ˹no more than a fleeting˺ enjoyment of this worldly life. But what is with Allah is far better and more lasting for those who believe and put their trust in their Lord

**The Pleasure That Passes**

The verse shifts from the sea and its signs to the question of human enjoyment, declaring that whatever pleasure the hearer has been given is only a fleeting enjoyment of this worldly life. The "whatever" is comprehensive, embracing wealth, health, status, and the simple comforts of existence, and the verse refuses to let any of these be treated as the ultimate good. This is a direct challenge to the Meccans, whose prosperity and pride in their way of life were the very things that made them resistant to the message.

**Better and More Lasting**

The verse calls what is with Allah "far better and more lasting," and the double description addresses both quality and duration, because the heavenly good excels the worldly not only in kind but in permanence. The "far better" speaks to the richness of the gift, the "more lasting" to its security, and together they answer the two fears that attach to earthly pleasure, that it is shallow and that it ends. This connects to the chapter's portrait of the gardens where the believer has whatever he desires, a desire fulfilled rather than frustrated. The verse so offers not a grim renunciation of joy but a promotion of it, from the brief and brittle to the abundant and eternal, and it does so in language that any hearer can weigh.

**The Believers the Next Verses Describe**
The condition the verse attaches to its promise — those who believe and put their trust in their Lord — is unpacked in the verses that follow, which describe what that trust looks like when it is lived. They turn away from great sins and shameful acts, and when they are angry they forgive; they answer their Lord's call and pray, and their affairs are settled by consultation among them; they spend out of what has been given them; and when wrong comes to them they defend themselves, though the sūrah then praises a better course, the man who forgives and makes peace (42:37-43). The reward named in our verse is the one the sūrah describes elsewhere for the same people: what is with God is better and more lasting (42:22; 16:96), and the Book makes that permanence its standing reason for preferring the next life to the markets of this one. The verse's two words do the work of the whole program: better names the quality of what is kept with God, and more lasting names its security. The reader who wants both is told in the following verses who receives them.

---

## Verse 42:37

> who avoid major sins and shameful deeds, and forgive when angered

**Who These People Are**
The sūrah has just described the punishment of the wrongdoers, and now it describes the people on the other side, and it does so with a list of qualities rather than a list of beliefs. The first quality is what they keep away from: the major sins and the shameful acts. The Book gives the same pair at the head of the list in the sūrah of the Star, where God names the men who avoid the great sins and the indecencies, and adds that the smaller slips are covered by His forgiveness (53:32), and the sūrah of the Heights gives the prohibitions by name, beginning with open and secret indecency (7:33). The Book's ethics start here rather than with the heroic acts: before a man is asked to do something great, he is asked to stop doing what he knows is wrong.

**The Anger They Do Not Act On**
The second quality is a sentence about anger: they forgive when they are angry. The Book puts the same behaviour in its portrait of the people of the garden in the Family of ʿImrān, where the men who spend in good times and bad, control their anger and pardon people are named as the ones God loves (3:134), and it says that the man who answers evil with good finds his enemy become a close friend (41:34). The Prophet put the standard in a sentence the community kept: the strong man is not the one who throws people down, but the one who controls himself when he is angry, in a report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. What the verse is describing, then, is not a people who never feel anger. It is a people who feel it and are the ones holding the brake.

**Why Anger Is Given This Weight**
The sūrah lists the quality in the middle of its account of a community that had spent years under pressure from men who had power over them. Anger was the most available and most expensive reaction in that situation; a quarrel with a powerful neighbour could cost the community the security of its families. The Book answers the pressure by putting the restraint in the same list as the worship and the good deeds, so that the man who chokes back a rage, or answers an insult with a greeting, knows that he has done something in the religion and not merely something in the street.

---

## Verse 42:38

> who respond to their Lord, establish prayer, conduct their affairs by mutual consultation, and donate from what We have provided for them

**Answering the Lord and Praying**
The verse continues the portrait with two things the same people do: they answer their Lord, and they keep up the prayer. The Book treats the answer to God as the first act of a believer's life and the prayer as the act that keeps it in place, and the sūrah of the Cow puts the pair at the head of its description of the people who are guided (2:3-5). What the answering means is stated by the Book when it tells the servants that God answers the man who calls on Him and asks them to answer Him in return (2:186), and it is the same exchange the sūrah has just been presenting: the messenger calls, and the man who answers is the one who takes the invitation seriously.

**The Affairs Settled by Consultation**
The third phrase is the one that gave this sūrah its name. Their affairs are settled by consultation among them, which the Book states here as a permanent mark of the community's life rather than a piece of advice. The Prophet was commanded to consult his companions in the very passage that describes the aftermath of Uḥud, and that command is the practical root of the practice: consult them in the matter, and when you have decided, put your trust in God (3:159). The Book gives the same principle inside a household, where the mother and father are told to settle the weaning of a child by mutual agreement and counsel (2:233). The jurists built the chapter of consultation on this verse, and the practice of the community's early leaders followed it: Abū Bakr and ʿUmar consulted the companions on the great decisions, and the collectors of the reports preserved the arguments of the meetings.

**What They Spend**
The verse ends with the act that tests the two before it: they give from what God has provided. The Book names the same mark in the description of the believers in the sūrah of the Cow and the sūrah of the Spoils (2:3; 8:3), and it puts the giving in the same sentence as the prayer each time, because the prayer is the claim to belong to God and the giving is the proof that the claim has reached the wallet. The portrait in this verse is therefore a working picture: a community whose disputes are talked through, whose worship is kept up, and whose property is not held as an end in itself.

---

## Verse 42:39

> and who enforce justice when wronged

**A Short Verse With a Long Reach**
This is one of the shortest verses of the sūrah and one of the most exact: when wrong comes to them, they defend themselves. The Book returns to the same ground two verses later, where it says there is no blame on the man who enforces justice after he has been wronged (42:41), and it states the permission for retaliation in the plainest legal form in the sūrah of the Cow and the sūrah of the Bee: the retaliation is equal to the injury, and no more (2:194; 16:126). What the Book refuses in the same breath is the idea that the injured man must take whatever is done to him; the verse in front of us is the answer to that idea, stated in four words.

**The Line the Book Draws**
The Book's treatment of self-defence has three edges, and this verse is the middle one. The first edge is the wrong itself, which is forbidden; the second is the equal answer to the wrong, which is permitted here; and the third is the answer that goes past the wrong, which is forbidden and punished (42:42). The sūrah of the Pilgrimage adds the promise that God will help the man who is wronged again after he has taken his equal measure (22:60), and the verse that follows this one praises the man who chooses to pardon and reconcile instead (42:40). The Book holds both readings of the situation in front of the reader, and it does not pretend that the choice is easy: the permitted thing is permitted, and the better thing is named as better.

**Why the Verse Matters for a Wronged Community**
The community the sūrah was first addressed to had been driven from its homes, tortured in the streets of Makkah and besieged in a valley, and its members had spent years with their hands tied by their own weakness. A body of teaching that told such men only to forgive would have been heard as one more demand being made of the victims. This verse gives them back the ground they had lost: the wronged man has a right to answer, and the Book says so in the same chapter that praises the man who does not.

---

## Verse 42:40

> The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah. He certainly does not like the wrongdoers

**An Eye for an Eye, and the Better Road**
The verse states the rule of equal return in its shortest form: the reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. The Book gives the same measure in several places, and it keeps the account strictly proportional. The sūrah of the Cattle says that whoever brings a bad deed is punished for one like it, while the good deed is repaid tenfold (6:160), and the sūrah of the Stories says that the reward of an evil deed is the like of it (28:84). The jurists read the rule into the law of retaliation for injury and the law of blood money, always with the ceiling that the Book itself supplies: the answer may not exceed the wrong.

**The Man Who Pardons**
The second half of the verse opens the door to something higher: whoever pardons and sets the matter right has his reward with God. The Book praises the same act in the sūrah of Fussilat, where the men who answer evil with what is best are described as finding their enemy become a friend (41:34), and it tells the Prophet to meet the bad treatment of his opponents with the better course (23:96). The reward is stated as being with God rather than named, and the commentators took that as a sign of its size: the man who gives up a claim he is entitled to has left the compensation to the One who saw the wrong. The Prophet's own life is the book of the practice: he forgave the men of Makkah who had killed his companions and driven him out, and his example was the strongest argument the early community had.

**The Sentence That Closes the Verse**
The verse ends with the words that keep the mercy from being read as indifference: God does not like the wrongdoers. The pardon is not a sign that the wrong does not matter; it is the choice of the wronged man to hand the account to a better judge. The Book makes the same distinction in the sūrah of the Women, where God says that He does not like evil speech to be voiced aloud except by the man who has been wronged (4:148). The order of the verse therefore matters: the equal return is lawful, the pardon is better, and the wrong itself remains a thing God does not like.

---

## Verse 42:41

> There is no blame on those who enforce justice after being wronged

**The Missing Piece in the Chapter's Ethics**
The verse is short and it carries the weight of the passage: there is no blame on the men who enforce justice after they have been wronged. It has to be read together with the verse before it, because the two say different things that a reader could easily confuse. The earlier verse praises the man who pardons and calls his reward great; this verse protects the man who does not pardon from being blamed for it. The Book makes the same protection explicit in the sūrah of the Women, where the wronged man is the one exception to the rule against speaking evil aloud (4:148), and it gives the wronged community the right to answer in equal measure in the sūrah of the Bee (16:126) and the sūrah of the Cow (2:194).

**Why the Book Had to Say It Twice**
A community that has been taught to forgive will always contain men who use the teaching as a weapon against the victims, telling the injured man that his pursuit of his right is a failure of piety. The verse closes that door. The Book's ethics are not a demand that the weak stay weak; they are a demand that the strong do not take more than they are owed. The man who insists on his right before a judge is not being told by this sūrah that he has sinned, and the man who chooses to let it go is not told that he has thrown anything away. The two verses stand side by side in the chapter, and the order in which they stand is itself the answer: the pardon is mentioned first because it is better, and the pursuit is mentioned second because it is blameless.

**The Rule That Comes After**
The next verse closes the passage by naming where the blame does fall: on the men who wrong other people and go on oppressing them in the land (42:42). The Book keeps the line bright through all three verses. The victim is not blamed, the just enforcer is not blamed, and the aggressor is condemned without qualification. It is a small set of sentences for such a large subject, and the community's jurists went back to it for centuries when they wrote about the difference between lawful retaliation and revenge.

---

## Verse 42:42

> Blame is only on those who wrong people and transgress in the land unjustly. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment

**Where the Blame Actually Sits**
The verse has to be read with the three before it, because it is the fourth panel of one argument. First the Book praised the men who forgive and reconcile and said their reward is with God (42:40); then it said there is no blame on the man who defends himself after being wronged (42:41); and now it fixes the blame where it belongs — on the men who wrong people and go on oppressing others in the land. The order is deliberate. The community is not told that resistance is as bad as aggression, and it is not told that the victim who answers back is the guilty party. The wrong begins with the one who strikes first, and the Book keeps the accounting straight so that an oppressor cannot hide behind the language of peace.

**Wronging People, and the Wider Harm**
The verse names two things: wronging people, and going on to transgress in the land without right. The second phrase covers the damage that spreads past the first victim — the family, the town, the market, the ordinary people who learn to keep their heads down. The Book uses the same language for the men who spread corruption and cut off the roads (5:33), and it joins the two offences in its account of the tyrant who must be stopped: fight them until there is no more oppression and the judgment belongs to God (8:39; 2:193). This is also why the verse's closing punishment is stated in the plural — it is they who will suffer a painful punishment — because what the men described here are doing is not a private quarrel. It is a public wrong, and the Book answers it in public.

**The Justice the Verse Leaves Open**
What the verse does not say is that the wronged man must do nothing. The Book gives the wronged person a right to speak — it says that God does not like evil to be voiced aloud, except by the one who has been wronged (4:148) — and it gives the community the right to stop the aggressor, as this same sūrah has just said (42:41). What it refuses is a third thing: the revenge that goes beyond the wrong (42:40), and the greed that disguises itself as justice. The verse therefore draws two lines at once. The oppressor is blamed and will be answered, and the community that moves to stop him is doing a lawful thing, so long as it stops when the oppression stops. Everything the passage wants the reader to be careful about is squeezed into the phrase, going on transgressing in the land without right.

---

## Verse 42:43

> And whoever endures patiently and forgives—surely this is a resolve to aspire to

**Patience and Pardon as a Rare Resolve**
The verse names the man who endures patiently and forgives, and it calls what he has done a resolve worth aiming at. The phrase is the Book's word for a settled purpose, the kind of decision that holds a person's life together, and it is used elsewhere for the quality that carries a man through the trials that break other people. The sūrah of Fussilat puts the same act beyond the reach of most men, saying that the good answer cannot be attained except by those who are patient and truly fortunate (41:35), and the sūrah of Luqmān's counsel to his son lists patient endurance as one of the marks of a person who is determined in his affairs (31:17). The Book's treatment of forgiveness is therefore never a sentimental one: it is presented as the behaviour of a strong man who has decided in advance what kind of person he is going to be.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Passage**
The verse completes the sūrah's sequence on being wronged: the equal return is permitted (42:39), the pardon is praised (42:40), the wronged man is not blamed (42:41), the blame is on the oppressor (42:42), and now the man who takes the higher road is named. The Book's own practice shows what the resolve cost. The Prophet and the early community were wronged in ways that filled the reports — the boycotted clan, the companions tortured in the streets, the families driven from their homes — and the example the community kept for its own instruction was the pardon at the conquest of Makkah, when the men who had arranged the persecution were told to go free.

**The Resolve in Ordinary Life**
The verse does not reserve the quality for great events. A man who swallows an insult from a neighbour, a family that gives up a rightful claim to keep a relationship, a worker who refuses to answer a cruel word in kind, all of them are doing the thing this verse names. The Book calls the choice a resolve because it costs something every time it is made: the person giving up the claim is giving up a thing he could have had. The reward is not described in this verse, and the reason may be that the sūrah has already described it twice — with God (42:40), and in the garden full of green for the men who did good (42:22).

---

## Verse 42:44

> And whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide after Him. You will see the wrongdoers, when they face the torment, pleading, “Is there any way back ˹to the world˺?”

The verse opens with a statement about the one whom Allah leaves to stray, and the phrase carries the weight of a settled consequence rather than an arbitrary act, because the leaving to stray is the culmination of a refusal that the person himself sustained. The Qur'an elsewhere makes clear that God does not mislead anyone except as a response to the person's own turning away, so the "leaving" here is the withdrawal of guidance from one who has already rejected it.

**The Plea for a Way Back**

The wrongdoers are depicted pleading, "Is there any way back to the world?" and the plea reveals the full collapse of their earlier confidence, because the ones who mocked the return now beg for it. The question is rhetorical in the mouth of the scripture, because the answer is already known to be no, and the very asking exposes the despair of those who once thought the Hour a jest. This echoes the chapter's earlier image of the disbelievers who asked to hasten the Hour mockingly, now reversed, as the same people beg for time they squandered. The verse uses the plea to show that the rejection of the world's warning leads, in the end, to the craving for the world's return, a return that will not be granted. The longing is the proof of the loss.

---

**No Helper After Him**
The verse gives the other side of the abandonment: *wa-mā lahu min waliyyin min baʿdih* — and he has no protector after Him. The wrongdoer who had patrons in the world, men whose favour was worth paying for, finds the account stripped to himself. The verse's picture is of a man watching what is coming and asking for the one thing that cannot be given.
**The Question Asked Too Late**
The Qur'an gives the same scene from inside the grave: when death comes, the wrongdoer says, my Lord, send me back, so that I may work a good deed in what I left behind — and the answer is no (23:99). The plea in this verse is the second airing of the same request, made now in the open before the whole gathering, and answered the same way. The asking is not the beginning of a change of heart; it is the proof that the heart understood at last, and understood when understanding no longer counted.

## Verse 42:45

> And you will see them exposed to the Fire, fully humbled out of disgrace, stealing glances ˹at it˺. And the believers will say, “The ˹true˺ losers are those who have lost themselves and their families on Judgment Day.” The wrongdoers will certainly be in everlasting torment

**The Picture of the Men at the Fire**
The verse shows the wrongdoers on the day they are brought before the Fire: humbled out of disgrace, glancing at it out of the corner of their eyes. The Book uses the same word for the exposure of Pharaoh's people, who are shown the Fire morning and evening while they wait for the hour to be established (40:46), and it uses the same humiliation in the sūrah of the Prostration, where the wicked stand with their heads bowed, confessing that they now see and hear and asking to be sent back (32:12). The detail the verse chooses is the sideways glance. The men who spent their lives looking away from the warning are given one last look at it, and it is a look they cannot hold.

**What the Believers Say on That Day**
The verse then gives the sentence the believers will speak when they see the scene: the real losers are the men who have lost themselves and their families on the day of judgment. The Book's picture of the family at the resurrection is built on this separation. The sūrah of the Troops describes the wrongdoers' regret over the relatives they brought with them, and the sūrah of the Cave ends its account of the two men with the sentence that the loser is the one who loses himself and his family on the day of standing (18:103-105), where the men who rejected the signs of their Lord and the meeting with Him have their deeds given no weight. The Book's phrase for the loss is not the loss of property but the loss of the person and the household, and the verse states it as the verdict the saved will give when the two groups are in view of one another.

**The Sentence at the End**
The verse closes with the statement that the wrongdoers are in a lasting punishment, and the phrase the Book uses for it carries the sense of a thing that does not stop. The sūrah has been running between the two ends of the world since its opening verses, and this is the last picture it paints of the first group before it turns to the men who are left without a guide (42:46). The detail that makes the verse work is that nobody in the scene is being told anything new. The men at the Fire are looking at what they were warned about, and the believers are simply saying what the truth turned out to be.

---

## Verse 42:46

> They will have no protectors to help them against Allah. And whoever Allah leaves to stray, for them there is no way

**No Protectors Against God**
The verse says that the men facing the punishment will have no protectors to help them against God, and it repeats the sentence the sūrah used four verses earlier: the man God leaves to stray has no road (42:44). The Book makes the same statement with the same word in the sūrah of the Cave, where the men who disbelieved are told that they will find no helper against God (18:17 uses the same vocabulary of the cave's companions), and it says in the sūrah of the Troops that the men who took protectors besides God will find that their protectors have no power over anything (39:3; 36:74-75). The point of the repetition in this sūrah is the arrangement of the passage: the verse before shows the group at the Fire, and this verse names the reason nobody is pulling them out.

**Whom God Leaves to Stray**
The second half of the verse states one of the Book's hardest doctrines, and it has to be read with everything the sūrah has said about the two roads. The Book attributes the straying to the man's own choice whenever it explains the process: the sūrah of the Cow says that God leaves to stray through the parable only the men who are already breaking the covenant (2:26-27), the sūrah of the Man says that God lets wander the man who turns away from the reminder and follows his own desire (18:28), where the Prophet is told not to obey the man whose heart has been made heedless of God's remembrance and who follows only his own desire, and the sūrah of the Kneeling says that God seals the hearing and the heart of the man who takes his own desire as his god (45:23). The verse in front of us is the sentence at the end of that road, not the beginning of it. A man who is left has no road, and the sūrah's readers were still on one.

---

## Verse 42:47

> Respond to your Lord before the coming of a Day from Allah that cannot be averted. There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺

**Answer Before the Day Arrives**
The verse turns from the pictures of the last day to the reader, and it gives him one instruction: answer your Lord before a day comes from God that cannot be turned back. The Book uses the same urgency in the sūrah of the Troops, where the command is to turn to God in repentance and submit before the punishment arrives, since after that there is no help (39:54), and it gives the men of the past their warning in the same words: warn the people of the day when the punishment will come on the wrongdoers and they will ask their Lord to delay them a little while (14:44). The Book's habit is to state the deadline before it states the reward, and the reason is in the verse: the day has an appointment that no one can move.

**No Refuge and No Denial**
The verse names the two things the men at that hour will reach for and will not find: a place to take shelter, and a way to argue. The sūrah of the Cave gives the same pair for the wrongdoers on that day, where they have no protector and no way to turn the sentence aside (18:52-53), where the associate-gods are called on and give no answer, and the wicked see the Fire and find no way to avoid it, and the sūrah of the Troops records the last bargain men offer when they see the punishment, asking to be sent back one more time (39:58). Neither door opens. What the verse leaves its reader is the plain arithmetic of the situation he is still in: the day cannot be cancelled, and the time he is holding is the time he can still use. The Book says elsewhere that God gives no one a moment's delay when the term arrives (63:11; 10:49), and the same Book tells the man reading this verse that the answer he is being asked for costs him nothing but a decision.

---

## Verse 42:48

> But if they turn away, We have not sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a keeper over them. Your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And indeed, when We let someone taste a mercy from Us, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it. But when afflicted with evil because of what their hands have done, then one becomes totally ungrateful

**The Message and the Messenger's Limit**
The verse tells the Prophet what his job is not: if the people turn away, he is not their keeper, and his duty is only to deliver the message. The Book says the same thing in several places, and the sūrah of the Cattle states it in the plainest form when it tells him that God has not made him a keeper over them (6:107). The sūrah of Jonah gives the same limit in the words the Prophet is told to say, that his duty is only the clear delivery (16:82), and the sūrah of the Overwhelming Event tells him that he is a reminder and not a compeller (88:21-22). The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān gives the same instruction when the Prophet is told that if they argue with him, his answer is that he has submitted himself to God and so have the believers with him (3:20). The Book repeats the limit because the pressure on the man was to feel responsible for the choices of other people, and the verse releases him from a burden that was never his.

**The Human Being in Two Weathers**
The second half of the verse names the pattern the Book returns to again and again: when God gives a person a taste of mercy he becomes proud of it, and when a bad turn touches him because of what his own hands have done, then he turns out to be ungrateful. The Book diagnoses the same man in the sūrah of the Romans, where people call on God when trouble comes and forget Him when the trouble is lifted (30:33), and in the sūrah of Jonah, where a person turns away as soon as the distress is over (10:12). The sūrah of the Children of Israel states it in two sentences that catch the whole swing: when God grants favours, the man turns away in arrogance, and when evil touches him, he loses all hope (17:83). The verse in front of us adds the reason the second weather comes at all: the bad turn is described as happening for what the man's own hands have done, which ties the whole passage back to the sūrah's earlier sentence about affliction (42:30).

**Why the Two Sentences Stand Together**
The arrangement of the verse is the argument: the messenger is not responsible for the response, and the responser is responsible for himself. The two halves answer two different complaints — the Prophet's discouragement and the people's habit of treating good fortune as their own achievement. The Book closes one more door in the same passage by reminding the reader that the mercy he is proud of was a gift he did not arrange, and the sūrah's next verses complete the thought with the account of God's ownership of the rain and the ships and the provision (42:49-51). The readers being addressed had just been shown the men before the Fire with no protector to help them against God (42:44-46).

---

## Verse 42:49

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He creates whatever He wills. He blesses whoever He wills with daughters, and blesses whoever He wills with sons

**The Kingdom Belongs to Allah Alone**

The verse opens by asserting that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah alone, and the claim of exclusive ownership is the foundation on which the rest of the verse rests, because the One who owns all creation is the One who disposes of its details. This returns the chapter to its opening themes of divine sovereignty, which had framed the whole surah from the first verses, and it does so at the point where the discussion turns to the granting of children. The "alone" guards against any partner in the dominion, answering the idolaters' assignment of offspring to God by reaffirming that the kingdom and its distribution are His unshared prerogative. The hearer is so reminded that the question of who gets a son or a daughter is not a matter of human merit or the intercession of idols but of the sole King's will, and the verse prepares that conclusion by first establishing the King.

---

The verse's list of God's gifts — the creation of the pairs, the making of mates, the assignment of daughters or sons, barrenness — is the chapter's usual way of arguing from what people already see, and the Book uses the same catalogue in the sūrah of the Women when it names what God gives and withholds (4:124 context). The sūrah of the Prophets puts the same list at the centre of its argument about power: to God belongs the kingdom, He gives and He withholds, and no one can prevent His decision (21:88 fold; 42:9). The verse's closing names, the Knowing and the Powerful, close the argument as elsewhere in the sūrah.

## Verse 42:50

> or grants both, sons and daughters, ˹to whoever He wills˺, and leaves whoever He wills infertile. He is indeed All-Knowing, Most Capable

**Sons, Daughters, and the Womb That Stays Empty**
The verse is the second half of a pair. The verse before it says that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to God, who creates what He wills, and gives to whom He wills daughters or sons (42:49); this verse completes the list: both together, or neither, since God leaves whom He wills barren, and He is All-Knowing and Most Capable. The Book's treatment of children as a gift runs through its accounts of the prophets. Zachariah asked for an heir in old age and was given John (21:89-90), and the same prayer appears in the Family of ʿImrān in the words of a man who had no hope of the answer and received it (3:38). The Book records the mother of Mary giving her child to God before she was born (3:35-36), and it records the wives of the prophets who had no children at all. The list of possibilities in the verse is therefore not an abstraction for the reader; it is the map of the households he knew in the stories.

**The Household in the Book's Hands**
The verse takes the question that occupied the men and women of the peninsula most — who will have children, and which kind — and puts it where the sūrah has put every other question, in the hands of a God who knows what He is doing. The Book supplies the two comforts that go with the doctrine. It says that God knows what every woman carries and what the wombs give up, and that everything with Him is measured (13:8), and it teaches the community to ask for the offspring that will be a joy rather than simply the offspring that will be numerous (25:74). The verse closes with the two names that fit the subject: the All-Knowing, who knows why the answer came the way it did, and the Most Capable, who can give a child to a woman of ninety and can withhold one from a young couple for a reason that is not a punishment.

---

## Verse 42:51

> It is not ˹possible˺ for a human being to have Allah communicate with them, except through inspiration, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger-angel to reveal whatever He wills by His permission. He is surely Most High, All-Wise

**The Three Ways God Speaks to a Human Being**
The verse sets out the whole mechanism of revelation in one sentence, and it is the sūrah's answer to the men who demanded to hear God speak for themselves. It is not for any human being that God should speak to him except in one of three ways: by inspiration placed in the heart, from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger who reveals what God wills by His permission. The Book supplies the examples for each. The first is the inspiration given to the mother of Moses, who was told what to do with her child without any messenger arriving (28:7), and the dreams of the prophets that the Book treats as revelation. The second is the speech from behind a veil that Moses heard at the burning bush, where he was called and answered (20:11-13), and the same account in the sūrah of the Heights, where Moses asks to see God and is told that he cannot (7:143). The third is the angel who brings the revelation down, which the Book names as Gabriel in the sūrah of the Cow (2:97) and describes in the sūrah of the Poets as the trustworthy spirit bringing it down into the Prophet's heart (26:192-194). The sūrah says in the same place that God spoke directly to some messengers and raised others in rank (2:253), which is the Book's own summary of the differences between the three modes.

**Why the Modes Matter to the Reader**
The verse is one of the clearest statements in the Book about what a prophet is and is not. The prophet does not see God; he receives what God sends, and the verse keeps the distance intact by naming the three channels in which a message can travel. The closing names, Most High and All-Wise, state the reason for the arrangement: the height of God is what makes a direct encounter impossible for a created being, and the wisdom is what chose the channels through which the message would come to a whole community rather than overwhelm a single man. The reader who has been following the sūrah's argument about the Book will find the verse's answer in the last line: the revelation that reached the Prophet came through the third of the three modes, and it came with permission rather than on its own initiative.

---

## Verse 42:52

> And so We have sent to you ˹O Prophet˺ a revelation by Our command. You did not know of ˹this˺ Book and faith ˹before˺. But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants. And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path—

**The Spirit Sent by God's Command**
The verse names what came to the Prophet: a spirit from God's command, sent to him by his Lord. The Book uses the same word for the revelation in the sūrah of the Bee, where God sends the angels with the spirit of His command to whom He wills of His servants (16:2), and it uses it for Gabriel, who is called the trustworthy spirit in the sūrah of the Poets (26:193). The commentators discussed which of the two the verse means and noted that the readings work together: the revelation is a spirit because it gives life to the heart, and it came by an angel who is also called a spirit. What the verse leaves beyond doubt is the source. The message was not a conclusion the Prophet reached; it was sent by a command.

**What the Prophet Did Not Know**
The verse then says the hardest thing about the man it is describing: before the revelation he did not know what the Book was or what faith was. The Book makes the same statement from another angle in the sūrah of the Spider, where it says that the Prophet could not read or write before this, so that the men who follow falsehood would have no ground for doubt (29:48), and the sūrah of the Stories says that he did not expect the Book to be given to him, and that it came as a mercy from his Lord (28:86). The two verses together make the point the sūrah needs: the man who brought the Book was not a scholar who assembled it, and the change in him was the work of the light he was given.

**The Light and the Path**
The verse's third statement is that God has made the revelation a light by which He guides whom He wills of His servants. The Book calls itself a light in several places: the sūrah of the Women describes the proof and the light sent down to humanity (4:174), the sūrah of the Table names the light and the clear Book that have come to the community (5:15), and the sūrah of the Cattle draws the picture of the man who walks among people by the light his Lord gave him, in contrast to the man sitting in darkness (6:122). The verse closes with the sentence that the Prophet leads to a straight path, which the next verse describes as the path of God to whom everything in the heavens and the earth belongs (42:53). The order is the sūrah's closing argument: the light came from God, it guides by God's will, and the road it opens ends at the One who owns everything it crosses, in the two closing verses of the sūrah (42:52-53).

---

## Verse 42:53

> the Path of Allah, to Whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Surely to Allah all matters will return ˹for judgment˺

**The Road and Its Owner**
The verse completes the sentence the previous verse began: the straight path is the path of God, to whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. The sūrah opened with the same statement of ownership, in the sentence that everything in the heavens and the earth is His (42:4), and it used the ownership against the men who took protectors beside Him (42:9). The closing verse therefore ties the two ends of the chapter together. The God whose revelation the reader has been following is the owner of the ground the road crosses, and the road is not a private arrangement between a messenger and a tribe.

**Where All Matters End**
The verse's last words say that all matters will return to God, which is the sentence the Book uses to close its accounts of the world in several of its chapters (3:109; 10:56 states the return and the giving of life and death). The sūrah has spent its length on the men who disputed about God and the men who answered Him, on the signs in the rain and the ships and the wombs, and on the messenger who was told that his duty was only to deliver. The verse ends all of it in the same place the sūrah began, with the One to whom the whole matter goes back, and the reader who finishes the chapter is left standing where the first verse put him: in a world whose Owner is not absent from anything it contains.
